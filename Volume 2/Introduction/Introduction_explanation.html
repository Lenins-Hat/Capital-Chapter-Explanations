Volume 2 • Introduction

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Core Problem

Ernest Mandel's Introduction to Volume 2 of Capital is not a neutral exposition. It is a polemical intervention on three fronts at once. The text opens with a strategic embarrassment: Frederick Engels's own private correspondence of 1884–85 predicted that the second volume would be "purely scientific," would "provoke great disappointment," would contain "not much material for agitation," and would remain "a sealed book" to the official economic literature of Germany. Engels was right — and worse than right. A decade elapsed before Tugan-Baranowski and Bulgakov made the first serious applications of the volume's conceptual innovations; nearly another decade passed before these concepts penetrated Germany and the Western world, and only through an international debate in which Tugan-Baranowski himself began revising Marx's "key theories." Mandel's thesis, thus, operates on a paradox: Volume 2 is "not only a 'sealed book', but also a forgotten one... To a large extent, it remains so to this very day."

The central problem Mandel sets out to solve is this: why is Volume 2 indispensable to Marx's theoretical construction, and what happens — intellectually and politically — when it is skipped? The famous charge is that students "pass straight from Volume 1 to Volume 3," treating Volume 2 as an interstitial technicality. Mandel argues the opposite: it is precisely Volume 2 that supplies the missing theoretical machinery — the theory of realization, the theory of the reproduction of the total social capital, the internal contradictions of the commodity as a motor of cyclical crisis — that makes Volume 3's analysis of the profit rate, competition, and distribution intelligible. To evacuate Volume 2 is to evacuate "all problems specific to the inner contradictions of the commodity — problems of the market, of the realization of value and surplus-value."

Marx himself clarified this. In his letter to Engels of 30 April 1868, he distinguished Book I from the later volumes in a single sentence: in Book I we assume that if £100 becomes £110, the latter "will find already in existence in the market the elements into which it will change once more. But now we investigate the conditions under which these elements are found at hand, namely the social intertwining of the different capitals, of the component parts of capital and of revenue (= s)." This "intertwining" — conceived as a movement of both commodities and money — is the analytic object of Volume 2, and it is what enables Marx to work out "at least the essential elements, if not the definitive form of a coherent theory of the trade cycle."

The Intellectual Objective

Mandel's objective is threefold. First, to reconstruct the conceptual architecture of Volume 2 — the three forms of capital (money, productive, commodity); the "metamorphosis" of capital; the dual aspect of turnover (value-realization and use-value adequacy); the reproduction schemas and their proportionality conditions; the productive/unproductive labour distinction; the reproduction of the total social capital. Second, to defend this architecture against misreadings — most importantly against the "neo-harmonicist" reading that used the schemas to "prove" that capitalism can grow smoothly if only the correct proportions are maintained. Third, and most ambitiously, to demonstrate that Volume 2 contains a theory of crisis — grounded in the contradiction between production's unlimited tendency and the restricted consumption of the masses, in the periodic hoarding of money, in the discontinuous renewal of fixed capital, and in the tendential fall of the rate of profit — that is superior to any single-cause theory, whether "under-consumptionist" or "disproportionist."

This places Mandel in direct and complex relation to several intellectual genealogies. He positions Marx on a lineage running from Quesnay's Tableau économique through Walras, Leontiev, and Keynes — Marx making "a leap forward" over Quesnay that is "immediately apparent," while neo-classical and vulgar macro-economics contains "elements of enormous regression." He must adjudicate between Marx and his followers (Hilferding, Bauer, Bukharin, Luxemburg, Grossmann, Dobb, Lange, Minc, Morishima, Sweezy, Baran, Cliff). He must intervene in the debates of his own moment: the productive/unproductive labour controversy of the 1970s, the state-expenditure controversy, the "permanent arms economy" debate, and the socialist-planning debates in the USSR and Eastern Europe. And he must connect the whole to the political present — May 1968, the generalized recession of 1974–75 — as evidence that Marx's "awe-inspiring prediction" has been borne out empirically.

Fit Within the Broader Framework

Mandel's Introduction fits into three frames simultaneously.

Historically, it belongs to the tradition of Marxist economic reconstruction that began with the publication of the Grundrisse (1941, 1953), Rosdolsky's The Making of Marx's Capital (1968), and the wave of Western Marxist engagement with the "circuit of capital" in the 1960s–70s. Mandel explicitly credits Rosdolsky as a key influence and interlocutor.

Politically, it is an intervention against the "neo-harmonicist" reading of Capital — the view, derived from Tugan-Baranowski, that organized capitalism could eliminate crisis. This reading, Mandel insists, is not merely theoretically wrong; it was politically disastrous, justifying everything from "state capitalism" apologetics to the Dobb-Lange-Minc doctrines of "socialist planning."

Methodologically, it is an exercise in what Mandel calls Marx's "method of successive approximation." The schemas operate at the level of "capital in general" (Das Kapital im Allgemeinen) — provisionally abstracted from competition and "many capitals." The reader must never mistake abstractions for historical assertions, nor treat the schemas as a descriptive model of capitalism's actual motion. Most critics of Marx — from Luxemburg to Harris — err precisely by collapsing these levels.

The Introduction's underlying thesis is thus deceptively simple: Volume 2 demonstrates that growth is possible under capitalism, but only within fragile proportional structures that capitalism's own laws of motion perpetually destroy. Equilibrium is the exception; disproportionality the rule; growth inherently uneven. The "sealed book" is sealed precisely because it delivers the most subversive message in the whole of Capital: that capitalism's dynamism is inseparable from its self-negating tendency.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Place of Volume 2 in Marx's General Analysis of Capitalism

Mandel opens with an archaeological gesture, unearthing Engels's letters as a kind of textual prophecy. The letters function rhetorically as an admission by Marx's closest collaborator that Volume 2 would be inaccessible to the "vulgar reader," the Kathedersozialisten, and the "apologists of Bismarck's Staatssozialismus." Engels predicted a "cautious silence" from official economic literature. That silence, Mandel argues, has been over-fulfilled: ten years before Tugan-Baranowski's first Russian application; nearly another decade before the concepts penetrated Germany through the "international debate."

Argument. The interpretive move here is to distinguish the volume's theoretical status from its reception history. A text can be a "sealed book" in the sense that its argumentative apparatus is difficult, without being theoretically marginal. Mandel wants to show that Volume 2 is in fact structurally central. His key evidence is Marx's own letter to Engels.

Concepts & Terminology. The letter introduces the phrase "social intertwining of the different capitals, of the component parts of capital and of revenue (= s)." Each term must be unpacked. "Different capitals" are the individual capitals of a capitalist economy in their mutual dependence — department I selling means of production to department II, department II selling consumer goods to the workers and capitalists of both. "Component parts of capital" refers to the internal division c (constant capital) + v (variable capital) + s (surplus-value). "Revenue (= s)" marks the key move: the surplus-value becomes revenue — income to be spent — for the capitalist class, and its circulation between departments is what equilibrates (or fails to equilibrate) reproduction. The parenthetical "(= s)" is decisive: it announces that the analysis of revenue in Volume 2 is the analysis of the disposal of surplus-value.

The distinction Volume 1 / Volume 2, Mandel insists, is not simply one of scale or detail but of question. Volume 1 asks how value and surplus-value are produced; Volume 2 asks how they are realized. The workbook/workshop distinction is deliberate: Volume 1 is centered on the factory; Volume 2 on the market-place. The "dramatis personae" shift accordingly: money-owner and money-lender, wholesale merchant, trader, "functioning capitalist" or entrepreneur.

Definition (Mandel's): The entrepreneur is broadly defined — not simply the industrialist but any capitalist who, having a certain amount of capital at disposal (own or borrowed — the distinction is made irrelevant by the abstraction), "tr[ies] to increase that capital through the purchase of means of production and labour-power, the production and then the sale of commodities, the reinvestment of part of realized profit in additional machinery, raw materials and labour-power, and the production of an increased quantity of commodities." This definition anticipates the entire arc of the volume: reproduction is not a single act but a circuit of transformations.

Nuance. Mandel acknowledges the potential surprise: workers appear in Volume 2 "essentially as buyers of consumer goods and, therefore, as sellers of the commodity labour-power," not as producers of value. This is a significant methodological casualty — one that will become catastrophic in the productive/unproductive labour debate later in this Introduction. The worker as producer of value belongs to Volume 1; the worker as buyer of consumer goods is a pivotal actor in the circulation process. Both roles are necessary; neither displaces the other.

The Inner Connection Between Production and Realization. Mandel now articulates what he calls the "basic contradiction" of commodity production. On one side, human production has outgrown the primitive subsistence-farming/handicraft mode of isolated producer-consumers; the division of labour, productivity growth, and improvements in transport and communication have rendered labour objectively socialized. On the other side, private ownership of the means of production and circulation, plus the rise of money capital, render private appropriation the starting point and goal. Thus "while labour is objectively more and more socialized, it remains to a greater degree than ever before organized on the basis of private production."

The implication is precise: the social character of private labour "cannot be immediately and directly established." It is recognized only a posteriori, "through the sale of the commodity, the realization of its value." Value production thus expresses a contradictory unity — goods are simultaneously products of social labour and of private labour, and their social recognition as values is deferred to the market. This is why the study of "capital in general" — provisionally abstracted from competition — must encompass both production and circulation. There is an "indissoluble unity" between production and circulation.

Nuance. Footnote 8 introduces a counter-position: Karl Renner's Die Wirtschaft als Gesamtprozess (1924) which "bends the stick too far in the other direction" by focusing entirely on circulation and treating it as the springboard for socialist transformation. Mandel's point is symmetrical: one must not eliminate either moment. Both production-fundamentalism and circulation-fundamentalism are errors.

From Circulation to Reproduction. Mandel now transitions to the volume's real subject-matter: the reproduction and circulation ("turnover") of the total social capital. Formally the title of only the third Part, but — Mandel argues — expressing the underlying subject-matter of the whole volume. Marx's reasoning: the reproduction of each individual capital must be seen as part of the reproduction of the social capital in toto. This methodological ordering is necessary not only because it precedes analysis of competition's effects on the division of surplus-value, but because a broader question must be answered: "How can an anarchic social system, based upon private determination of investment, 'factor-combination' and output, assure the presence of the objective, material elements necessary for further production and growth? What are the absolute preconditions of such growth?"

This is the question that demands the reproduction schemas. The schemas are Marx's answer.

The Logic of Accumulation. Mandel then rigorously pursues the concept of accumulation. Capitalist production is production for profit — value production oriented towards an accretion of value. Growth therefore means accumulation of capital. The key concepts are capitalization of (part of) surplus-value and expanded reproduction.

For economic growth, a portion of surplus-value must be spent productively: transformed into (i) additional constant capital — buildings, equipment, energy, raw materials, auxiliary products — and (ii) additional variable capital — money capital available to hire an increased labour force. Accumulation is "nothing other than this (partial) capitalization of surplus-value, i.e. the (partial) transformation of profit into additional capital."

Footnote 10's precision: Accumulation also requires that means of production producing additional means of production be added to means of production producing consumer goods or replacing those used up in current production. This is the department I / department II division in its most elemental form, and Mandel introduces it here — before the schemas — so that the reader grasps the material structure of expanded reproduction before seeing its algebraic expression.

Definition: Expanded reproduction. "A process whereby the turnover of capital... leads, after a certain number of intermediary stages minutely studied in Volume 2, to a larger and larger scale of productive operation. More raw materials are transformed by more workers using more machinery into more finished products, with greater overall value than in the previous turnover cycle. This results in higher total sales and final profits, which in turn allow a higher absolute sum (if not in all cases a higher percentage) of profit to be added to capital."

Two nuances deserve emphasis here. First, the qualification "if not in all cases a higher percentage" — the rate of profit may fall while the mass of profit rises. This distinction is foundational to Volume 3's analysis of the tendency of the rate of profit to decline, and Mandel wants it planted early.

Second, the phrase "the spiral of growth continues..." introduces what will become a motif: the spiral — a term Mandel notes Marx and Luxemburg borrowed from Sismondi (see §13 below).

The Dialectic of Use-Value and Exchange-Value. Mandel closes this section with the first major thematic claim: the whole of Volume 2's analysis is the unfolding of the "dialectical unity-and-contradiction of opposites contained in the commodity form" — use-value and exchange-value, "doubled in that of commodities and money." He notes that "insufficient attention has been paid" to this by academic and Marxist commentators alike, singling out Rosdolsky as an important exception (footnote 11).

Historicizing the Abstraction (Footnote 12). A critical precision: capital may appear and survive in pre- or post-capitalist societies, but "essentially outside the realm of production." It cannot dominate the main sectors of production except when productive capital arises — "the form proper to the capitalist mode of production." This is a reminder that the abstraction "capital in general" describes a historically specific form, not a perennial structure.

2.2 The Three Forms of Capital

Mandel now formalizes the morphological analysis that defines Volume 2's architecture. Capital, in the capitalist mode of production, appears in three forms:

  1. Money capital — "the original form and final purpose of the whole devilish undertaking."
  2. Productive capital — "the basic precondition of the constantly enlarging spiral." Without penetration into the sphere of production, the social product can only be re-appropriated, not increased. Otherwise, capitalists would be "parasites upon and plunderers of pre-capitalist (or post-capitalist) forms of production."
  3. Commodity capital — "the basic curse of capitalism." Commodities must pass through the phase in which they contain unrealized surplus-value. Before money capital can return to its original form, swollen by surplus-value, it must pass through "commodity-value" and submit to "the acid test by being sold."

Concept: Metamorphosis of capital. Marx's metaphor — the butterfly through larva, chrysalis, moth — is not decorative. It captures that the three forms are successive in the rotation of capital but also co-existent. Mandel labors this point: the discontinuous nature of reproduction and the discontinuity's organic link with the essence of capitalism is "one of the most important and brilliant sections of Volume 2."

Argument: Why co-existence? Because capitalist production is generalized commodity production. Money capital cannot merely precede and succeed the widespread appearance of capital — it must exist side by side with it. Money capital is not just the result of sale; its social existence is a precondition of that sale. Commodity capital is not simply the outcome of productive capital; it is also its necessary basis. Indeed, current production is possible — especially for commodities with an above-average life span or production period — only if not all commodities of the previous cycle have been sold to final consumers, i.e. if stocks and reserves of raw materials, energy, auxiliary products, intermediary products, and consumer goods are available at scale. Thus: "Continuity of the production process may be said to depend upon discontinuity or desynchronization of the turnover cycle of money capital, productive capital and commodity capital."

This is one of the most conceptually dense claims in the Introduction. Note the exact inversion: continuity depends upon discontinuity. The apparent paradox is resolved by recognizing that different capitals are at different points of the circuit at any moment. Some sell while others buy; some produce while others hoard; some invest while others devalorize. This desynchronization is what allows the total system to reproduce continuously. If all capitals tried to sell simultaneously, or all tried to hoard simultaneously, the system would seize up. The "anarchy of production" is thus not simply a defect but — in a limited, negative sense — the medium of systemic continuity.

Capitalist Relations and the Money Relation. Mandel emphasizes the structural necessity of money capital prior to production. The separation of "free" workers from their means of production and livelihood implies a constraint on the owners of the means of production to purchase labour-power before productive operations begin. They must have adequate money capital to effect the transaction. Marx's formulation is quoted: "In the relation between capitalist and wage-labourer, the money relation, the relation of buyer and seller, becomes a relation inherent in production itself." This is a crucial theoretical point: the market relation between capitalist and worker is not external ornamentation but internal to the capitalist relation of production.

Dialectics of Money and Commodities. Mandel's synthesis: Volume 2 examines the constant intertwining of appearance and disappearance of the three forms — "from the sphere of circulation into that of production, and back into the sphere of circulation, until the commodity is finally consumed." Each form passes into the other "without expelling it entirely from the sphere of circulation, let alone from the overall social arena." The "dialectics of money (money capital) and commodities (commodity capital) is the basic contradiction examined in Capital Volume 2."

The Time Factor. Mandel now introduces one of his most theoretically consequential claims: the crucial importance of the "time factor." Capital functioning cannot be understood if complete abstraction is made of time sequences and schedules, the duration of production and turnover cycles, the length of the turnover period. The distinction between circulating capital and fixed capital is grounded exclusively in the amount of time required for each part of money capital to revert to its original form. Circulating capital — spent on raw materials and wages — is recovered after each production and circulation cycle. Fixed capital is recovered in its entirety only after n cycles, where n depends on the longevity of machinery and buildings.

Let me state this formally. If the turnover time of a given mass of circulating capital is t, and the (average) useful life of fixed capital is n·t, then:

  • Circulating capital completes n turnovers in the period during which fixed capital completes one.
  • Fixed capital is advanced in toto at the beginning and recovered only gradually through depreciation charges across n cycles.

Marx's hypothesis — which Mandel reports approvingly — is that the average longevity of machinery (not buildings) is equivalent to and indeed determines the average duration of the trade cycle. This is the germ of the trade cycle theory developed in §11 of the Introduction.

Mandel then offers an extraordinary closing reflection on "time" in Marx's Capital: time appears (i) as the measure of production, value and surplus-value (labour time); (ii) as the nexus connecting production, circulation and reproduction (cycles of turnover); (iii) as the medium of the laws of motion of capital (trade cycles, cycles of class struggle, long-term historical cycles); (iv) as the very essence of man (leisure time, life span, creative time, time of social intercourse). This quadruple role is, one might say, the philosophical horizon of Volume 2.

From Abstract to Concrete: The Formula. The section closes by presenting the successive stages of concreteness through which circulation is represented. First, the most general form encountered in Volume 1:

$$M - C - M'$$ $$(M + \Delta M)$$

Money buys commodities so that they may be sold with an accretion — a profit — part of which is added to initial money capital.

Translating into real operations, we replace C with the specific operation of the industrialist: the purchase of means of production (Mp) and labour-power (Lp), so that the latter may produce additional value. The formula becomes:

$$M - C_{Lp/Mp} \dots \text{production} \dots C' - M' \; (M + \Delta M)$$

where ΔM = accumulated surplus-value.

This formula will reappear throughout the volume in its departmental form (department I and department II each executing their own M–C–M′).

2.3 The Dual Aspect of Capital Turnover in Marx's Economic Theory

This section is where Mandel most explicitly establishes his central interpretive claim: the turnover/reproduction problem is dual, rooted in the contradiction between use-value and exchange-value.

(a) The Value Side. For simple or expanded reproduction, the total value embodied in produced commodities must be realized — sold at their value. Here Mandel intervenes sharply against predecessors: contrary to Hilferding (Das Finanzkapital, 1923), Otto Bauer (Die Akkumulation des Kapitals, 1913), and Bukharin (Imperialism and the Accumulation of Capital, 1972), Marx did not regard the process of realization as "automatic"; nor did he derive it "from his reproduction schemas," as some naively suggested. A substantial section of the final Part of Volume 2, and most controversies since Luxemburg, turn around the question of how value represented by the schemas can be realized by purchasing power generated in the production process.

(b) The Use-Value Side. Simultaneously, reproduction requires that the use-value of commodities produced fulfils the material conditions for restarting production. Mandel gives two vivid hypotheticals to make this concrete.

Hypothetical 1 (the wrong basket): Imagine the technological base is below full automation and there are no food reserves; imagine the commodity package consists entirely of raw materials and machinery. Then workers and capitalists "would starve and disappear before the available machinery could be used to restart agricultural production."

Hypothetical 2 (the wrong basket, reversed): Imagine the entire output is consumer goods and raw materials, produced with sophisticated machinery, with no stocks of machinery or spare parts. Then "machinery and production would break down before the well-fed workers could build new machines out of simple raw materials."

Both are absurd — deliberately so — because the point is structural: the material composition of output must satisfy the requirements of reproduction. Use-value adequacy is not a trivial point but an independent condition of equilibrium. This is what academic economics, in its methodological dichotomy between "physical coefficients" and "money flows," fails to grasp as a unity.

Expanded Reproduction's Additional Material Requirements. Mandel adds three: (i) means of production to replace used-up equipment and raw materials; (ii) further means of production required to enlarge the scale of material production; (iii) consumer goods to feed both employed workers and additional recruits. To this, he adds a fourth (often neglected) element: a potential source of additional labour. The "industrial reserve army of labour" has a dual function: as regulator of wages (assuring the rate of surplus-value remains above a certain level) and as material precondition of expanded reproduction. Where "traditional" sources of the reserve army are drying up — e.g. when independent peasants and handicraftsmen decline, or when machine substitution slows — "new sources can always be tapped through sweeping transformation of housewives into wage-labourers; mass immigration of labour; extensive re-deployment of student youth onto the labour market, and so on" (footnote 15, referencing Mandel's Late Capitalism).

This is one of Mandel's most concrete analytical moves: he converts the abstract requirement of "additional labour" into a real, empirically locatable set of social processes. His incorporation of women's labour, immigration, and student labour anticipates later feminist and migration studies of the reserve army.

Marx's Innovation and Its Misfortunes. Mandel now states his critical assessment: Marx made a "giant step forward," but "until this very day, most academic economists have still not fully grasped this basic innovation." They broke the totality of reproduction into a "disconnected dichotomy." On the one hand, physical coefficients — Leontiev's input-output tables — i.e. use-values. On the other hand, Keynesian and post-Keynesian money flows — exchange-values "disembodied from the commodities in the production of which they originated."

Concepts: Leontiev's input-output tables. These represent the physical interdependence of branches — how much steel each industry consumes, how much coal, etc. Mandel's criticism (drawing on Koshimura, footnote 5): Leontiev's tables "reflect only the use-value inter-relationships ('exchanges') between different departments, and abstract from the question of the source of the purchasing power necessary to mediate these 'exchanges'." Crucially, they "ignore both the capital structure as a whole, and the component parts of commodities, i.e. c, v, and m." So Leontiev captures use-value structure but loses value structure.

Nuance: The Samuelson Critique (Footnote 16). Samuelson's Economics (4th ed., 1958) tries to correlate revenue flows and commodity flows via an inter-related system of 'supply-demand markets.' But — Mandel's polemical observation — in Samuelson's scheme, "the public buys consumer goods while selling land, labour and capital goods (i.e. factors of production) to business! 'Business' in turn buys land, labour and capital from 'the public' and sells consumer goods to it." Samuelson "does not seem to have noticed that, under capitalism, 'the public' (i.e. the mass of consumers) does not own 'capital goods' (i.e. raw materials and equipment) and that these are sold by certain 'businesses' to others." In Samuelson's system, capital goods are "sold" without having been produced. Mandel concludes: Marx's schemas are not only more rigorous but more realistic.

Nuance: Oskar Lange (Footnote 17). Lange's Theory of Reproduction and Accumulation (1969) abstracts from the dual flow of commodities and money and assumes a relationship of "pure barter" between the two departments. This is another error of the same family.

The Core Purpose of Volume 2. Mandel now formulates what he believes is Marx's principal aim: to show that the correlation between value-structure of output and income-structure is not automatic; that congruence depends upon "certain exact proportions and structures, both of exchange-values and of use-values"; that "wages never buy machines under capitalism"; and that these exact proportions "are extremely difficult to realize in the actual practice of capitalism."

Joan Robinson. Mandel's polemic against Joan Robinson deserves detailed treatment. Robinson reproached Marx for having "failed to realize how much the orthodox theory stands and falls with Say's Law and set himself the task of discovering a theory of crises which would apply to a world in which Say's Law was fulfilled, as well as the theory which arises when Say's Law is exploded."

Concept: Say's Law. The classical proposition that supply creates its own demand — that total production automatically generates sufficient purchasing power to buy total output. Under Say's Law, general glut is impossible; only partial, sectoral imbalances can occur. If Marx accepted Say's Law, his reproduction schemas would be a system of automatic equilibration — precisely the "neo-harmonicist" reading Mandel will attack later.

Mandel's riposte: Robinson herself, "following Keynes's concept of 'effective demand', fails to realize how much Marx's theory of the commodity as a unity-and-contradiction of use-value and exchange-value not only underpins his concept of the necessary fluctuation of supply and demand at a macro-economic level, but actually intertwines it with his theory of income distribution (demand distribution) in capitalist society." Under capitalism, income distribution has a class structure determined by the structure of the mode of production, governed in the medium term by the class interests of the capitalists. Therefore: "Any increase in 'effective demand' which, instead of increasing the rate of profit, causes it to decline will never lead to a 'boom' under capitalism." This basic truth — Mandel notes — was understood by Ricardo as well as Marx, though not by many latter-day Keynesians.

This is a crucial theoretical move. Robinson separates Marx from Ricardo by claiming Marx accepted Say's Law in one form and needed a crisis theory for a world where Say's Law held. Mandel rejoins by allying Marx with Ricardo on the primacy of profitability over effective demand. The class-determined structure of demand is the mediation that Robinson misses.

Equilibrium as Exception. Mandel now articulates the central theorem of the whole Introduction: Marx "never sought to prove that these proportions are automatically and constantly guaranteed by the 'invisible hand' of market forces." On the contrary, he insisted that these proportions are (i) difficult to realize, (ii) impossible permanently to retain, and (iii) "automatically upset by those same forces that bring them occasionally into being."

Footnote 19 quotes Volume 2 (pp. 570–71): "The fact that the production of commodities is the general form of capitalist production already implies that money plays a role, not just as means of circulation, but also as money capital within the circulation sphere, and gives rise to certain conditions for normal exchange that are peculiar to this mode of production, i.e. conditions for the normal course of reproduction, whether simple or on an expanded scale, which turn into an equal number of conditions for an abnormal course, possibilities of crisis, since, on the basis of the spontaneous pattern of this production, this balance is itself an accident."

The phrase "this balance is itself an accident" is the linchpin. It is a Marxian notion of equilibrium as statistical rarity, not gravitational center. Mandel will use this to argue that "the reproduction schemas show that equilibrium, not to speak of equilibrated growth, is the exception and not the rule under capitalism: that disproportions are far more frequent than proportionality, and that growth, being essentially uneven, inevitably produces the breakdown of growth — contracted reproduction or crisis."

The Dual Flow. Mandel now specifies what a schema actually represents: "a combined dual flow — a flow of value produced in the process of production, and a flow of money (money revenue and money capital) unleashed in the process of circulation in order to realize the value of the commodities produced." The schemas are not barter. Before department I's capitalists or workers can obtain goods, they must "prove themselves to have sufficient purchasing power to buy them from department II at their value."

The Legitimate Role of "New Money." Two crucial caveats. First, the difficulty cannot be solved by "some legerdemain such as the sudden introduction ex nihilo of additional sources of purchasing power." If new money sources appear — and they do play a key role — they "must be organically connected with the problem under examination," i.e. demonstrably co-existent with the process of production and circulation. This is Mandel defending Marx against the charge that the schemas assume exogenous money.

Second (footnote 20): Volume 2, like Volume 1, operates under "Capital in General." Therefore "prices of production play no part, and calculations are strictly value calculations." This is critical because it undercuts the objection (later raised by Donald Harris and others) that the schemas require unrealistic assumptions.

The Law of Value and the Schemas. Mandel insists: the dual nature of the schemas "in no way circumvents or contradicts the operation of the law of value." The law establishes that the quantity and quality of value produced — both of each individual commodity and of the total — is independent of use-value. But use-value remains a necessary precondition: "A good which nobody wants to buy because it fulfils no need cannot be sold, and therefore has no exchange-value. Labour expended in its production is socially wasted, not socially necessary labour."

The same applies at the aggregate level: a use-value structure of output — a given quantity of raw materials, equipment, consumer goods — is a material and social precondition of successful reproduction. But "the use-value of these commodities will only be realized if their market prices can be matched, that is, if they can be bought." Mandel's example is stark: "Millions can — and do! — starve under capitalism, even though all the food they need is there, because they lack the purchasing power to buy it." And equilibrium is attained "only if these commodities are broadly speaking sold at their value, that is to say, if the surplus-value produced by the working class is realized in the form of profit. And this is by no means assured under capitalism."

The Aggregate Demand Conditions. Mandel now formulates the aggregate condition separately from the interdepartmental exchange condition. "The sum total of output of both departments must be equal to, not smaller or larger than, the total demand generated by expanded reproduction."

Under simple reproduction:

$$I = I_c + II_c$$ $$II = I_v + I_s + II_v + II_s$$

Interpretation: Total department I output (the total mass of means of production produced) must equal the constant capital consumed in both departments. Total department II output (consumer goods) must equal wages plus surplus-value spent on consumption in both departments.

Under expanded reproduction:

$$I = I_c + \Delta I_c + II_c + \Delta II_c$$ $$II = I_v + \Delta I_v + (I_s - \Delta I_c - \Delta I_v) + II_v + \Delta II_v + (II_s - \Delta II_c - \Delta II_v)$$

Interpretation: Now the department I output must equal constant capital used up plus additional constant capital needed (in both departments). The department II output must equal wages plus additional wages (the additional variable capital to hire new workers) plus the surplus-value that is not accumulated but consumed.

Observe the structure: at both levels, the mass of means of production equals the mass of means of production used; the mass of consumer goods equals the demand for consumer goods. The schematic identity of supply and demand is a requirement, not an automatic outcome.

2.4 The Significance of Marx's Reproduction Schemas

Mandel now breaks down the conditions of proportionality in the two-department system. The classification is: Department I = means of production; Department II = consumer goods.

Simple reproduction condition (formulated by Marx himself):

$$I_v + I_s = II_c$$

In words: the value of the wages plus surplus-value of department I, which its workers and capitalists will spend on consumer goods, must equal the constant capital of department II, which department II must reconstitute by purchasing means of production from department I. The exchange is thus: department I's variable capital + surplus against department II's constant capital.

Expanded reproduction condition (derived by Otto Bauer and Bukharin; present in Volume 2 but not explicitly formulated by Marx):

$$I_v + \Delta I_v + I_{s_a} = II_c + \Delta II_c$$

Or, in Mandel's more general notation using the a/β/γ division of surplus-value (footnote 22):

  • a: unproductively consumed by the capitalists
  • β: accumulated as constant capital
  • γ: accumulated as variable capital

So for each department, $s = s_a + s_\beta + s_\gamma$. The expanded reproduction condition becomes:

$$I_v + I_{s_a} + I_{s_\gamma} = II_c + II_{s_\beta}$$

This reads: Department I's variable capital (I_v) plus the consumed portion of its surplus (I_sa) plus the portion of its surplus accumulated as variable capital (I_sγ) — i.e. department I's total demand for consumer goods — must equal department II's constant capital (II_c) plus the portion of department II's surplus accumulated as additional constant capital (II_sβ) — i.e. department II's total demand for means of production.

The Dual Meaning of the Proportionality Conditions. In conformity with the dual nature of the schemas, these conditions have two meanings.

(a) Exchange-Value Meaning: The exchange-value of goods sold by department I to department II must equal the value of goods sold by department II to department I. Otherwise there emerges an unsaleable surplus in at least one department.

(b) Use-Value Meaning: The specific use-value of the commodities produced in both departments must correspond to their mutual needs. The purchasing power in the hands of workers producing producer goods must encounter actual consumer goods equivalent to that sum of wages. "Under capitalism, workers are not supposed to spend their money on any commodities other than consumer goods."

The Commodity, Non-Barter Nature of the Schemas. Mandel stresses the dual flow between departments. When department I sells raw materials and equipment to department II, commodities flow I→II, money flows II→I. The question: "It has to be determined where that money initially came from." Conversely, when department II sells consumer goods to workers of department I, commodities flow II→I, money flows I→II. This two-way traffic in commodities and money is what distinguishes the schemas from any barter model.

The Schema as Model. Mandel now transitions from "conceptual tool" to "model corresponding to a social structure." He argues:

  1. From a purely technical point of view, the two-department schema is "the most elementary conceptual tool — an extreme simplification."
  2. Exchange requires at least two private capitals independent of each other. This is the underlying assumption.
  3. The schema can be extended: three departments (e.g. gold as department III), four departments (gold and luxury goods), five (splitting department I into means of production producing means of production and means of production producing consumer goods), or seven (further splitting department I's sub-departments into raw materials and machinery).
  4. Step by step, "we would approach an inter-branch model reflecting the actual structure of a modern capitalist industrialized economy."

The Nine-Department Schema (Footnote 24). This is the most elaborate extension Mandel sketches:

  • A: raw materials used in the production of means of production
  • B: equipment employed in the production of means of production
  • C: raw materials used for the production of mass consumer goods
  • D: equipment employed in the production of mass consumer goods
  • E: raw materials used for the production of luxury goods
  • F: equipment employed in the production of luxury goods
  • G: mass consumer goods
  • H: luxury goods (and other goods not entering into the reproduction process — e.g. weapons)
  • I: gold

With this notation, Mandel can express a crucial point about multi-department systems: department C (raw materials for consumer goods) could have a "surplus" in interchange with department E (raw materials for luxury goods) but a "deficit" with department B (equipment for producer goods). In such a case, equilibrium is possible provided all surpluses and deficits cancel out proportionately for each department, and provided each department realizes total value and disposes of sufficient purchasing power.

The Critical Difference Between Two-Department and Multi-Department Schemas: The two-department schema necessitates equivalence of exchange-values between the two departments, whereas the multi-department schema does not. In multi-department schemas, each department need only balance its overall position, and the exchange-value equivalences need not hold bilaterally. This matters enormously for understanding capitalism's actual structure of interdependence.

Footnote 23 provides historical context: Department III was first used by Tugan-Baranowski (Studien zur Theorie und Geschichte der Handelskrisen in England, 1901) and von Bortkiewicz for luxury goods or gold. Unknown to them, Marx had himself used a four-department schema in the Grundrisse (p. 441), introducing separate departments for raw materials and machinery and dividing means of consumption between workers' consumer goods and luxury goods ("surplus products") for capitalists.

Footnote 25 addresses a controversy: Rudolf Hickel criticized Mandel's use of a "department III" as being justified by the state buying weapons or by weapons being "waste." Mandel replies: "This critique is altogether unfounded. The objective basis of department III lies in the fact that it includes all commodities not entering into the reproduction process (with the possible exception of monetary gold, in a four-department schema)." The point is structural, not moralistic. Weapons, luxury goods, and gold all share this characteristic: they do not contribute material elements to reproduction.

The Two-Department Schema as a Social Structure. This is Mandel's most philosophically ambitious passage in the section. The two departments are not arbitrary — they correspond to "the essential character of human production in general." Man cannot survive without a material metabolism with nature; he cannot realize that metabolism without using tools. His material production will always consist of at least tools and means of subsistence. The two departments of Marx's schemas are "nothing other than the specific capitalist form of this general division of human production, in so far as they (1) take the generalized form of commodities, and (2) assume that the workers (direct producers) do not and cannot purchase that part of the commodity mountain which consists of tools and raw materials."

This is a crucial theorization. The two-department schema is thus derived from an anthropological constant (production requires both instruments and consumption goods), but expressed under the historically specific condition that labour-power sellers cannot buy means of production. This dual grounding gives the schema its enormous generality while preserving its historical specificity.

The Dual Flow Detailed. Mandel now walks through the dual flows.

Simple Reproduction. In department I, workers buy commodities from department II equal to their wages; capitalists buy equal to their profits. Both flows are continuous — "workers and capitalists alike have to eat every day" — regardless of whether department I's commodities have been sold. Therefore, even simple reproduction requires the prior existence of money capital and money reserves (for revenue expenditure) in the hands of the capitalist class, over and above the value of productive capital (footnote 26, referencing pp. 548–9). This is a point Marx develops in Volume 2, Chapter 17.

The mechanism then unfolds as follows. With the money received from the sale of their commodities, department II's capitalists buy means of production from department I. This money, after mediating purchase-and-sale within department I, reconstitutes the initial money capital and money-revenue reserve with which the whole turnover can recommence. Similarly, within department II, capitalists sell consumer goods to their own workers, thereby immediately reconstituting their own variable capital. They sell consumer and luxury goods to all industrialists active within department II, thus realizing the surplus-value contained in the sum total of consumer goods produced.

Expanded Reproduction. Workers and capitalists of department I buy consumer goods from department II equal to $I_v + I_{s_a}$. With this money, department II's capitalists buy means of production from department I to reconstitute their own constant capital used up. (Footnote 27: following the equilibrium formula $II_c + II_{s_\beta} = I_v + I_{s_a} + I_{s_\gamma}$, it is clear that II_c may be equal to, smaller, or greater than I_v + I_sa, depending on the relation of II_sβ to I_sγ.) Now department I's capitalists have the necessary means — if required, by drawing further on a reserve of money capital — to mediate the circulation of c within their own department and to hire additional workers, who will buy additional consumer goods (equal to $I_{s_\gamma}$) from department II. Department II's capitalists thereby acquire the purchasing power to buy from department I the additional means of production necessary for their own expanded reproduction ($II_{s_\beta} = \Delta II_c$), while the sale of consumer goods to workers and capitalists within department II operates as described. Finally, with the further means obtained by the sale of $\Delta II_c$ to department II, department I's capitalists can complete their own expanded reproduction, mediating the sale of $\Delta I_c$ within their department (as well as the purchase of the equivalent of $\Delta I_v$ from department II, if not already fully covered).

The order of exchanges is crucial. Mandel insists that each move generates the purchasing power for the next, in a chain of interdependent transactions. This is not barter — it is a sequential circuit with money operating as the connective tissue between productive and consumptive moments.

2.5 Use and Misuse of the Reproduction Schemas

Mandel now pivots to the reception of the schemas — and here his polemical intent becomes explicit. He identifies "one of the most paradoxical forms of abuse": "utilization of them as 'proof' that capitalism could grow harmoniously and unrestrictedly 'if only' the correct 'proportions' between the departments (the 'conditions of equilibrium') were maintained." The authors responsible overlooked Marx's basic assumption — "that the very structure of the capitalist mode of production, as well as its laws of motion, imply that the 'conditions of equilibrium' are inevitably destroyed; that 'equilibrium' and 'harmonious growth' are marginal exceptions to (or long-term averages of) normal conditions of disequilibrium ('overshooting' between the two departments) and uneven growth."

This is the "neo-harmonicist" misreading. It has numerous sources and genealogies — from Tugan-Baranowski to Hilferding's "general cartel" and Bukharin's state capitalism, to the Soviet textbook tradition, to Kautsky's later writings. Mandel attacks it at the level of fundamental logical error, not merely political disagreement.

The Two Reasons for Impossibility. Under capitalism, both the dynamics of value determination and the "non-determination of consumer expenditure" make exact proportions impossible.

Reason 1: Changing Values Under Technical Revolution. The nature of expanded reproduction under capitalism implies that production takes place "not only on a broader scale, but also under changed technological conditions." Constant revolutions in technique and cost of production are a "basic characteristic of the system which Marx underlined much more sharply than any of his contemporaries (including the admirers and sycophants of capitalism)." These revolutions entail that "the value of commodities as a social datum is subject to periodic change." Therefore: "values at input level do not automatically determine values at output level. Only after a certain interval will it be shown whether a fraction of the 'inputs' have been socially wasted."

This is a profound theoretical point. Even the numerical coefficients in the schemas — the amounts of c, v, s — are ex post realized values, not ex ante planned quantities. The law of value periodically reasserts itself through the devalorization of capital that accompanies crisis. "Neither the subjective will of 'monopolies' or 'the state', nor the cleverness of neo-Keynesian planners, can prevent the assertion of the law of value where private property and competition hold sway." Long-term shifts in commodity values lead to "a redistribution of living labour inputs among different branches of production (and, ultimately, a redistribution of means of production as well)."

Reason 2: The Impossibility of Forecasting Consumer Expenditure. The avoidance of crises requires proportionality not only between departments, but also between output and "final consumption" — i.e. consumption by the mass of wage and salary earners, who in modern industrialized societies form (with families) more than 80 percent of consumers. This is impossible for two reasons:

First, "the one freedom which cannot normally be taken away from the workers is the freedom to spend their wages as they wish — and there is no way in which it can be forecast with complete accuracy how they will do this (even if a prediction is 95 per cent correct, that could still leave a 5 per cent surplus of unsaleable consumer goods, which is enough to start an avalanche)."

Second, "the laws of motion of capitalism have the inherent tendency to develop the capacity of production (including the production of consumer goods) beyond the limits within which the mode of production confines the purchasing power of those condemned to sell their labour-power."

Footnote 28 quotes Marx: Grundrisse p. 414, and Capital Volume 3, Chapter 15,3: "the proportionality of the particular branches of production presents itself as a constant process through disproportionality."

The Theoretical Task Beyond "Disproportionality." Mandel is careful to insist that mere demonstration of inherent disproportion is insufficient: "it must also discover the precise mechanisms which relate that periodic disequilibrium to the basic laws of motion of capitalism." This is a methodological demand — the schemas must be complemented by a theory of trade cycle, not replaced by them.

The Misuse in "Socialist Planning." Mandel now turns to the Soviet and Eastern European debates. He does not deny that the schemas may be useful instruments for studying inter-department structure in various societies — but the character of the tool must be understood. The schemas refer to commodity production and to dual flows of commodities and money incomes. To extend their use to societies which have transcended generalized commodity production — where means of production are use-values distributed by the state according to plan, rather than commodities sold on the basis of their value — leads to "an accumulation of paradoxes, of which the authors are generally not even conscious." (Footnote 29: exceptions are means of production sold to agricultural cooperatives and small handicraftsmen, or illegally channelled into the black market.)

Maurice Dobb. Mandel's critique of Dobb is worth reconstructing. In the 1950s, Dobb participated in a "great debate" around Stalin's "law of the priority development of the means of production under socialism." Dobb "proved" (Mandel uses scare quotes) that an increased rate of growth of consumer goods in the future was "impossible" unless the present rate of growth of department I was higher than that of department II. Mandel's response:

  1. Dobb's argument is "completely spurious" — all his calculations show is that the value of consumer goods produced cannot grow at an increasing rate after x years unless the value of department I immediately rises at a faster rate.
  2. The normative content smuggled into this tautological calculation is problematic: "a policy which sacrifices the consumption of four generations of workers and their families merely to increase the rate of growth of that consumption starting with the fifth generation has nothing in common with an 'ideal socialist norm'."
  3. Crucially, "neither an individual worker nor the working class itself in a post-capitalist society (not to speak of a socialist commonwealth) is interested in a constantly rising rate of growth of the value of consumer goods. On the contrary, they are concerned with reducing that 'value' as much as possible by raising the productivity of labour, and with the withering away of commodity production and market economy."
  4. Therefore, to treat maximization of capitalist commodity-value as identical with socialist satisfaction of needs is "not only a grave theoretical error, but also a disastrous political and social miscalculation."

The "Structural Law of Socialism." Worse still were the 1960s attempts (P. Mstislawski, Helmut Koziolek, Rudolf Reichenberg) to revive a "structural law" of socialism according to which department I must expand faster than department II. These "abstract from the value nature of the reproduction schemas, and assume that optimum satisfaction of social needs implies both continuous, unlimited expansion of the output of means of production, and the allocation of an even higher fraction of the total labour potential of society to the creation of material producer goods (as against social services dealing with health, education, artistic creation, 'pure' scientific research, child-care, etc.)." Mandel's verdict: "None of these assumptions can be scientifically proven or justified. Indeed, their apologetic function — as a straightforward rationalization of existing practice in the USSR and the 'Peoples' Democracies' — is obvious to any critical observer."

Oskar Lange and Bronislaw Minc. Mandel grants that both — though not clarifying the distinction between capitalist and socialist reproduction schemas — "correctly demonstrated that increased productivity of labour and technical progress do not necessarily require department I to grow more quickly than department II; nor do they imply increased current outlay on means of production per unit currently (annually) produced."

Rosa Luxemburg's Partial Contribution. Luxemburg "well understood that the form of the reproduction schemas applies only to capitalist commodity and value production, and that the laws of motion corresponding to that form can have no validity in non-capitalist societies." But "even she erred by attaching to the 'equilibrium proportions' derived from the schemas an a-historical, eternal validity which they do not and cannot possess."

Footnote 33 provides the magnificent Luxembourg quote: "In every planned system of production it is, above all, the relation between all labour, past and present, and the means of production (between v+s and c, according to our formula), or the relation between the aggregate of necessary consumer goods (again, in the terms of our formula, v+a) and c which are subjected to regulation. Under capitalist conditions, on the other hand, all social labour necessary for the maintenance of the inanimate means of production and also of living labour power is treated as one entity, as capital, in contrast with the surplus labour that has been performed, i.e. with the surplus value s. The relation between these two quantities c and (v+s) is a palpably real, objective relationship of capitalist society: it is the average rate of profit."

From Value Reproduction to Socialist Reproduction. Mandel then asks: what happens when surplus-value is replaced by a "socially appropriated surplus product"? The equilibrium formula takes a new form expressing a different social goal of reproduction.

The key philosophical point: surplus-value is not simply a part of total value of commodities produced, nor just a fraction of newly produced value (national income). "It is also the goal of the capitalist production process." As such, it is "much more than a mere symbol in a reproduction schema." For Marx, the schemas refer to the reproduction of quantified use-value and exchange-value in a given proportion, but they also express the reproduction of capitalist relations of production themselves (footnote 34, referencing Capital Volume 3, Chapter 51). All that is implied in the formula $I_v + I_s = II_c$: $s$ is what makes the relation capitalist.

Once $s$ disappears under socialism, everything changes. When commodity production has withered away and the concept of surplus labour is reducible to that of social service and economic growth, the meaning of "equilibrium" derived from the proportionality formula is "subject to a fundamental transformation." When proportionality is upset in a commodity-producing society, both use-values and exchange-values decline, because both are inextricably linked. Under socialism, "no such inexorable nexus survives — not even as a necessary proportion (in the form of an 'eternal law') between labour inputs and use-value inputs."

Marx's Categorical Statement. Here Mandel makes a stunning assertion: in Capital Volume 2, Marx goes so far as to state categorically that "after the abolition of capitalism, there will be 'constant relative over-production' of equipment, raw materials and foodstuffs. 'Over-production of this kind', he says, 'is equivalent to control by the society over the objective means of its own reproduction.'" This is one of the most important passages in the entire Introduction, and it has radical implications. Under capitalism, "over-production" is a crisis symptom; under socialism, it is a form of social control.

The Work-Load Thought-Experiment. Mandel offers a thought experiment: imagine a society which, having reached a certain level of consumption, consciously decides to give absolute priority to one goal — reduction of the work load. Its efforts would be concentrated on assuring the production and distribution of an "ideal" package of use-values with fewer and fewer labour inputs. There would still be "simple reproduction" at the level of use-values, achieved with, say, a reduction in man-days of 4% per annum (if population increased by 1% and labour productivity by 5%). To call this a situation of "contracted reproduction" would be wrong: because a socialist society would calculate essentially with use-values, and because in Marx's schema "contracted reproduction" is logically connected with crisis, interrupted equilibrium, declining living standards, whereas the conditions described involve "smooth continuity of material production and reproduction, stable living standards and absence of any kind of crisis."

The Essential Reconciliation. Mandel concludes: this does not mean socialist planning could do without specific proportions in the flow of labour, means of production and consumer goods. "Such proportional allocation of resources is indeed the very essence of socialist planning." Means only that there is a qualitative and quantitative difference between value calculations and calculations in labour time — between (on one hand) the dynamics of appropriation and accumulation of surplus-value, and (on the other hand) rising efficiency (labour productivity) achieved in successive phases of production and measured in quantities of use-values produced during a fixed length of time.

Footnote 36 (Engels's Anti-Dühring) provides the fullest statement of the socialist calculation argument: "From the moment when society enters into possession of the means of production... the labour of each individual, however varied its specifically useful character may be, becomes at the start and directly social labour. The quantity of social labour contained in a product need not then be established in a roundabout way; daily experience shows in a direct way how much of it is required on the average. Society can simply calculate how many hours of labour are contained in a steam-engine, a bushel of wheat... It would never occur to it still to express the quantities of labour put into the products, quantities which it will then know directly and in their absolute amounts, in a third product, in a measure which, besides, is only relative, fluctuating, inadequate..." And Marx's 'association of free men' passage from Volume 1 — labour-time plays a double part: apportionment according to a definite social plan maintains correct proportion; measure of individual's part in common labour and share in product for individual consumption.

Footnote 36's Reichenberg Polemic. Reichenberg's confusion: first, he includes the material tools of the service sector in a department II of consumer goods. Then he speaks of an "intensification of expanded reproduction" from the "scientific-technical revolution," expressing itself in the fact that "if the difference between (I_v+I_s) and II_c remains the same, a process of increased accumulation is possible." Mandel's reductio: "But he fails to specify the object of this accumulation. Is it the value of II_c? Obviously that would be nonsense. The difference between two value quantities cannot change if the quantities themselves do not change. Perhaps it is accumulation of use-values! No doubt. But surely an increase in the mass of raw materials and tools... produced by a given quantity of socially necessary labour is the very definition of an increase in labour productivity. And, at the same time, Reichenberg implies that the value of these goods (and therefore the dynamics of expanded reproduction in value terms) has not changed!" Reichenberg thus confuses use-value accumulation with value accumulation.

Bronislaw Minc's Assertion and Mandel's Rebuttal. Minc asserts: "The basic theses of Marx's theory of expanded reproduction, as expressed in the schemas, are entirely valid under socialism." Mandel's response: contrary to the explicit theory of Marx and Engels, such 'socialist production' would remain "generalized commodity production, i.e. generalized production of value." The question then: "what kind of intrinsic 'law' of raising surplus labour would then be incorporated into these 'socialist production relations'?" For Marx, "distinctly states that such a law underlies the schemas of expanded reproduction referring to the production of surplus-value." (Footnote 38 quotes Volume 3, Chapter 13 on the mass of surplus-value growing along with a falling rate of profit, and Volume 3, Chapter 50 on stripping wages and surplus-value of their specifically capitalist character — concluding the difference in form implies a difference in quantities, "especially in those dynamic quantities which are growth trends.")

2.6 Productive and Unproductive Labour

Mandel here enters one of the most contested debates in Marxist economics. His thesis is twofold: (i) Marx's reproduction theory is firmly rooted in the labour theory of value — the schemas are based on labour-time as numeraire and measure "the distribution (and movement) of the labour force available to society among different departments and branches of material production"; and (ii) there is a systematic distinction between labour productive from the point of view of the individual capitalist and labour productive for capital as a whole.

Value = Abstract Social Labour. This is the foundational claim. Value is "abstract social labour." The schemas measure the distribution of living labour across branches, and therefore the productive/unproductive distinction is not a peripheral issue but is built into the very structure of the reproduction analysis.

The Morishima Confusion. Michio Morishima (Marx's Economics, 1973) has devoted effort to rehabilitating Marx as a forerunner of aggregation techniques but continues to detect a contradiction between a macro-economic theory of value based on aggregation and a micro-economic labour theory of value. Mandel rejects this: Morishima's subtle distinction between Marx's "two" labour theories of value "is based upon a simple conceptual confusion."

The argument:

  • For Marx, value and production of value are eminently social qualities, referring to relations between men, not "physical" attributes adhering to things. When Marx says the value of a commodity is the embodiment of human labour expended in its production, and when he says its value equals the socially necessary labour contained within it, he is "not making two different statements, but simply repeating the same thesis."
  • The value of a commodity is determined only by that portion of labour spent corresponding to the social average — average productivity and average socially recognized need. This is the definition of socially necessary labour.
  • Labour expended but not recognized by society is "not productive of value for the owner of that commodity."

The Aggregate Reality. Because value refers ultimately to the distribution and redistribution of the total available labour-power engaged in production, "that macro-economic aggregate is a basic economic reality, a basic 'fact of life'." The example: "If five million workers work 2,000 hours a year in material production, the total value product is ten billion hours, independently of whether the socially recognized value of each individual commodity is equal to, or larger or smaller than, the actual number of labour hours expended in its production."

The Cancellation Argument. "It follows that if the value of a given commodity is less than the labour actually spent on its production, then there must be at least one other commodity whose value is greater than the quantity of labour actually embodied in it. Social recognition of labour expenditure and actual labour expenditure can differ only for individual commodities, not for the total mass."

This is a subtle and powerful point. It is essentially a conservation law: over-valuation and under-valuation must cancel out at the aggregate. (Footnote 40 quotes Capital Volume 3, Chapter 10: "the market value of the entire mass, as governed by the average values, is equal to the sum of its individual values... Those producing at the worst extreme then have to sell their commodities below their individual value, while those at the best extreme sell theirs above it.") Morishima is thus right to stress that "in the last analysis, and for the capitalist mode of production (as distinct from petty commodity production), Marx's law of value is fundamentally an aggregate, macro-economic concept" — but wrong to see this as contradicting the micro-level labour theory. Both are aspects of the same thesis.

Productive vs. Unproductive Labour: The General Framework. The distinction has direct implications for the reproduction schemas: as value schemas, they express only value production and automatically exclude economic activities that are not productive of value. The question, then, is: what precisely is "productive labour"?

The Marxian Complication. Mandel admits that "the solution of this problem was made more difficult by Marx himself." There are "undeniable differences — if only of nuance" between the long section of Theories of Surplus-Value on productive/unproductive labour and key passages of Capital (especially Volume 2). The most striking example: commercial agents and travellers are classified as productive workers in the Theories but as unproductive workers in Capital Volumes 2 and 3. (Footnote 43 notes further contradictions even within Part 1 of the Theories: p. 157 classifies an actor working for a capitalist as productive; p. 172 classifies the actor's labour for a theatrical entrepreneur as unproductive because "their purchaser cannot sell them to the public in the form of commodities but only in the form of the action itself.")

The Preliminary Distinction. Mandel's methodological caution is crucial: Marx is not passing moral judgement, nor employing criteria of social (or human) usefulness, nor presenting a-historically objective classification. "The object of his analysis is the capitalist mode of production, and he simply determines what is productive and what is unproductive for the functioning, the rationale of that system, and that system alone."

The examples are stark:

  • A doctor's labour is indispensable for any human society — but it is unproductive labour from the point of view of the production or expansion of capital.
  • The production of dum-dum bullets, hard drugs, or pornographic magazines is useless or harmful to human interests — but if such commodities find customers, the surplus-value embodied in them is realized, capital is reproduced and expanded, and labour expended on them is productive labour.

This is the socially determined, historically relativized concept of productive labour.

The First Definition (Theories of Surplus-Value level): "All labour which is exchanged against capital and not against revenue, i.e. all labour which enriches one or several capitalists, enabling them to appropriate a portion of the total mass of surplus-value produced by the total mass of value-producing wage-labour."

This could be called "labour productive from the point of view of the individual capitalist(s)." All wage-labour engaged by capitalist enterprise — as opposed to labour functioning for private households — falls into this category. This is the level at which the Theories stops.

The Second Definition (Capital Volume 2 and 3 level): Now, "from the point of view of the capitalist mode of production in its totality, and especially from that of the growth or accumulation of capital, Marx now distinguishes labour productive for capital as a whole from labour productive for the individual capitalist." For capital as a whole, only that labour is productive which increases the total mass of surplus-value. All wage-labour which enables an individual capitalist to appropriate a fraction of the total mass of surplus-value without adding to that mass may be "productive" for the commercial, financial, or service-sector capitalist whom it allows to participate in the general sharing of the cake. "But from the point of view of capital as a whole it is unproductive, because it does not augment the total size of the cake."

This distinction is decisive. Only commodity production makes possible the creation of value and surplus-value. Only within the realm of commodity production is productive labour performed. "No new surplus-value can be added in the sphere of circulation and exchange, not to speak of the stock exchange or the bank counter; all that happens there is the redistribution or reapportionment of previously created surplus-value."

Manuscript Dating. Mandel notes (crucially for the history of the concept) that the relevant Volume 2 passages were drawn by Engels from Manuscripts II and IV, written in 1870 or between 1867 and 1870 — after the Theories of Surplus-Value of 1861–63, and even after the rough manuscript of Volume 3. "May therefore be considered to express Marx's definitive views on the question." The implication: where the Theories and Volume 2 conflict, Volume 2 wins.

Four Additional Problems.

Problem 1: "Immaterial Goods." Concerts, circus acts, prostitution, teaching, etc. In the Theories, Marx tends to classify these as commodities if produced by wage-earners for capitalist entrepreneurs. But in Volume 2, he "insists strongly and repeatedly on the correlation between use-values embodied in commodities through a labour process which acts upon and transforms nature, and the production of value and surplus-value." He provides a general formula implying the exclusion of wage-labour in "personal service industries" from productive labour:

"If we have a function which, although in and for itself unproductive, is nevertheless a necessary moment of reproduction, then when this is transformed, through the division of labour, from the secondary activity of many into the exclusive activity of a few, into their special business, this does not change the character of the function itself."

If this is true of commercial travellers or book-keepers, it "obviously applies all the more to teachers or cleaning services."

Mandel's summary: "The definition of productive labour as commodity-producing labour, combining concrete and abstract labour (i.e. combining creation of use-values and production of exchange-values) logically excludes 'non-material goods' from the sphere of value production." This conclusion is "intimately bound up with a basic thesis of Capital: production is, for humanity, the necessary mediation between nature and society; there can be no production without (concrete) labour, no concrete labour without appropriation and transformation of material objects."

Footnote 48 records the debate. Nagels and Bischoff adopt Mandel's position; Ian Gough supports the opposite view, based on Capital Volume 1, p. 644, where Marx explicitly includes wage-earners working for private capital (such as teachers) in the realm of productive labour. Mandel's response is that this passage, "like several in Theories of Surplus-Value, only indicates that Marx had not yet completed his articulation of the contradictory determinants of 'productive labour'." The two determinants: (i) exchange against capital rather than revenue; (ii) participation in the process of commodity production (unity-and-contradiction of labour process and valorization, use-value and exchange-value, concrete and abstract labour). Gough, in other words, chooses the wrong moment of Marx's development.

Footnote 50 (Gouverneur): Jacques Gouverneur attempts to extend Marx's formulation into "transformation of nature or the world," where "or the world" means "or society." Since wage-earning teachers "transform society" without "transforming nature," the implications are obvious — and, Mandel implies, absurd.

Transport Industry — The Case Study. Here Mandel offers his key concrete application. Marx's argument in Volume 2 for classifying the transport industry in the realm of value-production rather than circulation:

"The quantity of products is not increased by their transport. The change in their natural properties that may be effected by transport is also, certain exceptions apart, not an intended useful effect, but rather an unavoidable evil. But the use-value of things is only realized in their consumption, and their consumption may make a change of location necessary, and thus, in addition, the additional production process of the transport industry. The productive capital invested in this industry thus adds value to the products transported."

The Passenger Transport Problem. None of these arguments applies to carrying persons. Passenger transport is not an indispensable condition of the realization of use-values and adds no new value to any commodity. It is "a personal service on which individuals (whether capitalists or workers) spend their own revenue." Whether organized on the basis of wage-labour or not, the passenger transport industry "can no more be considered as increasing the total mass of social value and surplus-value than can wage-labour employed in the fields of commerce, banking or insurance."

The Contrast. Marx's argument in Volume 2, Chapter 6, §3, is in "striking contrast": while explicitly stating that transportation of persons by capitalist enterprise does not create commodities or use-values of any kind, he notes that it is nevertheless a "productive branch," even though the "useful effect" (Nutzeffekt) is only consumable during the production process itself. Mandel flags this as a tension in Marx's text.

General Rule: Service Industries. Mandel proposes: "as a general rule, all forms of wage-labour which exteriorize themselves in and thus add value to a product (materials) are creative of surplus-value and hence productive for capitalism as a whole."

Productive: - Manufacturing and mining - Transportation of goods - "Public service" industries — production and transport of water or energy (gas, electricity) - Selling of meals in restaurants - Building and sale of houses and offices as well as provision of material for constructing them - Agriculture

Unproductive: - Letting of apartment or hotel rooms - Service of transporting persons in buses, underground systems or trains - Medical, educational, or recreational wage-labour which is not objectivized outside the worker (sale of specific forms of labour rather than commodities) - Work of commercial or banking clerks - Employees of insurance companies or market research firms

Television Illustration: Production of television sets or films (including copies) is commodity production — productive labour. But the hiring-out of completed films or the renting of a single television set to successive customers is not productive. Similarly, wage-labour in making advertising films is productive, whereas "the cajoling of potential clients to purchase or order such films is as unproductive as the labour of commercial representatives in general."

Problem 2: Demarcation Between Production and Circulation. Volume 2 "leaves no room for doubt": only that labour which either adds to or is indispensable for the realization and conservation of a commodity's use-value adds to the total amount of abstract social labour embodied in that commodity (i.e. is productive of value). These passages, like the rest of Volume 2, are "but successive unfoldings of the basic analysis of the commodity — of its irreducible duality and the contradictions flowing therefrom."

Problem 3: Labour Within the Production Process Itself. Here Marx takes "a much less simplistic attitude than some of his latter-day disciples." The fundamental doctrine is that of the "collective labourer," developed in the "Results of the Immediate Process of Production" (the appendix to Volume 1).

Productive labour — as labour expended in the realm of production of commodities — is all wage-labour indispensable for that production process: not only manual labour, but also engineers, laboratory workers, overseers, and even managers and stock clerks, insofar as the physical production of a commodity would be impossible without that labour.

Unproductive labour — even within the factory — includes: - Wage-labour indifferent to the specific use-value of the commodity and performed only to extort maximum surplus-value (e.g. timekeepers) - Labour to assure the defence of private property (security guards in and around a factory) - Labour linked to the particular social and juridical forms of capitalist production (lawyers employed as salaried staff by manufacturing firms) - Financial book-keepers - Additional stock-checkers made necessary by the tendency to overproduction

"None of these is productive labour for capital. It does not add value to the commodities produced (although it may be essential to the overall functioning of the capitalist system, or of bourgeois society as a whole)."

Problem 4: Petty Commodity Producers. Independent peasants and handicraftsmen produce commodities — both use-values and exchange-values — but do not directly create surplus-value (except in marginal cases). They may contribute indirectly to the mass of social surplus-value — e.g. by depressing the value of food through their cheap labour. Mandel's verdict: Marx maintained the position of the Theories of Surplus-Value — "such strata perform labour which is neither productive nor unproductive from the point of view of the capitalist mode of production, because they operate outside its framework."

This is an important precision: there is a third category — labour external to the capitalist relation entirely. This will become relevant in the next section on class.

2.7 Are Unproductive Labourers Part of the Proletariat?

Mandel now draws out the political and sociological stakes of the productive/unproductive labour distinction. The stakes are enormous: the definition of the proletariat determines the scope of revolutionary agency.

The Extremes. The narrowest position — reducing the proletariat to the group of manual industrial workers — is "in complete contradiction with Marx's explicit definition of productive labour, and we need not dwell on it here." The other extreme — extending the concept to all wage and salary earners without limitation, "including army generals and managers earning 100,000 dollars a year" — is "obviously absurd."

Mandel's Definition (drawing on Marx). The defining structural characteristic of the proletariat in Marx's analysis is the socio-economic compulsion to sell one's labour-power. From this, three criteria follow:

  1. Non-ownership of means of production
  2. Lack of direct access to means of livelihood ("the land is by no means freely accessible!")
  3. Insufficient money to purchase the means of livelihood without more or less continuous sale of labour-power

Therefore, "all unproductive wage-labourers who are subject to the same fundamental constraints" belong to the proletariat. This includes: - Manual industrial workers - Commercial clerks - Lower government employees - Domestic servants

Excluded: All strata whose salary levels permit accumulation of capital in addition to a "normal" standard of living. "Whether such accumulation actually takes place is in itself irrelevant (although monographs and statistics tend to confirm that, to a modest or sizeable degree, this social group does engage in it; this is the case especially of the so-called managers, who — notwithstanding a platitude which continues to circulate in spite of all evidence to the contrary — are part and parcel of the capitalist class, if not necessarily of its top layer of billionaires)."

Footnote 58 (Contrasting Positions). Gillman groups "the advertising managers, the directors of public relations, the legal counsel, the tax experts, the 'sales engineers', the legislative lobbyists, their clerical assistants" together with "the rest (!) of the growing host of white-collar workers" in the general category of "third party consumers." Thereby tends to exclude them from the proletariat. This view influenced Paul Baran and Baran & Sweezy's Monopoly Capital. Boccara et al. (Le Capitalisme monopoliste d'état) explicitly exclude the "intermediate salaried layers" from the proletariat, reducing the latter to productive workers (workers producing surplus-value).

Footnote 59 (Defenders of Mandel's Position). Extensive documentation that the broad definition was "undoubtedly advanced by Marx and Engels and their most 'orthodox' followers: the mature (not the senile) Kautsky, Plekhanov, Lenin, Trotsky, Luxemburg et al." - Capital Volume 1, p. 798: unemployed sick, disabled, mutilated, widowed, elderly designated as 'pauperized sections [Lazarusschichte] of the working class' - Capital Volume 2 (p. 516): Marx defines the class of wage-labourers by constant compulsion to sell labour-power (p. 561 designates servants — die Bedientenklasse — as wage-labourers) - Rosa Luxemburg (Einführung in die Nationalökonomie): includes casually and occasionally employed workers, paupers, sick, unemployed - Trotsky (1905): includes domestic servants - Kautsky (The Class Struggle: Erfurt Program): includes commercial and industrial wage-earners - Plekhanov: defines proletariat as those forced to sell labour-power, extending to "persons who possess no means of production and of circulation... All these persons are forced by their economic position to sell their labour-power constantly or periodically." Lenin contested the "and of circulation" phrase but raised "no essential objection to the formulation."

The Weighty Objection. Here Mandel articulates a powerful objection that could be raised against his definition. If only productive labour produces value and thereby reproduces the equivalent of its own wages (besides creating surplus-value), then:

  • Aren't the wages of unproductive labour paid out of surplus-value produced by productive labour?
  • Doesn't this imply a major conflict of interests between productive and unproductive labour — the first seeking to reduce surplus-value to a minimum, the second wishing it increased?
  • How can such a conflict be reconciled with the inclusion of both sectors in the same social class?
  • Shouldn't industrial workers oppose any expansion of state expenditure, even in "social services", since it is financed out of surplus-value extracted from them?

This is a serious challenge. If the whole "unproductive layer" (clerks, civil servants, service workers, managers) is a drain on the surplus-value produced by industrial workers, then "the working class" as a unified political subject is a fiction — or worse, an obstacle to the industrial proletariat's real interests.

Counter 1: Not All Unproductive Labour Is Paid from Currently Produced Surplus-Value. An important part of unproductive labour — commercial employees, workers in the financial sector, those in unproductive service industries — is paid not out of currently produced surplus-value, but out of that portion of social capital invested in these sectors. Only the profits of these capitals form part of currently produced surplus-value.

Mandel: "It is true that social capital is the result of past extortion of surplus-value. But this applies also to variable capital, i.e. to wages currently paid out to productive workers." The important point: "since wages and salaries in all these sectors are not drawn from currently produced surplus-value, their payment in no way reduces the currently paid wages of productive workers."

Footnote 61 elaborates: These wages increase the total mass of social capital among which the given quantity of surplus-value has to be divided (lowering the average rate of profit). But for the industrialists this is a lesser evil: if there were no autonomous commercial capital and commercial wage-earners, their own capital outlays to cover the costs of circulation would be significantly higher, and the rate of profit still lower (see Volume 3, Chapter 17). Since this only concerns the distribution of a given mass of surplus-value between different forms of capital, with no direct bearing on the division of newly created value between wages and surplus-value (i.e. on the rate of exploitation of productive labour), "there arises no conflict of interest between productive and commercial wage-earners."

This is a subtle and important argument. The key is the level of determination: what determines the rate of exploitation is the division of newly created value between wages and surplus-value in the productive sector. What determines the distribution of the given surplus among capitals (industrial, commercial, banking) is a separate question. Confusing these two — treating the distribution of the given surplus as if it altered the rate of exploitation — is the root error of the "all state expenditure = surplus-value" position.

Counter 2: Part of Unproductive Labour Is Paid from Currently Produced Surplus-Value. This concerns "essentially the wages and salaries of state employees in public services and administration" — not state industries, where autonomous commodity production and value production occur. But even here, Mandel cautions, the conclusion is not automatic. To conclude that a reduction of state expenditure entails a reduction of surplus-value and an increase in real wages (or, equivalently, that the rise in state expenditure occurred through an increase in surplus-value and a reduction in real wages), "it would be necessary to undertake a very detailed analysis of the trend of the rate of exploitation and of workers' living standards and needs since the 'explosion' of state expenditure."

The "Gross Wages" Concept. Mandel's most important theoretical point in this section: "the concept of 'gross wages' (i.e. wages before tax) has no meaning in Marxist economic theory." Wages are means of reconstituting the worker's labour-power through the purchase of commodities and services. Thus, "money deducted from the worker's 'gross wage' to help the state buy aeroplanes has nothing at all to do with wages. It is from the outset part of social surplus-value." If fresh taxes actually lower previously attained levels of real wages, "they may indeed be said to have increased the rate of surplus-value. But again this will be measured by comparing successive amounts of net — real — wages, and not 'gross wages'."

Similarly, state medical, educational or transport services which help reconstitute the worker's labour-power (or maintain his family under normal living conditions) represent "rather a socialized portion of the wage, regardless of whether it passes through the form of 'state revenue', and regardless of whether it 'originated' in 'gross wages' (taxes paid by the worker), 'gross profits' (taxes paid by the capitalist), or the 'gross income' of independent middle classes."

Footnote 62 (Unemployment Compensation). Objection: unemployment compensation cannot be considered the equivalent of the "price" or "value" of the commodity labour-power, since by definition the unemployed do not sell their labour-power. Mandel: "this argument is based on a somewhat mechanistic reduction of the category 'socialized wages'." Analogy: if a worker places 10 percent of his current wages in a chocolate box or bank account to provide for unemployment, that amount does not cease to be part of his wages. There is "no fundamental difference" between this and the situation where all workers use a "collective chocolate box called the National Institute of Unemployment Insurance or Social Security" and the money is transferred directly from capitalist accounts. Only if this analysis is accepted "is it legitimate to demand that such funds be exclusively administered by the unions (for neither the employers nor the state should have any say in how the workers spend their own money!)."

Political Conclusion. Mandel's verdict: "It thus proves meaningful after all to examine the impact of a rise or fall in state expenditure on average working-class living standards, independently of its servicing (mediation) by unproductive state employees." Where living standards decline, the total price of labour-power (individual plus 'socialized' wages) has been reduced. Where they rise, "no sophism can prove that this entails an increase in social surplus-value."

The Dynamic, Aggregate Method. Marxist economic theory rejects the notion of a rigid "wages fund." Any analysis of the effects of varying levels of state expenditure upon the rate of exploitation would have to be aggregate and dynamic. "Nothing flows automatically from either the expansion or contraction of state expenditure." The question is whether — under given economic, social and political conditions — a reduction in expenditure would lead to higher real wages rather than higher profits for the capitalist class. Without such detailed proof, "the thesis would remain doubtful, to say the least." The analysis would have to take into account "the probable dynamic of the political and social class struggle (a function of, among other things, the great historical shifts in the economic correlation of class forces within a given bourgeois society) and its precise impact upon the structure of both state revenue and state expenditure."

Thematic Synthesis. Mandel acknowledges he has "strayed considerably" from productive/unproductive labour — but argues he has arrived at the heart of the problem. The correct Marxist classification of the proletariat — the class forced by socio-economic compulsion to sell its labour-power — implies that both: - variations in the level of the reserve army of labour, and - the variegated relations between the "purely physiological" and "moral-historical" components of the value of labour-power

are of decisive importance for the proletarian's immediate destiny.

Footnote 63 references Mandel's introduction to Volume 1 (pp. 66–72) and Late Capitalism (pp. 149–58).

The Growth of Unproductive Wage-Labour. Mandel now presents an empirical argument. The growth of unproductive wage-labour accompanies the absolute and relative increase in the size of the proletariat in contemporary capitalist countries.

The new minimum standard is "much higher than the one existing in Marx's time."

And the political response: "Correctly, the workers have reacted strongly against massive cuts in social state expenditure, thereby showing that their class instinct is clearer than the 'science' of those theoreticians who persist in calling all state expenditure 'surplus-value' (the logical consequence of which would be indifference to, or even approval of, the cut-backs)."

This is the political payoff of the whole section. 2.8 Luxury Production, Surplus-Value and Accumulation of Capital

The theme here: the function of labour producing luxury goods and its relationship to accumulation — and by analogy, the arms sector.

The Vorontsov Precedent. Controversy over the arms sector dates to the end of the nineteenth century, when the Russian populist V. Vorontsov first raised the possibility of avoiding crises of over-production through "absorption" of part of surplus-value by increased arms production. In the 1930s-40s, a long Marxist debate took up the role of rearmament in overcoming inter-war stagnation. Since World War II, the Vance–Cliff–Kidron school assigned a crucial position to the "permanent arms economy" in explaining the long boom. Arms production occupies a central place in the "surplus absorption" of Baran and Sweezy's Monopoly Capital. Mandel's own position (in Late Capitalism, Chapter 9) is in controversy with Cogoy and Mattick.

Theoretical Foundation. Marx's theory sees value's essence in abstract social labour, irrespective of the specific use-value of the commodity. The existence of some kind of use-value is a precondition of realization only in the immediate sense that nobody buys a good which has absolutely no use for him. But "the social fact of purchase is sufficient proof of the use-value of a commodity, that is, of its usefulness to its buyer." Hence: - Only unsold commodities do not embody socially necessary labour and thus have no value. - Those which are sold are by definition the product of socially necessary labour and increase through their production the mass of socially produced value.

Under capitalism, by definition, the production of all sold commodities created by wage-labour increases the total mass of surplus-value produced and realized (unless sold at a price so far below cost of production that society does not recognize any part of the surplus labour contained in them).

The Crucial Distinction. Volume 2 makes explicit the distinction between (i) production and realization of surplus-value and profit, and (ii) expanded reproduction of capital, i.e. accumulation. Not all commodities produced contribute to expanded reproduction. But all commodities produced and sold contribute to the increase of total surplus-value appropriated by the capitalists and their retainers.

Under conditions of simple reproduction, there would be no surplus-value and no profit whatsoever, since all surplus-value would be unproductively consumed. (This is a striking and counter-intuitive assertion: profit exists only in and through accumulation; simple reproduction — which consumes all surplus — leaves no surplus at all.)

Luxury Goods. The production of luxury consumer goods — purchased out of the non-accumulated portion of surplus-value — "remains within the sphere of the production of value and surplus-value, that is to say, it enlarges the mass of profit accruing to the capitalist class."

Arms Production. By the same token, "the production of arms or space equipment is a form of commodity production; the fact that the sole purchaser is here the state, whereas luxury products are exchanged for revenue of the bourgeoisie, makes no essential difference."

To determine whether arms production depresses or raises the average rate of profit, the same questions apply as for any "sub-department": 1. Is the organic composition of capital in that department equal, superior, or inferior to the average? 2. Does its rise or fall influence the average social rate of surplus-value?

Footnote 70: This follows automatically from the commodity nature of arms — from the fact that capital invested in that sector is engaged in commodity production and correspondingly employs labour in the production of surplus-value. As in the case of luxury goods, differences between the profit rate within that branch and the rate outside it "will lower or increase accordingly the social average rate of profit." In the Theories of Surplus-Value, Marx explicitly defends this position against Ricardo.

Two Basic Situations for Armaments' Contribution to Accumulation.

Situation 1: Full Employment of Capital. (This "can be, and often has been, accompanied by structural unemployment of wage-labour.") In this situation, weapons production — like luxury goods not entering into the reproduction of labour-power — "evidently does not contribute to the accumulation of capital." This is true in a double sense:

  • Weapons, like luxury products, do not provide the objective material elements of expanded reproduction: neither additional raw materials, machines, or energy, nor consumer goods capable of feeding an expanded workforce.
  • Nevertheless, the part of the national income which buys weapons could not have been spent on additional means of production or wages for additional productive workers.

Thus, both because of their specific use-value and because they are exchanged against the non-accumulated part of surplus-value, weapons do not contribute to expanded reproduction under conditions of full employment.

Nuance. This does not necessarily imply weapons production reduces capital accumulation, except in the most general sense that all forms of unproductive expenditure of surplus-value do so. To show that the appearance or expansion of an arms sector has actually reduced expanded reproduction, it would have to be demonstrated that it appeared (or expanded) at the expense of the sector of means of production. "If it has simply replaced luxury production, then, all other things being equal, neither the scope nor the potential rhythm of capital accumulation will have been changed."

The Substitution Question. What if the weapons sector has appeared or expanded at the expense of the sector producing consumer goods for the workers, still assuming full employment? Two possibilities:

(i) Where the substitution leads to a decline in the physical or moral working capacity of the labour force, the rate of capital accumulation will fall — perhaps after a certain time to the extent of contracted reproduction (footnote 71 references Mandel's Marxist Economic Theory, pp. 332–5, on the war economy).

(ii) Where the substitution leaves unchanged the capacity or willingness of the workers to accept the current "norm" of social labour, such a shift of resources from department II to department III would imply a rise in the average social rate of surplus-value. "The same value product would then be produced with the same labour-power, but at the cost of less variable capital. The working class would simply receive a smaller share of the existing national income."

Whether this leaves accumulation unaltered or leads to a higher level of accumulation would then depend on the way this rise in the rate and mass of surplus-value influenced the division of surplus-value between the unproductively consumed portion (including the weapons sector) and the accumulated part. This is a complex, multi-variable determination — no mechanical conclusion is possible.

Situation 2: Long-Term Plethora of Capital. Mandel abandons the "full employment of capital" supposition. This is not artificial: it was prevalent during the first massive arms drive preceding World War I (footnote 73: Lenin, Imperialism, Chapter 8), even more marked in the thirties (Japan's 'Manchurian Incident', German rearmament, generalized after 1936), and has remained the rule for over thirty years of permanent arming (footnote 74).

Definitions.

Over-production of capital: Value-side: emergence of large sums of capital hoarded in savings accounts or used for purchasing bonds and government securities, where they beget only the average rate of interest rather than the average rate of profit. Use-value side: sizeable stocks of unused raw materials and productive capacity in plant, as well as large reserves of unemployed workers.

If — as a result of the appearance and expansion of a significant arms sector — money (or quasi-money) capital is productively reinvested, then the production of value and surplus-value increases. The manufacture of arms is productive of value and surplus-value; hence, "capital grows richer because more workers are exploited in the production of greater surplus-value."

The Indirect Mechanism. Since department II does not contribute to the creation of the material elements of expanded reproduction, its expansion cannot directly ensure a higher level of capital accumulation. But it can do so indirectly:

  1. As additional workers are employed, the wages bill increases, leading to rising output and sale of consumer goods.
  2. The consumption of additional raw materials in the weapons industry stimulates production in mines and other centres of department I which had previously contracted output.
  3. Material production rises in all sectors of the economy, augmenting the material elements of expanded reproduction — provided reserves of "productive factors" are available (which follows from the "under-employment of capital" hypothesis) and/or provided at least part of the additional surplus-value is not absorbed by the armaments sector or other unproductive departments, but remains available for capital accumulation.

The "Ideal" Combination. These conditions apply with even greater force if the processes are accompanied by a changed distribution of the national income between wages and surplus-value — i.e. if rearmament is financed to some extent at the expense of the working class through a rise in the rate of surplus-value. The resultant combination would then be "ideal" for capital accumulation:

  • Expansion of the mass of workers employed and exploited (i.e. expansion of value product, surplus-value, market demand)
  • Increase in the rate of surplus-value and (probably) the rate of profit
  • Rise in the rate of accumulation (i.e. increase of investment in the productive sector, over and above growth in arms spending)

Footnote 75 distinguishes Hitler's war economy from the post-war boom. In the former, increased investment was by and large confined to the armaments sector; there was no real cumulative growth involving expansion of the "final consumers" market. This contrast matters enormously: it shows that arms spending can produce either a genuine boom or a war-economy dead end, depending on whether it sparks productive investment elsewhere.

The Limits — No Long-Term Solution. This provides no long-term solution. The very "success" of the operation "inevitably reproduces the initial contradictions":

  1. Increased capital accumulation → rise in the organic composition of capital → depresses the rate of profit.
  2. Higher level of employment (made possible by absorption of part of the unemployed in the army or state apparatus — a normal feature of substantial military spending) reduces the industrial reserve army; except under fascist-type dictatorship, this makes it more difficult to neutralize the effects of rising organic composition by driving up the rate of surplus-value.
  3. Decline in the rate of profit depresses productive investment → crisis of over-production and fall in the rate of capital accumulation.
  4. When the rate becomes negative, a process of devalorization of capital begins — the normal function of the crisis of over-production.

To counter such a new crisis through intensification of armaments production — where a sizeable arms sector already exists — would modify the basic proportions both of the division of surplus-value between the accumulated and consumed portions, and of the allocation of productive resources between departments I/II, on one hand, and department III, on the other. "Whatever effect upon the process of expanded reproduction was initially obtained would be increasingly neutralized."

Moreover, such a high rate of taxation of profits and wages would be necessary that, except under very special political conditions, the basic social classes (although not the sector of capitalists directly engaged in weapons production and procurement) would revolt. Mandel's verdict: "Such an expansion is thus no cure-all for the ills of capitalist over-accumulation and over-production. But it can trigger off shorter or longer periods of economic upturn as long as those preconditions indicated above are satisfied."

This is a dialectical rather than mechanical conclusion — the same theoretical rigor applied to luxury goods extended to arms, with full recognition of the multiple possible scenarios. The analysis has implications for understanding the post-war boom, the "military-industrial complex," and the limits of Keynesian demand management.

2.9 How Can Commercial and Financial Capital Participate in the Distribution of Social Surplus-Value?

The distinction between productive and unproductive labour partially dovetails with the distinction between two general sectors of capital:

  1. Productive capital: capital invested in commodity production (industry, agriculture, transport, productive branches of the so-called service industries)
  2. Unproductive capital: commercial capital, banking and insurance capital, and capital invested in "unproductive" branches of service industries

Wage-labour hired by the second category enables these capitalists to appropriate a fraction of the sum total of surplus-value — but does not itself add to that total.

The Posing of the Question. "Why do the industrial capitalists, or more precisely all those who invest in the 'productive' sectors, accept that a portion of the surplus-value produced by 'their' workers should be appropriated by capitalists whose capital does not contribute to the production of surplus-value?"

The Answer (Dealt with at length in Volume 3, touched on here). Although capital invested outside the sphere of material production does not directly augment the mass of surplus-value, it does contribute indirectly. Industrial and farming capitalists abandon a share of "their" surplus-value to traders and bankers "not out of the goodness of their hearts, but because these gentlemen help them to raise the mass of that surplus-value."

The Time-Dimension Mechanism. Marx introduces the "time dimension" — the key structuring principle of Volume 2's analysis of circulation and turnover. Consider:

  • Total turnover time of fixed capital stretches over many years. Small shifts in the length of the commodity-capital phase (commodities remaining unsold) do not essentially affect it.
  • The situation is entirely different for circulating capital.

Numerical Example. Suppose it takes three months to produce a given mass of commodities and three months to sell them. Then circulating productive capital will turn over only twice a year unless it receives assistance. The part exchanged for labour-power — and thus making possible the creation of surplus-value — "would then remain sterile for six months of the year."

If, however, commercial capital buys up a large proportion of the commodities as soon as they leave the factory, OR if banking capital advances the money to pay the raw materials bill immediately after the commodities are produced and before they are sold, then "owing to the assistance of these sectors of the capitalist class, productive circulating capital can be reinvested as soon as a production cycle is completed."

Consequence: variable capital "will never remain idle. It will set workers to produce surplus-value twelve months, and not six months a year — as a result of which, all other things being equal, the total annual mass of surplus-value will be twice as great as it would otherwise have been."

Therefore: "It naturally pays industrial capital to give a discount to wholesale traders, or to pay interest to bankers, if these rescue operations allow an overall increase in the production of surplus-value."

Implication: Functional Division of Labour Within the Capitalist Class. Only a fraction of total social capital is continuously engaged in production. Important segments remain constantly outside the realm of production — money capital, transportation and banking capital — to shorten circulation time. From the point of view of the capitalist class as a whole (the one Marx adopts in Volume 2; only in Volume 3 does he treat these sectors as competing), this is a "functional division of labour within that class."

Instead of each industrialist being his own treasurer, money-changer, transporter, seller on home and world markets, and advancer of additional money capital, all these functions are socially centralized by specialist sectors of the bourgeoisie. The division of labour carries a considerable rationalization: the costs of overall social circulation, transportation and banking are lower than if each capitalist firm had to accomplish these tasks itself. Overhead costs are reduced; the total mass of surplus-value is increased through continuous production. "It is thus profitable for the bourgeoisie as a whole to maintain (and even expand, as the record of the 'service industries' demonstrates!) this functional division of labour."

The Source of Capital Outside the Realm of Material Production. Mandel now poses a historical-materialist question: where does capital invested outside material production come from?

Partial Truth. Since all capital derives in the last analysis from surplus-value, and since under capitalism all surplus-value is created by "productive capital" (wage-labour in material production), it may appear that all commercial and banking capital ultimately derives from industrial and agricultural productive capital. This is partially true. Volume 2 shows how money capital is periodically "expelled" from the process of value production, becoming temporarily available for other purposes. The best example: the depreciation fund of fixed capital. Because it is reinvested only at certain intervals, rather than piecemeal, this fund serves for a time as an important source of money capital employed in credit operations.

The Critical Generalization. "However, such a view should not be generalized. Capital, after all, is older than the capitalist mode of production." Before surplus-value was produced in production, "vast wealth was accumulated through the plunder of peasants, the fleecing of feudal lords (for example, by over-pricing exotic merchandise), robbery of merchants (through piracy) and tribal communities (through the capture of slaves)." Merchant, commercial and banking capital existed long before "productive" capital was born in manufactures, not to speak of the industrial revolution.

Therefore: industrial capital "not only reproduces commercial and banking capital by paying over fragments of the surplus-value created by 'its own' workers; it also finds these other forms of capital present at the moment of its own birth, and indeed as a condition of this."

Commercial and banking capital, then, reproduce themselves both by (a) continuing their former practices — appropriating part of the social product originating outside the realm of capitalist relations of production, and transforming it into surplus-value and money capital — and (b) appropriating part of the surplus-value created within the capitalist process of production proper.

Consequences for Underdevelopment Theory. This dual origin has profound historical implications. The interpenetration of pre-capitalist, semi-capitalist and capitalist relations of production — imposed upon colonies and semi-colonies by the power of capital on the world market and the violence of foreign political and military domination — has been "an extremely important factor in the historical development of these twin sources of money capital accumulation." Through merchant, commercial, usury and banking capital, they have continued to play a key role in world-wide capitalist expansion, especially within third-world countries.

The Central Historical Thesis: "Primitive accumulation of capital and 'productive' accumulation of capital (through the creation of surplus-value in commodity production) are not only successive historical stages, but also simultaneous and combined phenomena. Nor does primitive accumulation automatically lead to a commensurate spread of 'productive' capital and industrialization; it may instead simply condense into a 'one-sided' expansion of the above-mentioned forms of 'unproductive' capital. This circumstance, together with the impact of foreign imperialist domination, clarifies one of the mysteries of underdevelopment under capitalism."

This is a major contribution to underdevelopment theory. It explains why some peripheral countries experience "one-sided" development of commercial, financial, and rentier capital without industrial transformation: primitive accumulation and primitive dispossession do not automatically lead to productive investment. The interpenetration of pre-capitalist and capitalist forms generates a structure that can block industrialization.

2.10 Luxemburg's Critique of Marx's Reproduction Schemas

This section is the theoretical and political core of the Introduction. Mandel adjudicates one of the most consequential debates in Marxist thought.

The Question. Luxemburg's The Accumulation of Capital raises formidable questions: Marx's theory of crisis; the historical limits of the capitalist mode of production (the Zusammenbruchstheorie or "breakdown theory"); the origins and functions of imperialism, colonialism, militarism and wars in the imperialist epoch.

Footnote 76 (Documentation). Main contributions: Otto Bauer (Die Neue Zeit, No. 24, 1913), Anton Pannekoek (Bremer Bürgerzeitung, 29 January 1913), G. Eckstein (Vorwärts, 16 February 1913), Bukharin's Imperialism and the Accumulation of Capital. Henryk Grossmann (Das Akkumulations- und Zusammenbruchsgesetz, 1929). Recent discussion: Arghiri Emmanuel, Le Profit et les crises, Paris, 1975; Joan Robinson's introduction to the English translation.

Luxemburg's Argument. Centered on a single theme: how can that part of the value of commodities corresponding to the accumulated portion of surplus-value be realized? What purchasing power is available? Why do capitalists expand production, if not because they expect additional customers? Who are these new customers?

Rejected Candidates:

  1. Workers. Rejected because the purchasing power of workers originates with capital; expansion of production merely to satisfy the new needs of an enlarged workforce would be inconceivable for the capitalist class as a whole. (Not true of capitalists taken individually, for whom all workers except their own are potential customers; but, "as Luxemburg flatly states, for the capitalist class as a whole, all workers are 'their own workers', and it makes no sense to treat them as a source of increased sales.")

  2. Other capitalists. Rejected: "How could the capitalist class in its totality enrich itself if the money to buy the surplus product came out of its own pocket?"

  3. "Third persons" (cronies, hangers-on, servants of the capitalist class, or landowners appropriating ground-rent). Rejected: "in the last analysis, the revenue of all these social layers is derived from surplus-value." If surplus-value were the only source of purchasing power, "it would mean that capitalists become richer by spending their own money."

Luxemburg's Conclusion. The additional purchasing power must come from outside capitalist relations of production — through forcing non-capitalist social classes (peasants and pre-capitalist landowners) ruinously to spend their revenue on capitalist commodities. Only in this way can expanded production and reproduction, capital accumulation, and capitalist economic growth take place.

The Breakdown Thesis. By destroying the non-capitalist milieu on which its expansion is based, capitalism "undermines the conditions of its own growth." The disappearance of the non-capitalist environment marks the absolute limit of capitalist development.

Footnote 79 (Predecessors). The notion that a non-capitalist milieu is necessary for expanded reproduction was first advanced by Heinrich Cunow ("Die Zusammenbruchstheorie", Die Neue Zeit, No. 1, 1898), later defended by Karl Kautsky ("Krisentheorien", Die Neue Zeit, No. 2, 1902) and Louis B. Boudin (The Theoretical System of Karl Marx, 1907, pp. 163-9, 241-53).

Secondary Criticisms — Easily Refuted.

(i) Money as means of circulation vs. income as prerequisite of realization. Luxemburg asserts Marx confuses these. Mandel: "she is quite evidently mistaken" (footnote 80 references pp. 143-5 and Mandel's own remarks at pp. 442-4 of the volume).

(ii) Schemas don't correspond to capitalist reality. She "mixes up levels of abstraction which are clearly differentiated in Marx's method."

(iii) Schemas don't incorporate laws of motion. Because Marx's figures allow for no increase in the organic composition of capital, "they could not incorporate these laws." Mandel dismisses this.

(iv) Department II sacrificed to Department I. She claims that "department II is somehow 'sacrificed to' or 'dependent upon' department I, in contradiction to the laws of private property and competition." Mandel: "it does not follow at all" from the primacy of department I.

Footnote 81 notes that schemas incorporating the laws of motion have been worked out by Bauer, Grossmann, Léon Sartre, Glombowski, Hosea Jaffe and many others. Whether they assure long-term equilibrium is, of course, "quite a different question."

The Three-Level Response. Mandel structures his response at three levels of abstraction.

Level 1: Capital in General vs. Many Capitals. Luxemburg commits a methodological error: situating within the framework of "capital in its totality" a problem that can only be considered in relation to the "competition of many capitals." It is impossible to conduct an analysis simultaneously at these two distinct levels, since capital in its totality abstracts by definition from many capitals, from competition.

The argument that the capitalist class cannot enrich itself by purchasing its own surplus product "overlooks the fact that, under a system of private property, the surplus product can never be owned by 'a single total capital'." Capitalist competition implies that capitalists can grow richer by buying one another's surplus product.

Marx explicitly: "the surplus-value created at one point requires the creation of surplus-value at another point, for which it may be exchanged" (Grundrisse, p. 407). He also indicates (Capital Volume 3, Chapter 15,3) that in the absence of competition, growth would actually disappear. Footnote 82: "This point was first made by Rosdolsky (pp. 63-72)."

The Key Methodological Thesis: "For Marx, growth is possible in a 'purely' capitalist milieu (i.e. where no part of the social surplus product can find 'non-capitalist' customers) provided that the interests and growth rates of all capitalists are assumed to be not identical, but on the contrary rooted in competition. The realization question does not, and cannot, arise within the realm of 'capital in general'; it appears, together with the theory of crises and the trade cycle, only within the sphere of 'many capitals'. This Marx repeatedly stated himself" (Theories of Surplus-Value, Part II, pp. 532-4).

The Corollary: reproduction schemas which imply competition should assume as a rule the existence of different, rather than equal, rates of accumulation in the two departments — only occasionally leading to equalization of the rate of profit. This corresponds to the real modus operandi of capitalism. It also points the way to a solution to the "technical problem" Luxemburg identified in the fact that the "unsaleable" portion of department II's commodities embodies part of the surplus-value created in that department.

The Solution: Luxemburg dismissed out of hand Marx's convincing solution, later developed at length by Otto Bauer. Part of the surplus-value produced in department II "is periodically transferred to department I, precisely when (and because) department I exhibits, over a considerable length of time, a higher organic composition of capital than that of department II."

Level 2: Quasi-Static Equilibrium vs. Discontinuous Dynamics. Level 1 poses the problem as quasi-static equilibrium. Level 2 — still abstract but nearer historical reality — examines accumulation as a discontinuous process to understand its actual dynamics.

Second Question Re-Posed: What is the effect upon realization of the value of commodities embodying the accumulated part of surplus-value if and when: 1. The organic composition of capital rises in both departments; 2. Department I grows faster than II (unavoidable result of the rising organic composition); 3. The rate of profit declines (growth in rate of surplus-value insufficient to compensate for rising organic composition)?

Answer: "Theoretically, full realization of surplus-value is possible, and several ingenious mathematical models have been constructed — by, among others, O. Benedikt, Shinzaburo Koshimura, Oskar Lange, J. Caridad Mateo and Hosea Jaffe — in order to show that it is." By contesting this, Luxemburg "denied that 'pure' capitalism was possible, thus taking a position exactly opposite to the one which Marx tried to demonstrate with his reproduction schemas."

Footnote 88 — Critical Qualification. "It should be immediately added, however, that the real socio-economic conditions expressed by these algebraic formulas have to be precisely defined."

Example: Koshimura, to reconcile equilibrium with a rising organic composition and a falling rate of profit, has to: - Modify the initial relations between three departments - Increase considerably the organic composition of department III (which makes little historical sense) - Lower the total price of production of department II (workers' wages) to the extent of absolute decline - "Offsetting" the falling rate of profit by a rising rate of surplus-value (plausible), arrive at an absolute decrease in workers', and even capitalists', consumption (not only implausible but contrary to Marx's basic assumption in Capital Volume 2, and to existing empirical data)

This is a hard-nosed qualification: algebraic solution is not the same as historical realism. The mathematical models "solve" the problem only under assumptions that contradict both Marx's own framework and observable reality.

Footnote 89 — Contradicting Luxemburg's Critics. Those critics who replied that the schemas "prove" by themselves the possibility of unlimited, smooth progress of reproduction "forgot one small point: capitalism has been generating periodic crises of over-production for more than 150 years, and continues to do so with the regularity of a 'natural law'." Reject out of hand the hypothesis that each successive crisis was due entirely to "specific" causes unrelated to the inner logic of the capitalist mode of production. "The very periodicity of these crises is enough to refute the 'harmony theorists'." In this respect, "the superiority of Luxemburg over certain of her critics is obvious" (footnote 90: see especially her 'Anti-Critique').

Mandel's Verdict on Luxemburg's Technical Work: "Nevertheless, did she succeed in proving her case in a technically satisfactory manner? We do not believe so; for she narrowed down the problem to an excessively monocausal one. In order to prove that, under capitalism, equilibrium must beget disequilibrium, that expanded reproduction must generate over-production, and that accumulation of capital must lead to devalorization of capital it is necessary to bring all the inter-related variables of the reproduction schemas into play. And this she does not do. Thus, while The Accumulation of Capital raises the correct problems, it does not provide acceptable solutions to them."

Footnote 91 — Grossmann Also Fails. Grossmann's standpoint — denial that the crises have at bottom problems of realization of surplus-value and disproportionality between production and consumption — is "fundamentally unsound." By converting the decline of the rate of profit into the sole cause of the final breakdown of capitalism, he overlooks the fact that this tendency is offset by periodical devalorization of capital. Whereas he seeks a mechanical unity between the theory of crises of over-production and that of the breakdown, "the real, dialectical link between the two embodies the following contradiction: crises of over-production are the precise mechanism which allows the decline in the rate of profit to be periodically overcome — both through devalorization of the total mass of social capital and through a rise in the rate of surplus-value."

This is a dialectical rather than mechanical relationship: crisis is not the terminal event of a self-destroying system, but rather the mechanism of its periodic renewal. The system survives through crises, not despite them.

The Technical Demonstration. Mandel now formalizes the impossibility of permanent equilibrium.

Equilibrium Formula for Expanded Reproduction: $$I_v + I_{s_a} + I_{s_\gamma} = II_c + II_{s_\beta}$$

Interpretation: The demand for consumer goods generated by department I equals the consumer-goods supply from department II (left side); equivalently, the demand for means of production generated by department II (replacement + addition) equals the means-of-production supply from department I (right side).

The Argument. The formula implies "an identity of the rate of growth of demand for consumer goods generated by department I, and the rate of growth of constant capital in department II." But:

  • The rise in the organic composition of capital entails that the demand for consumer goods generated in department I will normally grow more slowly than constant capital in that sector (unless the slower rate of growth of variable capital is compensated by a rate of growth of unproductively consumed surplus-value higher than that of constant capital — "extremely unlikely in the long run").
  • Therefore, the precondition of equilibrium is "a rate of growth of constant capital in department II lower than the one in department I." If the rates in the two departments are equal, equilibrium is upset.
  • But "a rate of growth of constant capital in department II which is permanently lower than that in department I is incompatible with private property and competition. There is no reason why capitalists engaged in the production of consumer goods should forever abstain from trying to incorporate all existing technology, all means of reducing costs of production, all potentially useable machinery."

Conclusion: "Therefore, $II_c + II_{s_\beta}$ will from time to time be greater than $I_v + I_{s_a} + I_{s_\gamma}$, just as, periodically, under conditions of rising organic composition of capital (biased development of labour-saving technology), $\Delta[II_c + II_{s_\beta}]$ will be equal to $\Delta[I_c + I_{s_\beta}]$, and $\Delta[I_c + I_{s_\beta}]$ will be greater than $\Delta[I_v + I_{s_a} + I_{s_\gamma}]$."

"It therefore seems impossible to avoid periodic over-production of consumer goods, as well as a decline in the rate of profit and of the ratio $\Delta c/c$, $s_v/s_v$, entailing an abrupt halt to the accumulation of capital."

The Donald Harris Objection. Donald Harris ("On Marx's Scheme of Reproduction and Accumulation," Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 80, 1972, pp. 505ff.) concluded from Marx's "assumptions" that equilibrium obtains only if (in a value system) there is proportional hiring of labour in the two departments, or if (in a prices of production system) there is an equal ratio of investment — accumulation — of surplus-value.

Mandel's Response: "All these calculations are based upon a misunderstanding of Marx's method. While Marx does assume an equal rate of exploitation in both departments (an assumption based on the concept of an average national value of labour-power, for which quite strong empirical evidence exists under developed capitalism), he does not 'assume' either that the organic composition of capital will remain equal or that the rate of surplus-value will stay the same." Marx's method of successive approximation led him to abstract, at a given stage, from a number of additional variables in order to clarify certain preliminary problems. "This has nothing to do with 'assuming' historical trends."

This is a methodological key: abstraction is a staging device, not an empirical claim. To treat Marx's abstractions as empirical assumptions is to fundamentally misunderstand his method.

Level 3: The Historical Process. "Finally, on the third level, that of the actual historical process of capital accumulation, Luxemburg seems fundamentally correct."

  • Capitalism was born essentially in a non-capitalist milieu.
  • It has immensely enriched itself by plundering that milieu.
  • The same value-transferring metabolism has continued to this very day.
  • "'Pure' capitalism has never existed in real life and, as Engels rightly predicted, it never will exist, because 'we shall not let it come to that'. The Russian October Revolution, and the subsequent expansion of a post-capitalist sector of world economy, indicates that Engels's instinct was a sure one in that respect."
  • Luxemburg's analysis of the ways and means whereby capitalism sucks wealth and value from pre-capitalist communities and classes was "an impressive first contribution to three-quarters of a century of anti-colonialist and anti-imperialist world literature. It has still to be equalled in either theoretical insight or economic lucidity." (Footnote 93: The Accumulation of Capital, Chapters 27-30.)

The Balanced Final Verdict. "The final balance-sheet of Luxemburg's critique, then, must be a nuanced one. We cannot say baldly that she is right or that she is wrong. While many of her partial theses, as well as her final answer, are inadequate, she certainly poses relevant questions and puts her finger on real problems which Volume 2 does not and cannot answer. In particular, the contradictory character of capitalist growth, discussion of which was stimulated by her seminal The Accumulation of Capital, cannot be simply subsumed under the formulas 'anarchy of production' and 'disproportionality'. The specific place which unavoidable disproportions between production and mass consumption occupy in the dynamics of capitalism has to be integrated into any overall explanation of capitalist disequilibrium and crisis."

2.11 Volume 2 and Marx's Explanation of Capitalist Crises of Over-Production

Mandel now transitions to crisis theory proper. Marx left no systematic treatment of crises — the four volumes contain partial considerations, with the sixth volume on the world market having been planned but not written. Mandel's task: to reconstruct the elements of a crisis theory from Volume 2.

First Point: Commercial Capital as Masking Agent. The role of commercial capital as intermediary between industrial capitalist and final consumer, while helping shorten circulation time and hasten turnover, "masks the growing disproportion between expanding production and lagging final demand."

Marx's formulation: "The periods in which capitalist production exerts all its forces regularly show themselves to be periods of over-production; because the limit to the application of the productive powers is not simply the production of value, but also its realization. However, the sale of commodities, the realization of commodity capital, and thus of surplus-value, is restricted not by the consumer needs of society in general, but by the consumer needs of a particular society in which the great majority are always poor and must always remain poor."

This is the under-consumptionist moment of Marx's own analysis.

The Famous Volume 3 Passage: "The ultimate reason for all real crises always remains the poverty and restricted consumption of the masses, in the face of the drive of capitalist production to develop the productive forces as if only the absolute consumption power of society set a limit to them."

The Apparent Contradiction. Marx, however, states no less categorically in Volume 2:

"It is a pure tautology to say that crises are provoked by a lack of effective demand or effective consumption. The capitalist system does not recognize any forms of consumer other than those who can pay, if we exclude the consumption of paupers and swindlers. The fact that commodities are unsaleable means no more than that no effective buyers have been found for them... If the attempt is made to give this tautology the semblance of greater profundity, by the statement that the working class receives too small a portion of its own product, and that the evil would be remedied if it received a bigger share, i.e. if its wages rose, we need only note that crises are always prepared by a period in which wages generally rise, and the working class actually does receive a greater share in the part of the annual product destined for consumption... It thus appears that capitalist production involves certain conditions, independent of people's good or bad intentions, which permit the relative prosperity of the working class only temporarily, and moreover always as a harbinger of crisis."

Mandel's Resolution. There is no contradiction.

What Marx rejects is the "common-or-garden reformist or 'liberal' platitude, according to which crises could be avoided if, in the period immediately preceding or coinciding with the onset of over-production, the purchasing power in the hands of the masses were to be significantly increased."

The Reformist View Overlooks Two Facts:

  1. Not all commodities are consumer goods. An important fraction of the "commodity mountain" consists of means of production, which cannot be and are not intended to be bought by workers. An increase in sales of consumer goods "tells us nothing of the course of sales of equipment and raw materials. It does not lead automatically to greater productive investment."

  2. A redistribution from profits to wages would collapse investment. A sudden large rise in wages would result in a collapse of investment, i.e. of sales of means of production. If this were to follow a period of actual decline in the rate of profit, "then capital accumulation would contract very violently indeed and the crisis would remain unavoidable."

The Definition of "Under-Consumptionism." "Inasmuch as they forget this basic correlation of the trade cycle with medium-term fluctuations of the rate of profit, all economists (whether Marxist or non-Marxist) who explain the crisis exclusively or mainly in terms of the relation between the purchasing power of consumers and the national income are truly guilty of 'under-consumptionism', that is to say, of a one-sided and therefore erroneous theory."

Footnote 100 (Targets). The most noteworthy Marxist author of this type: Nathalia Moszkowska (Zur Kritik moderner Krisentheorien, Prague, 1935); but Fritz Sternberg and Paul Sweezy should also be mentioned. Non-Marxist: Sismondi, Malthus, Lederer, Keynes.

The Counterpart Error: "Pure Disproportionism." "The same is true of the opposite theory, which concentrates exclusively or mainly on the 'disproportion' between the two departments, explaining crises by the anarchy of production and the difficulty (impossibility) of establishing the 'right proportions' spontaneously (as if 'organized capitalism' or a 'general cartel' could avoid crisis!)."

Footnote 101 references the "neo-harmonicist" versions of Hilferding and Bauer, both inspired by Tugan-Baranowski's Studien zur Theorie. Hilferding's striking Finanzkapital statement: "A general cartel regulating total social production and thereby overcoming crises is, in principle, economically imaginable, even if such a social and political state of affairs is an impossibility" (p. 372). Bukharin was influenced by the same trend, asserting that under state capitalism, where anarchy of production has been overcome, there could be no crises of over-production. Drawing on these arguments, "Tony Cliff and his disciples have attempted to justify their use of the term 'state capitalism' to define the Soviet economy — an economy which has witnessed no crisis of over-production for more than half a century."

The Specific Error of Pure Disproportionism. "Overlooked in such a thesis is the fact, which Marx himself pointed out, that the 'disproportion' between the tendency of unlimited development of the productive forces and the narrow constraints placed upon consumption by the bourgeois mode of distribution, is itself a specific source of disequilibrium, autonomous from the disturbance of 'equilibrium relations' between the two departments."

Supporters of pure disproportionism also forget, "like Tugan-Baranowski, the father of pure 'disproportionism', that unlimited growth of department I leads to ever faster growth of the productive capacity of department II (although not necessarily in the same proportion); in other words, that under capitalist commodity relations production can never fully emancipate itself from sales to the final consumer."

Footnote 103 (Grundrisse quote): "It is quite the same with the demand created by production itself for raw material, semi-finished goods, machinery, means of communication, and for the auxiliary materials consumed in production... This effective, exchange-value-positing demand is adequate and sufficient as long as the producers exchange among themselves. Its inadequacy shows itself as soon as the final product encounters its limit in direct and final consumption" (Grundrisse, p. 421).

The Integrative Conclusion. "Thus theories of 'pure disproportionism' are as wrong as ones of 'pure under-consumptionism'. The basic causes of periodic crises of over-production are, at one and the same time, the inevitable periodic decline of the rate of profit, the capitalist anarchy of production, and the impossibility under capitalism of developing mass consumption in correlation with the growth of the productive forces."

The Insoluble Contradiction (Mandel's Core Thesis). The basic curse of capitalism — that surplus-value embodied in commodities can only be realized if they are sold at their value — "implies the presence of an insoluble contradiction at a given point of expanded reproduction."

The contradiction has two horns:

  1. Any measure which tries suddenly to reverse the decline of the rate of profit provokes a shrinking of the market of final consumers.
  2. Any attempt suddenly to reverse that shrinking accentuates the decline of the rate of profit.

Capitalist growth and prosperity require both a rising rate of profit (currently realized and anticipated) and an expanding market (present reality and future trend). But "the coincidence of these conditions can never be permanent, for the very forces which bring it into being at a given point in the trade cycle work towards its undoing at a subsequent stage."

Necessary Crisis. "In that sense, crises of over-production are unavoidable under capitalism. According to even the most optimistic hypothesis, 'anti-cyclical policies' can only reduce their scope temporarily; they cannot prevent the very 'moderation' obtained during one period from leading, in the long run, to more explosive side-effects (such as the cumulative movement of inflation, or the precipitate growth of the burden of company debt)."

Footnote 105: "Among these should be included not only 'purely' economic factors, but also the intertwining of the trade cycle with the partially autonomous cycle of the class struggle."

Footnote 106: See Chapter 13 of Late Capitalism for the roots, functions, and consequences of permanent inflation.

The Law of Value and Crises. The objective logic of crises is "connected with the operation of the law of value, clarified by an important remark made by Marx in Capital Volume 2." Equilibrium presupposes that commodities are sold "at the value they had at the moment of their production." But the very dynamic of expanded reproduction involves regular revolutions in technology, unceasing attempts by industrialists to win competitive struggle by reducing costs, growing substitution of machines for manual labour. All these phenomena imply a tendency for the value of each commodity to decline.

"Seen in this light, crises of over-production are nothing other than objective mechanisms through which the adjustment of market prices to declining commodity-values is achieved." Capital thereby incurs important losses (devalorizations), whether directly (through reduction in value of commodity capital) or indirectly (through bankruptcy and closure of the least efficient firms).

Footnote 108: "Declining value expressed in gold prices and not, of course, in inflated paper currency."

This is a striking formulation. Crises are not simply destructive events but functional mechanisms of capitalist self-adjustment: they devalorize capital, close inefficient firms, and align market prices to the successively declining values produced by rising productivity. The crisis is simultaneously a catastrophe and a self-correcting mechanism.

The Fixed Capital Turnover-Cycle Nexus. Marx stresses a nexus between the trade cycle and the turnover cycle of fixed capital "distinct from the usually mentioned one of determination grosso modo of the length of the former by the latter."

Fixed capital expenditure is discontinuous in a double sense:

  1. Replacement in toto. Machines are replaced not piecemeal (except current repairs) but in toto — once every seven or ten years.
  2. Simultaneity. Replacement tends to occur at the same time in numerous, inter-connected key branches of industry, precisely because the process is "not only, or even essentially, a function of physical wear and tear, but rather a response to financial incentives to introduce more advanced technology."

Footnote 109: "'Moral' wearing-out of equipment (obsolescence) generally predates 'physical' breakdown under capitalism, given the pressure of competition and accelerated technical progress."

The Three Criteria of Profit Calculation for Fixed Capital Investment: 1. Availability of sufficient money capital reserves 2. Rising rate of profit and profit expectations 3. Existence and/or anticipation of a sudden market expansion

"These incentives coincide only at a certain point in the trade cycle; but when this occurs, there follows a massive investment in the renewal of fixed capital. This in turn sets up a dynamic of accelerated capital accumulation and economic growth, together with rapid expansion of markets, which leads finally to an increase in the organic composition of capital, a declining trend of the rate of profit and a tendency to slow down investment and renewal of fixed capital."

The Structural Asymmetry. "The difficulty is compounded by the fact that the productive capacity of the sub-branch of department I which produces means of production for the production of means of production, must normally be geared to the general demand for the renewal of fixed capital (at least in its social average). Thus while this sub-branch may be overtaken by peak demand at the moment of 'overheating', it will suffer from unused capacity during a considerable part of the trade cycle."

Footnote 110: Mandel notes that "academic economic theory later took over this essentially Marxist contribution to the theory of the trade cycle."

2.12 Money Circulation, Money Capital and Money Hoarding

Mandel now examines the monetary dimension — "one of the most 'modern' aspects of Marx's analysis."

The Key Insight. Marx "fundamentally anticipates the Keynesian problematic of money hoarding" — withdrawal of money from the process of productive circulation (circulation geared to the realization and reproduction of surplus-value).

The Initial Assumption. For reproduction to flow smoothly, all income generated in the production process must be spent on the commodities produced. "Any additional purchasing power injected into the reproduction process at a given point must be expelled at another point, if the process is to continue in a balanced way."

The Tendency to Hoard. The very functioning of the capitalist mode leads to periodic hoarding of money capital.

Mechanism 1: Circulation time fluctuations. Marx points out that successive expansions and contractions of the circulation time of commodities — related to phases of the trade cycle — result in periodic expansions and contractions of money capital as compared with productive capital.

Mechanism 2: Production time fluctuations. The shortening or lengthening of the production process itself (e.g. increase or reduction of the weight in the total product-mix of commodities requiring lengthy production time) gives rise to contraction or expansion of the volume of money capital in circulation. The shorter the production time, the quicker the turnover of productive capital, and the smaller the money reserves which capitalists must throw into circulation to cover wages and consumption until commodities are finished and sold. "Conversely, a lengthening of the production time will result in a lengthening of the turnover time of capital, and an increase in the reserves of money capital and money revenue that have to be injected into the circulation process to maintain consumption until the production and sale of the commodities is completed."

Footnote 111: See pp. 358-9, 364-6.

The Buy-Without-Sell / Sell-Without-Buy Problem. More generally, the harmonious flow of expanded reproduction is "constantly threatened (not permanently upset, of course), because there are always capitalists who buy without selling, and others who sell without buying. Money is continually being withdrawn from circulation, and additional money is forever being injected. Only if these movements roughly cancel each other out will the partially autonomous character of the money flow not conflict with the need to realize the total value of commodities produced."

The Banking System's Role. "While the banking system objectively strives to achieve that balance (and thus represents a force of social accounting and centralization far superior to anything private ownership could accomplish in the realm of production), it does not have the means to ensure automatic and continual balancing. Here there appears a further cause of discontinuity or interruption of expanded production — a cause which, though derived from monetary phenomena, is of course essentially rooted in the contradictory nature of the commodity and of the production of value and surplus-value."

This is a nuanced appreciation of banking: it is a force of centralization superior to what private property can achieve in production, yet it cannot ensure automatic balance because it operates within a system grounded in the contradictory commodity.

The Additional Proportions. "A series of proportions, additional to those which emerge prima facie from the reproduction schemas, play an important role in amplifying, if not triggering off, the trade cycle."

  1. The way the total money stock is divided between circulating money and hoarded money.
  2. The way circulating money is divided between circulating money capital and circulating revenue.
  3. The way hoarded money is divided between latent (potential) productive capital (money capital which will tend to contribute to increased production of surplus-value) and capital more or less permanently hoarded (withdrawn from both the sphere of production and the sphere of commodity circulation).

"All these proportions significantly influence the volume and rhythm of capital accumulation."

Footnote 112: See pp. 260-61. Footnote 113: Emmanuel correctly stresses the role of hoarding in Marx's theory of crises. He uses the expression vouloir d'achat (purchasing desire) as opposed to pouvoir d'achat (purchasing power).

Keynes and Marx Compared. Keynes was correct to discard the assumption of more or less permanent full employment of manpower and capital. He was also right to point out that capital or revenue not spent (i.e. hoarded) is an important source of disequilibrium and under-employment of productive resources. "In fact, Marx had argued as much sixty-five years earlier, in Capital Volume 2."

But: "the latter's understanding of the fundamental mechanisms of the capitalist mode of production proved more profound than that of Keynes. For Marx went a step further by distinguishing between productive investment (i.e. investment leading to increased production of surplus-value) and unproductive 'investment' (which cannot directly augment the total social wealth and real income, but only contribute indirectly to re-allocation and redeployment of existing resources). After all, building pyramids and digging canals in order to fill them up again does not have the same effect upon economic growth, capital accumulation and expanded reproduction as building new factories and opening up new oil fields. Buying government bonds in order to finance the building of pyramids is evidently not the same kind of activity as the investment of productive capital."

Footnote 114 (Mattick Critique). "Paul Mattick (Marx and Keynes, London, 1969) does not make the matter any clearer by a confused use of the concept 'waste production'. 'Waste', in the sense of products not entering into the reproduction process, and 'waste' in the sense of unsellable products, are not at all identical concepts. Luxury products are — like arms — commodities, and they find buyers. Public works and other infrastructural outlays are not carried out for the purpose of sale, but in order to accelerate the turnover of capital and thereby indirectly to increase the production of surplus-value. However, pyramids or canals which are dug and then filled up again are pure waste — they are neither commodities to be sold nor means of hastening the turnover of capital."

This is a tri-partite distinction: (a) unproductive but sold commodities (luxury goods, arms); (b) infrastructural outlays that accelerate turnover; (c) pure waste. Mattick's conflation of these categories, Mandel argues, undermines the analytical clarity of his work.

Four Causes of Rising Commodity Prices. From the monetary analysis dispersed throughout Volume 2, Mandel identifies four distinct causes of rising commodity prices:

(a) Falling productivity in a branch. A fall in average productivity of labour in a given branch (e.g. agricultural or mining, where decline in natural fertility is not offset by technological progress); prices rise as a result of an increase in value of particular commodities (i.e. the quantity of social labour necessary for their production).

(b) Falling value of gold. A sudden increase of labour productivity in the gold-mining industry (and thus a decline in the value of gold); all other things equal, the same mass of commodities exchanges for a greater amount of gold (produced by the same quantity of labour as before). In other words, the gold price of commodities rises.

(c) Market-price fluctuations around unchanged values. An upward trend of market price-fluctuations around an unchanged axis of values. This may occur even when the gold currency remains stable and there is no paper money inflation, at that precise stage of the trade cycle marked by the periodic contraction of the hoarded part of money as compared with the circulating part.

(d) Inflationary movement of money signs. A constant amount of gold — which exchanges against the same amount of commodities as before on the basis of an unaltered quantity of socially necessary labour — becomes represented by a greater sum of paper money signs (or of bank money, credit money).

Footnote 115: Karl Marx, A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, Moscow, 1971, pp. 118-20. See also Grundrisse, pp. 121-2 and 212-13.

This fourfold typology is significant because it demonstrates that "inflation" is not a single phenomenon. Gold-value declines, productivity declines, cyclical fluctuations, and monetary debasement are four distinct causal processes. Policy that treats them as one will fail.

2.13 Growth and Crisis

The final section of Mandel's Introduction is a synthesis and a rhetorical crescendo.

The Central Message. "The central 'message' of Volume 2, like that of Volume 1, refers to a terrifyingly dynamic process."

Volume 1 indicates why capital, by its very essence, is value in perpetual search of additional value, produced by workers in the process of production. "The unquenchable thirst for surplus-value is the fundamental motor of economic growth, technological revolution, 'research and development' spending, improvement of communications, 'third-world aid', the sales drive and market research." A corresponding quest for individual enrichment appears at the core of every level of bourgeois society, together with increasing alienation of workers and all human beings, and a growing threat that the forces of production will be transformed into forces of destruction.

"Paradoxically, mankind increasingly loses control over its own products and productive endeavour at the very moment when its mastery of nature and natural forces seems to be developing by leaps and bounds."

Footnote 116: "This domination of nature increasingly takes the form of the destruction (Raubbau) of nature, as is shown by the threats to ecological equilibrium."

This is a remarkable nod to ecology — in 1976, before the widespread development of eco-Marxism — and it highlights the darker dialectic of "mastery of nature" as "destruction of nature."

The Avalanche. In Volume 2, we follow commodities containing the surplus-value produced by workers on their travels outside the factory. A "spiralling movement" of growth is unleashed — "a veritable avalanche."

Footnote 117: "Marx and Luxemburg borrowed the image of the spiral as an expression of the form of capitalist development from Simonde de Sismondi."

The Dynamics. The sale of commodities at their value enables profit to be realized and additional capital accumulated. More capital begets more surplus-value, which in turn begets more capital. Obstacles on the road of self-expansion — "the enforced lingering of commodities in the sphere of circulation, or the protracted character of the production process itself" — are swept away by the avalanche, thanks to:

  • The social division of labour within the capitalist class
  • The appearance of commercial and banking capital
  • The constant striving to accelerate the transport of commodities, build up a world-wide system of communications, and reduce the length of the circulation process to a minimum

"An immense mountain of commodities is distributed with lightning speed around the globe, so that a steadily growing stream of value (money capital) may be concentrated in the hands of an ever smaller percentage (if not necessarily a shrinking absolute number) of the world's active population. Today's real masters are to be found in probably no more than 1,000 or 2,000 firms the world over."

Footnote 118 — The Pyramid Theory of Capitalism. This is a rich footnote. Mandel explains that the concentration described "does not mean... that the hundreds of thousands of smaller capitalist entrepreneurs, and the several million capitalist rentier families, are not part of the world bourgeoisie, but simply that they no longer command the decisive means of production or take the key investment decisions. Bourgeois society has the form of a pyramid in which the summit of monopolists could not survive without the support of different strata of large and medium bourgeois and their retainers (as well as the, at least partial, support of sections of the petty bourgeoisie)."

Crucially, Mandel warns against the anti-monopoly political strategy: "The notion that capitalism could be abolished by eliminating the monopolists alone does not take account of the fact that capitalism inevitably grows out of even petty commodity production where conditions of money circulation and widespread private ownership of the means of production prevail. If a significant sector of medium-sized capitalist firms is retained (and some of the 'non-monopolist' capitalists are rather large-scale ones!) then capitalism would not only survive, but flourish and open up the road leading to the formation of new monopolies."

This is a targeted political intervention against the "anti-monopoly alliance" strategy, influential in some Communist parties in the 1960s–70s. Mandel's point: neither the elimination of monopolists nor the preservation of "competitive" firms can overcome capitalism. The system reconstitutes itself out of petty commodity production as long as the general conditions of private property and money circulation persist.

The Human Cost. The "frenetic search for additional wealth in order to create even more wealth becomes increasingly divorced from basic human needs and interests, increasingly opposed to the 'production of a rich individuality' and the 'rich development of social relations' encompassing all human beings."

This is a humanist-Marxist moment. The quote from the Grundrisse about "rich individuality" and "rich development of social relations" (the famous "rich human being" passage) is invoked as a normative counterpoint to capitalist wealth.

The Inevitable Breakdown. "But the process cannot continue smoothly and uninterruptedly: capital is powerless to overcome the basic contradictions of the commodity and private property. From both sides, the contradictions of production for its own sake (i.e. production in order to augment the profits of those who own the major means of production) must lead to periodic discharge in huge social and economic convulsions."

The Empirical Confirmation. "Following the social explosion initiated in the Western world by May '68 in France, the severe generalized recession of 1974-5 has confirmed Marx's basic analysis."

Footnote 119: "See the last chapter of Late Capitalism, and my articles on the generalized recession of the international capitalist economy in Inprecor (16 January 1975, 5 June 1975, 18 December 1975 and 15 September 1976)."

Mandel wrote this Introduction in the midst of the 1974-5 recession. The dual event — May 1968's social explosion and 1974-5's economic recession — is presented as practical confirmation of the theoretical claims.

The Final Theorem. "Capitalist growth cannot but be uneven, disproportionate and unharmonious. Expanded reproduction necessarily gives rise to contracted reproduction. Prosperity inexorably leads to over-production. The search for the philosopher's stone which would enable market economy (i.e. private property, i.e. competition) to coincide with balanced growth, and mass consumption to develop apace with productive capacity (despite the capitalists' drive to force up the rate of exploitation) — this search will go on as long as the system survives. But it will be no more crowned with success than that which has already been conducted for more than 150 years. The only possible remedy for economic crises of over-production and social crises of class struggle is the elimination of capitalism and class society. No other solution will be found, either in theory or in practice. This awe-inspiring prediction made by Marx has been borne out by empirical evidence ever since Capital was written. There is no sign that it will be contradicted by current or future developments."

ERNEST MANDEL

This is the closing movement: from the abstract to the historical, from the Grundrisse's vision of "rich individuality" to the hard realities of 1974-5, from the reproduction schemas to the revolutionary conclusion. The prose rate accelerates into a kind of theoretical percussion. Expanded reproduction gives rise to contracted reproduction. Prosperity to over-production. The only remedy is the elimination of capitalism. No qualifications, no hedging.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Fundamental Principles Revealed

1. The Primacy of the "Intertwining" Over the "Elements." The deepest methodological lesson of Mandel's Introduction is that Marx's theoretical innovation in Volume 2 lies not in any single concept but in the intertwining itself — the demonstration that the reproduction of capital is impossible to understand except as a simultaneous movement of value-flow and money-flow, of production and circulation, of individual capitals and total social capital. All methodological errors Mandel diagnoses — from Samuelson's "public selling capital goods" to Luxemburg's "single total capital" to Dobb's value-blind socialist planning — arise from breaking the intertwining apart.

2. The Dual Nature of Everything Capitalist. The commodity's duality (use-value / exchange-value, concrete labour / abstract labour) is the seed of every duality Marx develops: the dual aspect of turnover (value-realization / use-value adequacy), the dual flow (commodity-flow / money-flow), the dual meaning of proportionality conditions, the duality of the two departments, the duality of "productive for the individual capitalist" vs. "productive for capital as a whole," the duality of consumption (productive / unproductive). Mandel's Introduction is, in a sense, a systematic unfolding of the consequences of the commodity form.

3. Equilibrium as Exception, Disequilibrium as Rule. This is the central theorem. The schemas prove that growth is possible under capitalism; they do not prove it is automatic. Equilibrium is a "marginal exception." Disproportionality, over-production, and crisis are not breakdowns of the system but its normal mode of functioning. Marx's dialectic — "proportionality presents itself as a constant process through disproportionality" — is not a verbal paradox but a substantive theoretical claim.

4. Abstraction as Staging, Not Assumption. Marx's method of successive approximation means that abstractions at one level of analysis are provisional, to be concretized at the next stage. Critics from Luxemburg to Harris who treat Marx's "assumptions" as empirical claims miss the methodological structure. This is a fundamental lesson for reading Capital as a whole.

5. The Historicity of Categories. Both the reproduction schemas and the productive/unproductive labour distinction are historically specific to the capitalist mode of production. To extend them to socialist or pre-capitalist societies is to commit errors of category, with consequences that are both theoretical and political. The socialist societies do not have "surplus-value" that grows at rates determined by reproduction schemas; they have surplus product subject to wholly different social goals (reduction of work-load, satisfaction of rational needs, withering of commodity production).

6. The Centrality of Time. Mandel's insistence on the "time dimension" is a major contribution to reading Volume 2. Time enters the analysis in multiple registers: as the measure of value (labour-time), as the medium of turnover (circuit of capital), as the horizon of laws of motion (trade cycles, class struggle cycles, long-term historical cycles), and as the existential substance of human life. To neglect time — as both pure production theory and Keynesian money-flow theory sometimes do — is to miss what makes Volume 2 the dynamic analysis that it is.

Practical, Theoretical, and Political Implications

For Crisis Theory. Mandel's reconstruction of a multi-causal theory — periodic decline in the rate of profit, anarchy of production, restricted consumption of the masses, hoarding, discontinuous fixed-capital renewal, devalorization of capital — is a decisive rejection of any single-cause theory. Both "under-consumptionist" (Sismondi, Malthus, Sweezy) and "disproportionist" (Tugan-Baranowski, Hilferding, Bauer) explanations are partial. The crisis is overdetermined. And crucially, crises are not merely destructive: they are mechanisms for the devalorization of capital through which the decline in the rate of profit is periodically overcome.

For the Theory of Imperialism. The section on commercial and financial capital's participation in surplus-value distribution silently reframes the whole problematic of underdevelopment. Primitive accumulation and productive accumulation are not successive stages but "simultaneous and combined phenomena." The one-sided expansion of unproductive capital (merchant, usury, banking) at the expense of industrialization explains why some peripheral regions fail to industrialize — not for lack of resources, but because the balance of class forces and the specific form of integration into world capitalism direct accumulation into unproductive channels. This is a structural, not merely moral, theory of underdevelopment.

For Class Analysis. Mandel's definition of the proletariat — the class compelled by socio-economic compulsion to sell its labour-power — includes unproductive wage-labourers (clerks, civil servants, service workers, domestic servants), while excluding managers whose salary permits capital accumulation. The political implication is that the working class has a material interest in defending socialized wages and social expenditure — the theory of "all state expenditure as surplus-value," far from being a radical position, is objectively a position of indifference to welfare cuts.

For Debates on State Capitalism. The critique of Bukharin, Cliff, and the "state capitalism" school is sharp: reading state capitalism out of the absence of crises in the USSR commits the error of assuming that eliminating the "anarchy of production" eliminates crises. Theoretically, this contradicts Marx's own analysis; empirically, the claim is devoid of meaning because it lacks a determinate class analysis of the Soviet social formation.

For Political Strategy. The footnote-118 warning against the anti-monopoly strategy — "capitalism inevitably grows out of even petty commodity production" — rejects the political theory that monopoly capitalism can be overcome by allying with "non-monopoly" capital. The system reconstitutes itself. Only the elimination of the general conditions — private property in the means of production and generalized money circulation — opens the path beyond.

For Ecology. The brief mention of Raubbau der Natur ("destruction/plunder of nature") and the ecological threats signals a nascent awareness — in 1976 — that "mastery of nature" and "destruction of nature" are two names for the same process. Twenty-first-century ecological Marxism owes much to this recognition.

For Methodology. The single most important lesson is the rigor with which Mandel insists on the distinction between levels of analysis. To confuse "capital in general" with "many capitals," to confuse value calculation with price of production calculation, to confuse the schemas' equilibrium conditions with historical assertions about capitalist development — these are not merely technical errors. They are errors that reproduce themselves politically: neo-harmonicism, the "law of priority development of department I," the "achievements of socialist planning," and the "all state expenditure = surplus-value" position all rest on the same fundamental confusion.

Broader Philosophical Implications

Mandel's Introduction is, finally, a work of philosophical anthropology in disguise. The two departments of reproduction are not arbitrary: they express the "essential character of human production in general" — that man must metabolize with nature, and can only do so by using tools. Capitalist reproduction is thus a specific form of a general human process, and its contradictions are specific contradictions within the general process. When Marx writes of "constant relative over-production" under socialism, and when Engels writes of directly calculating labour-time, and when both write of "rich individuality" and "rich development of social relations," they are pointing to a possible form of human reproduction in which the contradictions of the commodity form — the deferred recognition of social labour, the pathological separation of use-value and exchange-value, the impossibility of knowing in advance whether labour has been socially validated — would be overcome, not merely managed.

The "sealed book" is thus not sealed because it is technical. It is sealed because it contains the most radical and demanding message: capitalism's own dynamic — its spiraling growth, its technological revolutions, its global reach — is inseparable from its self-destruction. To read Volume 2 carefully is to understand why the search for a capitalism without crises is the search for a philosopher's stone. There is no such thing. There is only the elimination of the conditions that make the search necessary.

Mandel's concluding sentence — "There is no sign that it will be contradicted by current or future developments" — is a wager. Written in the aftermath of the 1974-5 recession, at the peak of Mandel's influence, at the close of a long period of capitalist expansion, it claims that the theoretical apparatus of Volume 2 remains adequate to every subsequent development. It is a wager that, fifty years on, we are still in a position to evaluate.
