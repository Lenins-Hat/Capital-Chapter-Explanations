Volume 2 • Chapter 4: The Three Figures of the Circuit

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 4 is the culminating and unifying chapter of the first great subdivision of Volume 2 — the analysis of the metamorphoses of capital and their circuits. In the three preceding chapters Marx had dissected, one at a time and in abstract isolation, three separate circuits: that of money capital (M…M'), that of productive capital (P…P), and that of commodity capital (C'…C'). Each was treated as if a single capital, in its entirety, passed through one of these circuits alone. The task of Chapter 4 is to abolish that abstraction — to show that the three circuits are not three alternatives from which a capital may choose, nor three historical stages, but three figures of one and the same real movement, which must exist together if the movement is to be a movement at all. The chapter's famous summary sentence states the thesis exactly: "Here, therefore, the entire circuit is the real unity of its three forms."

The central problem the chapter resolves is the apparent contradiction between the interrupted, jump-like, exclusionary character of each individual phase of the circuit and the continuity that capitalist production requires "by its technical basis, even if it is not always completely attainable." Considered in its pure immediacy, the circuit of capital is "a constant process of interruption": one stage is left behind, the next embarked upon; one form is cast aside and the capital exists in another; "each of these stages not only conditions the other, but at the same time excludes it." If the total capital had to lie wholly in one form at a time, production would proceed "only in spasms," like that of a Chinese handicraftsman working only for individual clients. The resolution is that the continuity of the whole is purchased by the division of the capital into parts that occupy different phases simultaneously: the industrial capital as a totality is "simultaneously in all of its stages, and in the various functional forms corresponding to them." Continuity of the aggregate is achieved through the spatial simultaneity — the coexistence (Nebeneinander) — of parts whose temporal sequence (Nacheinander) converts coexistence back into a result of succession. The unity of the three circuits is thus not a static identity but a dialectical unity of opposites, produced and perpetually re-produced by a division of the capital into functionally distinct fractions.

A second and equally fundamental thesis is that capital must be grasped "as a movement, and not as a static thing." Capital is self-valorizing value, and therefore an abstraction that is real: "Those who consider the autonomization [Verselbständigung] of value as a mere abstraction forget that the movement of industrial capital is this abstraction in action." Value passes through different forms, is preserved and increased in them, and is continually compared with itself across phases that are "in no way 'contemporary,' but rather occur in succession." This is the theoretical stake of the excursus against Samuel Bailey, whose doctrine that "value is a relation between contemporary commodities" denies precisely the temporal self-identity that constitutes value as capital. Value revolutions, crises in which the "independent value, acting with the force of an elemental natural process," overwhelms the foresight of individual capitalists, do not refute the autonomy of value — they confirm and intensify it.

A third thesis concerns method. The "simple intertwining of the metamorphoses" that all commodity circulation has in common cannot explain the replacement of the components of total social capital. "Up till now, mere phrases have been taken as sufficient in this respect"; the analysis requires "a different mode of investigation." The chapter thereby announces, from within, the limits of its own procedure and points forward to the turnover analysis (Part II) and, decisively, to the reproduction schemas of total social capital (Part III).

The chapter closes with an appended fragment — the editor notes that the text up to a marked point derives from Manuscript V, while the remainder is "a note found among extracts from books in a notebook of 1877 or 1878." This fragment states a fourth thesis at the level of the class as a whole: the industrial capitalist's demand is structurally and necessarily smaller than his supply, by exactly the surplus-value he produces. Demand = c + v; supply = c + v + s. The gap is valorization itself. Any assumption that the capitalist simply consumes his surplus-value and demands back what he supplies is "equivalent to assuming the non-existence of capitalist production." This is the seed of the realization problem and of the necessity of credit.

Situated at the hinge of Volume 2, the chapter converts the formal analysis of a single capital into the material premise for the analysis of total social reproduction — and, in doing so, provides the deepest Marxian statement of capital as a self-moving, self-positing, temporal subject.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Three Figures Set Out: Valorization as the Determining Purpose

The chapter opens by rendering the three circuits as a single set of figures, behind which stands the notation Tc — "the total circulation process." This symbol is a compression: it stands for the whole of the circulation phase, i.e., for the sequence C—M and M—C taken together (and their unity in the turnover of the capital), in contrast to P, the production phase. With Tc so defined, the three figures appear as:

  • (I) M—C … P … C'—M' — the circuit of money capital, beginning and ending in the money form, with production as an interlude between two circulation acts.
  • (II) P … Tc … P — the circuit of productive capital, beginning and ending in production, with the entire circulation process (both the sale of the product and the repurchase of the elements of production) as the interlude.
  • (III) Tc … P (…C') — the circuit of commodity capital, beginning with value already valorized and emerging in circulation, passing through production, and closing with the newly valorized commodity (the page's OCR fragment "(CO." is a corruption of the closing C'). (The mangled formula "(II) a II)" appearing later on page 14 is likewise a manuscript/page-break artefact; the argument is unaffected at the conceptual level, and I treat it as such.)

Marx immediately draws out what is common to all three. First: "the valorization of value as the determining purpose, the driving motive." This is the definitional core. Industrial capital, in the strict sense Marx uses throughout Volume 2, is capital functionally engaged in the production sphere — manufacturing, agriculture, mining, transport, etc. — as distinct from merchant's capital and interest-bearing capital, which are derived, secondary forms presupposing it. The chapter's point is that what unifies three otherwise heterogeneous sequences is not their shape but their telos: M' > M, P producing P' with expanded value, C' closing on C'' > C'.

Second, and with a fine discrimination of how the telos is expressed: in Figure I the purpose is expressed "actually … in the form" — the form itself, M—M', wears its drive visibly, since the first and last terms are money and the arrow between them is the whole secret. In Figure II, the form begins with P, "the valorization process itself" — value-creation stands openly at the head of the circuit, but the quantitative increase is no longer visible in the termini (P…P looks like the same P returning). In Figure III, the circuit "begins with the valorized value, and closes with the newly valorized value, even when the movement is repeated on the same scale." This last clause is important: even in simple reproduction — where the newly valorized value is merely consumed as revenue and the same capital sum is re-advanced — the C'…C' circuit still begins and ends with valorized value, and therefore still objectively exhibits surplus-value production as its point of departure and return. The form displays the result of valorization even where it does not display accumulation.

2.2 The Rotating Orbit: Why Circularity Requires Continuity

The chapter then invokes Volume 1, Chapter 3, on the general circulation of commodities. Since C—M for the seller is M—C for the buyer and vice versa, the circulation of capital, considered merely formally, "simply displays the general metamorphosis of commodities," and the laws governing the quantity of money in circulation apply to it. But Marx at once restricts this concession: "if we do not just dwell on this formal aspect … but consider the real connection between the metamorphoses of the various individual capitals, in fact the connection between the circuits of individual capitals as partial movements of the reproduction process of the total social capital," then the process "cannot be explained in terms of the simple change of form between money and commodity." The concession will be taken back — rigorously and by construction — in §2.19 below.

The image that organizes the next argument is that of a rotating orbit: "In a constantly rotating orbit, every point is simultaneously a starting-point and a point of return. If we interrupt the rotation, then not every starting-point is a point of return." This is more than rhetoric. In a completed circle, the beginning and the end are analytically indifferent — any point can be treated as a starting point because the motion is closed. The three circuits, in their perfect realization, therefore appear interchangeable, and "the entire distinction presents itself as merely one of form, a merely subjective distinction that exists only for the observer." But this appearance is produced precisely by assuming completion — by assuming the rotation never stops. In reality, each individual industrial capital is involved in all three circuits at the same time: "One part of the capital value, for example, which for the moment functions as commodity capital, is transformed into money capital, while at the same time another part passes out of the production process into circulation as new commodity capital. Thus the circular form of C'…C' is constantly described, and the same is the case with the two other forms." Hence the conclusion: "the entire circuit is the real unity of its three forms."

2.3 The Abstract Premise of the Whole Capital in One Form and the Resulting "Spasms" of Production

Marx now makes explicit the assumption under which the earlier chapters proceeded, in order to expose what it costs. We assumed "that the capital value appears either as money capital, productive capital or commodity capital to the full extent of its magnitude." In his running example, £422 exists first entirely as money capital, then is transformed entirely into productive capital, then exists as commodity capital to the value of £500 — of which £78 is surplus-value. (The arithmetic of the example: 10,000 lb. of yarn at £500 implies 1 shilling per lb.; 8,440 lb. × 1s. = £422, the capital value to be replaced, and 1,560 lb. × 1s. = £78, the surplus product borne by the same commodity mass. Rate of surplus-value = 78/78 = 100%, consistent with the Volume 2 running example in which v = 78.)

Given that premise, "the various stages here constitute an equal number of interruptions." While the £422 persists in the money form, the total capital functions only as money capital; once transformed into productive capital, it functions neither as money nor as commodity capital; its circulation is interrupted for the whole duration of production, exactly as its production is interrupted for the whole duration of circulation. The circuit P…P would then present itself "not only as a periodic renewal of the productive capital, but equally as an interruption in its function." Production would be carried on "only in spasms," its rhythm dictated by "periods of time of accidental duration," according to whether the two circulation stages proceeded faster or more slowly. Marx's illustration is the Chinese handicraftsman who "works only for individual clients, and whose production process comes to a halt between one order and the next." This figure is the negative image of capitalist production: production whose continuity depends on a market already made, not on a market yet to be found.

The point is generalized: "This is in fact true for each individual portion of capital in motion, and all portions of the capital go through this movement in succession." Take 10,000 lb. of yarn as one week's output of a spinning-mill. That mass moves in its entirety from production into circulation; the capital value in it must be entirely converted into money; and as long as it persists in money form it cannot re-enter production — it must first be transformed back into the elements of productive capital, L (labour-power) and mp (means of production). Hence: "The circuit of capital is a constant process of interruption; one stage is left behind, the next stage embarked upon; one form is cast aside, and the capital exists in another; each of these stages not only conditions the other, but at the same time excludes it." The dialectic compressed into that sentence — mutual conditioning alongside mutual exclusion — is the engine of the entire chapter.

2.4 The Real Continuity: Succession and Coexistence

Against this, Marx sets the technical requirement: "continuity is the characteristic feature of capitalist production, and is required by its technical basis, even if it is not always completely attainable." He then asks "how things proceed in reality," and answers with a description of overlapping phases. While the 10,000 lb. of yarn steps onto the market as commodity capital and undergoes its transformation into money — "whether as means of payment, means of purchase or simply money of account" (a reminder that the metamorphosis may be legally complete before money physically moves) — new cotton, coal, etc. come into the production process in its place. These have already been transformed back from both money form and commodity form into productive capital and begun to function as such. Simultaneously, while the first 10,000 lb. is being converted into money, a previous 10,000 lb. is already describing the second stage of its circulation, being reconverted from money into the elements of production. Hence: "industrial capital in the continuity of its circuit is simultaneously in all of its stages, and in the various functional forms corresponding to them." The vivid statement of this simultaneity: "Money is given out with one hand and taken in with the other; what is at one point the commencement of the circuit M…M' is simultaneously at another point its conclusion. The same applies for the productive capital."

The definitional conclusion follows: "The real circuit of industrial capital in its continuity is therefore not only a unified process of circulation and production, but also a unity of all its three circuits." And this unity is conditional on the division of the capital: it can be such a unity only in so far as "each different part of the capital runs in succession through the successive phases of the circuit, can pass over from one phase and one functional form into the other; hence industrial capital, as the whole of these parts, exists simultaneously in its various phases and functions, and thus describes all three circuits at once."

2.5 Coexistence as Result and as Condition — The Dialectic of Nacheinander / Nebeneinander

The theoretical pivot of the chapter is stated in one sentence: "The succession [Nacheinander] of the various parts is here determined by their coexistence [Nebeneinander], i.e. by the way in which the capital is divided." The German terms matter, because the whole argument is about a specific form of the relation between time and space in the movement of value. Nacheinander — one-after-another — is the temporal form of the individual portion, which can only be in one phase at a time. Nebeneinander — side-by-side — is the spatial form of the aggregate, whose parts are distributed across the phases. The continuity of the whole is thus a spatialization of time: simultaneity in the circulation process is achieved by dividing the capital into fractions whose phases differ.

Marx reinforces this with an analogy from the factory system: in developed manufacture and machinery production, "the product is continuously at the various stages of its formation, and in transition from one phase of production to another" — the same logic of overlapping partial processes. Then he specifies the quantitative precondition. Each individual industrial capital "has a definite size, which is dependent on the means of the capitalist and has a definite minimum for each branch of industry," so "definite numerical ratios must obtain in its division into parts." The size of the capital determines the scale of the production process; this determines the volume of commodity capital and of money capital that must function alongside the production process.

But — and here is the crucial dialectical inversion — "The coexistence which determines the continuity of production, however, exists only through the movement in which the portions of capital successively describe the various stages. The coexistence is itself only the result of the succession." Coexistence is both condition and result: as a given division of capital, it makes continuity possible; as a produced distribution, it is nothing but the standing precipitate of an ongoing succession. There is no static equilibrium image here. The "coexistence" is a mode of motion, not a state.

2.6 Determination of the Proportions and the Breakdown Mechanism (C'—M' Interrupted)

From this follows a theory of disturbance. "If C'—M' comes to a halt in the case of one portion, for example, if the commodity is unsaleable, then the circuit of this part is interrupted and its replacement by its means of production is not accomplished; the successive parts that emerge from the production process as C' find their change of function barred by their predecessors. If this continues for some time, production is restricted and the whole process brought to a standstill." This is the general mechanism of blockage, and it operates strictly within the circuit itself — independent of any theory of crisis rooted in distribution or in the falling rate of profit. The metaphor is of a pipeline: each arriving unit is blocked by the unremoved unit ahead of it, so a local stoppage in circulation converts directly into a general stoppage of production. The formal statement: "Every delay in the succession brings the coexistence into disarray, every delay in one stage causes a greater or lesser delay in the entire circuit, not only that of the portion of the capital that is delayed, but also that of the entire individual capital."

The emphasis on unsaleability (rather than on insufficient profit) is significant, and it connects to the chapter's later treatment of value revolutions: the circuit's vulnerability lies in the form-determined necessity of sale, i.e., in the fact that use-value must be validated as value on the market before the capital can resume its productive form. The chapter does not develop a crisis theory here — that belongs elsewhere — but it provides the mechanism on which any such theory must rest: the unity of the circuits is real, and therefore the interruption of one circuit propagates through all the others.

2.7 Immediacy vs. Totality: The Fluid Forms and the Unity of the Three Circuits

Marx now returns to the level of appearance. "The immediate form in which the process presents itself is that of a succession of phases, so that the transition of the capital into a new phase is determined by its abandonment of the previous one." This is the phenomenal form — the Erscheinungsform — and it is partial and misleading in the familiar Marxian way: it presents the circuit as a linear sequence, one phase following another, and each phase as an independent starting and returning point. Hence, "every particular circuit has one of the functional forms of the capital as its starting-point and point of return."

Against this immediate representation stands the totality: "the total process is in fact the unity of the three circuits, which are the different forms in which the continuity of the process is expressed." Each figure thus appears as an autonomous specific circuit, but each conditions the continuity of the whole: "the circular course of one functional form determines that of the others." For the total social capital, continuity of production means that the process is simultaneously a process of reproduction, "and hence the circuit of each of its moments." Different fractions pass through different stages and forms; each functional form therefore "passes through its circuit simultaneously with the others, though it is always a different part of the capital that presents itself in it." Marx enumerates: a part of the capital exists as commodity capital being transformed into money — "but this is an ever-changing part, and is constantly being reproduced"; another part exists as money capital being transformed into productive capital; a third as productive capital being transformed into commodity capital. "The constant presence of all three forms is mediated by the circuit of the total capital through precisely these three phases."

The chapter's culminating formulation is a hybrid of spatial and temporal determination: "As a whole, then, the capital is simultaneously present, and spatially coexistent, in its various phases. But each part is constantly passing from one phase or functional form into another, and thus functions in all of them in turn. The forms are therefore fluid forms, and their simultaneity is mediated by their succession." And further: "Each form both follows and precedes the others, so that the return of one part of the capital to one form is determined by the return of another part to another form." Continuity is thus not a property of any moment but of the relation among moments; and it is only "in the unity of the three circuits that the continuity of the overall process is realized, in place of the interruption we have just delineated." Finally, a distinction between individual and total capital is drawn that will govern the rest of Volume 2: "The total social capital always possesses this continuity, and its process always contains the unity of the three circuits."

2.8 The Degree of Interruption: Individual Capitals, Branches, Seasons

The chapter immediately qualifies the claim for individual capitals: "For individual capitals, the continuity of reproduction is at certain points interrupted, to a greater or lesser degree." Three sources of interruption are distinguished. First, quantitative disproportion: "the quantities of value are frequently distributed amongst the various stages and functional forms in unequal portions, at different times." Second, technically determined division: the portions are differently divided "according to the character of the commodity which has to be produced, thus according to the particular sphere of production in which the capital has been invested" — the length of the production period and the nature of the labour process set upper bounds on how finely a capital can be split. Third, seasonality, whether from "natural conditions (agriculture, fishing for herrings, etc.)" or "as a matter of convention as is the case with so-called seasonal work." Marx adds a telling observation: "It is in the factory and in mining that the process occurs most regularly and uniformly." The factory — the site of the mechanical and the regular — approximates the ideal form of the circuit; agriculture, the most seasonal, deviates most. But the crucial methodological conclusion is a limit on the variability: "this difference between branches of production does not give rise to any difference in the general forms of the circuit." The analysis concerns the forms; variation in continuity is a difference of degree and not of form.

2.9 Capital as Movement: The Autonomization of Value (Verselbständigung)

Marx now elevates the analysis to the level of concept. "Capital, as self-valorizing value, does not just comprise class relations, a definite social character that depends on the existence of labour as wage-labour. It is a movement, a circulatory process through different stages, which itself in turn includes three different forms of the circulatory process. Hence it can only be grasped as a movement, and not as a static thing." This is the ontological claim of the chapter. Capital is not a stock of things (machines, stocks, cash) but a process; the things are merely the temporal forms of that process. And the process is the process of value, which is why Marx calls it an abstraction: "Those who consider the autonomization [Verselbständigung] of value as a mere abstraction forget that the movement of industrial capital is this abstraction in action." This phrase — die Verselbständigung des Werts — is one of the strongest formulations in Marx's corpus for what capital is: an abstract social relation that has acquired an objective, quasi-natural existence of its own and governs the activity of those subject to it. This is the same conceptual object as the "automatic subject" (automatisches Subjekt) of Volume 1's account of self-expanding value; here it is presented dynamically, as movement. "Here value passes through different forms, different movements in which it is both preserved and increases, is valorized."

Marx then acknowledges what he has bracketed: "Since we are firstly dealing here simply with the forms of movement, we have not considered the revolutions that the capital value may suffer in its circulatory process." But even so, he states the constraint: "capitalist production can exist and continue to exist only so long as the capital value is valorized, i.e. describes its circuit as value that has become independent, and therefore so long as the revolutions in value are somehow or other mastered and balanced out." The category "value become independent" is not a metaphysical ornament but an operative condition of the system's reproduction.

The subjective mediation is then introduced. "The movements of capital appear as actions of the individual industrial capitalist in so far as he functions as buyer of commodities and labour, seller of commodities and productive capitalist, and thus mediates the circuit by his own activity." The capitalist is thus the personification of the movement — the agent through whom the abstract process is executed, and who appears to be its author. But the appearance is inverted by the reality: "If the social capital value suffers a revolution in value, it can come about that his individual capital succumbs to this and is destroyed, because it cannot meet the conditions of this movement of value. The more acute and frequent these revolutions in value become, the more the movement of the independent value, acting with the force of an elemental natural process, prevails over the foresight and calculation of the individual capitalist, the more the course of normal production is subject to abnormal speculation, and the greater becomes the danger to the existence of the individual capitals."

2.10 Value Revolutions as Confirmation of the Autonomy of Value

The dialectical punchline of the section is a textbook instance of Marx's method of demonstrating essence through appearance: "These periodic revolutions in value thus confirm what they ostensibly refute: the independence which value acquires as capital, and which is maintained and intensified through its movement." The "ostensibly refutes" clause captures the spontaneous verdict: crises in value seem to show that value is fragile, contingent, at the mercy of markets — that there is no stability in value, hence no "autonomy." Marx argues the contrary: it is precisely because value is an independent, self-moving movement that it can crash through the plans of individual capitals "with the force of an elemental natural process." That the system fails in the individual case is proof that it is a system, i.e., that there is a logic not reducible to the intentions of its agents. Value's independence is demonstrated by the damage it does.

Marx then specifies the theoretical content that makes self-comparison possible: "This sequence of metamorphoses of capital in process implies the continuous comparison of the change in value brought about in the circuit with the original value of the capital." Capital, as movement, is a continuous act of bookkeeping — value compares itself not with an external standard but with its own past magnitude. And he warns that this comparison is not the same as the relation between capital and labour: "The independence of value in relation to the value-forming power, labour-power, is introduced by the act M—L (purchase of labour-power), and is realized during the production process as exploitation of labour-power. But this independence does not reappear in the circuit in which money, commodity and elements of production are only alternating forms of the capital value in process, and in which the past magnitude of the value is compared with the present, changed value of the capital." The relation to labour-power — the social ground of value's power — is instituted at the moment of purchase and realized in exploitation; but it does not appear within the circuit as such, where all that is visible is value comparing itself with itself. This is the circuit-level form of fetishism: the relation to labour disappears from the phenomenal form of the movement.

2.11 The Bailey Polemic: Value, Time, and Self-Comparison

It is against this background that Marx mounts an excursus on Samuel Bailey. He quotes Bailey: "'Value,' says Bailey, opposing the autonomization of value which characterizes the capitalist mode of production, and which he treats as the illusion of certain economists, 'value is a relation between contemporary commodities, because such only admit of being exchanged with each other.'" Bailey's thesis — a forerunner of the numerous subjectivist and "pure relationist" theories of value — is that value cannot be a property of a commodity as such, since a commodity can only be exchanged with another at the same time; hence value is the ratio of exchange between commodities that actually confront each other at the same moment on the market.

Marx's reply unfolds in three moves. First, a reduction: Bailey's thesis, applied to intertemporal comparison, means "a comparison between the expenditure of labour required in different epochs for the production of the same kind of commodities," a comparison which is perfectly admissible if the value of money is taken as fixed. Bailey's prohibition on comparing values across time therefore has substantive consequences he has not grasped: it would abolish the very possibility of measuring the productivity of labour historically. Second, a diagnosis: Bailey's error "derives from his general misunderstanding, according to which exchange-value equals value, the form of value is value itself." This is a version of the charge Marx levelled elsewhere at the "vulgar economists": mistaking the form for the substance. If the form of value is value itself, then when commodities are not currently being exchanged they have no value, and no intertemporal comparison is possible. Third, the properly Marxian point: "He does not in the least suspect, therefore, that value functions as capital value or capital only in so far as it remains identical with itself and is compared with itself in the different phases of its circuit, which are in no way 'contemporary,' but rather occur in succession." The categories "capital" and "capital value" are therefore conceptually inseparable from temporal identity: value becomes capital only by maintaining its identity across a temporal succession of forms. Bailey's "contemporaneity" is not merely an error, it is a category mistake about what capital is.

The editorial footnote appended to the quotation supplies a nuanced assessment: Bailey's A Critical Dissertation on the Nature, Measures, and Causes of Value (London, 1825), was written "in reference to the writings of Mr Ricardo and his followers," and although Bailey was "a vulgar economist who held value to be merely relative," he "did expose certain contradictions in the Ricardian theory," and the reader is directed to Theories of Surplus-Value, Part III, Chapter XX. Marx's stance toward Bailey is thus double-edged: Bailey's critique of Ricardo's attempts to find an "absolute" measure of value that is not labour is deflationary but partially correct; yet in rejecting the temporality of value he destroys the very concept of value and hence of capital. This is a case where Marx uses a critic of classical political economy against that critic's own general framework, while subsuming both under his own dialectical account.

2.12 The Pure Circuit Assumption: Value Revolutions inside the Circuit (M…M' vs. P…P and C'…C')

Having established the autonomy of value, Marx turns to its limits under assumptions of constancy. "In order to consider the formula of the circuit in its pure state, it is not sufficient to assume that commodities are sold at their values; this must also take place in circumstances that in other respects, too, remain the same." For the form P…P, in particular, three sources of disturbance must be bracketed: (a) technical revolutions in the production process that may devalue the productive capital of a particular capitalist; (b) any repercussion of a change in the value of the elements of productive capital on the value of an existing commodity stock (which "may rise or fall if there is a stock of this on hand"); (c) — implied by the general argument — movements of prices that merely reflect market fluctuations rather than changes in value. The method here is worth naming: Marx is deploying a ceteris paribus assumption not as a methodological convenience but as a controlled abstraction, in order to isolate a structural difference between the three circuits.

Take C' = 10,000 lb. of yarn, sold at its value of £500. Of this, 8,440 lb. = £422 "replaces the capital value contained in it" — the return to capital. But if the value of cotton, coal, etc. rises, then this £422 "may not be sufficient to replace completely the elements of the productive capital; additional money capital is then necessary, i.e. money capital is tied up." Conversely, if these prices fall, money capital is set free. Hence the general condition: "The process takes place quite normally only if value relations remain constant; in practice it runs its course as long as disturbances in the repetition of the circuit balance each other out; the greater the disturbances, the greater the money capital that the industrial capitalist must possess in order to ride out the period of readjustment." This yields a structural consequence of the first rank, which the chapter flags and then leaves for later development: since "the scale of each individual production process grows with the progress of capitalist production, and with it the minimum size of the capital to be advanced, this circumstance is added to the other circumstances which increasingly turn the function of industrial capitalist into a monopoly of large-scale money capitalists, either individual or associated." The sheer cushion of money capital required to survive value disturbances generates a tendency to concentration. This is a tendency grounded not in competition or technology alone, but in the monetary form of the circuit itself — a distinctive contribution of this chapter.

Marx then draws a formal distinction that the opening of the chapter had apparently denied. "We may remark here, in passing, that when there is a change in the value of the elements of production, a distinction arises between the form M…M' on the one hand, and the forms P…P and C'…C' on the other." This is the re-emergence of a real distinction among the circuits, after the earlier demonstration that their difference is "merely one of form."

In M…M', taken as the formula for newly invested capital — capital first appearing as money capital — a fall in the value of means of production (raw materials, ancillaries, etc.) means that a smaller outlay of money capital is required to open a business of a given size. The reasoning: "given that the level of the productive forces remains the same, the scale of the production process depends only on the volume and scale of the means of production that a given quantity of labour-power can cope with, and not on the value of those means of production, or on that of the labour-power (the latter simply has an effect on the magnitude of the valorization)." The physical-technical scale of production is determined by the material volume of means of production per worker; their value is not what sets the scale. So a fall in their value reduces the money needed to start; a rise increases it. In both cases "it is only the amount of the money capital to be newly invested that is affected": in the first, some money capital becomes superfluous; in the second, more is tied up — "provided that the rate of increase of a new individual industrial capital proceeds as is usual in the given branch of production."

The crucial contrast is that this applies only to new investment. A capital already in operation is not in its first circuit but in reproduction.

2.13 The Three Cases of a Fall in Value and the Opposite Cases of a Rise

Marx now elaborates why P…P and C'…C' "behave in the same way as M…M' only in so far as the movement of P and C' is at the same time accumulation, i.e. in so far as excess m, money, is transformed into money capital." Otherwise, they are affected differently by a change in the value of the elements of productive capital, "since here it is not the original outlay that is directly affected, but rather an industrial capital involved not in its first circuit but in its process of reproduction, i.e. C'…CC(mp), the conversion of commodity capital back into its elements of production, in so far as these consist of commodities."

For a fall in value (or price) of replacement elements, three possibilities:

  1. Continue reproduction on the same scale. A part of the former money capital is set free and stored up (hoarded) — but crucially, this hoarding is not the result of real accumulation or of the "preliminary and accompanying transformation of m (surplus-value) into an accumulation fund." In other words, the money capital set free is a release of existing capital, not the metamorphosis of surplus-value. This is a technical point of great importance for the analysis of money hoards: hoards can arise not only from accumulation of surplus-value but from value-release within reproduction, and the two are functionally different.
  2. Expand reproduction to a larger scale than otherwise, if the technical proportions permit it — thus binding the release to the material conditions of production (the physical means of production must be available in the right proportions).
  3. Build up a larger reserve of raw materials, etc. — a hoarding in kind.

For a rise in the value of replacement elements, the mirror-image alternatives are:

  1. Reproduction no longer on its normal scale — "e.g. working hours may be cut." (Note the assumption that the length of the working day is adjustable, and that its shortening is the capitalist's way of absorbing the rise without additional outlay.)
  2. Additional money capital must be injected to continue at the former scale — "money capital is tied up."
  3. The monetary accumulation fund, where there is one, must serve in whole or part to pursue reproduction on the old scale instead of expanding it.

Here Marx draws a distinction with important consequences for his later theory of credit: "This also involves the tying up of money capital, although here the additional money capital does not come from an external source, from the money market, but rather from the resources of the industrial capitalist himself." The distinction between money capital drawn from the banker and money capital drawn from one's own hoard is the germ of the distinction between credit and self-financing on which much of the analysis of capital circulation depends. The chapter is explicit that this is left undeveloped, and it reiterates: "What interests us here is simply the real distinction which emerges between M…M' and the two other forms of the circuit with respect to changes in value of the elements of productive capital."

2.14 Modifying Circumstances: Stocks of Raw Material and Finished Goods; the Timing of C'—M'

Material stocks modify the general case, and Marx supplies a rich set of illustrations. If the cotton spinner holds a large reserve of raw cotton — "i.e. a large part of his productive capital is in the form of a cotton stock" — then a part of his productive capital is devalued by a fall in cotton prices (and revalued by a rise). If, on the other hand, he has large quantities tied up as commodity capital, e.g. in the form of yarn, a fall in cotton prices devalues a part of his commodity capital, and thus a part of his overall capital in the circuit. The general result: a change in the value of the elements of production is not contained at the point of purchase but propagates through whatever form the capital currently inhabits. This is a subtlety that the abstract formulas conceal, and it shows that the "unity of the three circuits" has an existential edge for the individual capital: every form is a risk, so to speak.

The most precise case is C'—M—C(mp). Here the timing of the value change relative to the sale matters decisively. If C'—M, the realization of commodity capital, has taken place before the change in value of the elements of C, the capital is affected only in the second act of circulation M—C(mp); the sale has already been transacted at the old values, and the capital only confronts the change when it repurchases. But if the value change occurs before the completion of C'—M, then — "with other circumstances remaining the same" — a fall in the price of cotton leads to a corresponding fall in the price of yarn, and a rise in cotton leads to a rise in yarn. (This is the mechanism that makes the value of the finished commodity follow the value of its constituents, where the sale has not yet been concluded at the old values.) Hence: "The effect on the various individual capitals invested in the same branch of production can be very different according to the different circumstances in which they are found." Two capitals of equal size in the same branch, holding different proportions of stocks and different positions in their circuits, are affected quite differently by the same value change. The "same" movement of total value thus has heterogeneous, position-dependent effects on individual capitals — a result that anticipates the differential-advantage analysis of Volume 3.

Marx closes the section by deferring a related phenomenon: "Money capital may also be set free or tied up as the result of differences in the duration of the circulation process, i.e. in the speed of circulation. This however belongs to the discussion of turnover." This is a deliberate forward-reference to Part II of Volume 2 — the doctrine of turnover, in which the release and tying-up of money capital through variations in circulation time becomes a central mechanism. It also marks the boundary of what Chapter 4 can establish.

2.15 The World Market and the Many-Sided Origins of Circulation

Marx now widens the frame from the individual capital to the social forms that enter its circuit. In the act M—C(mp), "in the epoch when the capitalist mode of production is already developed, and hence dominant," a large part of the commodities composing the means of production are themselves the functioning commodity capital of others; from the seller's standpoint the same act is C'—M', the transformation of commodity capital into money capital. "But this does not hold good absolutely. On the contrary." The circuit of industrial capital "cuts across the commodity circulation of the most varied modes of social production, in so far as this commodity circulation simultaneously reflects commodity production." Marx enumerates the possible provenances of the commodities confronting industrial capital: slave-based production; peasant production (Chinese, Indian ryots); community production (Dutch East Indies); state production based on serfdom (as in earlier epochs of Russian history); the products of "half-savage hunting peoples," etc. Whatever their origin, "as commodities and money they confront the money and commodities in which industrial capital presents itself," and enter both the latter's circuit and that of the surplus-value borne by the commodity capital, in so far as the latter is spent as revenue — i.e., both branches of the circulation of commodity capital (capital replacement and revenue expenditure). "The character of the production process from which they derive is immaterial; they function on the market as commodities." The consequence is a characterization of the capital circuit's material openness: "the circulation process of industrial capital is characterized by the many-sided character of its origins, and the existence of the market as a world market."

Then the corresponding point about money: "What holds for foreign commodities holds also for foreign money; as commodity capital functions in relation to money simply as commodity, so this money functions towards commodity capital simply as money; the money functions here as world money." This anticipates the analysis of money in its world-money function (the third of the functions analysed in A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy and Volume 1, Chapter 3), and it grounds the subsequent assumption of metallic money.

Two further points complete this section. First, on the obliteration of provenance: "As soon as the act M—mp is completed, the commodities (mp) cease to be commodities and become one of the modes of existence of industrial capital in its functional form P, productive capital. Their provenance is therefore obliterated; they now exist simply as forms of existence of industrial capital, and are incorporated into it." Yet their replacement requires their reproduction, so "the capitalist mode of production is conditioned by modes of production lying outside its own stage of development." And here Marx states the tendency: "Its tendency, however, is to transform all possible production into commodity production; the main means by which it does this is precisely by drawing this production into its circulation process; and developed commodity production is itself capitalist commodity production. The intervention of industrial capital everywhere promotes this transformation, and with it too the transformation of all immediate producers into wage-labourers." This is one of the chapter's most substantial historical propositions: the unity of the three circuits is not merely an analytic form of the developed capital; it is an engine of subsumption, drawing non-capitalist production into itself and converting direct producers into wage-labourers.

Second, on the social form of the confronting commodities: whatever their origin, the commodities entering the circulation process of industrial capital — "and these include the necessary means of subsistence into which variable capital is transformed after being paid to the workers so that they can reproduce their labour-power" — confront industrial capital "straight away in its form of commodity capital, they themselves having the form of commodity-dealing or merchant's capital; and this by its very nature embraces commodities from all modes of production." The parenthetical remark on variable capital is worth noting: the wage purchased by the worker is itself, in aggregate, a mass of commodities drawn from the circuit of commodity capital — the reproduction of labour-power goes through the market in means of subsistence, which is one reason the circuit of the social capital must include the department producing them (a step developed much later in the reproduction schemas).

From this follows the argument on scale of sale: "As the capitalist mode of production presupposes production on a large scale, so it also necessarily presupposes large-scale sale; sale to the merchant, not to the individual consumer." Where the consumer is himself a productive consumer — an industrial capitalist in another branch — there is direct sale "in the form of orders, etc." Each industrial capitalist is a direct seller in so far as he is his own merchant — which he also is, moreover, when he sells to a merchant.

2.16 Merchant's Capital Presupposed: Sismondi's Naive Account

"Commodity trade is presupposed, as a function of merchant's capital, and this develops ever further with the development of capitalist production." Marx then makes the crucial methodological stipulation: although in illustrating particular aspects we occasionally take the existence of merchant's capital for granted, "in this general analysis we assume direct sale without the intervention of the merchant, since this intervention conceals various moments of the movement." This is a statement about abstraction in political economy, and it is worth unpacking. The merchant's capital is not an inessential excrescence: it is a real functional specialization of total social capital. But precisely because its intervention interposes itself between the act of sale and the realization of the industrial capital, it obscures the temporal structure of the industrial circuit — the gap between the closure of C'—M' and the onward expenditure of the money, the independence of sale from the identity of the buyer, and the phases of the money capital itself. The general analysis must first be carried out without this intervention in order to fix the forms; the later analysis (Part II and Part III, and Volume 3's account of the division of profit between merchant and industrial capital) can then examine the intervention itself.

Marx then quotes Sismondi "who presents the matter rather naively." Sismondi's account: commerce employs a considerable capital which "appears at first glance not to form part of that whose course we have charted." The value of cloth accumulated in the draper's stores seems completely different from the wage paid by the rich man to the poor man. But "this capital has simply replaced that of which we have been speaking." Sismondi's illustration: if 100,000 crowns sufficed for the whole circulation between manufacturer and consumer, this would be shared equally among manufacturer, wholesale merchant and retailer. The manufacturer, receiving only a third of the total, "does the same work as he did when he received the whole lot, because the moment its manufacture is completed, he finds the merchant to buy it much sooner than he would have found the consumer." The wholesaler's capital is replaced by that of the retailer sooner. "The difference between the sums advanced in wages and the purchase price for the final consumer forms the profit on the capitals. It is divided between the manufacturer, the wholesaler and the retailer, after they have divided their functions between them, and the task accomplished is the same, even though it has employed three persons and three fractions of capital in place of one." And finally: "All these" — the merchants — "indirectly participated in production; for as the aim of production is consumption, it cannot be considered accomplished until it has placed the object produced at the disposal of the consumer."

Why "naive"? Several points are approximated correctly: Sismondi sees that the merchant's capital does not add value but replaces a part of the productive capital that would otherwise have to function in the circulation phase; that the merchants "indirectly participated in production"; that the various fractions of capital divide the surplus produced at the point of production (a distribution, not a creation of profit). Against this, the naivety is compounded of three things. First, the account is teleological in a consumptionist key: "the aim of production is consumption" — a proposition Marx has already repudiated in the chapter's own appended note, where he insists the driving motive is "enrichment itself" and not enjoyment. Second, the phrase "the difference between the sums advanced in wages and the purchase price for the final consumer forms the profit on the capitals" is dangerously close to the error of deriving profit from the difference between purchase and sale (the very error Marx's whole theory of surplus-value is designed to refute), and its dependence on "the final consumer" obscures the production relation in which surplus-value is constituted. Third, the "division" of functions among manufacturer, wholesaler and retailer is presented as a costless technical improvement; Marx's own analysis of the "many-sided origins" of circulation implies that the intervention of merchant's capital is not merely an optimization but a form of alienation within the circuit — the specialized existence of a function (realization) separated from the capital that creates the value to be realized. The quote from Sismondi at the close of the section is thus structurally important: it shows what an honest pre-critical account of merchant's capital can see and where it must stop.

2.17 The Money Assumption: Metallic Money, Means of Purchase, Means of Payment

The chapter now states an assumption that governs the remainder of Volume 2 and states the reasons for it: "In considering the general forms of the circuit, and throughout this second volume in general, we take money to be metal money, excluding symbolic money, mere tokens of value which are specific to particular countries, as well as credit money, which we have not yet developed."

Two justifications are offered. First, historical: "this is the course taken by history: credit money played no role, or at least not a significant one, in the early period of capitalist production." Second, theoretical: "the necessity of this course can be proved theoretically, in so far as everything critical that has so far been said about the circulation of credit money by Tooke and others compelled them time and again to look back at how the matter would present itself on the basis of mere metallic circulation." This is a striking methodological remark, and it deserves attention. Thomas Tooke — a leading figure of the Banking School whose History of Prices Marx studied closely and used in Theories of Surplus-Value — was forced, whenever he had to justify claims about the circulation of credit money, to reduce his analysis to the case of metallic circulation. For Marx this is not a weakness to be corrected but a structural feature of the object: credit money is a derivative form whose intelligibility depends on the prior analysis of the metallic form. Hence the pure case must be analysed first, not because credit is unimportant, but because it is derivative.

A caveat is appended: "It should not be forgotten, however, that metallic money can not only function as means of purchase, but also as means of payment. For the sake of simplification, we generally take it, in this second volume, only in the first functional form." This distinction — means of purchase (Kaufmittel) vs. means of payment (Zahlungsmittel), the latter involving the temporal separation of purchase and payment — is fundamental to Volume 1, Chapter 3 and to the theory of credit. But Volume 2's analysis of the circuit abstracts from it in the interest of the pure form.

2.18 General Circulation Laws and Their Limits in the Capital Circuit

The chapter then applies the laws of general commodity circulation to the capital circuit, and immediately limits them. "The circulation process of industrial capital, which forms only one part of its individual circuit, is determined, in so far as it represents only a series of acts within the general commodity circulation, by the general laws that have already been developed (Volume 1, Chapter 3)." Concretely: "The same quantity of money, e.g. £500, puts correspondingly more industrial capitals into circulation (i.e. individual capitals in their form as commodity capitals), the greater the velocity of circulation of the money, thus the faster each individual capital passes through its series of metamorphoses into commodities and money." Conversely, "Capital of the same value accordingly requires less money for its circulation, the more the money functions as means of payment (e.g. the more that it is only balances that have to be settled when a commodity capital is replaced by its means of production), and the shorter the periods of payment (e.g. in the payment of wages)." And on the other side: "given that the velocity of circulation and all other circumstances remain the same, the amount of money needed to circulate as money capital, is determined by the sum of the prices of the commodities (price multiplied by the quantity of commodities), or alternatively, given the quantity and values of the commodities, by the value of the money itself." This is the qualitative statement of the quantity-of-money law: the amount of circulating medium is proportional to the sum of prices (price × quantity) and inversely proportional to the velocity of circulation, and — given the quantity and values of commodities — inversely proportional to the value of the money commodity itself (a higher gold value means less metal needed).

But the limitation is decisive: "the laws of general commodity circulation apply only in so far as the circulation process of capital is a series of simple acts of circulation, and not in so far as the latter form functionally specific sections of the circuits of individual industrial capitals." The point: from the standpoint of the quantity of money in circulation, there is nothing special about capital's circulation — it obeys general laws; but from the standpoint of the function of each act within the circuit (the sale that realises surplus-value, the purchase that reconstitutes the elements of production, etc.), the general laws are silent. The quantity-theoretic laws are adequate for the mass of circulation, not for its articulation.

2.19 The Intertwining of Metamorphoses Is Not an Intertwining of Capitals

"To make this clear, it is best to consider the circulation process in its uninterrupted interconnection, as it appears in the two forms." These two forms — the page-crossing editorial fragment and OCR corruption notwithstanding — are the general forms of commodity circulation, C—M—C and M—C—M, both of which present, as a series of acts of circulation in general, "two opposing series of commodity metamorphoses, each individual metamorphosis including the opposite metamorphosis on the part of the other person's commodity or money that confronts it." The classic formulation of Volume 1 is restated: C—M on the part of the commodity possessor is M—C on the part of the purchaser; the first metamorphosis of the commodity in C—M is the second metamorphosis of the commodity that steps forth as M. Hence the intertwining of metamorphoses established in Volume 1 chapter 3 holds for capital circulation in so far as the capitalist is buyer and seller of commodities, and his capital accordingly functions as money toward others' commodities or as a commodity toward others' money. "This intertwining, however, is not by this token alone an entwining of the metamorphoses of capitals."

Two arguments establish the non-identity.

First: M—C(mp) may depict an intertwining of the metamorphoses of various individual capitals. The cotton-spinner's yarn, for example, is partly replaced by coal. Here "a part of his capital exists in the money form and is converted from this into the commodity form, while the capital of the mine-owner exists in the commodity form and is therefore converted into the money form; the same act of circulation here represents opposite metamorphoses on the part of two industrial capitals (which belong to different branches of production), i.e. an entwining of the series of metamorphoses of these capitals." But this is contingent, not necessary. First, "the mp into which M is converted need not be commodity capital in the categorical sense, i.e. need not be a functional form of industrial capital, produced by a capitalist. It is always M—C on the one hand, and C—M on the other, but not always an entwining of metamorphoses of capital." (This is the point developed in §2.15 above: the means of production may come from peasants, communities, state production, etc.) Second, and still more pointedly: "M—L, the acquisition of labour-power, is never an entwining of capital metamorphoses, for, while labour-power is certainly a commodity for the worker, it becomes capital only when it is sold to the capitalist." The purchase of labour-power is an act of circulation that is not the converse of any act of capital circulation; it is the conversion of an ordinary commodity — labour-power — into a constituent of capital, an act that is simultaneously the institution of the capital relation itself. Third: "In the process C'—M', on the other hand, M' does not need to be converted commodity capital; it can be the expression in money of the commodity labour-power (i.e. wages), or of a product produced by an independent worker, a slave, a serf or a community." The money the industrial capitalist receives need not come from another industrial capitalist realising surplus-value; it may be revenue, wages, the proceeds of non-capitalist production.

Second: "it is by no means always the case that the functionally determined role played by every metamorphosis that takes place within the circulation process of an individual capital represents the corresponding opposite metamorphosis in the circuit of the other capital, particularly if we assume that the whole of production for the world market is pursued on a capitalist basis." Two illustrations are given. In the circuit P…P, "the M' that turns C' into cash may be on the side of the buyer simply the monetary expression of his surplus-value (if the commodity is an article of consumption)" — i.e., the buyer may be spending revenue, so the sale of C' does not correspond to any reconstitution of his capital. Alternatively, in M'—C'…(mp), "i.e. where accumulated capital is involved," the money may be for the buyer of mp "simply a replacement for his capital advance, or it may not re-enter his capital circulation at all, particularly if this branches off into expenditure of revenue."

The conclusion is the chapter's methodological declaration: "The way in which the various components of the total social capital, of which the individual capitals are only independently functioning components, alternately replace one another in the circulation process — both with respect to capital and to surplus-value — is thus not the result of the simple intertwining of the metamorphoses that occurs in commodity circulation, and which the acts of capital circulation have in common with all other processes of commodity circulation, but rather requires a different mode of investigation." And a sharp rebuke to a prevailing style of argument: "Up till now, mere phrases have been taken as sufficient in this respect, although, when these are analysed more closely, they contain nothing more than indefinite notions, simply borrowed from the intertwining of metamorphoses that is common to all commodity circulation." This is a self-limiting declaration: the tools forged in Chapters 1–4, centred on the individual capital and its forms, are insufficient for the analysis of total social capital; a different method — the schemas of reproduction, developed in Part III — is required. But — and this is the deeper point — the failure of the simple intertwining argument is itself a result of the chapter's own analysis: it is because the components of the total social capital "alternately replace one another … both with respect to capital and to surplus-value" that a two-department analysis becomes necessary.

2.20 The "Different Mode of Investigation": Commodity Market as Permanent Source and Sink

Before closing, Marx sets out the peculiarity that forces the new method. "One of the most obvious peculiarities of the circuit of industrial capital, and thus of capitalist production, is the situation that on the one hand the elements from which productive capital is formed stem from the commodity market, and must be continually renewed from it, bought as commodities; and on the other hand the product of the labour process emerges from it as a commodity, and must constantly be sold anew as a commodity." The circuit is thus doubly dependent on the market: as a sink for its output and as a source for its inputs. The illustration is a stark contrast between two agricultural modes: "A modern farmer in the lowlands of Scotland might for example be contrasted with an old-fashioned small peasant on the Continent. The former sells his entire product and thus has to replace all its elements, even the seed-corn, on the market, while the latter consumes the greater part of his product directly, buys and sells as little as possible, and as far as possible produces his tools, clothing, etc. himself." The contrast is not a matter of scale but of degree of market dependence: the modern farmer buys even his seed-corn, i.e., reproduces himself entirely through the market; the small peasant holds production and consumption within the same unit.

2.21 Natural Economy, Money Economy, Credit Economy — Marx's Three Objections

It is at this point that Marx addresses the classical trichotomy: "Natural economy, money economy and credit economy have for this reason been counterposed as the three characteristic economic forms of motion of social production." (This is the tripartite scheme familiar from Bruno Hildebrand and from the German historical school, and central to the popular economic histories of the era.) Marx's critique proceeds in three numbered steps.

First, the three forms "do not represent phases of development of the same status." The so-called credit economy is not a third stage alongside natural and money economy; "it is itself only a form of the money economy, in so far as both terms express functions or modes of commerce [Verkehr] between the producers themselves. In developed capitalist production, the money economy simply appears as the basis of the credit economy." Therefore money economy and credit economy correspond only to different stages of development of capitalist production; they are in no way independent forms of commerce opposed to natural economy. And the converse consequence: "It would be just as valid to counterpose the very varied forms of natural economy as equal in status to the other two." The trichotomy is thus logically arbitrary: if the criterion is "mode of commerce," there are many more than three forms.

Second, a question of what is being classified: "what is emphasized in the categories money economy and credit economy, and stressed as the distinctive feature, is actually not the economy proper, i.e. the production process itself, but rather the mode of commerce between the various agents of production or producers that corresponds to the economy." If the defining criterion is the mode of commerce, then consistency demands applying it to the first term too: "Instead of natural economy, we would then have barter economy. A completely enclosed natural economy, such as the Inca state of Peru, would fall into none of these categories." The Inca state — an example Marx elsewhere uses for a fully communal, self-sufficient production — would be excluded entirely from a scheme that classified by mode of commerce, since it had no commerce to speak of with the outside. The scheme thus fails to accommodate the very type it intends to name.

Third, and decisively for Marx's own categories: "money economy is common to all commodity production, and the product appears as a commodity in the most diverse organisms of social production." Commodity production and money economy are not specific to capitalism; they occur in a variety of social forms. Therefore, "it would simply be the scale on which the product was produced as an article of trade, as a commodity, and thus also the extent to which its own formative elements must again enter the economy from which it derives as articles of trade, as commodities, which would characterize capitalist production." The distinguishing feature of capitalism is not the existence of money but the universality of commodity production — the extent to which the product, and even the elements of production, must be bought and sold.

From this Marx draws the definitional conclusion: "In point of fact, capitalist production is commodity production as the general form of production, but it is only so, and becomes ever more so in its development, because labour itself here appears as a commodity, because the worker sells labour, i.e. the function of his labour-power, and moreover, as we have assumed, at a value determined by the costs of its reproduction." The universality of commodity production rests on the commodification of labour-power. And this commodification has a determinate extension: "The producer becomes an industrial capitalist to the same extent that labour becomes wage-labour; hence capitalist production (and thus also commodity production) appears in its full extent only when the direct agricultural producer is also a wage-labourer." This is why the earlier reference to the Scottish lowland farmer matters: it is not merely an example of market dependence but the very form of the completion of the capitalist mode of production in agriculture — the transformation of the direct agricultural producer into a wage-labourer.

2.22 The Verkehr Footnote and the Priority of Production over Commerce

The term Verkehr in the passage just discussed carries an editorial footnote of some importance. The note explains that Verkehr plays an important role in The German Ideology, where it is conventionally translated as "intercourse," but that the concept was later rejected by Marx and Engels in favour of "relations of production," as Göran Therborn explains in Science, Class and Society. The present passage, written in 1877, "seems to be the only time that Verkehr reappears in a conceptual sense in any of the volumes of Capital." Its meaning here, however, "has clearly little to do with the early concept of The German Ideology. It rather covers what Marx and Engels more usually referred to as 'exchange' (Austausch), in the sense of 'mode of production and exchange'." The note then proposes a reason for the lexical choice: Marx needs to use Tausch (exchange or barter) to refer to a particular form of "commerce" between producers — the non-monetary exchange corresponding to a "natural economy" — and, since Tausch and Austausch are nearly interchangeable in German usage, he selects the looser term Verkehr "to emphasize the general concept of which barter and monetary exchange (with the latter's sub-type credit) are the variants." The footnote thus documents a conceptual shift: from the early "intercourse" (a broad category of social interaction) to the mature "relations of production," with the residual use of Verkehr here serving a precise taxonomic function — to name the genus of which barter, money exchange and credit are species.

The conceptual payoff is completed in the immediately following paragraph. "In the relation between capitalist and wage-labourer, the money relation, the relation of buyer and seller, becomes a relation inherent in production itself. But this relation rests fundamentally on the social character of production, not on the mode of commerce; the latter rather derives from the former. It is typical of the bourgeois horizon, moreover, where business deals fill the whole of people's minds, to see the foundation of the mode of production in the mode of commerce corresponding to it, rather than the other way round." This is the chapter's sharpest formulation of the materialist inversion: the trichotomy of natural/money/credit economy is not merely imprecise, it is ideological — it mistakes the form of circulation for the ground of production. And the sentence about business deals filling people's minds connects directly to the "naivety" of Sismondi's account and to the pervasive tendency of apologetic economics to treat the market as the substance of economic life rather than as its phenomenal form.

2.23 The Appended Note of 1877–78: The Capitalist's Supply and Demand

At this point, the editor's note marks a textual break: "Up to here, Manuscript V. The remainder of this chapter consists of a note found among extracts from books in a notebook of 1877 or 1878." The material that follows belongs to a much later period of Marx's work — the period of the critical engagement with Adolph Wagner's Lehrbuch, and of extensive reading in French and Russian economic literature — and it addresses a question distinct from, though connected with, the preceding analysis: the aggregate relation between the demand and supply of the capitalist class considered as such.

The note opens with a formulation of striking economy: "The capitalist casts less value into circulation in the form of money than he draws out of it, because he casts in more value in the form of commodities than he has extracted in the form of commodities." This is the value-theoretic statement of what the circuit does: the capitalist advances money (less in value) and withdraws money (more in value), and the difference corresponds to the surplus-value he has added to the commodities in production (via labour-power), which he now sells. "In so far as he functions merely as the personification of capital, as industrial capitalist, his supply of commodity-value is always greater than his demand for it."

The critical function of this proposition is to be the opposite of a doctrine of balanced exchange. If the capitalist's supply and demand matched, "this would be equivalent to the non-valorization of his capital; it would not have functioned as productive capital; productive capital would have been transformed into commodity capital that had not been impregnated with surplus-value; it would not have extracted from labour-power during the production process any surplus-value in the commodity form, and thus not functioned as capital at all." The asymmetry of supply and demand is thus constitutive of capital, not a deviation from its norm. The circuit's very form requires it: the capitalist must "sell dearer than he has bought," but he can do so only because the production process transforms cheaper commodities (means of production and labour-power) into more valuable commodities. The solution to the apparent circulationist paradox — how can one sell dearer than one bought, if values are exchanged at their values? — is that the "dearness" is not a price surcharge but a value difference produced in the labour process: "He sells dearer, not because he sells above the value of his commodities, but because he sells commodities of a value greater than the sum of values of the ingredients required to produce them."

The connecting proposition follows immediately: "The greater the difference between the capitalist's supply and his demand, i.e. the greater the additional commodity value that he supplies over the commodity value that he demands, the greater the rate at which he valorizes his capital. His goal is not simply to cover his demand with his supply, but to have the greatest possible excess of supply over demand." So the degree of valorization is measured by the gap. And the highest-level statement: "What is true for the individual capitalist, is true also for the capitalist class."

2.24 The Composition of Demand and the V-Shaped Asymmetry

Marx now decomposes the capitalist's demand. "In so far as the capitalist simply personifies industrial capital, his own demand consists simply in the demand for means of production and labour-power. His demand for mp is smaller in value terms than the capital he has advanced; he buys means of production to a smaller value than the value of his capital, and hence to a still smaller value than that of the commodity capital that he supplies." (He demands, by value, only c, while his supply contains c + v + s.) "As far as his demand for labour-power is concerned, it is determined in its value by the ratio between his variable capital and his total capital, i.e. v:C. In capitalist production, therefore, this demand grows at a smaller rate than his demand for means of production. The capitalist buys more of mp than of L, and to a steadily increasing extent." This is a structural result of the circuit: because mp figures as the substance of capital's productive form and v is determined by the technically and historically conditioned reproduction cost of labour-power, the demand for mp tends to outstrip the demand for labour-power. (Marx does not here use the terminology of the organic composition of capital, but the argument is the basis for it: the ratio v:C in value terms governs the wage demand.)

Next, the indirect demand. "In so far as the worker converts his wages almost wholly into means of subsistence, and by far the greater part into necessities, the capitalist's demand for labour-power is indirectly also a demand for the means of consumption that enter into the consumption of the working class. But this demand equals v, and not an atom more (if the worker saves something out of his wages — we necessarily leave the matter of credit out of consideration here — this means that he transforms a part of his wage into a hoard and to this extent does not appear as a customer)." Two points deserve emphasis. First, the working class's total demand for means of subsistence, mediated by wage expenditure, is equal to v in value terms — a theorem of the circuit, derived from the demand being, by construction, the value of labour-power. Second, the parenthetical on saving contains a small but important methodological point: saving by the worker is hoarding, a withdrawal of demand from the market, not an increase in demand; and the analysis abstracts from credit, in which the hoard would be placed at the disposal of others.

The maximum limit is then stated: "The maximum limit of the capitalist's demand is C = c+v, but his supply is C+V+J" — i.e., c + v + s. The illustrative composition is 80c + 20v + 20s, so his demand is 80c + 20v, "a value one fifth smaller than his supply." (A note on the figures: with c = 80, v = 20 and s = 20, the demand is 100 and the supply 120, so the deficit is 20 — i.e., exactly s. The text also renders this as "one fifth" and later, in the turnover illustration, as "four fifths" and "one fifth smaller"; arithmetically, 100/120 = 5/6, so the text's fractional expressions are loosely stated. The substantive proposition is exact and independent of the arithmetic illustration: the capitalist's demand as capitalist falls short of his supply by precisely the amount of surplus-value in value terms. The comparison is one of value magnitudes, not of physical quantities.) Marx then states the general form: "The greater the percentage of s produced (the rate of profit), the smaller his demand in relation to his supply."

The inter-capitalist dimension is then introduced. "Although, as production advances, the capitalist's demand for labour-power, and hence indirectly for necessary means of subsistence, becomes progressively smaller than his demand for means of production, it should not be forgotten that his demand for mp is always smaller than his capital, considering this day by day. His demand for means of production must thus always be smaller in value than the commodity product of the capitalist who works with the same capital and under otherwise similar conditions, and supplies him with these means of production." The point is that within the interlocking of capitals, the demand of each capitalist for means of production is insufficient, in value terms, to purchase the product of the capitalists who supply them. "That many capitalists are involved here, and not just one, in no way affects the matter." Then the numerical illustration: capital = £1,000, constant part = £800, so his demand on all these capitalists is £800. "Together they supply for each £1,000 … means of production to a value of £1,200; thus his demand only covers two thirds of their supply, while his own total demand is only four fifths of his own supply, considered in value terms." The two-thirds is exact on the numbers (800/1,200); the "four fifths" is the loose rendering of a 1,000/1,200 relation. The argument to be noted: even before considering turnover or accumulation, the value relations imply a systematic gap between the demand of the productive consumers and the supply they must absorb.

2.25 Turnover, Amortization, Accumulation — and the Impossibility of Full Consumption of Surplus-Value

Marx then checks whether turnover alters the result. "Assume that his total capital is £5,000, of which £4,000 is fixed and £1,000 circulating; this 1,000 = 800c + 200v, according to the above assumption. His circulating capital must turn over five times in the year in order for his total capital to turn over once. His commodity product is then £6,000, i.e. £1,000 greater than the capital he advanced, which once again gives the same ratio of surplus-value as above: 5,000 C : 1,000 S = 100 (c+v) : 20 s. Thus this turnover in no way alters the ratio of his total demand to his total supply, the former remaining one fifth smaller than the latter." The point: increasing the velocity of the circulating portion multiplies the annual product and the annual surplus-value proportionally, so the ratio of demand (c + v, annualised) to supply (c + v + s, annualised) is invariant. The velocity of circulation does not solve the deficit.

The treatment of fixed capital introduces a further nuance. "Let us assume that his fixed capital has to be renewed in ten years. Each year, then, he amortizes 1/10 = £400. [After the first year] he has a value of £3,600 in fixed capital and £400 in money." The amortisation — the annual depreciation fund — is money that is withdrawn from the circuit but not yet re-invested in fixed capital; it accumulates as a hoard. On repairs: "In so far as repairs are necessary, and these do not exceed the average amount, they are simply capital that is invested at a later date. We can consider the matter as if he had allowed for all the repair costs when he assessed the value of his invested capital, in so far as this enters into the annual commodity product, so that these are included in the one tenth amortization. (If his repair needs are lower than average, this is simply a bonus for him, just as it is to his disadvantage if they are higher.)" Then the conclusion: "although (on the assumption that his total capital turns over once in the year) his annual demand remains £5,000, the same as the original capital value he advanced, it increases with respect to the circulating part of the capital, while it steadily declines with respect to the fixed part." This is a subtle point with significance for the later analysis of reproduction: the annual composition of demand shifts over the life of the fixed capital, even though the aggregate remains equal to the advanced capital.

Finally, the decisive argument against the "full-consumption" hypothesis. "We now come to reproduction. Assume that the capitalist consumes the entire surplus-value m and reconverts only the original capital sum C into productive capital. The capitalist's demand is now equal in value to his supply." But — Marx insists — this only holds on a comptometric reading. "But this is not so in respect of the movement of his capital; as capitalist he exerts a demand only on the basis of four fifths of his supply (in value terms). The remaining fifth he consumes as non-capitalist, not in his function as capitalist, but for his private requirements or pleasures." The "account, reckoned in percentages," is given:

Demand as capitalist: 100 — Supply: 120 Demand as man of the world: 20 — Supply: – Total demand: 120 — Supply: 120

The point of the ledger is that the balance is achieved only by dual-counting the capitalist: 100 as capitalist + 20 as consumer = 120 = supply. In so far as he is a capitalist, he demands only c + v; the s can only be demanded by a different persona — the private consumer, or the accumulator. And here Marx delivers the decisive judgment: "This assumption is equivalent to assuming the non-existence of capitalist production and therefore the non-existence of the industrial capitalist himself. For capitalism is already essentially abolished once we assume that it is enjoyment that is the driving motive and not enrichment itself."

Two arguments are given for why the full-consumption assumption is not merely counterfactual but impossible. First, it is technically impossible: "The capitalist must not only form a reserve capital to guard against price fluctuations, and in order to be able to await the most favourable conjunctures for buying and selling; he must accumulate capital, in order to extend production and incorporate technical advances into his productive organism." Two distinct needs: a reserve against uncertainty (the money-capital cushion we encountered in §2.12), and accumulation for the expansion of production and the incorporation of technical change. Second, accumulation goes through a hoard phase: "In order to accumulate capital, he must first withdraw from circulation a part of the surplus-value that he obtained from it, and let it grow in the form of a hoard until it has assumed the requisite dimensions for an extension of his old business or the opening of a new line. As long as the hoarding continues, the capitalist's demand is not increased; the money is immobilized and does not withdraw from the commodity market an equivalent in commodities for the money equivalent that it has withdrawn for commodities supplied." This is the hoarding phase of accumulation, and it introduces a gap between the sale of the capitalist's commodity capital and the corresponding demand: the money withdrawn from the commodity market is not immediately put back. The circuit therefore has a structural tendency to generate a lag between supply and demand — a lag that, if not mediated by credit, would be an idleness of value. And on this point the note closes with the characteristic gesture to the next level of analysis: "We have ignored credit here, and it pertains to credit if the capitalist deposits the money that he accumulates in a bank, for example, on current account bearing interest." The credit system is precisely the mechanism through which hoarded, immobilized money is returned to circulation — a point that Volume 3 will develop.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

3.1 The Fundamental Principles Revealed

First, capital is a movement, and its analysis must be a theory of forms, not a theory of stocks. Chapter 4 establishes with full rigour the proposition that the objects of capitalist wealth — money, means of production, commodities — are not capital as such but forms of existence of a process. The circuit cannot be understood as a sequence of material stocks; it can only be understood as the self-moving of value through its forms. This is the methodological Aufhebung of the static balance-sheet view of the economy. It has immediate implications: any analysis that treats capital as a magnitude located "at" a particular moment — as if the machine and the money and the finished good were simply different assets on a balance sheet — will miss the structural features on which the system's reproduction depends. The "coexistence" of the forms is not a portfolio but a phase-distribution.

Second, the dialectic of succession and coexistence is the formal key to the continuity of the whole. The chapter's central discovery is that continuity is not a property of any part, but a relation among parts: simultaneity is achieved through division. This is a general principle with applications far beyond its original domain — in systems theory, in the analysis of production processes, and in biology (the same logic of overlapping partial processes underlies any reproduction that is continuous while its individual units are finite). And the dialectical structure matters: coexistence is both presupposition and result. It is the precipitates of succession, yet it is what makes the succession possible. The circuit is a self-conditioning totality.

Third, the interruption of one circuit is a disturbance of all. The theory of the "blockage" — of C'—M' uncompleted causing the accumulation of unsold stock and the stalling of production — provides a form-determined mechanism of economic disruption: a mechanism of crisis latent in the structure of the circuit itself, independent of any contradiction between the productive forces and the relations of production, or of any tendency of the rate of profit to fall. The chapter does not elaborate a general theory of crisis; but it provides the formal locus of disruption, which subsequent analysis can fill with content.

Fourth, the autonomization of value is the real existence of an abstraction. The category of Verselbständigung — value become independent, value as a self-moving subject — is the chapter's most philosophically consequential claim. Capital is an abstraction in action: an abstract social relation that has acquired objective, quasi-natural existence and governs the activity that constitutes it. Value revolutions, in this light, are not contraventions of capital's logic but revelations of it: the "elemental natural process" of value's motion dominates the foresight and calculation of individual capitalists, and the same is true of the system as a whole.

Fifth, the temporality of value is the ground of value's identity. The Bailey polemic is not a marginal note: it establishes that value, to function as capital, must remain identical with itself across a temporal succession of forms, i.e., it must be able to be compared with itself as a past magnitude. Bailey's "contemporaneity" — value as a relation between commodities that happen to coexist on the market — abolishes this temporality, and thereby abolishes capital. This is one of the strongest arguments in the Marxian corpus against subjectivist and relationist theories of value, and it grounds a general methodological principle: temporality is not an accident of economic magnitude but is constitutive of it.

Sixth, the different fractions of capital — the parts of the circuit — are not reducible to one another. The real difference between M…M' and the two other circuits, which emerges in the treatment of value revolutions, shows that the chapter's opening reduction of the difference to "merely one of form" is a moment in a dialectical argument, not its conclusion. The circuits are unified, but they are not identical: the circuit that starts from money, the circuit that starts from production and the circuit that starts from the already-valorized commodity suffer value revolutions differently, because the value changes hit the capital at different points of its circuit. This connects to the concept of "release and tying-up" of money capital, which is elaborated in Part II.

Seventh, the supply–demand asymmetry of the capitalist class is structural. The appended note establishes that a capitalist's demand as capitalist is necessarily smaller than his supply, and that the deficit is exactly surplus-value. This is not a pathological feature but a definitional one: a capitalist whose supply and demand matched would not be a capitalist at all. The consequence is that the sum of all capitalists' demand cannot, in value terms, absorb the sum of all their supply as capital: the surplus must be demanded by someone else, or accumulated (which involves a hoard-lag), or disposed of through credit (which is exactly what the system invents). This is the formal ground of the realization problem.

3.2 Broader Theoretical Implications

In the architecture of Volume 2 as a whole, Chapter 4 is pivotal in three ways. It closes Part I by demonstrating the unity that the preceding chapter-by-chapter analysis presupposed; it opens Part II by exposing the money-capital release/injection phenomenon, the distinction between internally and externally sourced money capital, and the hoarding phase of accumulation, all of which the theory of turnover will develop; and it announces Part III by declaring that the intertwining of metamorphoses is inadequate to the analysis of total social reproduction and that "a different mode of investigation" is required.

More broadly, the chapter suggests a general theory of continuously reproduced systems: that continuity of the whole is achieved through the differentiated distribution of parts across phases; that this distribution has material prerequisites (a definite minimum size of capital, definite numerical proportions, the technical character of the branch of production); and that the system's exposure to disturbance is a function of the rigidity of these proportions. The theory of "coexistence" implies that any economy whose value-form requires the synchronisation of physically independent processes — production and circulation — is vulnerable to breakdown through asynchrony: an interruption of one link disrupts the whole. This is a foundation for the analysis of crisis that is neither "underconsumptionist" nor "profit-squeeze" but formal: crisis is latent in the very necessity of sale as a precondition of production.

Finally, the chapter bears on the theory of the historical expansion of capital. The analysis of the "many-sided origins" of circulation establishes the world market as a structural feature of the circuit, and the transformation of all possible production into commodity production — and thereby of all immediate producers into wage-labourers — as the tendency of industrial capital's expansion. The "natural economy, money economy, credit economy" critique reinforces this: the category of "natural economy" is not a stage that capitalism supersedes, but a residual that must be drawn in, transformed, and (in Marx's terms) subsumed. The trichotomy of modes of commerce is the ideological form of the real historical process by which capital subsumes pre-capitalist production; and the position of the direct agricultural producer — the passage from small peasant to wage-labourer — is not a marginal remark but the completion of the mode of production. The chapter ends, then, where it began: with the circuit, but the circuit as a self-expanding, world-embracing, form-determining process — the abstraction in action.
