Volume 2 • Chapter 1: The Circuit of Money Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem: The Forms Capital Assumes as It Moves

Chapter 1 of Volume 2 of Capital — titled "The Circuit of Money Capital" — is not a re-telling of Volume 1 but a deliberate shift of analytical register. In Volume 1, Marx's object was the production process: how surplus-value is created in the factory, how the exploitation of labour-power works, how the value of commodities decomposes into constant capital, variable capital, and surplus-value. The forms that capital "clothes itself in" as it moves — money, commodities, productive assets — appeared there only instrumentally, "in so far as this was necessary for the understanding of the second stage, the capitalist production process." The forms themselves "remained uninvestigated."

Volume 2, and this chapter specifically, opens a new problem: the metamorphoses of capital. The chapter's stated, immediate object is those forms "in their pure state." This is a study of capital as a movement — as a process that is perpetually discarding one form and assuming another, that must pass through circulation, then production, then circulation again, and must return to its starting point to repeat the cycle. Marx's central thesis here is deceptively simple but extraordinarily consequential: capital is not a thing but a process, and it is only intelligible as a continuously self-reproducing circuit that assumes, discards, and re-assumes three distinct functional forms — money capital, productive capital, and commodity capital.

The chapter's chosen entry point is the circuit of money capital, symbolised as:

$$M - C \dots P \dots C' - M'$$

This formula is the analytic spine of the entire chapter. Every section is an excavation of what makes this sequence a circuit rather than a mere set of transactions, and of what each symbol presupposes.

The Intellectual Objective: Abstracting to the Pure Forms of Movement

Marx is explicit about his method at the outset. To "grasp these forms in their pure state," he must "first of all abstract from all aspects that have nothing to do with the change and constitution of the forms as such." Concretely, he brackets two things: (a) that commodities are sold at their values, and (b) that the circumstances of sale do not change, and (c) any changes of value occurring during the cyclical process. This is a theoretical purge — a controlled simplification designed to isolate the anatomy of the circuit. The task is thus formal and dialectical rather than empirical or historical.

The intellectual objective is threefold: 1. To establish the circuit as the fundamental unity of circulation and production. Capital's life is not reducible to either sphere alone. Volume 1 showed that surplus-value arises in production. Volume 2's opening chapter shows that this surplus-value cannot be realised without circulation, and that the whole process must return to its starting point for capital to function as capital at all. 2. To identify the specific functional forms of industrial capital. Money capital, productive capital, and commodity capital are not "independent varieties of capital, whose functions constitute the content of branches of business that are independent and separate from one another." They are particular functional forms of one thing — industrial capital — which takes on all three in turn. This is a critical polemic against the "scholastic economics" that would treat these as separate capital-types. 3. To expose the ideological illusions generated by the money form. Because the circuit begins and ends with money, it produces the "crude, non-conceptual" appearance of money breeding money (M...M'), which is the root of the "Monetary System" and the "Mercantile System." The chapter is thus simultaneously an anatomy of capital and a critique of the ideological forms thrown up by capital's most superficial appearance.

Where the Chapter Sits in the Theoretical Framework

This is the opening of Volume 2, "The Process of Circulation of Capital," and it functions as the bridge between Volume 1 (production) and Volume 3 (the process of capitalist production as a whole). The chapter introduces the most fundamental abstraction of Volume 2: the circuit — the cyclical self-movement of capital through its forms. Two sibling circuits will be examined later: the circuit of productive capital (P...P) and the circuit of commodity capital (C'...C'). This chapter establishes the money-capital circuit while constantly foreshadowing the others, noting that "before the second circuit is complete, the first circuit C'...C' has been described" and that M...M' overlaps and interlocks with P...P.

The chapter also introduces and deploys a series of key Volume 2 categories: money capital, productive capital, commodity capital, industrial capital, and the distinction between the formal metamorphoses of the circulation sphere and the real metamorphosis of the production process. It presupposes and builds upon Volume 1's results — the theory of surplus-value, the wage form, the class relation, the historical separation of producers from means of production — without re-deriving them. The result is a work of exceptionally dense theoretical architecture: a piece of dialectical analysis in which every symbol of a formula becomes the occasion for deep excavation of social and economic relations.

The Argumentative Strategy

Marx proceeds in four great movements, each corresponding to a numbered section:

  1. The first stage, M–C — the transformation of money capital into productive capital. Here Marx shows that the act of purchase is simultaneously an act of capital because of its material content: the money divides into L (labour-power) and mp (means of production), in a quantitative ratio conditioned from the start by the surplus labour the workers must perform. This section contains some of the chapter's most important theoretical work: the analysis of M–L as the "characteristic moment," the diagnosis of the two "interconnected errors" in the conception of money capital, the demonstration that the class relation is presupposed in the money relation, and the crucial concept of distribution of the elements of production themselves.

  2. The second stage, P — the function of productive capital. This is the interruption, the "real metamorphosis." Here Marx shows how the wage-labourer's hand-to-mouth existence necessitates a permanent supply of money capital, how the division of labour deepens with commodity production, how capitalist commodity production dissolves earlier forms of production, and how the specific way workers and means of production are connected distinguishes economic epochs. He introduces the constant/variable capital distinction as it appears in the circuit and derives the value formula P + s.

  3. The third stage, C'–M' — the transformation of commodity capital into money capital. Here Marx works out, with almost obsessive numerical precision, the structure of the value of a commodity product (C = C + c, £422 + £78 = £500 in the yarn example), the "value ratio" that makes C into C', the double role of money as the returning form of the capital value and the first transformative form of the surplus-value, and the "crude, non-conceptual" character of M' — the homogeneity of a money sum in which every trace of the process has been effaced. This section culminates in the demonstration that the form M...M' (M + m) is "irrational" because "part of a sum of money appears as the mother of another part of the same sum of money," and that this irrationality only disappears in gold production, where M' is intrinsically a commodity product.

  4. The circuit as a whole — the synthesis, in which Marx names industrial capital as the only mode of existence of capital in which both the appropriation and the creation of surplus-value are functions of capital. Here he lays out the four defining features of the M...M' circuit, the "deceptive" and "illusory character" it necessarily carries, and the two ideological systems (Monetary, Mercantile) that arise from taking that appearance as the essence. The chapter ends by resolving the apparent priority of M–C into the deeper truth: the circuit of money capital is the "general form" of the circuit of industrial capital, precisely because it presupposes capitalist production — hence P...P, the circuit of productive capital, is logically prior even though M...M' appears to come first.

The Rhetorical and Analytical Style

Readers should be prepared for a chapter that is at once a work of high formalism and a work of sharp polemic. Marx moves fluidly between abstract formula and concrete numerical example (the spinning mill, the yarn), between dialectical elucidation and historical illustration (Russian landowners after emancipation, capitalist world trade with China, India, the Arabs), and between economic analysis and philosophical critique (the "irrationality" of the wage form, the contrast between the "rational" form of C' and the "effaced" form of M'). The prose is compressed; subordinate arguments are often buried in footnotes or parentheticals; and Engels's editorial interventions (notably the parenthetical about "fits of giddiness" inserted in the 1893 second edition, and the footnote about begrifflos versus begrifflich) are themselves part of the chapter's received texture. The reader must therefore be alive to the palimpsest quality of the text — it is drawn from multiple manuscripts (Manuscripts II, VII, VI, V) composed at different times, which explains some shifts of emphasis.

The overarching argument that emerges is this: the circuit of money capital is the most "one-sided, hence most striking and characteristic" form of appearance of the circuit of industrial capital, because its aim and driving motive — "the valorization of value, money-making and accumulation" — "leaps to the eye." But this very vividness is a trap: "buying in order to sell dearer" is the appearance, while the substance is the real metamorphosis of production, the creation of surplus-value, and the historically specific class relation that makes it possible. The chapter's real thesis is thus simultaneously an insight and a warning: the form in which capital presents itself most directly to consciousness is the form in which its inner nature is most obscured.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Framework: Three Stages and the Formula of the Circuit

Marx opens by restating, from the vantage of Volume 1, the three stages of the circuit of capital. The first stage is the appearance of the capitalist on the commodity and labour markets "as a buyer"; his money transforms into commodities — the act of circulation M–C. The second stage is "productive consumption by the capitalist of the commodities purchased": he functions as "capitalist producer of commodities," his capital "passes through the production process," and the result is "commodities of greater value than their elements of production." The third stage is his return to the market "as a seller": his commodities transform into money — the act of circulation C–M.

Out of these three stages, Marx writes the formula of the circuit of money capital:

$$M - C \dots P \dots C' - M'$$

Three notational conventions deserve careful attention, because subsequent arguments depend on them. The dots (…) indicate that "the circulation process is interrupted" — that between buying inputs and selling outputs there lies a sphere not governed by the laws of circulation but by those of production. The primes (' ) denote "an increase in C and M as the result of surplus-value" — that C', as the value-expression of the product, is quantitatively greater than the C that entered, and that M', as the money eventually withdrawn, is greater than the M originally advanced. The notation thus encodes, in a single line, the entire logic of capital: it must circulate, must exit circulation into production, must return to circulation, and must return augmented.

Marx immediately issues a methodological manifesto: in Volume 1, the first and third stages were discussed "only in so far as this was necessary for the understanding of the second stage." The forms — the successive modes of existence of capital — "remained uninvestigated." These forms will "now be the immediate object of our inquiry." The analytic abstraction is then specified: commodities are sold at their values, circumstances do not change, and any changes of value occurring "in the course of the cyclical process" are ignored. This is a controlled theoretical environment in which the form-dynamics of the circuit can be observed without the noise of value-change. Note the footnote: "This introductory section is taken from Manuscript II," signalling the composite nature of the text.

Section I: The First Stage, M–C

The Twofold Nature of M–C: Form versus Material Content

The first substantive move is a distinction that governs the entire analysis of M–C. Taken abstractly, M–C "represents the conversion of a sum of money into a sum of commodities; the buyer transforms his money into commodities, the sellers their commodities into money." This is the ordinary, symmetrical act of commodity circulation — nothing distinguishes it formally from any other purchase. What makes this particular act "a part of the whole process with a well-defined function in the independent circuit of an individual capital" is "not primarily the form of the act, but rather its material content" — the "specific use character of the commodities that change place with money."

This is the first deep lesson of the chapter: the capitalist character of the act M–C is not visible in its form as a circulation act, but only in what is bought. Those commodities are, on one hand, means of production (mp), on the other, labour-power (L) — "the material and the personal factors of commodity production." Marx notes that "their precise nature must of course depend on the type of article to be produced."

He introduces notation to capture this. Let labour-power = L, means of production = mp, and the sum of commodities purchased = C. Then:

$$C = L + mp$$

Or, abbreviating, $C \binom{L}{mp}$. The act M–C, "considered in respect of its content," is thus represented as $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$, and it breaks up into two distinct purchases:

$$M - L \quad \text{and} \quad M - mp$$

The money M "divides into two portions, one for the purchase of labour-power, the other for means of production." Crucially, "the two sets of purchases pertain to completely different markets: one to the commodity market proper, the other to the labour market." The single act M–C is therefore internally bifurcated — a unity that is also a duality, straddling two socially distinct spheres.

The Qualitative and Quantitative Division of Money Capital

Marx now presses beyond the qualitative division to a quantitative one, and this is one of the chapter's most important arguments. He writes that "apart from this qualitative division of the commodities into which M is transformed, $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$ also exhibits a most characteristic quantitative relationship."

The argument proceeds from the nature of the wage. "The value or price of labour-power is paid to its proprietor… in the form of wages, i.e. as the price of a sum of labour that contains surplus labour." Marx illustrates: if the value of a day's labour-power is 3 shillings, the product of five hours' labour, "this sum may figure in the contract between buyer and seller as the price or wage for perhaps ten hours' labour." If such a contract is made with fifty workers, "they have to provide the buyer with a total of 500 hours' labour each day, half of this — 250 hours, or twenty-five ten-hour working-days — consisting simply of surplus labour."

The consequence is direct and structural: "The means of production to be purchased must be sufficient in quantity and volume to employ this amount of labour." This is the quantitative link between the two halves of M–C. The purchase of mp is not free-standing; it is calibrated to the mass of labour (and specifically the mass of surplus labour) that the purchase of L has secured. Thus $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$ expresses "not simply the qualitative relationship in which a certain sum of money, e.g. £422, is transformed into means of production and labour-power of a corresponding sum, but also a quantitative ratio between the portions of the money spent on labour-power L and on means of production mp, this ratio being conditioned from the start by the excess or surplus labour that the number of workers involved have to expend."

The numerical example is worth spelling out because it prefigures the yarn example used in the third stage. Fifty workers in a spinning mill, weekly wages £50; £372 to be spent on means of production, "if this is the value of the means of production that a working week of 3,000 man-hours, 1,500 of these being surplus labour, transforms into yarn." So the ratio between the money spent on L and the money spent on mp is not arbitrary — it is determined by the amount of labour the workers will actually perform, of which half is surplus. The £50 buys 3,000 hours of labour-power (as the price of a week's labour-power for fifty workers), and the £372 buys the material that those 3,000 hours can work up into yarn.

Marx then articulates the material necessity that underlies the ratio. "The degree to which the expenditure of excess labour requires an excess value in the form of means of production is quite unimportant here." What matters is that "under all circumstances the part of the money that is spent on means of production… must be sufficient, i.e. must be reckoned up from the start and be provided in appropriate proportions." The means of production "must be sufficient in mass to absorb the mass of labour which is to be turned into products through them."

He states the two failure modes with binary clarity: If sufficient means of production are not present, then the surplus labour the purchaser controls "cannot be made use of; his right to dispose of it will lead to nothing." If more means of production are available than disposable labour, then "these remain unsaturated with labour, and are not transformed into products." This is a statement about the technical and social preconditions of valorisation: capital must assemble L and mp in the correct proportions or the whole project fails, either because labour stands idle or because machinery stands idle. The quantitative structure of M–C is thus the material precondition of the valorisation that will occur in P.

The Emergence of Productive Capital

Once M – C $\binom{L}{mp}$ is completed, Marx argues, the purchaser does not merely have the means to produce a useful article. He has "a greater capacity to set labour-power in motion, or a greater quantity of labour, than is needed to replace the value of the labour-power," together with "the means of production that are required to realize or objectify this amount of labour." He thus controls "the factors of production for articles of a greater value than their elements of production, for a mass of commodities containing surplus-value."

The value advanced in the form of money "thus now exists in a natural form in which it can be realized as value which breeds surplus-value (in the shape of commodities). In other words, it exists in the state or form of productive capital, with the ability to function as creator of value and surplus-value. We call capital in this form P."

Two clarifications follow. First, the magnitude of value is unchanged by the transformation of form: "The value of P, however, equals the value of L + mp, that of the money M transformed into L + mp. M is the same capital value as P, only in a different mode of existence, i.e. capital value in the state or form of money — money capital." This is the first explicit naming of money capital as a form — not a distinct kind of wealth but a functional mode of existence of capital value.

Second, Marx specifies the meaning of the act: "M – C $\binom{L}{mp}$, or M–C in its general form, a sum of commodity purchases — this act of general commodity circulation is thus at the same time, as a stage in the independent circuit of capital, the transformation of capital value from its money form into its productive form, or more briefly the transformation of money capital into productive capital." And: "In the first figure of the circuit to be considered here, money appears as the original bearer of the capital value, and hence money capital appears as the form in which capital is advanced."

Money Performing Monetary Functions — and the Two Errors

A subtle but decisive passage now follows. "As money capital, it exists in a state in which it can perform monetary functions, in the present case the functions of general means of purchase and payment." (Payment, because labour-power is paid for after it has done its work; and payment for mp too, where these are ordered rather than bought off the shelf.) "Money capital does not possess this capacity because it is capital, but because it is money."

This is a warning against a confusion that Marx will later name explicitly. On the other hand, "the capital value in its monetary state can perform only monetary functions, and no others. What makes these into functions of capital is their specific role in the movement of capital, hence also the relationship between the stage in which they appear and the other stages of the capital circuit." So money functions as capital not because of any intrinsic property but because of its position in the circuit. In M–C, "money is converted into commodities which in their combination constitute the natural form of productive capital; this form therefore already bears latently within it, as its possibility, the result of the capitalist production process."

Then comes a crucial structural observation: "A part of the money that performs the function of money capital in M – C $\binom{L}{mp}$ passes over, by accomplishing this very circulation, into a function in which its capital character vanishes though its money character remains." This refers to the money that the worker receives (M–L from the capitalist's side is L–M from the worker's side) — for the worker, this money is simply money, a means of circulation for buying means of subsistence; its capital character is invisible from his standpoint.

Marx then presents the worker's side in full. "M–L, on the capitalist's part, is the purchase of labour-power; it is the sale of labour-power on the part of the worker, the owner of labour-power (we can say 'labour' here, as the wage form is presupposed)." What is M–C (M–L) for the purchaser is "here, as in every sale, L–M (C–M) for the seller (the worker), in this case the sale of his labour-power." The latter is "the first stage of circulation, or the first metamorphosis of the commodity" (referring back to Volume 1, Chapter 3). "The worker spends the money thus received bit by bit on a sum of commodities that satisfy his needs, on articles of consumption." His overall circulation is thus:

$$L - M - C$$

"i.e. firstly L–M (C–M) and secondly M–C, i.e. in the general form of simple commodity circulation C–M–C, where money figures simply as an evanescent means of circulation, as merely mediating the conversion of one commodity into another." Note this carefully: for the worker, the exchange he enters is C–M–C. Money is a mere intermediary. The same physical coins that are money capital in the capitalist's circuit are mere money in the worker's circuit. This is a vivid instance of the chapter's central thesis that the character of a money-function depends entirely on its place in the circuit of capital, not on any property of the money itself.

M–L as the Characteristic Moment; the Irrationality of the Wage Form

Marx now states: "M–L is the characteristic moment of the transformation of money capital into productive capital, for it is the essential condition without which the value advanced in the money form cannot really be transformed into capital, into value-producing surplus-value. M–mp is necessary only in order to realize the mass of labour bought by way of M–L. This is why M–L was presented from this point of view in Volume 1, Part Two, 'The Transformation of Money into Capital.' Here we have to consider the matter from a further aspect, with special reference to money capital as a form of appearance of capital."

He then turns to a polemic against a common view. "M–L is generally regarded as characteristic of the capitalist mode of production. But this is in no way for the reason just given" — i.e. not because it secures surplus labour. "It is rather on account of its form, because in the form of wages labour is bought with money, and this is taken as the characteristic feature of a 'money economy.'"

Marx objects that this reading takes the appearance for the essence. "Here again, it is not the irrationality of the form that is taken as characteristic. This irrationality is rather overlooked." What is the irrationality? "The irrationality consists in the fact that labour as the value-forming element cannot itself possess any value, and so a certain quantity of labour cannot have a value that is expressed in its price, in its equivalence with a certain definite quantity of money." This is a compressed restatement of Volume 1's critique of "the wage form of value." Labour is the source of value and cannot itself be bought as a value; what is bought is labour-power, whose price is wages. But this price "presents itself" as the price of the labour actually set in motion, so that "the value produced by this labour-power in six hours' labour, say, is expressed as the value of its twelve-hour functioning or labour." The wage form thus systematically conceals the extraction of surplus labour by presenting the value produced in the whole working day as if it were the price of a single day's labour.

The deeper point — developed against the "money economy" reading — is that "M–L is taken as the characteristic feature or hallmark of the so-called money economy because labour appears here as the commodity of its possessor, and hence money as its buyer — in other words because of the money relation (sale and purchase of human activity)." But: "money appears very early on as a buyer of so-called services, without its being transformed into money capital, and without any revolution in the general character of the economy." Paying money for services is ancient and tells us nothing about the character of a mode of production. Marx writes: "It is quite immaterial, as far as the money is concerned, what sort of commodities it is transformed into." Money is "the universal equivalent form of all commodities," which already show in their prices that they "ideally represent a specific sum of money, expect to be transformed into money, and only receive the form in which they can be converted into use-values for their possessor by changing places with money." Once labour-power appears on the market as a commodity, its sale "taking place in the form of a payment for labour, in the wage form, then its sale and purchase is no more striking than the sale and purchase of any other commodity. What is characteristic is not that the commodity labour-power can be bought, but the fact that labour-power appears as a commodity." In other words, the bare fact that labour-power is bought and sold is not itself the signature of capitalism; what is distinctive is the social relation that forces the mass of producers to put their labour-power up for sale.

The Class Relation Presupposed in the Money Relation

This leads to one of the chapter's most celebrated passages. "By way of $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$, the transformation of money capital into productive capital, the capitalist effects a connection between the objective and the personal factors of production, in so far as these factors consist of commodities."

Marx first examines the temporal order from the capitalist's standpoint: "If money is to be transformed for the first time into productive capital, or to function as money capital for the first time for its possessor, then he must first buy the means of production, i.e. buildings, machines, etc. before he buys labour-power; for when the labour-power passes into his control, the means of production must also be present before it can be applied as labour-power."

From the worker's side, the picture is altogether different. "The productive application of his labour-power is possible only when this has been associated with the means of production, as the result of its sale. Before the sale, this labour-power exists in a state of separation from the means of production, from the objective conditions of its application." In this separation, labour-power "can be directly used neither for the production of use-values for its possessor, nor for the production of commodities which he could live from selling. But as soon as it is associated with the means of production, by being sold it forms a component of the productive capital of its buyer just as much as the means of production do."

The heart of the argument: "although in the act M–L the possessor of money and the possessor of labour-power relate to each other only as buyer and seller, confront each other as possessor of money and possessor of a commodity, and are thus from this point of view simply in a money relationship with each other, the buyer appears right from the start as the possessor of the means of production which form the objective conditions for the productive expenditure of labour-power by its possessor." These means of production "confront the possessor of labour-power as someone else's property." Conversely, "the buyer… is confronted by the seller of labour as another's labour-power which must pass into his control, and has to be incorporated into his capital in order for this really to function as productive capital."

From this it follows: "The class relation between capitalist and wage-labourer is thus already present, already presupposed, the moment that the two confront each other in the act M–L (L–M from the side of the worker). This is a sale and purchase, a money relation, but a sale and purchase in which it is presupposed that the buyer is a capitalist and the seller a wage-labourer; and this relation does in fact exist, because the conditions for the realization of labour-power, i.e. means of subsistence and means of production, are separated, as the property of another, from the possessor of labour-power."

This is a decisive theoretical point. The "money relation" of buyer and seller in the labour market is superficially one of equality — equal contracting parties. But it presupposes a deeper inequality: the buyer's access to the objective conditions of labour, and the seller's dispossession of them. The class relation is not generated by the money relation but presupposed by it and expressed through it.

Marx then addresses the question of origin. "We are not concerned here with how this separation arises. If M–L takes place, it already exists." The present argument abstracts from the history that produced the separation (which is treated in Volume 1, Part Eight, on primitive accumulation). What matters now is the structural point: "if M–L appears as a function of money capital, or money appears here as a form of existence of capital, then this is in no way simply because money is involved here as the means of payment for a human activity with a useful effect, for a service; thus in no way because of money's function as means of payment."

Money "can be spent in this form only because labour-power is found in a state of separation from its means of production (including the means of subsistence as means of production of labour-power itself); and because this separation is abolished only through the sale of labour-power to the owner of the means of production, a sale which signifies that the buyer is now in control of the continuous flow of labour-power, a flow which by no means has to stop when the amount of labour necessary to reproduce the price of labour-power has been performed." That last clause captures the essential point: because labour-power can be made to work beyond the point at which it has reproduced its own price, the buyer acquires a claim on a flow of labour that yields surplus.

The conclusion is one of the chapter's key aphorisms: "The capital relation arises only in the production process because it exists implicitly in the act of circulation, in the basically different economic conditions in which buyer and seller confront one another, in their class relation. It is not the nature of money that gives rise to this relation; it is rather the existence of the relation that can transform a mere function of money into a function of capital."

The Two Errors in the Conception of Money Capital

Marx now makes explicit the confusion he has been diagnosing. "In the conception of money capital we customarily find two interconnected errors (for the time being we only deal with money capital in connection with the specific function in which it confronts us here)."

First error: "the functions that capital value performs as money capital, and which it is able to perform because it happens to be in the money form, are erroneously ascribed to its character as capital, whereas they are simply due to the money state of the capital value, its form of appearance as money." — i.e., people mistake money-functions (means of purchase, means of payment) for capital-functions.

Second error, "inversely": "the specific content of the money function that makes it simultaneously a function of capital is deduced from the nature of money (money is here confused with capital), whereas this function presupposes social conditions, as here in the act M–L, that are in no way given simply by commodity circulation and the money circulation corresponding to it." — i.e., people imagine that because money can buy labour-power, money somehow inherently possesses the property of generating capital. Both errors converge on the same confusion: they obliterate the distinction between the general properties of money and the historically specific social relation that makes a money-function into a capital-function.

The slavery analogy sharpens the point. "The purchase and sale of slaves is also in its form a purchase and sale of commodities. Without the existence of slaves, however, money cannot fulfil this function. If there is slavery, then money can be spent on the acquisition of slaves. But money in the hand of the buyer is in no way a sufficient condition for the existence of slavery." Money is necessary but not sufficient for a social relation; the relation must exist first.

Primitive Separation, Distribution of the Elements of Production, and the Russian Landowners

Marx then escalates from the individual act to the social totality. "If the sale of one's own labour-power (in the form of the sale of one's own labour, or the wage form) is not an isolated phenomenon, but the socially decisive precondition for the production of commodities, i.e. if money capital performs the function here considered, $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$, throughout society, this fact implies the occurrence of historic processes through which the original connection between means of production and labour-power was dissolved; processes as a result of which the mass of the people, the workers, come face to face with the non-workers, the former as non-owners, the latter as the owners, of these means of production." He adds the important caveat: "It is quite irrelevant whether the original connection, before it was destroyed, took the form that the worker belonged together with the other means of production as a means of production himself, or whether he was their owner." — i.e., whether the prior condition was slavery or independent proprietorship, the point is the same: the connection has been dissolved.

This yields a crucial definition of distribution: "Thus the situation that underlies the act $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$ is one of distribution; not distribution in the customary sense of distribution of the means of consumption, but rather the distribution of the elements of production themselves, with the objective factors concentrated on one side, and labour-power isolated from them on the other." This is a theoretical redefinition of "distribution" — away from the distribution of goods (which is what conventional economics means) and toward the distribution of the means of production, i.e. the class structuring of property. Underlying the simple purchase M–L is a whole social distribution of productive resources that makes the purchase possible.

"The means of production, the objective portion of productive capital, must thus already face the worker as such, as capital, before the act M–L can become general throughout society." At the level of society, the means of production must be capital — owned by non-workers — before wage labour can be universal.

Marx then cites Volume 1: "We have already seen how capitalist production, once it is established, not only reproduces this separation in the course of its development, but also expands on an ever greater scale until it has become the generally prevailing social condition." Note the dialectic: the separation precedes capitalist production historically, and capitalist production reproduces and expands it. There is a circularity — a self-reproducing structure — that is essential to the system's stability. But the chapter also notes the preconditions of this reproduction: "For capital to be formed and to take hold of production, trade must have developed to a certain level, hence also commodity circulation and, with that, commodity production; for articles cannot go into circulation as commodities except in so far as they are produced for sale, i.e. as commodities. It is only on the basis of capitalist production that commodity production appears as the normal, prevailing character of production." — So there is a historical progression from developed trade → commodity production → capitalist commodity production.

The empirical illustration is one of the chapter's most vivid. Russian landowners, after the "so-called emancipation of the peasants," now farm with wage-labourers instead of serf forced labour. They complain of two things. First, "the lack of money capital" — they say that before the harvest is sold, wage-labourers must be paid a considerable amount, and they lack ready cash. "Capital in the form of money must constantly be available, precisely for the payment of wages, in order that production may be conducted on a capitalist basis." Marx's deadpan reply: "the landlords need not worry. Everything comes to those who wait, and in time the industrial capitalist will have at his disposal not only his own money, but also l'argent des autres [other people's money]." — a jab at the emerging credit system.

The second complaint is more theoretically revealing: "even when they have money, the labour-power to be bought is not available in sufficient quantity and at the right time. This is because the Russian agricultural worker, owing to the common ownership of the soil by the village community, is not yet fully separated from his means of production, and is thus still not a 'free wage-labourer' in the full sense of the term." — The Russian peasant, retaining access to communal land, is not compelled to sell his labour-power; he is not fully "free in the double sense" that Volume 1 identified (free to sell, free of property). Hence the wage-labour market cannot function reliably. "But the presence of such 'free wage-labourers' throughout society is the indispensable condition without which M–C, the transformation of money into commodities, cannot take the form of the transformation of money capital into productive capital."

The conclusion of the whole first-stage argument follows: "It goes without saying, therefore, that the formula for the circuit of money capital: M–C…P…C'–M', is the self-evident form of the circuit of capital only on the basis of already developed capitalist production, because it presupposes the availability of the class of wage-labourers in sufficient numbers throughout society." And: "Since the first precondition of M–C…P…C'–M' is the continuous availability of the class of wage-labourers, it already implies the existence of capital in the form of productive capital, and hence the form of the circuit of productive capital." — The circuit of money capital implies the circuit of productive capital; the apparent priority of M will be shown to be merely apparent. This is the seed of the chapter's concluding argument.

Section 2: The Second Stage — The Function of Productive Capital

Circulation Interrupted

The circuit began with M–C (purchase). "This circulation must therefore be supplemented by the opposite metamorphosis C–M, the transformation of commodities into money, i.e. sale. But the direct result of $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$ is an interruption in the circulation of the capital value advanced in the money form." Why? "By the transformation of money capital into productive capital, the capital value has received a natural form in which it cannot circulate any further, but has to go into consumption, that is into productive consumption."

The reasoning is precise: "The use of labour-power, labour, can be realized only in the labour process. The capitalist cannot sell the worker again as a commodity, for he is not his slave, and the capitalist has bought nothing more than the utilization of his labour-power for a certain time. He can make use of this labour-power only in so far as it enables him to make use of the means of production to fashion commodities. The result of the first stage is thus capital's entry into the second stage, its productive stage."

The movement is thus $M - C \binom{L}{mp} \dots P$, "the dots indicating that the circulation of capital is interrupted; but its circuit continues, with its passage from the sphere of commodity circulation into that of production." Hence: "The first stage, the transformation of money capital into productive capital, thus appears as no more than the prelude and introduction to the second stage, the function of productive capital." — The circulation act is subordinate to the production act; the whole point of M–C is to enable P.

Marx adds a structural note about M–C: "$M - C \binom{L}{mp}$ presupposes that the individual who performs this act does not just have at his disposal values in some useful form or other, but that he possesses these values in money form, that he is the possessor of money. The act, however, consists precisely in letting go of money, and the possessor of money can only remain so in so far as the money will implicitly flow back to him as a result of the very act of letting go of it. This act thus presupposes that he is a commodity producer." — To spend money and remain a money-possessor, one must be able to recover it. So the money-spender in M–C is already a producer of commodities for sale.

The Wage-Labourer's Hand-to-Mouth Existence and the Permanence of Money Capital

A crucial argument now emerges about money capital's necessity. "M–L. The wage-labourer lives only from the sale of his labour-power. Its maintenance — his own maintenance — requires daily consumption. His payment must therefore be constantly repeated at short intervals, to enable him to repeat the purchases — the act L–M–C or C–M–C — that are needed for this self-maintenance. Hence the capitalist must constantly confront him as money capitalist, and his capital as money capital." This is footnote-flagged as important: the wage relationship structurally requires that capital continuously appear in the money form.

"On the other hand, however, in order that the mass of direct producers, the wage-labourers, may perform the act L–M–C, they must constantly encounter the necessary means of subsistence in purchasable form, i.e. in the form of commodities. Thus this situation in itself demands a high degree of circulation of products as commodities, i.e. commodity production on a large scale. As soon as production by way of wage-labour becomes general, commodity production must be the general form of production."

Division of Labour, M–mp, and the Universalisation of Commodity Production

"Assuming this to be the case, commodity production in turn brings about an ever growing division of social labour, i.e. an ever greater specialization of the products produced as commodities by particular capitalists, an ever greater division of complementary production processes into independent ones. M–mp therefore develops to the same degree as M–L, i.e. the production of means of production is separated to the same extent from the production of the commodities whose means of production they are; these too confront each commodity producer as commodities which he does not himself produce, but buys for the purpose of his particular production process. They come from branches of production that are pursued in complete separation and independence from his own, and enter his branch of production as commodities, which must therefore be bought. The material conditions of commodity production confront him to an ever greater extent as the products of other commodity producers, as commodities. The capitalist must appear to the same extent as a money capitalist, i.e. his capital must function in a greater measure as money capital."

This is an important passage about the co-development of money capital's necessity and social division of labour. As production becomes more specialised, the capitalist must buy more of his inputs from others — which means he must appear on the market as a buyer of commodities more extensively — which means his capital must take the money form more frequently and extensively. The very division of labour that raises productivity also deepens capitalism's dependence on the money form and circulation.

The Dissolving Effect of Capitalist Commodity Production on Earlier Forms

Marx then sketches the historical dynamics: "On the other hand, the same circumstance that produces the basic condition for capitalist production, the existence of a class of wage-labourers, encourages the transition of all commodity production to capitalist commodity production. To the extent that the latter develops, it has a destroying and dissolving effect on all earlier forms of production, which, being pre-eminently aimed at satisfying the direct needs of the producers, only transform their excess products into commodities. It makes the sale of the product the main interest, at first without apparently attacking the mode of production itself; this was for example the first effect of capitalist world trade on such peoples as the Chinese, Indians, Arabs, etc. Once it has taken root, however, it destroys all forms of commodity production that are based either on the producers' own labour, or simply on the sale of the excess product as a commodity. It firstly makes commodity production universal, and then gradually transforms all commodity production into capitalist production."

This is a compressed account of capitalist expansion — first, the corrosive effects of world trade on non-capitalist economies (making sale the main interest without immediately changing the mode of production), then the full transformative impact (destroying artisan and petty-commodity production, making commodity production universal, then converting it into capitalist production). The footnote indicates this section is from Manuscript VII (up to p. 12, where it switches to Manuscript VI).

The Union of Workers and Means of Production as Epoch-Distinguishing Criterion

Marx then offers an abstract formulation: "Whatever the social form of production, workers and means of production always remain its factors. But if they are in a state of mutual separation, they are only potentially factors of production. For any production to take place, they must be connected. The particular form and mode in which this connection is effected is what distinguishes the various economic epochs of the social structure." — The mode of connection between workers and means of production is the ultimate criterion distinguishing historical epochs. This is a foundational methodological claim of the entire Marxian framework.

"In the present case, the separation of the free worker from his means of production is the given starting point, and we have seen how and under what conditions the two come to be united in the hands of the capitalist — i.e. as his capital in its productive mode of existence. The actual process which the personal and material elements of commodity formation, brought together in this way, enter into with each other, the process of production, therefore itself becomes a function of capital — the capitalist production process, whose nature we have gone into in detail in the first volume of this work."

Then the crucial historical claim: "All pursuit of commodity production becomes at the same time pursuit of the exploitation of labour-power; but only capitalist commodity production is an epoch-making mode of exploitation, which in the course of its historical development revolutionizes the entire economic structure of society by its organization of the labour process and its gigantic extension of technique, and towers incomparably above all earlier epochs." — This is the thesis of capitalist specificity: not commodity production as such, not exploitation as such, but their capitalist integration and its revolutionary consequences for technique and organisation.

Constant and Variable Capital; P + s

The chapter then introduces, from the standpoint of the circuit, the constant/variable distinction. "By the different roles that they play during the production process in connection with the formation of value, and thus in the creation of surplus-value, means of production and labour-power, in so far as they are forms of existence of the capital value advanced, are distinguished as constant and variable capital." Note that the distinction is specified as the roles played in the valorisation process — not as a distinction between physical types of things, but between functions of value.

"They are further distinguished, as different components of productive capital, by the fact that the means of production, once in the possession of the capitalist, remain his capital even outside the production process, whereas labour-power becomes the form of existence of an individual capital only within this process. If labour-power is only a commodity in the hands of its seller, the wage-labourer, it only becomes capital in the hands of its buyer, the capitalist, to whom falls its temporary use. The means of production, for their part, become objective forms of productive capital, or productive capital proper, only from the moment that labour-power, as the personal form of existence of productive capital, can be incorporated into them. The means of production are no more capital by nature than is human labour-power. They receive this specific social character only under certain particular conditions that have historically developed, just as it is only under such conditions that precious metals are stamped with the character of money, or money with that of money capital."

This last sentence is a compressed rejection of any trans-historical "capital-ness" of things: neither machines nor money nor labour-power are in themselves capital. They become capital only under a historically specific social relation. The parallel between money's character (a socially-specific stamp on precious metals) and capital's character (a socially-specific relation on things) is one of the chapter's most philosophically resonant passages.

"In the course of its functioning, productive capital consumes its own components, to convert them into a mass of products of a higher value. Since labour-power operates only as an organ of capital, the excess value with which surplus labour endows the product, over and above that of its constituent elements, is also the fruit of capital. Labour-power's surplus labour is labour performed gratis for capital, and hence forms surplus-value for the capitalist, a value that costs him no equivalent."

The value formula: "The product is therefore not only a commodity, but a commodity impregnated with surplus-value. Its value is P + s, the value of the productive capital P consumed in its production plus that of the surplus-value s it engenders. Let us suppose that this commodity consists of 10,000 lb of yarn, with means of production to the value of £372 and labour-power to the value of £50 used up in its production. During the spinning process, the spinners transferred to the yarn the value of the means of production consumed in the process by means of their labour, £372, while they simultaneously produced a new value of, say, £128, corresponding to their expenditure of labour. The 10,000 lb. of yarn is therefore the bearer of a value of £500."

So we have: means of production consumed = £372 (constant capital, transferred value); labour-power value = £50 (variable capital, price of a week's labour-power for 50 workers); new value produced = £128 (of which £50 reproduces the wage and £78 is surplus-value); total value = £372 + £128 = £500. Equivalently, P = £422, s = £78, so P + s = £500. This is the standard Marxian value-decomposition. The stage is set for the third-stage analysis, where this £500 is transformed into money.

Section 3: The Third Stage — C'–M'

Commodity Capital and the "Birthmark" of Value

"Commodities become commodity capital as the functional form of existence of the already valorized capital value that has arisen directly from the production process itself." If commodity production were fully capitalist, "every commodity would be from the start the element of a commodity capital, whether it consisted of pig-iron or Brussels lace, sulphuric acid or cigars." Marx then mocks the "charming vexations that scholastic economics inflicts on itself" in trying to determine which commodities are destined "by their properties" to be capital and which to be "common commodity service." This is a jab at those who reify capital as a property of things rather than a form of a value-relation.

"In commodity form, capital must perform commodity functions. The articles it consists of, which are produced from the start for the market, must be sold, transformed into money, and thus pass through the movement C–M." Here is the central question: "What is it that makes this simple act of all commodity circulation simultaneously a function of capital?" Marx's answer: "It cannot be a change undergone in the act itself, neither with respect to its useful character, for it is as an object of use that the commodity passes to the buyer, nor with respect to its value, for this does not suffer a change of magnitude, but only one of form. It first existed in yarn, and now exists in money."

So what? "There is thus an essential distinction between the first stage M–C and the final stage C–M. Formerly the money advanced functioned as money capital because it was converted through circulation into commodities with a specific use-value. Now the commodity can function as capital only in so far as it actually brings this character with it from the production process, before its circulation begins." — Money capital is made into capital by the act M–C (by what is bought); commodity capital is already capital before the act C'–M' (because it has come out of the production process as bearer of surplus-value).

The specific numbers: "During the spinning process the spinners created yarn to the value of £128, of which £50, say, was simply an equivalent to the capitalist for his outlay on labour-power, and £78 formed surplus-value — a rate of exploitation of labour-power of 156 per cent. The value of the 10,000 lb. of yarn thus contains, firstly, the value of the consumed productive capital P, its constant part being £372, its variable part £50 and their sum £422 = 8,440 lb. of yarn. The value of the productive capital P is equal to C, the value of its formative elements, which in the stage M–C confronted the capitalist as commodities in the hands of their sellers. Secondly, however, the value of the yarn contains a surplus-value of £78 = 1,560 lb. of yarn."

Note the rate of exploitation: £78 / £50 = 156%. This is independent of whether we read it as value or as physical yarn-pounds; either way, the same ratio. The 10,000 lb. of yarn at £500 = 10,000 shillings (1 shilling per pound); 8,440 lb. is worth £422 (the capital value reproduced), and 1,560 lb. is worth £78 (the surplus-value).

"Thus as the value expression of the 10,000 lb. of yarn, C = C + ΔC, C plus an increment (£78) which we shall call c, as it exists in the same commodity form as the original value now does." The footnote here flags an editorial decision: since C, M, and P are used for the three forms of industrial capital, 'c' must be used for the increment — the surplus-value in its commodity form — while English convention reserves c, v, s for constant capital, variable capital and surplus-value, so this notation reappears in Volume 2. This explains the footnote's warning about "a possible source of confusion."

C' as a Value Ratio, Not a Magnitude

"the value of 10,000 lb. of yarn, £500, is thus C + c = C'." What makes C into C'? "What makes C, as the value expression of the 10,000 lb. of yarn, into C' is not the absolute amount of its value (£500), for this is determined, like the value expression of any other sum of commodities, by the amount of labour objectified in it. It is rather the relative magnitude of its value, its value compared with the value of the capital P consumed in its production. The value contained in it is this value plus the surplus-value provided by the productive capital. Its value is greater, i.e. it exceeds the capital value P, by the surplus-value c."

This is a subtle but critical point: C' is not C' because £500 is a large number — any commodity mass could be worth £500 — but because £500 is £422 + £78, i.e. because it is the value of the productive capital plus the surplus-value that productive capital has bred. The ' indicates a relation of value-increase, not an absolute magnitude.

"Thus C' expresses a value ratio, the ratio of the value of the commodity product to that of the capital consumed in its production, i.e. it expresses the composition of its value out of capital value and surplus-value. The 10,000 lb. of yarn are commodity capital, C', only as the transformed form of the productive capital P, thus in a relationship that exists at first only in the circuit of this individual capital, or for the capitalist who has produced yarn with his capital."

Then the crucial observation about the internal character of this relation: "It is so to speak only an internal relation, not an external one, that makes the 10,000 lb. of yarn, as bearer of value, into commodity capital. The yarn bears its capitalist birth-mark not in the absolute magnitude of its value, but in its relative magnitude, in the magnitude of its value compared with the value of the productive capital contained in it before it was transformed into commodities."

This phrase — "capitalist birth-mark" — captures the point memorably. When the yarn is sold, it appears in circulation simply as yarn, worth £500, indistinguishable from any other yarn. Its character as commodity capital is not seen on its face; it is a relation between the value it embodies and the value of the capital that produced it. "If the 10,000 lb. of yarn is sold at its value of £500, this act of circulation, considered in itself, is C–M, the simple transformation of a value that remains the same from the commodity form into the money form. However, as a particular stage in the circuit of an individual capital, this same act is the realization of a capital value of £422 plus a surplus-value of £78, both borne by the commodity, i.e. C'–M', the transformation of the capital value from its commodity form into the money form."

The Realisation Problem and the Sale as Essential Moment

Marx then makes an economics argument that anticipates much of later Marxist theory of realisation crises. "The function of C' is now that of every commodity product, to be transformed into money and sold, to pass through the phase of circulation C–M. As long as the now valorized capital persists in the form of commodity capital, is tied up on the market, the production process stands still. The capital operates neither to fashion products nor to form value. According to the varying speed with which the capital sheds its commodity form and assumes its money form, i.e. according to the briskness of the sale, the same capital value will serve to a very uneven degree in the formation of products and value, and the scale of the reproduction will expand or contract."

This introduces a new source of variation in capital's efficiency — the speed of circulation. "It was shown in the first volume that the degree of effectiveness of a given capital is conditioned by forces in the production process that are to a certain extent independent of its own magnitude. Now we see that the circulation process sets in motion new forces independent of the magnitude of value, which affect the degree of effectiveness of the capital, its expansion and its contraction." — So the turnover time (foreshadowing later chapters on turnover) becomes a determinant of capital's productivity, independent of its magnitude.

He then analyses the proportion of the commodity mass that must be sold for the capitalist to realise his capital and surplus. "The mass of commodities C', as bearer of the valorized capital, must fully undergo the metamorphosis C'–M'. The quantity sold is here the essential determinant. The individual commodity figures only as an integral part of the total quantity." Then the graduated schedule: If the capitalist sells only 7,440 lb. at £372, he has "only replaced the value of his constant capital." If he sells 8,440 lb., he "still replaces only the value of the total capital advanced." "He must sell more, if he is to realize surplus-value, and he must sell the entire 10,000 lb. of yarn if he is to realize the whole surplus-value of £78 (= 1,560 lb. of yarn)."

This is a schematic demonstration that surplus-value realisation depends entirely on the volume sold. The surplus is not created in circulation — Marx insists "He receives in the £500 only an equal value for the commodities sold; his transaction within the circulation sphere is simply C–M" — but it can only be realised if the sale succeeds at value. If sale below value, the capital value itself may fail to be replaced.

Marx also notes a counterfactual about the wage: "If he had paid his workers £64 instead of £50, then his surplus-value would be only £64, instead of £78, and the rate of exploitation only 100 per cent instead of 156 per cent. But the value of his yarn would be unchanged; only the ratio of its various component portions would be different; the circulation act M–C would still be the sale of 10,000 lb. of yarn for £500, its value." — The total value of the yarn is determined by the labour embodied in it, not by the wage rate. A higher wage would simply redistribute the same £500 between the capitalist's variable capital and his surplus. This is a subtle reminder that the division between v and s is a distributional question internal to the labour process, and does not affect the total value of the product.

The Expanded Formula and the Division of Capital Value from Surplus-Value

"C' = C + c (=£422 + £78). C is equal in value to P or the productive capital, and this is also equal in value to the M advanced in M–C, the purchase of the elements of production: in our example, £422. If the mass of commodities is sold at its value, then C = £422, and c = £78, the value of the surplus product of 1,560 lb. of yarn. If we call c, expressed in monetary terms, m, we have C'–M', or (C + c)–(M + m), and the circuit M–C…P…C'–M' in its expanded form is thus

$$M - C \binom{L}{mp} \dots P \dots (C + c) - (M + m)."$$

The Double Character of the Realisation: Final vs First Metamorphosis

Marx now introduces his most theoretically dense analysis of the third stage, showing how the same act (the sale C'–M') is structurally different for the capital value and for the surplus-value.

"In the first stage, the capitalist withdraws articles of use, both from the commodity market proper and from the labour market; in the third stage he puts commodities back, though only into one market, the commodity market proper. But if he withdraws more value from the market by way of his commodities than he originally put into it, this is only because he puts in a greater value of commodities than he originally withdrew. He puts in the value M and withdraws the same value C; he puts in C + c, and withdraws the same value M + m. In our example, M was equal in value to 8,440 lb. of yarn; the capitalist, however, puts 10,000 lb. of yarn into the market, i.e. gives back a greater value than he took from it. On the other hand, he has only put in this increased value because he produced surplus-value (as an aliquot part of the product, expressed in surplus product) in the production process, by the exploitation of labour-power. It is only as the product of this process that the mass of commodities is commodity capital, the bearer of the valorized capital value."

The same circulation act C'–M' "differs for the capital value and for the surplus-value in so far as it expresses in each case a different stage of their circulation, a different section in the series of metamorphoses that they have to pass through within the circulation sphere." For surplus-value: "The surplus-value, c, first came into the world within the production process. It is thus now entering the commodity market for the first time, and moreover in the commodity form; this is its first form of circulation, and hence the act c–m is its first act of circulation or its first metamorphosis, which thus still has to be supplemented by the opposite circulation act, the converse metamorphosis m–c." A footnote notes this holds regardless of how capital value and surplus-value are divided within the commodity: "10,000 lb. of yarn contain 1,560 lb. = £78 surplus-value, but 1 lb. of yarn = 1 shilling also contains 2.496 oz. = 1.872 d. surplus-value."

For the capital value: "It is a different matter with the circulation accomplished by the capital value C in the same circulation act C'–M', which for it is the circulation act C–M, where C = P, equal to the originally advanced M. This started its first act of circulation as M, money capital, and it now returns to the same form in the act C–M; it has thus passed through the two opposing phases of circulation (1) M–C and (2) C–M, and exists once again in the form in which it can begin the same cyclical process afresh. The transformation from the commodity form to the money form, which is for the surplus-value its first transformation, is for the capital value its return or transformation back into its original money form."

Hence the neat formulation: C'–M' is for capital value a return (final metamorphosis) and for surplus-value a first entry (first metamorphosis). This duality will have deep consequences for the relation between capital and surplus-value in the total social reproduction (Volume 2, Part III).

The Material Metamorphosis of Production as an Interruption in Circulation

Marx continues to develop the theme that production is a real transformation, not merely a formal one, in the circuit of capital value.

"The money capital was converted into a sum of commodities of equal value, L and mp, by way of M–C. These commodities now no longer function as commodities, as articles for sale. Their value now exists in the hands of their buyer, the capitalist, as the value of his productive capital P. And in the function of P, productive consumption, they are transformed into a kind of commodity materially different from the means of production, into yarn, with the value not only being maintained, but increased, from £422 to £500. Through this real metamorphosis, the commodities withdrawn from the market in the first stage M–C are replaced by materially different commodities of different value, which must now function as commodities, be transformed into money and sold."

"Hence the production process appears simply as an interruption in the circulation of capital value, which up till then has only passed through the first phase M–C. It passes through the second and final phase, C–M, with C altered both materially and in value. But as far as the capital value taken by itself is concerned, all it has undergone in the production process is a change in its use form [Gebrauchsform]. It existed as £422 of value in L and mp, and it now exists as £422, the value of 8,440 lb. of yarn."

So from the perspective of the capital value taken alone: it went through M–C (money into commodities as elements of production), then C–M (commodities as product into money). "We thus have M–C–M, a form of circulation which, by way of a two-fold displacement in opposite directions, the transformation of money into commodities and commodities into money, necessarily determines the return of the value advanced as money to its money form: its transformation back into money." — This is a key analytic move. For the capital value considered in abstraction from its surplus-value, the circuit reduces to the classic M–C–M form of simple circulation, but with the crucial difference that the intermediate production stage has been inserted (in the dots).

The Two-Fold Role of Money at the Close of the Circuit

"Two things should be noted here. Firstly, the ultimate transformation of capital value back into its original money form is a function of commodity capital. Secondly, this function includes the first formal transformation of the surplus-value from its original commodity form into the money form. The money form plays a double role here; on the one hand it is the returning form of a value originally advanced in money, i.e. the money returns to the form of value that opened the process; on the other hand it is the first transformed form of a value that originally enters into circulation in the commodity form."

This double role is crucial for understanding why the circuit of money capital looks like it begins and ends with money. The two roles are: (1) a return of the capital value to its original form; (2) a first appearance of the surplus-value in money form. The first aspect makes the circuit appear as M...M' (money to money), and the second aspect gives the "+ m" — but these two aspects are external to one another, not integrated. The surplus-value was created in production; the capital value simply changed form.

"If the commodities of which the commodity capital consists are sold at their value, as we assume here, then C + c is transformed into M + m with the same value; it is in this last form, M + m (£422 + £78 = £500), that the realized commodity capital now exists in the hands of the capitalist. Capital value and surplus-value now exist as money, i.e. in the form of the universal equivalent."

The Circuit Named

"At the end of the process, the capital value is thus once again in the same form in which it entered it, and can therefore open the process afresh and pass through it as money capital. And indeed because the initial and concluding form of the process is that of money capital (M), we call this form of the circuit the circuit of money capital. It is not the form of the value advanced, but only its magnitude, that is changed at the end."

This passage gives the chapter its title, and its argument is significant: the reason we call it the circuit of money capital is purely formal — because money is the first and last form. The substance (value-form vs value-magnitude) is the same; only the magnitude has changed.

M' as a Crude, Non-Conceptual Form

"M + m is nothing more than a sum of money of a certain magnitude, in our case £500. But as the result of the circuit of capital, as realized commodity capital, this sum of money contains the capital value and the surplus-value; moreover, these are no longer inextricably entwined, as in the yarn; they are now simply juxtaposed. Their realization has given each of the two an independent money form. 211/250 of the money is the capital value, £422, and 39/250 the surplus-value of £78. This separation effected by the realization of the commodity capital does not only have the formal content we shall speak of in a moment; it is important in the reproduction process of capital, according to whether m is added on to M in its entirety, in part, or not at all, thus according to whether or not it continues to function as a component of the capital value advanced. M and m can even pass through quite different circulations."

Two arithmetic ratios to note: £422/£500 = 211/250; £78/£500 = 39/250. This points to the deep anonymity of the money form: £422 and £78 are simply portions of £500, without any differentiating mark. This is the beginning of Marx's argument about the "conceptual" poverty of money.

"In M', the capital returns once more to its original form M, its money form, but in a form in which it has been realized as capital. Firstly, there is a quantitative difference. It was M, £422; it is now M', £500, and this difference is expressed in M…M', the quantitatively different extremities of the circuit, the actual movement of which is indicated simply by the dots. M' is greater than M; M' minus M = s, the surplus-value. But all that exists as the result of the cycle M…M' is M'; the process of formation has been obliterated in the product. M' now exists independently in its own right, it is independent of the movement that produced it. The movement is past, and M' is there in its place."

"But as M + m, £422 advanced capital plus an increment of £78 on the same, M' or £500 also exhibits a qualitative relation, although this qualitative relation itself exists only as a relation between the parts of a corresponding sum, i.e. as a quantitative ratio. M, the capital advanced, which is once again present in its original form (£422), exists now as realized capital. It has not only maintained itself, but it has also realized itself as capital, in so far as it has differentiated itself from m (£78), which is related to it as its increase, its fruit, an increment that it itself has bred. It is realized as capital, because it is value that has bred value. M' exists as a capital relation; M no longer appears as mere money, but is expressly postulated as money capital, expressed as value that has valorized itself, i.e. thus also possesses the property of valorizing itself, of breeding more value than it itself has. M is posited as capital by its relation to another part of M' as to something posited by itself, as to the effect of which it is the cause, as to the consequence of which it is the ground. M' thus appears as a sum of values which is internally differentiated, undergoes a functional (conceptual) self-differentiation, and expresses the capital-relation."

This is a highly dialectical passage. M' is not just a bigger sum of money; it is a self-differentiating sum, in which M (the capital) is related to m (the surplus) as cause to effect, ground to consequence. The capital-relation is present in M' — but "this is expressed simply as a result, without the mediation of the process whose result it is."

Then the passage on the homogeneity of money. "Portions of value are not qualitatively distinguished from each other as such, save in so far as they appear as the values of different articles, concrete things, thus in various different useful forms, as values of different bodies of commodities — a distinction that does not arise from their existence as mere portions of value. In money, every difference between commodities is obliterated, because money is precisely the equivalent form common to all of them. A sum of money of £500 consists of nothing but isomorphous elements of £1. Since the mediating effect of its history is obliterated in the simple existence of this sum of money, and every trace of the specific difference which the various component parts of capital possess in the production process has vanished, the only remaining distinction is the crude, non-conceptual [begrifflos — see the editorial footnote on the original's begrifflich] distinction between a 'principal' as it is called in English, i.e. the capital of £422 which was advanced, and an additional sum of value of £78."

The editorial footnote is significant: Marx wrote begrifflich ("conceptual") but the context requires begrifflos ("non-conceptual"). Engels's footnote tells us that the translators have assumed the latter. This reveals the palimpsest nature of the text — and it also confirms that the point is the absence of conceptual distinctions in the money sum.

"Let M' be £110, of which £100 is M, the principal, and £10 is s, surplus-value. There is absolute homogeneity, a complete absence of conceptual distinction, between the two constituent parts of the sum of £110. Any £10 is always one eleventh of the total sum of £110, whether it is a tenth of the principal advanced, or the additional £10 over and above this. Principal and increment, capital and surplus, can therefore both be expressed as fractions of the total sum; in our example ten elevenths is the principal or capital, and one eleventh the surplus. At the conclusion of its process the realized capital therefore appears as a sum of money, within which the distinction between principal and surplus expresses, in a naive, non-conceptual manner, the capital-relation."

C' versus M': Rationality of Form

"This is also true, moreover, for C' (= C + c). But with the difference that C', in which C and c are simply proportional value portions of the same homogeneous mass of commodities, indicates its origin in P, whose direct product it is, whereas in M', a form arising directly from the circulation sphere, the direct connection with P has vanished."

Then a crucial dialectical point about the evanescence of M': "The superficial distinction between principal and increment that is contained in M', in so far as this expresses the result of the movement M…M', vanishes immediately, as soon as M' functions actively once more as money capital, rather than being fixed as the money expression of the valorized industrial capital. The circuit of money capital can never begin with M', but only with M (even though it is M' that now functions as M); i.e. never as an expression of the capital relation, but only as the form in which the capital value is advanced. As soon as the £500 is advanced afresh as capital, in order to be valorized once more, it is the starting-point rather than the point of return. Instead of a capital of £422, one of £500 has now been advanced; more money than before, more capital value, but the relation between the two components has gone. The sum of £500 now functions as capital, rather than £422, just as, originally, a sum of £500 might have functioned, rather than a sum of £422."

The point: the capital relation expressed in M' is not preserved in the circuit's repetition. When £500 is advanced anew, it is simply £500 of capital; the fact that it contains a portion (£78) that was originally surplus-value is invisible. This is why "M' is only the result of the realization of C'." "It is not the active function of money capital to present itself as M'; its own presentation as M' is rather a function of C'." In the original C'–M', C' was the direct product of P and carried the marks of its origin; when the money form M' appears, those marks are effaced.

"It is not the active function of money capital to present itself as M'; its own presentation as M' is rather a function of C'. Already in simple commodity circulation, (1) C1–M, (2) M–C2, M functions actively only in the second act M–C2; its presentation as M is only the result of the first act, by virtue of which it first appears as the transformed form of C1. The capital relation contained in M', the connection between one of its parts as a part of capital value and the other as the value increment to this, does receive a functional significance, however, in so far as M' divides into two circulations, the circulation of capital and the circulation of surplus-value, when the circuit M…M' is constantly repeated. The two parts M and m then fulfil functions that differ not just quantitatively, but also qualitatively. Considered in itself, however, the form M…M' does not include the consumption of the capitalist, but expressly only capital's self-valorization and accumulation, in so far as the latter is first expressed in the periodic growth of the money capital that is constantly advanced afresh."

This is a crucial point about accumulation: in the form M…M', consumption does not appear; only valorisation and accumulation do. This means the formal representation is one-sided — it abstracts from the fact that the capitalist must also consume (his own upkeep) and that worker consumption is required to reproduce labour-power.

"Although it is a crude and conceptually undifferentiated form of capital, M' = M + m is at the same time money capital in its first realized form, money that has bred money. This must be distinguished from the function of money capital in the first stage $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$. In this first stage, M circulates as money. It functions as money capital simply because it is only in its monetary state that it performs a monetary function, and can be converted into the elements of P that face it as commodities, L and mp. In this act of circulation, it functions only as money; but because this act is the first stage of capital value in process, it is simultaneously a function of money capital, by virtue of the specific useful form of the commodities L and mp that are bought."

"M' on the other hand, composed of M, the capital value, and m, the surplus-value created by it, expresses valorized capital value, the purpose and the result, the function of the total process of the circuit of capital. If it expresses this result in money form, as realized money capital, this is not because it is the money form of capital, money capital, but rather the reverse, because it is money capital, capital in the money form, and that it was in this form that capital opened the process, was advanced in its money form. The transformation back into the money form is a function of the commodity capital C', as we saw, not of money capital. And as far as the difference m between M' and M is concerned, this is only the money form of c, the increment to C; M' is only equal to M + m because C' equals C + c. In C', therefore, this difference, and the relation between the capital value and the surplus-value bred by it, is present and is expressed before they are both transformed into M', into a sum of money in which the two portions of value confront each other from a position of independence and can therefore also be applied to independent and different functions."

So the intermediate conclusion is: C' is more "rational" than M'. In C', the relation between C and c is directly visible as a relation between the value of the productive capital and the surplus-value that productive capital has engendered, because the commodity is the direct product of P. In M', this relation is expressed only via the sale, and it appears only as a proportional relation between two portions of a homogeneous sum.

"M' is only the result of the realization of C'. Both of these, C' as well as M', are only different forms, the commodity form and the money form, of the valorized capital value; both have it in common that they are valorized capital value. Both are realized capital, because here capital value exists as such together with surplus-value as the fruit that is separate from it but produced by it, although this relation is expressed only in the naive form of the ratio between two parts of a sum of money or a commodity value."

The "Bizarre Form" and Gold Production

"As expressions of capital, however, both related to and distinct from the surplus-value created by it, i.e. as expressions of valorized value, M' and C' are the same, and express the same thing, only in different forms; they are not distinguished from each other as money capital and commodity capital, but rather as money and commodity. In so far as they represent valorized value, capital active as capital, they simply express the result of the function of productive capital, the only function in which capital value breeds value. What they have in common is that both of them, money capital and commodity capital, are modes of existence of capital. The one is capital in its money form, the other in its commodity form."

Then Marx introduces the concept of the "bizarre form" of M'. "The specific functions that distinguish them can thus be nothing other than distinctions between the money function and the commodity function. The commodity capital, as the direct product of the capitalist production process, recalls its origin and is therefore more rational in its form, less lacking in conceptual differentiation, than the money capital, in which every trace of this process has been effaced, just as all the particular useful forms of commodities are generally effaced in money. Hence it is only when M' itself functions as commodity capital, when it is the direct product of a production process and not the transformed form of this product, that its bizarre form disappears — i.e. in the production of the money material itself."

So: the "bizarre form" of M' is that a sum of money M appears to give birth to another sum of money m — i.e. that money breeds money, without the intervening process. This form is bizarre because it effaces the process. And this bizarre form disappears only when M' is itself a commodity product — i.e. when money is being produced, as in gold mining.

"The formula for the production of gold, for example, would be $M - C \binom{L}{mp} \dots P \dots M' (M + m)$, where M' figures as the commodity product in so far as P provides more gold than was advanced for the elements of production of gold in the first M, the money capital. The expression M…M' (M + m) is irrational, in that, within it, part of a sum of money appears as the mother of another part of the same sum of money. But here this irrationality disappears."

This is a crucial dialectical move. In gold production, the output M' is itself the physical commodity — ounces of gold. So the output N ounces of gold are physically visible as a sum of gold while their composition into capital-value and surplus-value is also visible: N ounces decompose into N1 (the value of the capital consumed) and N2 (the surplus). The "irrationality" of M breeding M disappears because here M is not just money, it is a produced commodity whose genesis in production is directly visible.

The "irrationality" is worth pausing over. Money-capital M is advanced, and after a cycle, money-capital M' = M + m emerges. The pure form M…M' (or M + m) appears to say that money generates money — like a mother birthing a child. This is "irrational" — it is the appearance of self-valorising money (Geld heckt Geld, in the old slogan), which capitalism's ideological forms of consciousness latch onto. In gold production, by contrast, the process is literal: the capitalist advances gold, buys inputs, and produces more gold. So the process is not hidden behind the money veil; it is the money itself.

Section 4: The Circuit as a Whole

The Total Movement as an Interrupted Metamorphosis

Marx now steps back to consider the circuit in its entirety.

"We have seen how the circulation process, after its first phase $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$ has elapsed, is interrupted by P, in which the commodities bought on the market, L and mp, are consumed as material and value components of the productive capital; the product of this consumption is a new commodity, altered both materially and in value. The interrupted circulation process, M–C, must be supplemented by C–M. But it is C' that appears as the bearer of this second and concluding phase, a commodity different materially and in value from the original C. The circulation series thus presents itself as (1) M–C1; (2) C'2–M', in which the first commodity C1 has been replaced in the second phase by one of higher value and a different useful form, C'2, during the interruption that is occasioned by the function of P, i.e. the production of C' from the elements of C, the forms of existence of the productive capital P."

Compare with Volume 1's discovery. "The first form of appearance in which we met with capital, on the other hand (Volume 1, Chapter 4), M–C–M' (broken down: (1) M–C1; (2) C1–M''), exhibits the same commodity twice over. It is the same commodity into which money is transformed in the first phase and which is transformed back into more money in the second phase." But in the circuit of capital, the second commodity (C'2) is materially and quantitatively different from the first (C1). Despite this essential difference, "both circulations have in common that in their first phase money is transformed into commodities and in their second phase commodities into money, that the money that is spent in the first phase flows back again in the second. On the one hand they have in common this stream of money back to its starting-point, on the other hand the excess of the money that flows back over that advanced. In this respect, M–C…C'–M' too appears to be contained in the general formula M–C–M'."

"It further results here that in both metamorphoses pertaining to the circulation sphere, M–C and C'–M', equally large and simultaneously present values always confront and replace each other. The change in value belongs solely to the metamorphosis P, the production process, which thus appears as the real metamorphosis of capital, as opposed to the merely formal metamorphoses of the circulation sphere."

This is one of the chapter's most important theoretical distinctions: real metamorphosis (production, where value changes) vs formal metamorphoses (circulation, where only the form of value changes). This distinction will reappear throughout Volume 2.

Industrial Capital and Its Three Functional Forms

"Let us now consider the total movement M–C…P…C'–M', or its expanded form $M - C \binom{L}{mp} \dots P \dots C'(C + c) - M'(M + m)$."

"Here capital appears as a value that passes through a sequence of connected and mutually determined transformations, a series of metamorphoses that form so many phases or stages of a total process. Two of these phases belong to the circulation sphere, one to the sphere of production. In each of these phases the capital value is to be found in a different form, corresponding to a different and special function. Within this movement the value advanced not only maintains itself, but it grows, increases its magnitude. Finally, in the concluding stage, it returns to the same form in which it appeared at the outset of the total process. This total process is therefore a circuit."

"The two forms that the capital value assumes within its circulation stages are those of money capital and commodity capital; the form pertaining to the production stage is that of productive capital. The capital that assumes these forms in the course of its total circuit, discards them again and fulfils in each of them its appropriate function, is industrial capital — industrial here in the sense that it encompasses every branch of production that is pursued on a capitalist basis."

The definition of industrial capital is key: it is not the specific capital of manufacturing industry, but any capital that passes through all three forms and gives itself to all three functions. "Money capital, commodity capital and productive capital thus do not denote independent varieties of capital, whose functions constitute the content of branches of business that are independent and separate from one another. They are simply particular functional forms of industrial capital, which takes on all three forms in turn."

This is a decisive polemic. The three "kinds" of capital that political economy treats as separate branches (banking capital, manufacturing capital, commercial capital) are really forms of the same industrial capital. Their separate existence as branches of business arises only through the social division of labour, which "renders them independent and one-sidedly extended."

Interruptions and the "Fettering" of the Circuit

"The circuit of capital proceeds normally only as long as its various phases pass into each other without delay." Then a set of failure modes: "If capital comes to a standstill in the first phase, M–C, money capital forms into a hoard; if this happens in the production phase, the means of production cease to function, and labour-power remains unoccupied; if in the last phase, C'–M', unsaleable stocks of commodities obstruct the flow of circulation."

"It lies in the nature of the case, however, that the circuit itself determines that capital is tied up for certain intervals in the particular sections of the cycle. In each of its phases industrial capital is tied to a specific form, as money capital, productive capital or commodity capital. Only after it has fulfilled the function corresponding to the particular form it is in does it receive the form in which it can enter a new phase of transformation."

The term "fettered" (or in other translations "tied up") points to a structural feature of capital: it must be committed to a particular form for a period before it can move on. Money capital must actually be spent; productive capital must actually complete its production; commodity capital must actually be sold. During each interval, the value is locked into a form that limits its fluidity. This sets up the whole problematic of turnover time.

The Fixed-Capital Caveat

"In order to make this clear, we have assumed in our example that the capital value of the mass of commodities created in the production stage is equal to the total value originally advanced as money, in other words that the whole capital value advanced as money moves all at once from one stage into the subsequent one. We have already seen, however (Volume 1, Chapter 8), that a part of the constant capital, the actual instruments of labour (e.g. machines), serve continuously throughout a greater or smaller number of repetitions of the same production process, and for this reason give up their value to the product only bit by bit. We shall show later on how far this circumstance modifies the circuit of capital."

Marx then explains why this caveat does not ruin the analysis. "In our example, the value of the productive capital, £422, contained only the average calculated wear and tear of factory buildings, machinery, etc., thus only the portion of value that they carry over in the course of transforming 10,000 lb. of raw cotton into 10,000 lb. of yarn, the product of a weekly spinning process of sixty hours. The instruments of labour — buildings, machinery, etc. — therefore figured in the means of production into which the constant capital advanced was transformed, as if they were simply hired on the market in return for a weekly payment. This however alters absolutely nothing as far as the substance of the matter is concerned. We need only multiply the weekly output of yarn, 10,000 lb., by the number of weeks contained in a given series of years, and the entire value of the instruments of labour bought and used up in this period will have been carried over. It is clear then that the money capital advanced must first be transformed into these means of production, and must therefore have made its exit from the first phase M–C, before it can function as productive capital P."

So the analysis in this chapter is a first approximation, valid for the annual (or multi-week) period over which the fixed capital wears out. The complicating factor (fixed capital) is bracketed for later analysis (Chapter 8 of Volume 2, on fixed and circulating capital).

The Transport Industry

Marx then addresses a puzzle: what if the product of production is not a new material thing? "In the general formula, the product of P is considered as a material thing different from the elements of the productive capital, an object that has an existence of its own, apart from the production process, possessing a useful form different from that of the elements of production. In so far as the result of the production process does appear as a thing, this is always the case, even when a part of the product enters once more as an element into the renewed production process. Thus grain serves as seed-corn for its own production, but the product consists only of grain, and thus has a different physical shape from the elements applied together with it: labour-power, instruments of labour, fertilizer."

Then: "There are however particular branches of industry in which the product of the production process is not a new objective product, a commodity. The only one of these that is economically important is the communication industry, both the transport industry proper, for moving commodities and people, and the transmission of mere information — letters, telegrams, etc."

Marx quotes A. Chuprov: "The manufacturer can produce articles first and look for customers afterwards. (His product, after it is ejected in finished form from the production process, passes into circulation as a commodity separate from this process.) Production and consumption thus appear as two acts separated in time and space. In the transport industry, however, which does not create new products, but only displaces people and things, these two acts coincide; the 'services' (the change of place) 'are necessarily consumed the moment they are produced. This is why the area within which railways can seek their customers is at most 50 versts' (53 km.) 'on either side.'" The footnote cites Chuprov's 1875 Moscow work The Railway Industry, pp. 69-70.

Marx elaborates: "The result in each case, whether it is people or commodities that are transported, is a change in their spatial location, e.g. that the yarn finds itself in India instead of in England, where it was produced. But what the transport industry sells is the actual change of place itself. The useful effect produced is inseparably connected with the transport process, i.e. the production process specific to the transport industry. People and commodities travel together with the means of transport, and this journeying, the spatial movement of the means of transport, is precisely the production process accomplished by the transport industry. The useful effect can only be consumed during the production process; it does not exist as a thing of use distinct from this process, a thing which functions as an article of commerce and circulates as a commodity only after its production."

"However the exchange-value of this useful effect is still determined, like that of any other commodity, by the value of the elements of production used up in it (labour-power and means of production), plus the surplus-value created by the surplus labour of the workers occupied in the transport industry. In respect of its consumption, too, this useful effect behaves just like other commodities. If it is consumed individually, then its value vanishes with its consumption; if it is consumed productively, so that it is itself a stage of production of the commodity that finds itself transported, then its value is carried over to the commodity as an addition to it. The formula for the transport industry is thus $M - C \binom{L}{mp} \dots P \dots M'$, for it is the production process itself, and not a product separable from it, that is paid for and consumed. This therefore has almost exactly the same form as that for the production of precious metals, except that M' is here the transformed form of the useful effect produced in the course of the production process, and not the natural form of the gold and silver that is produced during this process and ejected from it."

This is an important theoretical extension: some production processes (transport, communications) produce no separable commodity; what they sell is the useful effect itself, consumed at the moment of production. Their value-formula is M–C…P…M' — the same formal structure as gold production, though for a different reason (gold production sells gold as the natural form of value; transport sells an immaterial service that is directly money).

Industrial Capital as Hegemony

"Industrial capital is the only mode of existence of capital in which not only the appropriation of surplus-value or surplus product, but also its creation, is a function of capital. It thus requires production to be capitalist in character; its existence includes that of the class antagonism between capitalists and wage-labourers. To the degree that it takes hold of production, the technique and social organization of the labour process are revolutionized, and the economic-historical type of society along with this. The other varieties of capital which appeared previously, within past or declining conditions of social production, are not only subordinated to it and correspondingly altered in the mechanism of their functioning, but they now move only on its basis, thus live and die, stand and fall together with this basis. Money capital and commodity capital, in so far as they appear and function as bearers of their own peculiar branches of business alongside industrial capital, are now only modes of existence of the various functional forms that industrial capital constantly assumes and discards within the circulation sphere, forms which have been rendered independent and one-sidedly extended through the social division of labour."

This is a major theoretical claim about the historical primacy of industrial capital. Pre-capitalist forms of capital (usury capital, merchant capital) existed before industrial capital and operated on other bases. But once industrial capital dominates, all other forms of capital become subordinate to it — they "now move only on its basis." Independent money capital and commodity capital (banks, merchants) are not separate species of capital but the branches of the division of labour formed out of the money-function and commodity-function of industrial capital.

The Independent Movement within the General Circulation

"On the one hand, the circuit M…M' is inextricably linked with the general circulation of commodities, issues from it and flows back into it, forming a part of it. On the other hand, it forms for the individual capitalist an independent movement peculiar to his capital value, a movement which proceeds in part within the general circulation of commodities, in part outside it, but which always retains its independent character."

Three reasons for the independence: 1. "both of the phases that it goes through in the circulation sphere, M–C and C–M, possess a functionally specific character as phases of the movement of capital; in M–C, C is determined in its material content as labour-power and means of production; in C'–M' the capital value is realized together with the surplus-value." 2. "P, the production process, includes productive consumption." 3. "the return of money to its starting-point makes the movement M…M' a cyclical movement complete in itself."

"On the one hand, therefore, each individual capital, in the two halves of its circulation M–C and C'–M', is an agent of the general circulation of commodities, in which it functions and of which it forms a link, either as money or as commodity. Hence it is a member of the general series of metamorphoses of the commodity world. On the other hand, it describes its own independent circuit within the general circulation, one in which the sphere of production forms a transitional stage, and in which it returns to its starting-point in the same form in which it left it. Within its own circuit, which includes its real metamorphosis in the production process, the magnitude of its value also changes. It returns not only as money value, but as increased and expanded money value."

The Four Defining Features of M...M'

Marx then lays out four features that distinguish the circuit of money capital from the others.

1. Money as starting-point and point of return. "It appears as the circuit of money capital because industrial capital in its money form, as money capital, forms the starting-point and the point of return of the whole process. The formula itself expresses that the money is not spent here as money, but is only advanced, and is thus simply the money form of capital, money capital. It further expresses the fact that it is the exchange-value, not the use-value, that is the decisive inherent purpose of the movement. It is precisely because the money form of value is its independent and palpable form of appearance that the circulation form M…M', which starts and finishes with actual money, expresses money-making, the driving motive of capitalist production, most palpably. The production process appears simply as an unavoidable middle term, a necessary evil for the purpose of money-making."

A memorable sentence: "(This explains why all nations characterized by the capitalist mode of production are periodically seized by fits of giddiness in which they try to accomplish the money-making without the mediation of the production process.)" — flagged in an editorial footnote as a sentence introduced by Engels in the second (1893) edition. This is a jab at speculative bubbles and financial manias.

2. Production as mere means. "In this circuit, the stage of production, the function of P, forms an interruption in the circulation process M–C…C'–M', whose two phases are in turn only a mediation of simple circulation M–C–M'. The production process here appears formally and explicitly, in the actual form of the circuit itself, for what it actually is in the capitalist mode of production, a mere means for the valorization of the value advanced; i.e. enrichment as such appears as the inherent purpose of production."

3. The role of money as independent form of value. "Because the sequence of phases is opened by M–C, C'–M' is the second term in the circulation; the starting-point is M, the money capital to be valorized, the conclusion M', the valorized money capital M + m, in which M figures alongside its offshoot m as realized capital. This distinguishes the circuit of money capital from the two other circuits P and C', and in two ways. On the one hand, through the money form of the two extremes; money is the independent and palpable form of existence of value, the value of the product in its independent value form, in which all trace of the commodities' use-value has been effaced. On the other hand, the form P…P does not necessarily become P…P' (P + p), while in the form C'…C', no value difference at all is visible between the two extremes." — So M...M' is unique in that the value-increase is visible right at the extremes: M ends as M + m; whereas in P...P the same capital (P) might return as P (without surplus shown); and in C'...C' the same C' both begins and ends, hiding any value difference.

"It is thus characteristic of the formula M…M', on the one hand, that the capital value forms the starting-point and the valorized capital the point of return, so that the advancing of the capital value appears as the means, the valorized capital value as the goal of the whole operation; on the other hand, that this relation is expressed in the money form, the independent value form, hence money capital as money breeding money. The creation by value of surplus-value is not only expressed as the alpha and omega of the process, but explicitly presented in the glittering money form."

4. Accumulation and the silence on consumption. "Since M', the money capital realized as the result of C'–M', the complementary and concluding phase of M–C, exists in absolutely the same form as that in which it opened its first circuit, it can, as it emerges from this, reopen the same circuit as augmented (accumulated) money capital, M' = M + m; at least it is in no way expressed in the form of M…M' that the circulation of m separates itself from that of M when the circuit is repeated. Considered by itself in isolation, from the formal standpoint, the circuit of money capital thus expresses only the process of valorization and accumulation. Consumption, therefore, is expressed in it only as productive consumption, $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$; this is all that is accounted for in this circuit of the individual capital."

Then the crucial argument about worker and capitalist consumption. "M–L is L–M or C–M from the point of view of the worker, i.e. the first phase of the circulation that mediates his individual consumption: L–M–C (means of subsistence). The second phase, M–C, no longer falls within the circuit of the individual capital; but it is introduced by it and presupposed by it, for the worker, in order to continue to exist on the market as exploitable material for the capitalist, must before all else keep alive, and therefore maintain himself by individual consumption. This consumption itself, however, is assumed here only as a precondition for the productive consumption of labour-power by capital, thus only in so far as the worker maintains and reproduces himself as labour-power by his individual consumption. The means of production (mp), however, the actual commodities that are involved in the circuit, are simply the means of nourishment for productive consumption. The act L–M mediates the individual consumption of the worker, the transformation of means of subsistence into his flesh and blood. But the capitalist must also exist, thus also live and consume, in order to function as capitalist. In actual fact, he needs to consume only as a worker, and hence no more than this is assumed in this form of the circulation process. But even this is not expressed formally, since the formula closes with M', i.e. a result that can function again immediately as increased money capital."

"C'–M' directly contains the sale of C'; but C'–M', which is from one side a sale, is M–C, a purchase, from the other side, and in the last instance commodities are bought only for the sake of their use-value (we ignore intermediate transactions here), in order to enter the process of consumption, either individual or productive, according to the nature of the article bought. This consumption, however, does not enter the circuit of the individual capital of which C' is the product; the product C' is precisely ejected from the circuit as a commodity to be sold. It is expressly destined for the consumption of others."

Then the mercantilist illustration. "We therefore find among the exponents of the Mercantile System (which is based on the formula M–C…P…C'–M') long sermons to the effect that the individual capitalist should consume only in his capacity as a worker, and that a capitalist nation should leave the consumption of its commodities and the consumption process in general to other more stupid nations, while making productive consumption into its own life's work. These sermons are often reminiscent in both form and content of analogous ascetic exhortations by the Fathers of the Church."

A footnote identifies the source of this view: D'Avenant, An Essay on the East-India Trade, London, 1697, quoted in Theories of Surplus-Value, Part I, p. 179: "By what is consum'd at Home, one loseth only what another gets, and the Nation in General is not at all the Richer; but all Foreign Consumption is a clear and certain Profit." — So Marx is targeting the mercantilist obsession with exports and the glorification of production-in-general over consumption.

The Circuit as Unity of Circulation and Production

This passage, likely from one of the later manuscripts, reads: "The circuit of capital is thus a unified process of circulation and production, it includes both. In so far as the two phases M–C and C'–M' are processes of circulation, the circulation of capital forms part of the general circulation of commodities. But by taking part in functionally determined sections or stages in the circuit of capital, which do not just pertain to the sphere of circulation, but also to that of production, capital performs its own circuit within the general circulation of commodities. This general circulation enables it, in the first stage, to assume the form in which it can function as productive capital; in the second stage, to cast off the commodity function in which it cannot renew its circuit; it equally gives it the possibility of separating its own capital circuit from the circulation of the surplus-value that has adhered to it."

The Primacy and Persistence of the Circuit of Money Capital

Marx now asserts that the circuit of money capital is the most characteristic form of appearance — and then immediately warns about its limits. "The circuit of money capital is thus the most one-sided, hence most striking and characteristic form of appearance of the circuit of industrial capital, in which its aim and driving motive — the valorization of value, money-making and accumulation — appears in a form that leaps to the eye (buying in order to sell dearer). The fact that the first phase is M–C displays the provenance of the components of productive capital on the commodity market. It also shows that the capitalist production process is conditioned by circulation, trade. The circuit of money capital is not just commodity production; it only comes into being by way of circulation, and presupposes this."

"It is shown by the fact that the form M pertaining to circulation appears as the first and pure form of the capital value advanced, which is not the case with the two other forms of the circuit."

"The circuit of money capital remains the permanent general expression of industrial capital, in so far as it always includes the valorization of the value advanced. In P…P, the money expression of the capital emerges only as the price of the elements of production, thus only as value expressed in money of account, the form in which it is found in book-keeping."

"M…M' becomes a particular form of the circuit of industrial capital in so far as newly appearing capital is first advanced as money and is withdrawn in the same form, whether on its transfer from one branch of business to another, or when industrial capital is withdrawn from business altogether. This includes the capital function of the surplus-value first advanced in the money form, and emerges most strikingly when this functions in a business other than that from which it originates. M…M' can be the first circuit of a capital, it can be its last; it can be taken as the form of the total social capital; it is the form of capital that is newly invested, whether as newly accumulated capital in the money form, or old capital that is completely transformed into money in order to be transferred from one branch of production to another."

The Money Form as Constitutive of the Wage Relation

"As a form that is comprised in all circuits, money capital performs this circuit precisely for that part of the capital that creates surplus-value, the variable capital. The normal form of advance for wages is payment in money; this process must be steadily repeated at short intervals, as the worker lives from hand to mouth. Hence the worker must constantly come face to face with the capitalist as money capitalist, and with his capital as money capital. Here there can be no question, as in the purchase of means of production and the sale of productive commodities, of a direct or indirect balancing of accounts (so that the greater part of money capital actually figures only in the form of commodities, money only in the form of money of account, and finally cash only for the settlement of the balances)."

This is an important structural remark: for fixed capital and circulating capital transactions (purchases of machinery and sales of outputs), the money-form is often only bookkeeping — accounts are netted out, cash plays a residual role. But for wages, this is impossible: the worker needs cash on the nail, at short intervals, because he lives hand-to-mouth. So money capital as actually circulating coin remains structurally necessary, at least for the variable capital portion.

"On the other hand, a part of the surplus-value arising from the variable capital is spent by the capitalist for his personal consumption; this pertains to the retail trade, and, after however roundabout a journey, is ultimately spent as cash, in the money form of the surplus-value. Whether this part of the surplus-value is great or small in no way affects the matter. The variable capital constantly appears anew as money capital invested in wages (M–L), and m as surplus-value that is spent to defray the personal needs of the capitalist. Thus both M, as the variable capital value advanced, and m, as its increment, are necessarily retained in the money form, to be spent as such."

The Deceptive/Illusory Character and Mercantile Ancestry

"The formula M–C…P…C'–M', with the result M' = M + m, contains in its form a certain deception; it bears an illusory character that derives from the existence of the advanced and valorized value in its equivalent form, in money. What is emphasized is not the valorization of the value, but the money form of this process, the fact that more value in the money form is finally withdrawn from the circulation sphere than was originally advanced to it, i.e. the increase in the mass of gold and silver belonging to the capitalist."

The ideological consequences are two. "The so-called Monetary System is simply the expression of the superficial form M–C–M', a movement that proceeds exclusively in the circulation sphere, and hence can only explain the two acts (1) M–C and (2) C–M' by saying that C in the second act is sold above its value, and therefore withdraws more money from the circulation sphere than was cast into it by its purchase. On the other hand, however, M–C…P…C'–M', when regarded as the exclusive form, is the basis for the more developed Mercantile System, in which it is not simply the circulation of commodities but also their production that appears as a necessary element."

A footnote clarifies the historical relation: "The Monetary System (sometimes called bullionism) preceded the Mercantile System or mercantilism. Marx describes the Mercantile System as a 'variant' of the Monetary System. He distinguishes the two most clearly in the Grundrisse (Pelican edition, p. 327): 'The Monetary System had understood the autonomy of value only in the form in which it arose from simple circulation — money… Then came the Mercantile System, an epoch where industrial capital and hence wage labour arose in manufactures… The Mercantilists already have faint notions of money as capital, but actually again only in the form of money, of the circulation of mercantile capital.'"

So the ideological forms of pre-classical political economy — bullionism and mercantilism — arose from taking aspects of the circuit of money capital as the essence of capital rather than as one of its forms. Bullionism fetishises money as the form of value; mercantilism recognises production but still locates the source of profit in exchange (selling dearer).

"The illusory character of M–C…P…C'–M', and the corresponding illusory significance it is given, is there as soon as this form is regarded as the sole form, not as one that flows and is constantly repeated; i.e. as soon as it is taken not just as one of the forms of the circuit, but rather as its exclusive form. In itself, however, it refers to other forms."

The Four "Points" and the Return to P...P

Marx now supplies four arguments for the thesis that the money-form is not, in the last instance, primary.

Firstly, this circuit presupposes capitalist production and its social relations. "M–C = $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$, but M–L implies that the wage-labourer, and therefore the means of production too, are a part of the productive capital; hence the labour and valorization process, the production process is already a function of capital."

Secondly, the money form is evanescent. "If M…M' is repeated, the return to the money form appears just as evanescent as the money form in the first stage. M–C vanishes, in order to make way for P. The permanently repeated advance in money, as well as its permanent return in money, themselves appear simply as evanescent moments in the circuit."

Thirdly, the developmental sequence shows the priority of productive capital. "M–C…P…C'–M'. M–C…P…C'–M'. M–C…P… etc. With the second repetition of the circuit, we already have the circuit P…C'–M'. M–C…P, before the second circuit of M is even complete, and thus all further circuits can be considered in the form P…C'–M'–C…P; M–C, therefore, as the first phase of the first circuit, simply forms an evanescent prelude to the constantly repeated circuit of productive capital, as is in fact the case when industrial capital is invested for the first time, in the form of money capital. Furthermore, before the second circuit is complete, the first circuit C'–M'…M–C…P…C' (abbreviated C'…C') has been described, the circuit of commodity capital. Thus the first form already contains the two others, and the money form vanishes, in so far as it is not just an expression of value, but an expression of value in the equivalent form, in money."

This is a critical observation: as soon as the circuit is repeated, the money form becomes evanescent. After the first cycle, capital is already in productive or commodity form when the next cycle begins; M–C is only ever the first phase of a virgin circuit or a transitional phase when capital shifts branches.

Finally, even for a newly invested capital, M–C is posited by the production process, not the other way around. "Let us take a newly appearing individual capital, which describes the circuit M–C…P…C'–M' for the first time: M–C is a preparatory phase, the precursor of the first production process performed by this individual capital. This phase M–C is therefore not the presupposition, but is rather posited or conditioned by the production process. However, this holds only for this individual capital."

But at the level of society, the general form still appears as M…M': "The general form of the circuit of industrial capital is the circuit of money capital, in so far as the capitalist mode of production is presupposed, i.e. within a specific state of society determined by capitalist production. Hence the capitalist production process is the basic pre-condition, it is prior to all else, if not within the first circuit of the money capital of a newly invested industrial capital, then outside it; the continued existence of this production process assumes the constantly repeated circuit of P…P. This assumption is already made within the first stage $M - C \binom{L}{mp}$, in so far as this stage presupposes on the one hand the existence of the class of wage-labourers, and on the other hand what is the first stage M–C for the purchaser of the means of production, and C'–M' for their seller. It presupposes, therefore, that C' is commodity capital, and therefore that the commodity itself is the result of capitalist production; with this we must also presuppose the function of productive capital."

The chapter thus ends by dissolving the apparent primacy of the money form. When we look only at an individual newly-invested capital, M–C looks like the beginning; but at the level of society, capital production is the presupposition (the wage-labour class must already exist, the means of production must already be capital, the commodities must already be capitalist products). Therefore the circuit of productive capital (P…P) is logically and historically prior. But the appearance — the form in which capital presents itself to consciousness — is M…M'. The chapter closes on this dialectical tension: appearance and essence are not identical, and the analyst's task is to trace the mediations that connect them.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Fundamental Principles Revealed

1. Capital is a movement, not a thing. The chapter's deepest lesson is anthropologico-philosophical as much as economic: capital is not a stock of assets or a sum of money but a process — a self-reproducing circuit that assumes, discards, and re-assumes forms. Money, commodities, and machines are not "capital" by nature; they become capital only through their role in the circuit. "The means of production are no more capital by nature than is human labour-power. They receive this specific social character only under certain particular conditions that have historically developed, just as it is only under such conditions that precious metals are stamped with the character of money, or money with that of money capital." This is the theoretical death-blow to any position that treats capital as a trans-historical category or a physical property of things.

2. The three forms of capital are functional forms of one thing — industrial capital. Money capital, productive capital, and commodity capital are not species but modes of existence of industrial capital. Their apparent independence (as banks, factories, merchants) arises only through the social division of labour, which has "rendered them independent and one-sidedly extended." Capitalism's financial and commercial sectors are not add-ons to industry but the formally separated functions of money capital and commodity capital, subordinate to industrial capital once it dominates.

3. The circuit is simultaneously a unity and a duality. M–C is one act but splits into M–L and M–mp, on different markets. C'–M' is one act but is a final metamorphosis for capital value and a first metamorphosis for surplus-value. The circuit is one movement but contains three distinct stages and three distinct forms. The dialectical structure of unity-in-difference governs both the analytic categories and the material process.

4. Production is the site of the real metamorphosis; circulation only affords formal metamorphoses. Value-change occurs in P, not in M–C or C'–M'. The circulation acts merely transform the form of value; they cannot create it. This is the chapter's most basic refutation of any explanation of profit from exchange alone — a critique aimed at both the Monetary System (profit as selling dearer) and the Mercantile System (profit as exchange with productive auxiliary).

5. The class relation is presupposed in the money relation. The famous sentence — "The capital relation arises only in the production process because it exists implicitly in the act of circulation, in the basically different economic conditions in which buyer and seller confront one another, in their class relation" — establishes that the market's apparent equality of contracting parties rests on an underlying inequality of property. The "distribution" that matters is not distribution of consumer goods but distribution of the means of production themselves. The Russian landowners' complaints illustrate that where the separation of producers from means of production is incomplete, wage labour cannot be fully generalised.

6. The circuits imply each other. M…M' is not external to P…P and C'…C'; it contains them and is contained by them. Once the circuit is repeated, "M–C is simply an evanescent prelude to the constantly repeated circuit of productive capital," and "before the second circuit is complete, the first circuit C'…C' has been described." The three circuits are not alternative descriptions but aspects of one self-differentiating totality.

Broader Practical, Theoretical, and Philosophical Implications

For the critique of political economy. The chapter is a systematic dismantling of the ideological forms thrown up by capital itself. The Monetary System and Mercantile System are not merely intellectual errors but necessary appearances generated by the money form. Their mistake is not arbitrary but structural: they mistake the most visible form of the circuit for its essence. Marx's method here is genealogical — he shows how a category (money as capital) acquires an autonomous existence in thought through a real, material process (the social division of labour making money capital a separate branch of business). This anticipates the theory of fetishism and the critique of ideology in its full form.

For the theory of crises and cycles. The chapter plants several seeds. (a) The structural dependence of capital on continuously selling C' introduces the possibility of realisation crises: if the commodity cannot be sold, "unsaleable stocks of commodities obstruct the flow of circulation" and production stands still. (b) The "fettering" of capital in each phase (money hoarding, idle means of production, unsold commodities) creates frictions that can only be resolved by further expansion or crisis. (c) The dependence of surplus-value realisation on the volume sold introduces a structural over-production tendency: capital must keep selling everything to realise its surplus. (d) The special role of the wage form in requiring cash means that a shortage of money (or credit) can paralyse production even when value-producing potential is present — a theme of the "monetary" theory of crises.

For the theory of accumulation. The circuit's closure with M' = M + m makes accumulation (the re-advance of m as capital) structurally possible and, under competition, necessary. But the formal circuit silences the question of consumption — both the worker's and the capitalist's. This silence is not accidental; it is a structural feature of the money-form, which effaces every trace of the process. The chapter thus sets up the later problem (in Volume 3 and in Rosa Luxemburg's and others' critiques) of whether a fully-capitalist society can absorb its own product, or whether capital needs external markets.

For the theory of turnover and time. The caveat about fixed capital (machines transferring value "bit by bit") and the emphasis on circulation speed point forward to Volume 2's later chapters on turnover. The chapter argues explicitly that "the circulation process sets in motion new forces independent of the magnitude of value, which affect the degree of effectiveness of the capital, its expansion and its contraction." The same capital value, turned over more rapidly, produces more surplus-value per annum than a slower-turning capital. Time, no less than magnitude and composition, is a determinant of capital productivity.

For the theory of financialisation. Marx's depiction of the "fits of giddiness" in which nations "try to accomplish the money-making without the mediation of the production process," combined with the analysis of money breeding money in M…M' and the social independence of money capital as a branch of business, anticipates a theory of speculative manias and of the financial system's tendency to detach from production. The chapter does not develop such a theory but provides its essential categories.

For historiography. The chapter offers a materialist theory of historical epochs: "The particular form and mode in which this connection [of workers and means of production] is effected is what distinguishes the various economic epochs of the social structure." This is the framework within which primitive accumulation, the transition to capitalism, and the dissolution of pre-capitalist forms (illustrated by world trade with China, India, and the Arabs, and by the Russian land question) are to be understood. The chapter stresses that the separation of workers from means of production is not produced by capitalism originally but is presupposed by it; yet capitalist production reproduces and expands the separation at ever greater scale. This is the basis for the theory that capitalism tends to universalise itself by destroying every alternative mode of production it encounters.

For philosophical anthropology. The chapter makes an implicit but powerful claim about the human condition under capital: the wage-labourer's "labour-power" exists "in a state of separation from the means of production" and "can be directly used neither for the production of use-values for its possessor, nor for the production of commodities which he could live from selling." Only when sold does it become productive. The worker's very capacity to labour is a commodity whose use-value can only be realised by being handed over to another. There is a profound alienation implicit in this — the worker's own activity becomes "an organ of capital," and the product is "a commodity impregnated with surplus-value" whose surplus-value "costs [the capitalist] no equivalent." The chapter does not use the word "alienation," but it is working out the material anatomy of the alienated relation.

For the critique of dogmatism. Marx's warning against the "crude, non-conceptual" character of money and his insistence that the relation, not the thing, is primary is a methodological lesson in how to analyse social forms. Money is not the cause of the class relation; the class relation makes money into a function of capital. Slavery is not caused by the existence of money; money can be spent on slaves only if slavery already exists. Throughout the chapter, Marx insists that forms of consciousness — the categories of political economy — are conditioned by the material processes they express, and that the direction of explanation must always run from relations to forms, not the reverse.

Final Reflections

Chapter 1 of Volume 2 is a masterpiece of dialectical exposition. It takes a deceptively simple formula (M–C…P…C'–M') and unfolds from it the entire anatomy of capital as a self-reproducing process. The chapter's method is relentless abstraction: it strips away changes of value, irregularities of sale, consumer preferences, state intervention, and everything else that would obscure the pure forms of movement. Having isolated those forms, it interrogates each one — money capital, productive capital, commodity capital — to reveal the social relations each presupposes and expresses.

The chapter's intellectual legacy is substantial. It established the framework within which the entire circulation process of capital would be analysed — turnover, reproduction, credit, and crises. It set the terms for later debates about financialisation, realisation crises, and the autonomy of circulation. And it produced some of Marx's most important formulations: the class relation as presupposed in the money relation, the distribution of the elements of production as the fundamental "distribution," the real versus formal metamorphoses, and the distinction between the circuit's vivid appearance (money making money) and its concealed essence (production of surplus-value by labour-power). It is a chapter that demands slow reading — every paragraph contains a hinge on which the argument turns — and it repays that reading, because it shows how capital's most superficial appearance and its most fundamental structure are connected through a determinate series of mediations. To understand this chapter is to understand why political economy, even at its best, tends to mistake the form of appearance for the essence of capital: because capital itself, in its normal motion, produces exactly that illusion.
