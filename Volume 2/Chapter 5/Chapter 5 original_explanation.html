Volume 2 • Chapter 5: The Time of Circulation

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 5 of Capital Volume 2, drawn (as the opening note indicates) from Marx's Manuscript IV, occupies a decisive hinge-position in the architecture of the second volume. Having in preceding chapters established the circuit of capital as a movement through three phases — money capital, productive capital, commodity capital — and having shown that this circuit is not a single event but a repetitious, continuous process, Marx must now reckon precisely with the time structure of that movement. The chapter's central object is the temporal division of the circuit into two analytically distinct but materially fused durations: production time (the time capital spends in the production sphere) and circulation time (the time it spends in the sphere of circulation). Its thesis is that the total time of the circuit — the turnover — is the sum of these two, and that this sum is not a neutral aggregate. It has a singular and asymmetrical consequence: circulation time is a pure deduction from production time, and therefore a negative limit upon the creation of surplus-value.

The chapter's intellectual objective is threefold. First, to decompose "production time" itself, demonstrating that it is by no means identical with working time — the time during which labour actually acts on its object. Marx shows that production time includes: the functioning of means of labour across repeated labour processes (including their mere presence at the site of production when idle); the holding of reserve stocks of raw and ancillary materials; and — crucially — the natural, labourless intervals in which the object of labour is left to undergo physical or chemical transformations (fermentation, ripening, tanning). Second, to establish the valorization-theoretic consequence of this divergence: wherever production time exceeds working time — wherever productive capital exists latently or functions without absorbing labour — capital absorbs no living labour, and therefore no surplus labour, and therefore valorizes nothing. This yields the corollary that the higher the ratio of working time to production time, the greater the self-valorization of a given capital in a given period, and that capitalist production has a systematic tendency to compress the excess of production time over working time (hence its drive toward night work, continuous processes, etc.).

Third — and this is the chapter's most subtle and polemical dimension — Marx attacks the appearance that circulation time is a source of value. Because the effect of circulation time on valorization is negative but its results (higher prices for capitals with longer turnover, the apparent growth of capital in the circulation sphere during accumulation) appear positive, political economy and even "scientific economics" are deceived into treating the circulation sphere as a mystical, autonomous fount of self-valorization independent of the exploitation of labour. Marx enumerates three phenomena sustaining this illusion: the profit calculus that converts a negative into a positive basis for price; the fact that circulation time is only one moment of turnover; and the fact that the transformation of commodities into variable capital occurs in circulation.

This chapter thus sits at the intersection of the volume's theory of turnover (developed in the adjacent chapters) and Volume 1's theory of surplus-value. It is the point at which the time-structure of capital's circuit is shown to be an economic form that both expresses and conceals the extraction of surplus labour — and it lays the groundwork for the eventual turnover chapter, the analysis of the annual rate of surplus-value, and the critique of the trinity formula whereby capital, land, and labour appear as independent sources of value.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Founding Definition: The Circuit as the Sum of Two Times

Marx opens by recalling the established framework: capital moves successively "through the production sphere and the two phases of the circulation sphere," and these movements are "accomplished successively in time." They are, that is, not simultaneous but sequential — capital cannot be in production and in circulation at the same instant (a claim that becomes central later). From this sequentiality Marx extracts the foundational equation:

Total circuit time = production time + circulation time

The "duration of its stay in the production sphere forms its production time," and that in the circulation sphere forms its circulation time. Two phases of the circulation sphere are noted — the transformation of commodity into money (C–M) and money into commodity (M–C) — so that circulating capital undergoes two metamorphoses. This simple additive formula is the chapter's scaffolding: the whole problem is that the two terms are not homogeneous in their relationship to value, and that the second term exerts an entirely one-sided pressure on the first.

2.2 Production Time ≠ Working Time: The Means of Labour and the Reserve Stock

The first substantive movement of the chapter is the decomposition of production time. "The production time includes, of course, the period of the labour process; but this is not all." Marx supplies two reasons why production time overruns working time even before we reach the case of natural processes.

First, the fixity of means of labour. A part of constant capital exists in means of labour — "machines, buildings, etc." — that serve "for constant repetitions of the same labour process until they are worn out." A nightly interruption of the labour process interrupts the function of these means of labour but does not remove them from the place of production: "They belong to this not only when they function, but also when they do not function." Here we meet the important distinction between a machine's functioning and its mere sojourn in the production sphere. It is present as productive capital, but not actively engaged.

Second, the reserve stock. The capitalist must hold "a certain stock of raw and ancillary materials, so that the production process can keep going for shorter or longer intervals on the previously determined scale, without depending on the accidents of daily supply on the market." This reserve is "only gradually consumed productively." It is therefore capital that is already productive capital (it is in the production sphere, in the hands of capital, destined for production) but is not yet in the production process. Marx introduces the critical term here: such capital is latent or idle — it "acts neither to form products nor values," yet "its idleness forms a condition for the uninterrupted flow of the production process."

Thus the production time of means of production comprises, in Marx's threefold enumeration: (1) the time during which they function as means of production; (2) the pauses in which the production process (and hence their functioning) is interrupted; and (3) the time during which they are held in reserve as conditions of the process, already representing productive capital but not yet engaged in it. The footnote clarifies that "production time" is here used in an active sense: "the production time of the means of production is not the time that it takes to produce them, but that for which they participate in the production process of a commodity product."

2.3 The Second Divergence: Interruptions Internal to the Production Process

Having established the divergence between capital's stay in the production sphere and its engagement in the production process, Marx now introduces a distinctly different and more important divergence — one internal to the production process itself. The production process "may itself involve interruptions of the labour process and hence of working time, intervals in which the object of labour is exposed to the action of physical processes, without further addition of human labour."

The examples are canonical and later become textbook material: corn that is sown, wine that ferments in the cellar, and material of labour exposed to chemical processes as in tanning. In these cases the production process — and hence the function of the means of production — continues even though the labour process (and hence the function of the means of production as means of labour) is suspended. Here:

Production time > Working time

This excess "is always based on the fact that the productive capital exists in a latent state in the production sphere, without functioning in the production process itself, or that it functions in the production process without being involved in the labour process."

This is a pivotal conceptual distinction with two limbs: capital can be (a) in the production sphere but not in the process (latent reserve), or (b) in the production process but not in the labour process (the natural-process interlude). Both give rise to the same excess — production time over working time — but through different mechanisms. Marx keeps them analytically separate because their treatment differs slightly in the next beat.

2.4 The Anatomy of Latent Capital: Idle Reserve, Storage Apparatus, and the Two Value-Channels

Marx now performs a fine-grained disambiguation of the latent capital, distinguishing parts of it according to whether and how they bear on value.

Part one — the pure reserve (cotton, coal in the spinning mill). This part "acts neither to form products nor values. It is idle capital." It will shortly enter the process, but for now it is quiescent and value-neutral.

The storage apparatus. The buildings and apparatus necessary for storing the reserve (i.e. for maintaining the latent capital in its preliminary stage) are themselves "conditions of the production process and hence form components of the productive capital advanced." They fulfil their function "by maintaining the productive components in the preliminary stage." They make the raw material "dearer" (they add to the cost of the raw material) — but Marx immediately makes a value-theoretic move peculiar to his labour theory: "since a part of this labour, in the same way as a part of all other wage-labour, is not paid for, it is productive labour and creates surplus-value." In other words, the labour that builds and maintains the storage apparatus is exploited wage-labour (only partly paid), and is therefore value- and surplus-value-creating. The storing labour is productive in the strict Marxist sense even though the stored stock is idle.

The interruptions. "The normal interruptions of the overall production process, i.e. the intervals in which the productive capital does not function, produce neither value nor surplus-value." These are the regular pauses (nights, weekends). Because they are unproductive of value, capital has an incentive to eliminate them — hence "the drive towards night work" (cross-referenced to Volume 1, Chapter 10, §4). This is one of the first bridges in the chapter between an abstract temporal category and a concrete historical tendency of capitalist production.

The natural-process intervals within working time. "The intervals in the working time that the object of labour has itself to undergo during the production process create neither value nor surplus-value; but they further the product, form a part of its life, a process that it must pass through." So the corn ripening or the hide tanning consumes production time without adding value — yet it is necessary to the product's becoming. This is the crucial paradox: unproductive time that is nonetheless an obligatory condition of the productive result.

Marx then clarifies the value-transfer mechanism: "The value of the apparatus, etc. is carried over to the product in proportion to the entire period during which it functions." The point is that the object of labour is placed in the required state "by labour itself," and the use of the apparatus is "just as much a condition of production as the reduction to dust of a part of the cotton that does not go into the product, but still carries its value over to it." Here Marx uses an analogy from the previous chapter: just as a portion of cotton is reduced to dust and does not enter the product but still transfers its value to it, so the use of the whole apparatus during the natural-process interval transfers value even though labour is suspended — because the apparatus's employment was mediated by labour.

Part two — means of labour interrupted only by regular pauses (buildings, machines). The "other part of the latent capital" — "the means of labour whose function is interrupted only by the regular pauses in the production process" — "adds value, without entering into the formation of the product." Crucial qualifier: "irregular interruptions as a result of a restriction of production, crises, etc. are pure loss." So normal pauses are absorbed into the average life of the machine and thus into value transfer, while abnormal pauses (crises) are uncompensated losses. Marx then delivers a precise and often-missed formulation:

"The total value that the means of labour add to the product is determined by the average length of their life; they lose value because they lose use-value, not only in the time during which they are functioning, but also in the time during which they are not."

This is a subtle but essential point: wear-and-tear includes the wasting that happens when machines sit idle, provided the idleness is normal. Value depreciation tracks the loss of use-value across the machine's whole life, not just the hours of operation.

2.5 The Heart of the Matter: Why the Excess Yields No Valorization

Marx now draws the central conclusion for the chapter — the pivot around which the entire analysis turns. "Finally, the value of that part of the constant capital that continues in the production process even when the labour process is interrupted appears once again in the result of the production process." The means of production are placed by labour itself into conditions where they "undergo by themselves certain specific natural processes, the result of which is a specific useful effect or a changed form of their use-value." Labour carries over the value of the means of production to the product "to the extent that it actually consumes these deliberately as means of production."

Then the crucial value-theoretic theorem: "Nothing is altered here by whether the labour must, through the means of labour, act continuously on the object of labour, in order to produce this effect, or whether it need only give the first impulse by placing the means of production in conditions in which they themselves undergo the intended alteration, without labour's further collaboration, as a result of natural processes."

This is the answer to a possible objection: someone might think that if a machine only initiates the natural process (as opposed to operating throughout), it is somehow less of a value-transmitter. Marx denies this: the transmissibility of value depends on labour's deliberate consumption of the means of production, not on the duration of labour's action.

From this follows the theorem on valorization: "Whatever may be the reason for the excess of production time over working time... in none of these cases do the means of production function to absorb labour. If they absorb no labour, then they absorb no surplus labour. Hence there is no valorization of the productive capital, as long as this finds itself in that part of its production time that is in excess of the working time, no matter how inseparable these pauses may be from the accomplishment of the valorization process."

This is the core statement. The logic is syllogistic: no absorption of labour → no absorption of surplus labour → no valorization. The excess production time is necessary for production but is value-theoretically sterile. Hence Marx's proportionality theorem:

The nearer production time and working time approach equality, the greater the productivity and valorization of a given productive capital in a given space of time.

This yields the tendency of capitalist production to shorten as much as possible the excess of production time over working time. Yet Marx immediately cautions that the excess cannot be eliminated: "although the production time of capital may diverge from its working time, it always includes the latter, and the excess itself is a condition of the production process." Production time is thus defined as "the time that the capital takes to produce use-values and valorize itself, hence to function as productive capital, although it includes time in which it is either latent or produces without being valorized." The category is therefore broader than valorization, but internally ordered by it.

2.6 Entering the Circulation Sphere: Metamorphosis Without Mystification

Having completed the analysis of production time, Marx turns to circulation time. His first move is a de-mystifying one: within the circulation sphere, capital exists "as commodity capital and money capital," and its two circulation processes are its transformation from commodity form to money form and from money form to commodity form. The crucial point is that even though C–M (in this context C'–M') is also the realization of surplus-value, and M–C is also the reconversion of capital value into the form of its production elements, this "in no way changes the fact that these processes, as processes of circulation, are processes of simple commodity metamorphosis." Whatever additional capital-specific function these acts serve, considered as circulation they are no different from the plain metamorphoses studied in Volume 1, Chapter 3. This sets up the argument that circulation time as such is not a value-creating process.

2.7 The Mutual Exclusivity of Circulation Time and Production Time: The Negative Limit

Marx states flatly: "Circulation time and production time are mutually exclusive. During its circulation time, capital does not function as productive capital, and therefore produces neither commodities nor surplus-value." This is the theorem that the two times cannot overlap for the same aliquot portion of capital.

He then examines the simplest case: "if we consider the circuit in its simplest form, so that the entire capital value always moves at one stroke from one phase to the other, then it is obvious that the production process is interrupted, and with it therefore the self-valorization of capital, so long as its circulation time lasts." In this simplest case, the entire capital is periodically frozen — it produces value only in the production interval and lies fallow in the circulation interval, so lengthening circulation time simply shortens the productive fraction of the circuit.

Then he introduces the more realistic case of successive phases across aliquot parts: "If the various parts of the capital pass through the circuit in succession, so that the circuit of the total capital value is successively accomplished in the circuit of its various portions, then it is clear that the longer its aliquot parts remain in the circulation sphere, the smaller must be the part that functions at any time in the production sphere." So even with a divided capital — some parts in production, others in circulation at any moment — the fraction in production shrinks as circulation time grows.

From this Marx derives the negative limit theorem: "The expansion and contraction of the circulation time hence acts as a negative limit on the contraction or expansion of the production time, or of the scale on which a capital of a given magnitude can function." And the limit approaches zero when circulation is idealized:

The more that the circulation metamorphoses of capital are only ideal, i.e. the closer the circulation time comes to zero, the more the capital functions, and the greater is its productivity and self-valorization.

The limiting illustration: "If a capitalist works to order, receives payment on the delivery of his product, and is paid in his own means of production, then his time of circulation approaches zero." In that ideal case, capital is almost continuously productive, and valorization is at its maximum.

2.8 The Great Illusion: How a Negative Effect Appears as Positive

The chapter now makes its polemical turn. "Capital's circulation time generally restricts its production time, and hence its valorization process. Moreover, it restricts this in proportion to its duration." Restriction can vary enormously in degree. But — and here is the crux — political economy "sees is only the appearance, i.e. the effect of the circulation time on the valorization process of capital in general. It conceives this negative effect as positive, because its results are positive."

This is a formulation worthy of close attention. The effect of circulation time on valorization is negative (it subtracts from valorization). But the observable results of longer circulation time — higher prices, expanded accumulations in the circulation sphere — are positive quantities. Because the observable results are positive, the underlying cause is misread as positive. Political economy clings to this "illusion" all the more firmly because "it seems to provide it with the proof that capital possesses a mystical source of self-valorization that is independent of its production process and hence of the exploitation of labour, and derives rather from the sphere of circulation." Even "scientific economics" — a footnote clarifies this means classical political economy, cross-referenced to Volume 1, Chapter 1, §4, note 34 — is taken in by this illusion.

Marx then lists three phenomena that sustain and "confirm" the illusion:

(1) The capitalist calculation of profit. In the capitalist's calculus, "the negative reason appears as positive, in that with capitals in different spheres of investment, in which only the circulation times differ, longer circulation time is the basis for a higher price, in short, is one of the bases in the equalization of profits." This is a foreshadowing of the equalization of the general rate of profit: capitals tied up longer must (for the capitalist to deploy them at all) earn higher prices — which, when viewed superficially, looks like circulation time generating value rather than merely requiring compensation for value withheld.

(2) Circulation time as a moment of turnover. "The circulation time forms only one moment of the turnover time; but the latter includes the production time or reproduction time." When turnover is treated as the entire measure of capital's rhythm, circulation time is folded in with production time and the productive source of value is obscured.

(3) Accumulation and the conversion into variable capital. "The conversion of commodities into variable capital (wages) is conditioned by their previous transformation into money. In the case of capital accumulation, therefore, the conversion into additional variable capital takes place in the circulation sphere, or during the circulation time. Hence the accumulation arising therefrom appears to be due to the circulation time." Since additional variable capital requires that commodities first be sold (C–M), the passage through circulation appears to produce accumulation — a conclusion directly contradicting the labour theory of value.

2.9 The Anatomy of Circulation Time Itself: The Greater Weight of Sale

Marx then decomposes circulation time into its two constituent phases. Capital passes through the "two opposing phases C–M and M–C, in whichever order." Circulation time therefore "breaks down into two parts, the time needed for its transformation from commodity into money, and the time that it needs for its transformation from money into commodities."

Drawing on Volume 1, Chapter 3, Marx asserts that C–M, the sale, is "the most difficult part of its metamorphosis, and thus forms the greater part of the circulation time in normal circumstances." The reason is a form-of-value argument: "As money, value exists in its ever convertible form. As commodity, it must first receive this form of direct exchangeability and hence constant readiness for action by being transformed into money." Money is immediately exchangeable; the commodity is not — it must first be sold, i.e. it must find a buyer at an acceptable price. So the harder, longer phase is the sale.

The M–C phase involves "its transformation into those commodities which form the specific elements of productive capital in a given sphere of investment." Here Marx enumerates the contingencies that can lengthen M–C: means of production "may not be present on the market, needing first to be produced, or they may have to be drawn from distant markets," or there may be "dislocations in their normal supply, changes of price, etc." None of these circumstances is visible "in the simple change of form M–C," yet they materially determine its duration. Importantly, C–M and M–C "may also be separated in space, the selling and the buying markets being in different places. In factories, for example, buyers and sellers are frequently even different persons."

2.10 Circulation Agents and the Confusion of Functions

Because circulation is as necessary as production, "agents of circulation are just as necessary as agents of production." The reproduction process requires that both functions be represented — "either by the capitalist himself, or by salaried workers, his agents." But Marx resists any inference that this justifies confusing the categories. "This is just as little a reason for confusing the circulation agents with the production agents as it is a reason for confusing the functions of commodity capital and money capital with those of productive capital."

The relevant economic point: circulation agents must be paid by production agents. The labour of buying and selling is not itself is value-creating in the strict sense — it is faux frais, a deduction from the social product. Marx anticipates a standard objection: "if capitalists who buy and sell among themselves create by this act neither products nor value, this situation is not altered when the scale of their business enables them to pass this function onto others, and indeed makes it necessary to do so." In many businesses "sellers and buyers are paid in the form of a percentage of the profit." Then he dismisses the popular wheeze that "they are paid by the consumers": "The consumers can pay only in so far as they themselves produce, as agents of production, an equivalent in commodities, or alternatively appropriate this from the production agents, whether by a legal title (as their partners, etc.), or through personal services." Whatever the surface mechanism, the payment of circulation agents ultimately flows back to the production of value.

2.11 The Capitalist Determination of the Asymmetry Between Sale and Purchase

Marx then isolates a distinction between C–M and M–C that is not a mere matter of form. "In and for themselves, both C–M and M–C are mere translations of the given value from one form into the other. But C'–M' is at the same time the realization of the surplus-value contained in C'. Not so M–C." Consequently:

"The sale is more important than the purchase."

M–C "is in normal conditions a necessary act for the valorization of the value expressed in M, but it is not a realization of surplus-value; it is a prelude to its production, not an appendix to it." This clarifies that the two phases are asymmetric not only in difficulty (sale is harder) but in function: the sale realizes the surplus-value already produced; the purchase merely re-positions value for a new cycle. The purchase is thus prior to valorization in a logical sense; the sale is posterior to it. This asymmetry is a capitalist one — it exists because C' already carries surplus-value within it and M does not.

2.12 Perishability: The Absolute Barrier of Commodity Capital's Circulation

The chapter closes with a distinct and materially important argument: the use-value of the commodity imposes an absolute temporal limit on C'–M'. "The very form of existence of commodities, their existence as use-values, sets certain limits to the circulation of the commodity capital C'–M'." If commodities "do not enter into productive or individual consumption within a certain interval of time, according to their particular characteristics, in other words if they are not sold within a definite time, then they get spoiled, and lose, together with their use-value, the property of being bearers of exchange-value. Both the capital value contained in them and the surplus-value added to it are lost."

The essential dialectic here — and it is thoroughly Marxian — is that use-value is not irrelevant to exchange-value, but rather its material substrate. Use-values "remain the bearers of perennial and self-valorizing capital value only in so far as they are constantly renewed, are replaced by new use-values of the same or another kind." Sale — entry into consumption — "is however the constantly repeated condition for their reproduction." Commodities "must change their old use form within a certain time, and continue their existence in a new one. It is only through this constant renewal of its body that the exchange-value maintains itself."

The implication is that different commodities have different perishability gradients. "The use-values of different commodities may decay at different speeds; thus a greater or lesser interval may elapse between their production and their consumption, and they may thus persist for a shorter or longer time in the circulation phase C–M as commodity capital, endure a shorter or longer circulation time as commodities." Marx then names the general principle:

"The limitation of the circulation time of commodity capital imposed by the spoiling of the commodity body itself is the absolute limit of this part of the circulation time, or of the time for which the commodity capital can circulate as commodity capital."

From this Marx derives a series of geographic and industrial consequences. "The more perishable a commodity, the more directly after its production it must be consumed, and therefore sold, the smaller the distance it can move from its place of production, the narrower therefore is its sphere of spatial circulation, and the more local the character of its market." Hence: "the more perishable a commodity, the greater are the absolute barriers to its circulation time that its physical properties impose, and the less appropriate it is as an object of capitalist production."

This produces a dialectical reversal. Since capitalist production requires extended circulation (large markets, long distances), perishability is a structural obstacle to it. But capitalism finds countervailing instruments: "Capitalism can only deal in commodities of this kind in populous places, or to the extent that distances are reduced by the development of means of transport." And further: "The concentration of the production of an article in a few hands, however, and in a populous place, can create a relatively large market even for an article of this kind, as is the case with the big breweries, dairies, etc." So the natural barrier of the commodity body becomes a solvent driving concentration of production, urban agglomeration, and the development of the means of transport — the material underpinnings of the credit system, the transport revolution, and the large-scale industrial form. In this closing movement, Marx shows that even the "natural" properties of use-values are socially metabolized by specifically capitalist forces.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter's fundamental principle is that time is not neutral in the circuit of capital. Two temporal durations — production and circulation — are summed into a single turnover, but they enter that sum with opposite signs with respect to valorization. Production time contains working time (the sole source of surplus-value, through the absorption of labour), while circulation time replaces it, and is therefore subtracted from valorization. This asymmetry — the "negative limit" theorem — is the analytical core on which everything else turns.

From this core, the chapter yields several systemic lessons.

First, production time is a broader category than working time, and the gap between them is regulated by the material properties of use-values and by the imperatives of continuous production. Because reserve stocks must be held, natural processes must complete, and interruptions must be endured, capital's "production time" always overruns labour time. The paradox is that capital, whose sole aim is self-valorization, must waste production time precisely in order to valorize. It therefore has a systematic drive — the drive toward night work, toward accelerating natural processes by chemical and technical means, toward shortening supply chains — to close the gap. The mean ratio of working time to production time thereby becomes a concrete measure of the efficiency of valorization of a given capital, and the historical tendency of capitalist technology can be read as an assault on this gap.

Second, circulation time is a deduction, but appears as an addition. The chapter's most important critical move is to expose the fetishistic inversion: because circulation time lengthens the circuit, and because the prices and profits realized in the market bear the imprint of that lengthening, political economy mistook a negative cause for a positive one, treating the sphere of circulation as a mystical source of value. Marx names three supporting phenomena that sustain this misrecognition — the profit calculus of price-equalization across spheres with different circulation times; the absorption of circulation time within the broader turnover time; and the fact that accumulation requires the prior sale of commodities, which makes accumulation seem to originate in circulation. This is the classic Marxian operation of penetrating through surface phenomena (the "appearance") to the underlying value relation, and it directly prefigures the Volume 3 critique of the trinity formula, in which capital, land, and labour appear as three independent sources of revenue.

Third, within circulation time, the sale (C'–M') dominates, both in duration and in significance. Duration, because money is the immediately exchangeable form and commodities are not — so the burden of uncertainty, of finding a buyer, of realizing the embodied surplus-value falls on C–M. Significance, because C'–M' realizes the surplus-value already in the product, whereas M–C only prepares a new valorization. This asymmetry is a specifically capitalist one: it arises because C' already contains surplus-value while M does not. The sale is thus the moment at which the social validity of privately performed labour is decided — a compact statement of the labour-theoretic core of Marx's theory of value.

Fourth, circulation requires agents, and their remuneration is a deduction from production. Marx insists that circulation agents — capitalists themselves or their salaried workers — are necessary to the reproduction process, but their labour does not create value; it must be paid out of value produced elsewhere. Even the "paid by consumers" evasion collapses once it is recognized that consumers pay only out of what they themselves produce or legally appropriate from producers.

Fifth, the physical properties of commodities — perishability — set an absolute upper bound on circulation time and thereby shape the geography, industrial structure, and technology of capitalism. The seeming triviality of spoilage turns out to be a determinant of urban concentration (populous places), of transport development (railways, refrigeration, fast shipping), and of concentrated industrial forms (large breweries, dairies). Perishability, in short, is a material fact that capital must metabolize through social and technical reorganization.

Taken together, these lessons position Chapter 5 as the analytical bridge between Volume 1's theory of surplus-value (labour as the sole source of value) and Volume 2's theory of turnover (how the temporal rhythm of capital's circuit conditions the actual production of surplus-value over the year). It also prefigures Volume 3's analysis of the equalization of profit rates, the division of surplus-value among capitals according to turnover and composition, and ultimately the critique of capital fetishism. The deeper philosophical implication is that capital's apparent self-expansion is a phenomenon of appearance grounded in a hidden extraction of surplus labour; circulation time, precisely because it appears productive, is one of the most effective forms in which that concealment operates. Marx's chapter is thus not merely a technical passage on temporal accounting but a sustained exercise in the critique of economic appearance — a demonstration that the temporal structure of capital is at once the material condition of surplus-value production and the ideological medium of its concealment.
