Volume 2 • Chapter 2: The Circuit of Productive Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem and Thesis

This chapter undertakes one of the most technically demanding maneuvers in all of Marx's Capital: the analysis of the circuit of productive capital, whose general formula is written P...C'–M'–C...P. Marx's overarching thesis is that while this circuit appears, on its surface, to be nothing more than a periodically repeated production process — "reproduction" — it is in fact a valorization process, i.e. a process not merely of producing but of periodically reproducing surplus-value. The chapter's objective is to demonstrate that the circuit of productive capital is the decisive form for grasping capitalist production as a whole, because it is the form in which the production of surplus-value and its subsequent conversion back into the elements of production are shown to be internally related moments of a single, self-renewing whole.

The chapter's core intellectual objective is threefold. First, it seeks to distinguish this circuit rigorously from the circuit of money capital (M...M') analyzed in the previous chapter, showing that the two are not interchangeable perspectives but structurally distinct totalities with different implications for crises, hoarding, and accumulation. Second, it seeks to resolve a set of apparent paradoxes that arise within the formula — most critically, whether the formula depicts simple reproduction or reproduction on an expanded scale, a decision upon which "the very definition of the first circuit depends." Third, it seeks to demonstrate the unity of production and circulation: that the entire circulation process of industrial capital, in this form, is not the substance of the process but merely an interruption and a mediation between productive capital as the opening extreme and productive capital as the closing extreme.

The Paradoxical Structure of the Formula

The chapter opens by noting the strange "inversion" that defines this circuit. In form I (money capital), the production process interrupts the circulation of money capital and appears only as a mediator between the two phases M–C and C'–M'. In form II (productive capital), by contrast, the entire circulation process appears only as the mediator of the periodically repeated reproduction, itself an interruption and a mediation. Circulation proper — the sphere of markets, buying and selling, monetary exchange — loses its apparent primacy and is subordinated to the reproduction of productive capital. Where the money circuit presented itself as M–C–M', the productive circuit presents itself, in its circulatory segment, as C–M–C — that is, as the form of simple commodity circulation. This is a deeply ironic result: the circuit of the most developed form of capital appears in the least developed circulatory form.

Situating the Chapter in Marx's Broader Framework

This chapter belongs to the sequence in Volume II that analyzes the three circuits of industrial capital: money capital (Ch. 1), productive capital (Ch. 2), and commodity capital (Ch. 3). Chapter 2 therefore functions as the pivot of the volume's dialectical structure. Having established in Chapter 1 that money capital's circuit conceals the origin of surplus-value by presenting capital as self-breeding money, Marx here works through the form in which production — and thus the extraction of surplus labour — is explicitly the starting point. The chapter presupposes the fundamental results of Volume I: the theory of surplus-value as unpaid surplus labour; the distinction between constant capital (c) and variable capital (v); the concept of the value composition of capital; and the analysis of accumulation as the transformation of surplus-value into capital.

Crucially, the chapter also prepares the ground for the volume's later treatments of turnover (Part Two), the reproduction schemes (Part Three), and — through its repeated asides on crises, hoarding, and the money form — the synthesis with the treatment of credit and crisis in Volume III. Marx's notes reference Tooke, the classics, and the "vulgar economists," placing the analysis in explicit controversy with the existing tradition. The chapter's culminating assertion — "The circuit of productive capital is the form in which the classical economists have considered the circuit of industrial capital" — signals that Marx's project is not merely to describe the circuits but to critically reconstruct the categories through which bourgeois political economy has thought them.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The General Formula and Its Two Striking Features

Marx opens with the bare formula of the circuit of productive capital:

P...C'–M'–C...P

Every symbol here carries a precise theoretical charge. P is productive capital — capital that exists in the form of the material elements of the production process, namely means of production (raw materials, machinery, auxiliary materials) and labour-power. The dotted line (...) indicates that the process is continuous and interrupted — there is a phase (production) so internal to the circuit's continuity that it is not itself an act of exchange; it is the "hidden" phase in which value is created. C' is commodity capital, capital in the form of a mass of commodities that carries both the original capital value and the surplus-value created in production; the prime (′) marks it as augmented. M' is the money form of that same augmented value. The final C...P shows the return to the same starting point: money is used to buy means of production and labour-power, re-establishing productive capital.

Marx immediately specifies what the formula signifies: "the periodically repeated function of the productive capital, i.e. reproduction." Two features of this signification demand notice. First, reproduction here is "in respect of valorization" — the repetition is not merely material (producing the same physical goods) but value-theoretic: the periodic reproduction of surplus-value. Second, the new beginning is "given by the point of departure itself" — the circuit is self-starting and self-renewing, a self-enclosed totality.

Marx then introduces a qualification that will recur throughout the chapter: a part of C' may, "in certain cases, in the investment branches of industrial capital," directly re-enter the same labour process from which it emerged as a means of production. This is a reference to cases like agriculture (seed corn) or the mining/extraction industries, where a portion of the product re-enters production in kind. In such cases, the "only independent expression it receives is as money of account" — it never actually enters the circulation process. The same applies to the part of C' that the capitalist consumes in kind. Marx dismisses this as "insignificant for capitalist production; at most it comes into consideration in agriculture." This is a methodological simplification: for analytic clarity, Marx brackets in-kind re-entry and in-kind consumption, assuming that all product is sold and all means of production bought in the market.

The first striking feature of the formula is the inversion of the relationship between production and circulation. In form I (M...M'), production interrupts circulation and functions as a mediator. In form II (P...P), "the entire circulation process... merely forms an interruption, and hence a mediation, between the productive capital that opens the circuit as the first extreme and closes it in the same form as the last extreme." Circulation is demoted to a mediating moment; reproduction is elevated to the primary content.

The second striking feature is the formal inversion of the circulation sequence itself. In form I it was M–C–M (money buys commodity, commodity sells for more money). In form II it is C–M–C (commodity sells for money, money buys commodity) — "i.e. the form of simple commodity circulation." This is not a mere surface detail. It means that, viewed from the circulation side alone, the circuit of the most advanced form of capital looks identical to simple commodity production — the very confusion that generates vulgar economics.

I. SIMPLE REPRODUCTION

C'–M'–C Between the Extremes P...P

Marx now narrows his focus to the circulatory segment C'–M'–C that runs its course between the extremes P...P. The starting point of this circulation is the commodity capital:

C' = C + c = P + c

Here the equation must be read carefully. C' is the commodity capital as a whole. It decomposes into C + c: the portion representing the capital value (C) and the portion representing surplus-value (c). The second equality, = P + c, says that this capital value C is the commodity-form of the value of the productive capital P (P is now measured in money terms as the value advanced), plus the surplus-value c. So the total value of the commodity mass = value of productive capital + surplus-value.

The function C'–M' — the realization of the capital value P (which now exists as a commodity component C) and of the surplus-value c contained in the same commodity mass — was already treated in Chapter 1, where it formed the concluding phase of the money circuit. "Here it forms the second phase of the circuit, but only the first phase of circulation." This positional shift is critical: what was a terminal act is now an initiating act, because the circuit has a different starting point and therefore a different internal ordering.

Marx then turns to the crucial indeterminacy. In form I, the circuit ended with M'. Since M' — as much as the original M — can reopen a circuit as money capital, it "was at first unnecessary to see whether the M and m (surplus-value) contained in M' continue their paths together, or whether they describe different paths." The question only became urgent if one pursued the first circuit into its repetition. But in the circuit of productive capital, "this point must be decided" — because the definition of the first circuit now depends on it, since in this form C'–M' appears as the first phase of circulation (not the last), and must be supplemented by M–C. On this decision depends "whether the formula depicts simple reproduction or reproduction on an expanded scale." The character of the circuit is altered. This is a decisive methodological point: the same formula P...P can represent either simple or expanded reproduction, depending on what happens to m — and the form itself forces us to confront the fork.

The Assumption of Simple Reproduction

Marx proceeds by first taking up simple reproduction: the case in which the entire surplus-value goes into the capitalist's personal consumption. He assumes, as in Chapter 1, that other circumstances remain the same and commodities buy and sell at their values. Under this assumption, once C' is transformed into money, the part representing capital value recirculates within the industrial capital circuit, while the other part — surplus-value turned into gold — leaves this circuit and enters the general circulation of commodities. It is "money circulation proceeding from the capitalist, but it takes place outside the circulation of his individual capital." This spatial metaphor of "outside" is essential: the surplus-value's circuit, once monetized, is no longer part of this capital's self-reproduction.

The Yarn Example and the Arithmetic of Value Components

Marx sets out a concrete numerical illustration. We have a commodity capital C' of 10,000 lb. of yarn to the value of £500. Of this, £422 was the value of the productive capital, and it continues the capital circulation as the money form of 8,440 lb. of yarn. Meanwhile the surplus-value of £78 — the money form of 1,560 lb. of yarn, "the excess portion of the commodity product" — makes its exit and describes a separate path within general commodity circulation.

The arithmetic checks: 8,440 + 1,560 = 10,000 lb. And £422 + £78 = £500. The ratios: £422/£500 = 0.844 = 8,440/10,000; £78/£500 = 0.156 = 1,560/10,000.

Marx then describes m–c: a series of purchases made with the money the capitalist spends, "whether on commodities as such or on services, for his esteemed self and family." These purchases are fragmented and take place at different times. The money therefore exists temporarily as a money reserve or hoard destined for current consumption — because "it is in the form of a hoard that any money whose circulation is interrupted exists." But crucially, in its function as means of circulation (which includes its temporary hoard form), this money "does not enter into the circulation of the capital in its money form M." The money is not advanced, but spent. This distinction between money advanced (which expects to return with a surplus) and money spent (which is consumed and disappears) is fundamental to Marx's distinction between capital and revenue.

The Machine Problem: Indivisibility and Ideal Value Fractions

Marx then poses a crucial complication. Suppose the commodity product were not divisible yarn but a machine worth £500 with the same value composition. There would still be a value portion equal to the £78 surplus-value, but this £78 would exist only in the total machine. It could not be divided into capital value and surplus-value "without being broken into pieces and thus destroying its value together with its use-value." The two value components could only be depicted ideally as components of the physical body of the commodity, not as independent elements.

The contrast with yarn is instructive. Because yarn is homogeneous and divisible, each pound can be treated as an independent commodity element. The machine, by contrast, must be sold in its entirety before m can embark on its own particular circulation. Yet in the yarn case, the sale of 8,440 lb. leaves the remaining 1,560 lb. exhibiting "a completely separate circulation of the surplus-value in the form c (1,560 lb. of yarn)–m (£78)–c (articles of consumption)."

Marx then gives the full per-unit decomposition of the yarn. The 10,000 lb. can be partitioned into: - Constant capital value (c): 7,440 lb. of yarn with a value of £372 - Variable capital value (v): 1,000 lb. of yarn with a value of £50 - Surplus-value (s): 1,560 lb. of yarn with a value of £78

Check: 7,440 + 1,000 + 1,560 = 10,000 lb.; £372 + £50 + £78 = £500.

And each individual pound is partitioned proportionally. One pound = 16 ounces. The fractions: - c: 11.904 oz. with a value of 8.928d. - v: 1.600 oz. with a value of 1.200d. - s: 2.496 oz. with a value of 1.872d.

Check the ounces: 11.904 + 1.600 + 2.496 = 16.000 oz. ✓ Check the values: 8.928 + 1.200 + 1.872 = 12.000d = 1 shilling per lb. ✓ (since £500/10,000 lb. = 1s. per lb., and 20s. = £1, so £500 = 10,000s.)

This per-unit decomposition establishes that the capitalist can successively consume the elements of surplus-value contained in the total yarn by selling it in successive portions, and can also realize c + v in the same way. But, Marx stresses, "this operation similarly presupposes that the entire 10,000 lb. is sold, and that the value of c and v is therefore replaced by the sale of 8,440 lb." The whole must be sold for the parts to be separable in practice.

The upshot: by way of C'–M', both the capital value and the surplus-value acquire a separable existence — the existence of different sums of money. Both M and m are "the transformed form of the value that originally possessed its own expression merely as the price of the commodity, i.e. a merely ideal expression." The separation is thus a realization of a distinction that was only ideal in the commodity.

The London Building Trade: Real Fractions of an Unfinished Whole

Marx adduces a striking empirical illustration. In the London building trade, conducted largely on credit, the contractor receives advances in stages as the house is built. None of these stages is a house; each is "a really existing component of a future house that is coming into being." Despite its reality, it is "only an ideal fraction of the whole house, but it is sufficiently real, all the same, to serve as security for an additional advance." This example shows that the ideal fractionation of value can have real economic effects — here, it underwrites credit. It demonstrates that the conceptual separability of capital value and surplus-value is not a mere analytic convenience but is operative in real capitalist practice.

The Three Consequences of the C'/M' Split

Marx now enumerates the consequences of the split between C and c (capital value and surplus-value) that occurs in C'–M':

First, when the commodity capital is realized — i.e. C'–(M + m) — the movement of capital value and surplus-value, which in C'–M' was still common to both and borne by the same mass of commodities, becomes divisible, since the two now possess independent forms as sums of money.

Second, if this division takes place — m spent by the capitalist as revenue, M continuing the path prescribed by the circuit — then the first act C'–M', together with the subsequent acts M–C and m–c, "can be depicted as two different circulations: C–M–C and c–m–c; both of these, in their general form, are series that belong to the ordinary commodity circulation." This is a pivotal result: the single circuit of capital bifurcates into two formally identical commodity circulations. It is precisely this bifurcation that makes capital's circuit look like simple commodity circulation.

Third, if the common movement of capital value and surplus-value only partially divides (so that part of the surplus-value is not spent as revenue) — or does not divide at all — then a change in capital value takes place within the circuit of the capital value itself, before the circuit is completed. In Marx's example, the value of the productive capital was £422. If M–C continues as £480 or £500, then it traverses the final stages of the circuit "as a value £58 or £78 greater than it originally was." This can occur "in combination with a change in its value composition." This third consequence is the bridge to accumulation: a partially or wholly unspent m means the capital value re-entering production is larger.

Marx then restates the positional shift of C'–M'. It is "the second stage of circulation and the concluding stage of circuit I (M...M'); ... the second stage of the present circuit and the first stage of commodity circulation in it." In so far as circulation is concerned, it must be supplemented by M–C. But M–C "does not just have the process of valorization already behind it (in this case the function of P, the first stage), but its result, the commodity product C', has already been realized." Hence: "The valorization of capital, as well as the realization of the commodity product in which the valorized capital value is represented, thus ends with C'–M'."

The Vulgar Economists' "Cretinism"

Having assumed simple reproduction — m–c completely separating from M–C — Marx observes that "since both circulations, c–m–c and C–M–C, belong in their general form to commodity circulation (and thus do not exhibit any difference in value between their extremes), it is quite easy to conceive the capitalist production process, as the vulgar economists do, as the simple production of commodities, use-values destined for consumption of some kind or other, which the capitalist produced only in order to replace them with commodities of a different use-value." This is the "characteristic cretinism" of vulgar economics: because the circulatory forms C–M–C and c–m–c are formally identical to simple commodity exchange, the vulgar economist mistakes capital's circuit for simple commodity production, suppressing the valorization that is the whole point of the process.

Marx counters: "C' appears from the start as commodity capital, and the aim of the entire process, enrichment (valorization), by no means excludes a growth in the capitalist's consumption in line with the increase in the magnitude of surplus-value. In fact it absolutely includes it." The apparent "consumption orientation" of the process is not an alternative to valorization; it is a consequence of it.

Yet Marx does not entirely dismiss the c–m–c moment. He notes that in the circulation of the capitalist's revenue, the commodity produced "serves in point of fact only to convert this revenue into money and from money into a series of other commodities for the purpose of private consumption." But — and this is the crucial "little fact" — "c is a commodity value which has not cost the capitalist anything; it is the embodiment of surplus labour, which originally stepped forth onto the stage as a component of the commodity capital C'." Therefore c is "already linked in its existence to the circuit of the capital value in process," and if the circuit halts or is disturbed, "it is not only the consumption of c that is restricted, or completely ceases, but in addition the market for the set of commodities that form the replacement for c." This is a dialectical reversal: the consumption of surplus-value, though it appears to exit the circuit, remains dependent on the circuit's continuity. The "freedom" of revenue consumption is conditioned by the realization of capital.

The Two Circulations: C–M–C and c–m–c

Marx now insistently distinguishes the two circulations in their identity and difference. As soon as c becomes independent through m–c, "in the form as a whole, c–m–c, it does not enter into the movement of the capital advanced by the capitalist, even though it proceeds from this." It is related to capital "only in so far as the existence of capital presupposes the existence of the capitalist, and this latter is conditional on his consumption of surplus-value." This is a sociological rather than strictly economic link: capital's existence presupposes a capitalist who must consume to live, but the consumption as such is not a moment of capital's circuit.

Within the general circulation, C' functions "for example as yarn, simply as a commodity; but as a moment of the circulation of capital it functions as commodity capital." When the yarn is sold to a merchant, "it is removed from the circuit of that capital whose product it is, but still continues as a commodity in the orbit of general circulation." The circulation of the mass of commodities continues "even though it has ceased to form a moment in the independent circuit of the capital of the spinner." Therefore the really definitive metamorphosis of the mass of commodities — C–M, "its final abandonment to consumption" — "can be completely separated in time and space from the metamorphosis in which this mass of commodities functions as his commodity capital." The same metamorphosis already accomplished in the circulation of this capital "remains still to be completed in the sphere of the general circulation."

This is a profound point about the division between the individual circuit and the aggregate. "The general circulation includes the intertwining of the circuits of the various independent fractions of the social capital, i.e. the totality of individual capitals, as well as the circulation of those values that are not placed on the market as capital." The relation between the circuit of capital as part of general circulation, and as links in an independent circuit, is displayed by considering M' = M + m. M (as money capital) continues the circuit; m (spent as revenue, m–c) goes into general circulation but is cast out of the circuit of capital. "Only that part of it enters the latter circuit that functions as additional money capital." And, Marx notes: "In c–m–c, money functions simply as coin; the purpose of this circulation is the individual consumption of the capitalist." Here again the polemic blasts vulgar economics for depicting "this circulation, which does not enter into the circuit of capital — the circulation of the portion of the value product that is consumed as revenue — as the characteristic circuit of capital."

M–C: The Return to Productive Capital

In the second phase, M–C, the capital value M = P is once again present, "having rid itself of the surplus-value, i.e. with the same value magnitude as in the first stage of the circuit of money capital M–C." Despite the different position, the function of the money capital "remains the same: its transformation into mp and L, means of production and labour-power."

The capital value in the function of commodity capital C'–M' "has thus passed through the phase C–M, at the same time as C–M" — i.e. the transformation of the whole commodity mass into money simultaneously realizes capital value and surplus-value — and it now moves into the complementary phase M–C^(L/mp), i.e. the purchase of L (labour-power) and mp (means of production). Its overall circulation is thus: C–M–C^(L/mp).

Marx then lays out three observations about money capital in this form:

First, money capital M appeared in form I as the original form in which capital value was advanced. Now it appears from the start as part of the sum of money into which the commodity capital has been transformed in the first phase of circulation C'–M'. Here money capital exists "neither as the original nor as the concluding form of the capital value," since the phase M–C is completed only by "repeatedly stripping off the money form." Hence the portion of M–C that is simultaneously M–L (the purchase of labour-power) "appears no longer as a mere advance of money for acquiring labour-power, but as an advance in which the same 1,000 lb. of yarn with a value of £50 is advanced for the labour-power in the money form, and this forms a portion of the commodity value produced by the labour-power." This is a revolutionary point: the money advanced to the worker is only the transformed equivalent form of a portion of the commodity value that the worker himself produces. "And for this reason alone, the act M–C, in so far as it is M–L, is in no way simply the substitution of commodities in use form for commodities in money form, but includes other elements that are independent of the general circulation of commodities as such." The wage relation is not a mere market exchange; it is a moment of the production relation of capital.

The Draft on Future Labour and the Demolition of the Stock Doctrine

Marx now develops one of the most philosophically rich arguments in the chapter. M' appears as the transformed form of C', which is itself the product of the past function of P, the production process. "The entire sum of M' thus appears as the monetary expression of past labour." He reiterates the decomposition: 10,000 lb. of yarn = £500 = product of the spinning process; 7,440 lb. = c = £372; 1,000 lb. = v = £50; 1,560 lb. = s = £78. If only the original £422 is advanced afresh, "the worker merely receives as the next week's advance in M–L a portion of the 10,000 lb. of yarn produced in this week." As the result of C–M, "the money is throughout the expression of past labour."

But now the dialectical twist. In so far as the complementary act M–C is performed on the commodity market, "there is again a conversion of past labour from one form (money) into another (commodity)." Yet M–C is separate from C–M in time. It can in exceptional cases be simultaneous — if the capitalist performing M–C and the capitalist for whom this act is C–M transfer commodities simultaneously and M merely settles the balance. "The difference in time between the execution of C–M and that of M–C may be more or less considerable."

Although, as the result of C–M, M represents past labour, "M can represent for the act M–C the transformed form of commodities that are not yet present on the market at all, but will be there only in the future." Examples: coal may be purchased before it is extracted from the mine; in so far as m figures as accumulation of money, it can represent cotton that will only be produced next year. The same applies to the expenditure of revenue m–c, and even to the wages of labour = £50: "this money is not only the monetary form of the workers' past labour, but also a draft on simultaneous or future labour that will only be realized, or is supposed to be realized, in the future."

The worker may use his wage to buy a coat that will only be made a week later. "This is in particular the case with the very large number of necessary means of subsistence that must be consumed almost immediately, the moment they are produced, if they are not to spoil." In the money with which his wage is paid, "the worker receives the transformed form of his own future labour or that of other workers." With one part of his past labour the capitalist gives him a draft on his own future labour. "It is his own simultaneous or future labour which forms the as yet non-existent reserve stock with which his past labour is paid for."

The conclusion is startling: "Here the idea that a stock has to be formed is completely demolished." This is a direct polemic against the bourgeois (and particularly the "Tooke-ist" and quantity-theory-adjacent) notion that production requires prior accumulation of a stock of goods to pay workers and purchase inputs. Marx shows that the money form is not a stock but a claim — a draft on present and future labour. The reserve "stock" that pays the worker does not exist as a thing; it is the ongoing, simultaneous and future labour of the worker himself.

Money as Means of Circulation and Means of Payment (Against Tooke)

Marx now develops three further points about the money form:

Second, in the circulation C–M–C^(L/mp), "the same money changes its position twice; the capitalist first receives it as a seller, and then gives it out again as buyer." The transformation of commodity into money "only serves to transform it from the money form into the commodity form again, and so the money form of capital, its existence as money capital, is thus only an evanescent moment in this movement." Alternatively, "the money capital, in so far as the movement is fluid, appears as a means of circulation only when it serves as a means of purchase; it appears as an actual means of payment only when capitalists buy from each other, hence when there is simply a balance of payments to be settled."

Here the editor's note attaches Marx's manuscript remark: "Against Tooke." The note explains that Thomas Tooke, author of the six-volume A History of Prices (1838–57) — "the last English economist of any value" in Marx's words — was criticized for failing to distinguish between money as means of circulation and money as capital. Marx's point (elaborated in Volume III, Ch. 23) is that money's functioning as mere medium of exchange is not the same as its functioning as capital; the money-capitalist who gets his money back "must always loan it out again, so long as it is to function for him as capital." The Chapter 2 discussion thus lays groundwork for the later critique of the banking/currency debates.

Third, the function of money capital — whether as means of circulation or means of payment — "is simply to mediate the replacement of C by L and mp," i.e. to replace the yarn (the commodity product, after deduction of the surplus-value spent as revenue) "with its own elements of production." Its ultimate role is "to mediate the transformation of commodity capital back into productive capital."

The Requirements of Normal Circulation and the Problem of Value Change

For the circuit to run its normal course, "C' must be sold at its value and as a whole." Furthermore, C–M–C "does not just include the replacement of one commodity by another, but its replacement in the same value relations." Marx concedes that this is an assumption. "In fact, however, the value of the means of production varies; capitalist production is precisely marked by a continuous change in value relations, if only because of the constant change in the productivity of labour that characterizes it." He defers full treatment of this (Volume II, Ch. 15, §5 — "pp. 360–68") but flags it now. The transformation P into C' proceeds in the sphere of production; C' back into P takes place in the circulation sphere, mediated by the simple metamorphosis of commodities. "Its content, however, is a moment of the reproduction process considered as a whole. C–M–C, as a form of circulation of capital, includes a functionally specific interchange of material." It requires that C equal the elements of production of C', and that these maintain their original value relations. "If this is not the case, then the process cannot run its normal course."

The Evanescent Money Form and the Critique of Form I

Marx now draws the decisive contrast. In M...M', M is the original form of capital value, "cast aside only to be re-assumed later." In P...C'–M'–C...P, M is "only a form assumed in the process, and is already cast aside again within this." Here "the money form appears simply as an evanescent form of value of the capital; the capital as C' is anxious to assume the money form but the capital as M' is equally anxious to get rid of it, as soon as it has pupated into it, in order to convert itself once more into the form of productive capital."

Crucially: "As long as it persists in the shape of money, it does not function as capital, and thus is not valorized; the capital remains idle." M functions here as a means of circulation — though a means of circulation of capital. The appearance of independence that the money form possesses in form I "vanishes in this second form, which thus constitutes a critique of form I, and reduces this to a mere particular form." This is a major methodological claim: form II is not merely another form; it is a critique of form I, showing that the apparent self-sufficiency and self-breeding quality of money capital is an illusion generated by considering the circuit in isolation.

Marx then notes the consequences of interruption. "If the second metamorphosis M–C comes up against obstacles (e.g. if the means of production are unobtainable on the market), then the circular flow of the reproduction process is interrupted, just as if the capital was tied up in the form of commodity capital." The difference, however, is that capital "can last out longer in the money form than in its previous commodity form." It does not cease to be money when it functions as capital; "but it does cease to be a commodity, and in fact a use-value in general, if it is detained too long in its function as commodity capital." A key distinction: money is durable and general; a commodity detained too long may spoil or become unsaleable. "Secondly, in the money form it is able to assume a form other than its original one of productive capital, while as C' it can move no further." Money capital can be redirected; commodity capital cannot.

Marx adds: "In its form, C'–M'–C includes for C' only acts of circulation which are moments of its reproduction; but the real reproduction of the C into which C' is converted is necessary to the performance of C'–M'–C', this is however conditional on reproduction processes outside the reproduction process of the individual capital depicted in C'." This is another pointer to the interconnection of individual capitals — the material replacement of C requires that other capitals, in other branches, have produced the needed means of production.

The chapter restates the positional contrast: "In form I, M–C^(L/mp) simply prepared the first transformation of money capital into productive capital; in form II it prepares the transformation of commodity capital back into productive capital." Thus, in so far as industrial capital remains invested in the same business, it prepares the transformation of C' "back into the same elements of production from which it emerged." It appears "as a return to this process, a repetition of it, hence as a forerunner to the reproduction process, and so also to the repetition of the valorization process."

Marx reiterates the crucial point: "M–L is not simple commodity exchange, but the purchase of a commodity L that is to serve for the production of surplus-value, while M–mp is only a procedure that is materially indispensable to the accomplishment of this end." Once M–C^(L/mp) is complete, M has been transformed back into productive capital and the circuit begins afresh.

The Expanded Formula and the Materialist Interchange

Marx expands the formula P...C'–M'–C...P as follows: the transformation of money capital into productive capital is the purchase of commodities for the purpose of commodity production. "It is only in so far as consumption is productive consumption of this kind that it falls within the actual circuit of capital; the condition for consumption to occur is that surplus-value is made by means of the commodities thus consumed." This is "something very different from production, even commodity production, whose purpose is the existence of the producers." The replacement of commodity by commodity conditioned by surplus-value production "is something quite other than an exchange of products that is simply mediated by money. But this is how the matter is presented by the economists, as proof that no overproduction is possible." This is a direct sally against Say's Law and its variants.

Marx then addresses the worker's own circulation. Besides the productive consumption of M (transformed into L and mp), the circuit contains the first link of M–L, which for the worker is L–M = C–M. Of the worker's circulation L–M–C, which includes his consumption, only the first link falls into the circuit of capital, as the result of M–L. "The second act, i.e. M–C, does not fall into the circulation of the individual capital, although it proceeds from it. The constant existence of the working class, however, is necessary for the capitalist class, and so, therefore, is the consumption of the worker mediated by M–C."

C'–M' and the Merchant: The Appearance of Consumption and the Crisis

Marx now develops the argument that is "an important one in considering crises." The act C'–M' merely assumes that C' is transformed into money, is sold, "so that the circuit of the capital value can continue, and the surplus-value can be consumed by the capitalist." The commodity is bought only because it is a use-value, suitable for some kind of consumption, productive or individual. "But if C' circulates further, e.g. in the hands of the merchant who has bought the yarn, this in no way disturbs — initially at least — the continuation of the circuit of the individual capital that has produced the yarn and sold it to the merchant." The whole process follows its course.

"As soon as C' is sold, is transformed into money, it can be transformed back into the real factors of the labour process, and hence of the reproduction process. Hence whether C' is bought by the final consumer or by the merchant who intends to sell it again does not directly alter the matter in any way." The volume of the mass of commodities brought into being by capitalist production "is determined by the scale of this production and its needs for constant expansion, and not by a predestined ambit of supply and demand, of needs to be satisfied." Besides other industrial capitalists, mass production can have "only wholesale merchants as its immediate purchasers."

"Within certain bounds, the reproduction process may proceed on the same or on an expanded scale, even though the commodities ejected from it do not actually enter either individual or productive consumption." The consumption of commodities "is not included in the circuit of the capital from which they emerge." As long as the product is sold, everything follows its regular course, as far as the capitalist producer is concerned. His circuit is not interrupted.

And if the process is expanded (which includes an expansion of productive consumption of means of production), "then this reproduction of capital can be accompanied by a more expanded individual consumption (and thus demand) on the part of the workers, since this is introduced and mediated by productive consumption." Production of surplus-value and consumption of the capitalist can grow, "and the whole reproduction process find itself in the most flourishing condition, while in fact a great part of the commodities have only apparently gone into consumption, and are actually lying unsold in the hands of retail traders, thus being still on the market."

Here is the germ of the crisis theory. "One stream of commodities now follows another, and it finally emerges that the earlier stream had only seemed to be swallowed up by consumption. Commodity capitals now vie with each other for space on the market. The late-comers sell below the price in order to sell at all. The earlier streams have not yet been converted into ready money, while payment for them is falling due. Their owners must declare themselves bankrupt, or sell at any price in order to pay. This sale, however, has absolutely nothing to do with the real state of demand. It has only to do with the demand for payment, with the absolute necessity of transforming commodities into money. At this point the crisis breaks out." Crucially: "It first becomes evident not in the direct reduction of consumer demand, the demand for individual consumption, but rather in a decline in the number of exchanges of capital for capital, in the reproduction process of capital." This is a decisive claim: crises erupt first in the inter-capitalist sphere (capital–capital exchanges), not in the sphere of final consumer demand — a direct refutation of underconsumptionist theories that locate the cause of crises in insufficient workers' consumption.

The Hoard Within the Circuit

Marx then analyzes the money that must be held in reserve within M–C. To fulfil its function as money capital, M is converted into mp and L. If these commodities must be purchased or paid for at different dates, M–C "then takes the form of a series of successive purchases and payments, so that a part of M performs the act M–C, while another part persists in the money state," and only serves for simultaneous or successive acts M–C at a time determined by the process. "It is withdrawn from circulation only temporarily, to step into action and fulfil its function at a definite point in time." This storing of money "is itself a function determined by its circulation and for circulation. Its existence as a fund for purchase and payment, the suspension of its movement, its state of interrupted circulation, is then a situation in which the money fulfils one of its functions as money capital." The money temporarily dormant is itself a part of M (of M' minus m = M). "Furthermore, all the money that is withdrawn from circulation exists in the form of a hoard. The hoard form thus becomes here a function of the money capital."

Marx notes: "But it also proves true once again here that, within the circuit of industrial capital, money capital performs no other functions than those of money, and these money functions have the significance of capital functions only through their connection with the other stages of the circuit." This is the crucial money-as-such principle: money does not acquire new functions by being capital; its existing money functions (means of circulation, means of payment, store of value) acquire the significance of capital functions through their position in the circuit.

"The expression of M' as a relation between m and M, as a capital relation, is not a direct function of the money capital, but rather of the commodity capital C', which in turn expresses, as a relation between c and C, only the result of the production process, of the self-valorization of the capital value that takes place within it." If the circulation process meets obstacles — "the state of the market, etc." — and M persists in its money state, "then this is again a form of hoarding... It is the involuntary formation of a hoard." In our case, "the money thus has the form of latent money capital, money capital that lies idle." Marx defers further treatment. "In both cases, the persistence of money capital in its money state appears as the result of interrupted movement, whether this is expedient or inexpedient, voluntary or involuntary, functional or dysfunctional."

2. ACCUMULATION AND REPRODUCTION ON AN EXPANDED SCALE

Marx opens this section with a technical premise: "Since the proportions in which the production process can be expanded are not arbitrary, but are prescribed by technical factors, the surplus-value realized, even if it is destined for capitalization, can often only grow to the volume at which it can actually function as additional capital... by repeating a number of circuits." Until then, "it must be stored up." The surplus-value thus builds up into a hoard, and in this form "constitutes latent money capital." "Latent, because as long as it persists in the money form, it cannot function as capital."

The editor's footnote here is important: the term "latent" is borrowed from the physical concept of latent heat, "which has now been more or less displaced by the theory of the transformation of energy." In Part Three (a later draft), Marx used "potential capital" (borrowed from potential energy) or, by analogy with D'Alembert's "virtual velocities," "virtual capital." This note shows the physical-scientific provenance of Marx's conceptual vocabulary — a reminder that Marx's method of abstraction is self-conscious and borrows deliberately from natural science.

Marx clarifies: "Thus the formation of a hoard appears here as a moment that is comprised within the process of capitalist accumulation, accompanies it but is at the same time essentially different from it. For the reproduction process is not itself expanded by the formation of latent money capital. On the contrary." The hoard is formed "because the capitalist producer cannot directly expand the scale of his production."

If the capitalist sells his surplus-product to a gold or silver producer, "who thereby throws new gold or silver into circulation — or, what comes to the same thing, if he sells it to a merchant who uses part of the national surplus product to import additional gold or silver from abroad — then his latent money capital forms an increment to the national gold or silver hoard." In all other cases, "the £78, say, that was means of circulation in the hands of the purchaser, has assumed in the hands of the capitalist only the form of a hoard; thus all that has taken place is a new distribution of the national gold or silver hoard." This is a crucial point about the aggregate: outside of gold/silver production, hoarding is redistribution, not net addition.

If money functions as means of payment (so the commodity is paid for later), "then the surplus product destined for capitalization is not transformed into money, but into claims for payment, titles to property equivalent to a sum that the purchaser either already has in his possession or expects to come into. It does not enter into the reproduction of the circuit, any more than the money that is invested in interest-bearing securities, etc." This prepares the analysis of credit later.

Marx then restates the fundamental character of capitalist production: "determined by the valorization of the capital value advanced, thus in the first instance by the production of the greatest possible amount of surplus-value; secondly, however (see Volume 1, Chapter 24), by the production of capital, i.e. the transformation of surplus-value into capital." Accumulation, or production on an expanded scale, "which first appears as a means towards the constantly extended production of surplus-value, hence the enrichment of the capitalist, as the personal end of the latter, and is part of the general tendency of capitalist production, becomes in the course of its development... a necessity for each individual capitalist. The constant enlargement of his capital becomes a condition for its preservation."

Marx then addresses a methodological point about the formula. He first considered simple reproduction, assuming the whole surplus-value is spent as revenue. "In actual fact, a part of the surplus-value must always be spent as revenue in normal circumstances, and another part capitalized, and it is quite immaterial in this connection that at certain periods the surplus-value produced is completely consumed, and at others completely capitalized." If the movement takes its average course, "there is a bit of both." "In order not to complicate the formula, it is better to assume that the whole of the surplus-value is accumulated."

The formula for expanded reproduction is then given:

P...C'–M'–C^(L/mp)...P'

This "expresses: productive capital which is to be reproduced on a larger scale and with greater value, and begins its second circuit — or what comes to the same thing, repeats its first circuit — as augmented productive capital." As soon as the second circuit begins, "we once again have P as the point of departure; it is simply that P is now a larger productive capital than the first P was." Similarly, in M...M', the second circuit begins with M', and M' functions as M, as money capital of a specific magnitude which has been advanced. "All reference to its augmentation through the capitalization of surplus-value has vanished, once it steps forth in the function of money capital advanced. This origin was obliterated in its form as money capital just beginning its circuit. It is just the same with P', as soon as it functions as the point of departure for a new circuit." This obliteration of origin is an important theme: once a circuit begins, the starting capital appears simply as a given magnitude, its past accumulation erased.

Marx then compares P...P' with M...M'. "Each has a quite different significance." M...M', taken by itself as an isolated circuit, "simply expresses that M, the money capital... is money breeding money, value breeding value, and brings forth surplus-value." In the circuit of P, "on the contrary, the process of valorization is already complete as soon as the first stage, the production process, has taken place, and once it has passed through the second stage C'–M'... capital value and surplus-value already exist as realized money capital, as M', which in the first circuit appeared as the final extremity."

The fact that surplus-value is produced "was depicted in the first form of P...P that was considered by c–m–c, the second stage of which falls outside the circulation of capital and represents the circulation of surplus-value as revenue. In this form, in which the entire movement is represented by P...P, and there is thus no difference in value between the two end points, the valorization of the value advanced, the creation of surplus-value, is depicted as much as it is in M...M'; it is simply that the act C'–M' appears as the final stage in M...M', but as the second stage in the circuit, and first of the circulation stages, in P...P'."

But now the crucial distinction: "In P...P', P' does not express the fact that surplus-value is produced, but rather that the produced surplus-value is capitalized, i.e. that capital has been accumulated, and hence P', as opposed to P, consists of the original capital value plus the value of the capital accumulated through its movement."

M', as the simple conclusion of M...M', as also C' wherever it appears, "express, taken by themselves, not the movement, but rather its result; the valorization of the capital value realized in the commodity or money form." But — and this is the crucial materialist claim — "neither in the form C' nor in the form M' is the valorization that has taken place a function of the money capital or the commodity capital. As a specific and distinct form or mode of existence that corresponds to the particular functions of industrial capital, money capital can perform only money functions, and commodity capital only commodity functions; the distinction between them is simply that between money and commodity."

Similarly, industrial capital in its form as productive capital "can consist only of the same elements as those of any other labour process that fashions products: on the one hand the objective conditions of labour (means of production), on the other hand productively (purposively) active labour-power." Just as industrial capital in the sphere of production can exist only in the combination corresponding to the production process in general (including non-capitalist production), so in the sphere of circulation it can exist "only in the two forms of commodity and money that correspond to this."

The elements of production proclaim themselves as productive capital "in so far as the labour-power is the labour-power of others which the capitalist has bought from its owners; just as he has bought his means of production from the owners of other commodities." The production process itself appears as a productive function of industrial capital; "so money and commodities appear as forms of circulation of this industrial capital, and thus also their functions as its circulation functions." "It is only through their connection as functional forms which industrial capital has to go through in the various stages of its circuit that the money function and the commodity function are here at the same time functions of money capital and commodity capital."

From this follows a major methodological warning: "It is wrong, therefore, to seek to ascribe the specific properties and functions that characterize money as money and commodities as commodities to their character as capital, and it is just as wrong, conversely, to derive the properties of productive capital from its mode of existence in the means of production." This is a two-sided caveat: neither reify money/commodity properties as capital properties, nor derive capital properties from the physical forms of the means of production. This is a decisive anti-fetishistic point.

When M' or C' are depicted as M + m, C + c, i.e. as a relation between capital value and surplus-value as its offshoot, "this relation is expressed in one case in the money form, and the other case in the commodity form, but this does not alter the matter in any way. This relation thus does not arise from properties and functions that can be ascribed either to the money or the commodity as such." "In both cases, the characteristic property of capital, that it is money which breeds money, is only expressed as the result. C' is always the product of the function of P, and M' is always simply the form into which C' has been transformed in the circuit of industrial capital."

Hence, "as soon as the realized money capital recommences its particular function as money capital, it ceases to express the capital-relation contained in M' = M + m. When the movement M...M' has been passed through, and M' begins the cycle anew, it does not figure as M', but rather as M, even if the entire surplus-value contained in M' has been capitalized." In our case, the second circuit begins with £500 instead of £422. "The money capital that opens the circuit is £78 greater than previously and this difference exists when one circuit is compared with another, but such a comparison is not made within the individual circuit itself. The £500 now advanced as money capital, of which £78 existed earlier as surplus-value, does not play a different role from the £500 which another capitalist might use to open his first circuit." The same applies in the circuit of productive capital: the enlarged P' appears as P when the circuit is begun again, "just like P in the simple reproduction P...P."

Finally, Marx clarifies a terminological point. At the stage M'–C', "the augmented magnitude is indicated simply by C', and not by L' and mp'." Since C is the sum of L and mp, "it is already indicated by C' that the sum of the L and mp contained in it is greater than the original P." Secondly, however, "the designations L' and mp' would be false, as we know that the growth of capital involves a change in its value composition, in the course of which the value of mp constantly grows, while that of L always declines relatively, and often even absolutely." This is a compressed statement of the tendency toward rising organic composition of capital: as accumulation proceeds, the value of means of production (mp) rises while the value of labour-power (L) relatively declines.

3. ACCUMULATION OF MONEY

Marx here analyzes the conditions under which surplus-value in its golden form (m) can actually be added to the functioning capital. Key point: whether m "is immediately added on to the capital value in process, and can thus embark on the circuit together with the capital M, making a total magnitude of M, depends on circumstances that are independent of the mere presence of m."

If m is to serve as money capital in a second independent business alongside the first, "it is clear that it can be invested in this only if it possesses the minimal magnitude required for such a business." If it is invested in extending the original business, "then the relationship between the material factors of P, as well as their value relationship, similarly determines a certain minimal magnitude for m."

Marx emphasizes: "Between all means of production operating in this business there is not only a qualitative relation, but also a quantitative ratio, a proportionality." The material factors and the value relationships borne by them "determine the minimum size that m must possess in order to be convertible either into additional means of production and labour-power, or into the former alone, as an increase of productive capital."

His example: "The mill-owner cannot increase the number of his spindles without simultaneously purchasing a corresponding number of carding machines and roving-frames, to say nothing of the increased outlay on cotton and wages that this extension of his business would demand." For him to extend in this way, "the surplus-value must already amount to a fair sum (£1 per additional spindle is generally reckoned on)." As long as m has not reached this minimum size, "the capital circuit must be repeated several times, until the sum of the m's successively produced by it can function together with M in the form M'–C^(L/mp)."

"Even detailed changes in the spinning machinery, for example, that make it more productive, require greater outlay on raw material, extension of the roving machinery, etc."

"In the meantime, therefore, m is stored up, and its accumulation is not its own function, but the result of repeated P...P. Its own function is its persistence in the money state until the repeated circuits of valorization, i.e. an external factor, have added to it sufficiently for it to have attained the minimum magnitude required for it to function actively." At that magnitude, "it can really function for the first time as money capital, i.e. in the given case enter into the function of the money capital M as an accumulated portion of the latter." In the meantime "it is stored up, and exists only in the form of a hoard in the process of formation and growth."

"Thus the accumulation of money, the formation of a hoard, appears here as a process that temporarily accompanies an extension of the scale on which industrial capital operates. Temporarily, because as long as the hoard persists in its state as a hoard, it does not function as capital, does not participate in the valorization process, but remains a sum of money that grows only because money available to it without any effort on its part is cast into the same coffer."

"The form of the hoard is simply the form of money not in circulation, money that is interrupted in its circulation and is therefore preserved in its money form." As far as the process of hoard formation itself is concerned, "this is common to all commodity production, and it is only in the undeveloped pre-capitalist forms of the latter that it plays a role as an end in itself." In our case, "the hoard appears as a form of money capital, and hoard formation as a process that temporarily accompanies the accumulation of capital, because and in so far as money figures here as latent money capital; because the formation of a hoard... is a functionally determined preparatory stage that proceeds outside the circuit of capital, and paves the way for the transformation of surplus-value into really functioning capital."

"This characteristic is what makes it latent money capital, and is also why the scale that it must have attained in order to enter the process is determined by the value composition of the productive capital in each particular case. As long as it persists in the state of a hoard, it does not yet function as money capital, it is still money capital lying fallow; not interrupted in its function, as in the previous case, but rather as yet incapable of performing this function."

Marx distinguishes this "original real form" (a real hoard of money) from other forms. "It can also exist merely in the form of favourable balances, of sums owed to the capitalist who has sold C'." As for other forms — "interest-bearing deposits in a bank, bills of exchange or securities of one kind or other" — these "do not belong here. In that case, the surplus-value realized in money performs particular capital functions outside the circuit of the industrial capital from which it arose; functions which have nothing to do with that circuit as such, and assume the existence of functions of capital distinct from the functions of industrial capital, which have not yet been developed here." This is a deferral to later volumes (credit, interest-bearing capital).

4. THE RESERVE FUND

"The accumulation fund can also perform particular ancillary services, i.e. it can enter into the circulation process of capital, without the latter possessing the form P...P'," i.e. without expanded-scale reproduction. If the process C'–M' extends beyond its normal duration, "then the commodity capital is abnormally delayed in its transformation into the money form; alternatively, if, when the transformation is completed, the price of the means of production into which the money capital must be converted has risen... above the level that it had at the beginning of the circuit, then the hoard that functions as accumulation fund can be used to take the place of money capital, or a part of this. The money accumulation fund then serves as a reserve fund to cope with disturbances in the circuit."

As a reserve fund, it is different from the fund for purchase and payment considered in the circuit P...P. The latter "was a part of the functioning money capital (thus the form of existence of a part of the total capital value in process), the parts of which functioned successively at different points in time. It formed a constant reserve of money capital in the continuity of the production process," since one day money is received and no payments have to be made until later, while another day large quantities of commodities are sold, and only later must large quantities be bought; within these intervals, "a part of the circulating capital always exists in the money form."

The reserve fund, on the other hand, "is not a component part of the functioning capital, or, more precisely, the money capital, but rather capital going through a preliminary stage of its accumulation, surplus-value that has not yet been transformed into active capital." Marx notes practically: "It goes without saying, of course, that when the capitalist is in need, he in no way ponders over the specific functions of the money that he has in his hands, but uses whatever he has in order to get the circulation process of his capital moving again."

In the example: M = £422, M' = £500. If part of the £422 exists as a fund for purchase and payment, "it is reckoned that, with circumstances remaining the same, it will enter as a whole into the circuit, and will also be sufficient for this purpose. The reserve fund, however, is a part of the £78 surplus-value; it can enter the circuit of the capital of £422 only in so far as this circuit is accomplished in altered circumstances; for it is a part of the accumulation fund, and it figures here without an expansion in the scale of reproduction." "In the money accumulation fund, money already exists as latent money capital, and is thus transformed into money capital."

The General Formula

The chapter closes with the general formula that comprises both simple and expanded reproduction:

P...C'–M'–C^(L/mp)...P (P')

And the clarification: "If P = P, then M in C^(L/mp) is M' minus m; if P = P', then M in C^(L/mp) is greater than M' minus m; i.e. m has been wholly or partly transformed into money capital." In other words: if the circuit returns to the same magnitude (simple reproduction), the money advanced for L and mp equals M' minus m (i.e. the original capital value). If the circuit returns to a larger magnitude (expanded reproduction), the money advanced for L and mp exceeds M' minus m — because part or all of m has been added to the capital advanced.

The final sentence: "The circuit of productive capital is the form in which the classical economists have considered the circuit of industrial capital." This is a critical-historical verdict: the classics (Quesnay, Smith, Ricardo, and Marx's predecessors) grasped the circuit in this form, and their failure to distinguish it rigorously from the money circuit generated their confusions (e.g. Smith's "vicious circle" of price-components, the erroneous reduction of value to wages + profit + rent). The chapter thus closes on a polemical note: form II is the practical and historical form of the circuit, but its correct conceptualization requires the critiques of Chapters 1 and 3.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Fundamental Principles Revealed

The chapter yields a set of interconnected systemic laws. First, the primacy of production over circulation within capitalist reproduction. The circuit of productive capital demonstrates that circulation, far from being the substance of capital's movement, is a mediation and an interruption between productive capitals — and yet a necessary mediation, since without C'–M'–C the material replacement of the elements of production cannot occur.

Second, the internal unity of valorization and material replacement. Production (P → C') and circulation (C' → P) are not separate processes but moments of a single reproduction. The formula P...P is therefore the most adequate expression of industrial capital as a self-renewing total process, in which the value relation and the material interchange are inseparably connected.

Third, the critique of the money form's apparent self-sufficiency. Form II "constitutes a critique of form I": the money circuit's apparent self-breeding quality is dispelled once we see money as an evanescent moment that capital is "anxious to get rid of." This is the theoretical foundation for the demystification of interest-bearing capital.

Fourth, the separation of capital value from surplus-value and its consequences. The C'/M' split generates the possibility of bifurcation into C–M–C and c–m–c; the former sustains capital, the latter sustains the capitalist. But because c is "a commodity value that has not cost the capitalist anything," the revenue sphere remains dependent on the circuit's continuity, refuting the notion that capitalist consumption is the engine of the system.

Fifth, the doctrine of the "draft on future labour." The money form is not a pre-existing stock but a claim on present and future labour. This shatters the stock conception of capital and grounds Marx's refutation of the wage-fund theory and of the notion that capital advances to labour from a previously accumulated fund.

Sixth, the theory of crisis location. Crises break out first "in a decline in the number of exchanges of capital for capital," not in a fall in direct consumer demand. The possibility of crisis is structurally inscribed in the separation between sale (C'–M') and consumption, and between the individual circuit and general circulation.

Seventh, the quantitative determination of accumulation. The minimum magnitude required for m to function as capital — determined by the technical and value proportions of the means of production (the mill-owner's spindles, carding machines, roving-frames, raw material) — demonstrates that accumulation is not smooth or continuous but lumpy, requiring the hoarding of latent money capital. Accumulation presupposes a prior phase of dysfunction — money lying fallow.

Eighth, the twofold correction of fetishism: it is as wrong to ascribe money/commodity properties to capital as it is to derive capital's properties from the physical forms of the means of production. Capital is a social relation expressed through, but not identical with, its material bearers.

Broader Practical, Theoretical, and Philosophical Implications

Theoretically, the chapter resolves a foundational problem for the analysis of reproduction as a whole: it establishes that the circuit of productive capital is the most concrete of the three circuits, because it does not abstract from production but incorporates it as the initiating and terminating moment. This makes it the appropriate starting point for the reproduction schemes of Part Three (Volume II, Chs. 18–21), where the interconnections among Departments I and II (means of production vs. means of consumption) are worked out.

Practically, the chapter yields a structural theory of capitalist crises that resists both underconsumptionist and Say's-Law extremes. Because commodities may be sold without being consumed (the merchant purchases yarn that "apparently" goes into consumption), reproduction can proceed "in the most flourishing condition" while unsold commodities pile up — but the reckoning eventually arrives through the "demand for payment," not the demand of consumers. This is a sophisticated argument that locates the point of eruption of crises in the credit and inter-capitalist sphere while acknowledging the underlying anarchy of production.

Philosophically, the chapter reflects Marx's dialectical method at its most rigorous. The formula P...P is not a static identity but a self-moving contradiction: production is interrupted by circulation, circulation by production; the money form is both essential and foreign; surplus-value both exits the circuit (as revenue) and remains bound to it (as latent capital). The "obliteration of origin" when a new circuit begins — the fact that the £500 opening capital hides its £78 surplus-value provenance — captures the fetishistic appearance of capital as a given, self-originating magnitude. Throughout, the chapter insists on the Historische und logische unity of capitalist production: the same process is both a continuous reproduction of value relations and a periodic rupturing of them. In the hoard, the reserve fund, and the crisis, Marx demonstrates that capital's apparent smoothness conceals constitutive instability — the very instability that makes its self-expansion a contradictory and ultimately historical process.
