Volume 2 • Chapter 16: The Turnover of Variable Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Thesis

Chapter 16 of Capital, Volume II, "The Turnover of Variable Capital," is one of the most technically pivotal and philosophically consequential chapters in the entire work, and its central thesis can be stated with deceptive simplicity: there is a rigorous, quantifiable difference between the real rate of surplus-value and the annual rate of surplus-value, and the law governing that difference is S′ = s′n. Marx's stated thesis, given almost verbatim in the text, is that "the annual rate of surplus-value is always s′n, i.e. the real rate of surplus-value produced in a turnover period by the variable capital consumed during this period, multiplied by the number of turnovers of this variable capital during the year." Everything else in the chapter — the numerical examples, the resolution of the paradox, the move to the social standpoint, the railway fever, and the India trade — radiates outward from this one law.

The Central Problem

The chapter opens with what looks like a purely arithmetical puzzle and turns out to be an ideological battlefield. Marx constructs two capitals. Capital A is £500 in variable capital, turning over ten times a year, producing an annual surplus-value of £5,000, and thus yielding an annual rate of surplus-value of 1,000 per cent. Capital B is £5,000 in variable capital, turning over once a year, producing the same annual surplus-value of £5,000, and thus yielding an annual rate of just 100 per cent.

The two capitals are identical in every respect that the theory of surplus-value had, up to this point, declared relevant: the same amount of variable capital is applied each week (£100), the same degree of exploitation obtains (100 per cent), the same number of working days of the same length and the same internal division into necessary and surplus labour. Yet the annual rates differ by 900 percentage points. This is the problem.

And Marx immediately signals that this is not an academic puzzle. He writes that the phenomenon "makes it appear, moreover, as if the rate of surplus-value did not depend only on the amount of variable capital and the rate of exploitation of the labour-power set in motion by it, but also on inexplicable influences deriving from the circulation process; and in fact the phenomenon has been interpreted in this way," and then delivers the charge that "since the beginning of the 1820s, this phenomenon has led to the complete destruction of the Ricardian school." The apparent dependence of the rate of surplus-value on circulation appeared to refute the labour theory of value, because it seemed that something other than labour — the mere speed of the circuit of capital — entered into the determination of the rate. If the rate of exploitation could be doubled or decupled by an act of circulation, then the entire architecture of the labour theory of value stood in jeopardy. This is the intellectual stakes of the chapter. Marx is not merely doing arithmetic; he is rescuing the foundational principle of his whole system.

The Intellectual Objective

The objective is threefold and ascending.

First, to dissolve the paradox by means of a conceptual distinction. The apparent anomaly is entirely generated by the conflation of two different magnitudes: the variable capital advanced and the variable capital applied (i.e. actually functioning in the labour process). Once A and B are genuinely placed "in exactly the same conditions" — that is, once B's £5,000 is spent on labour-power in the same five-week interval as A's £500 — the rates converge. The annual rate is then revealed as a function not of circulation's generative power but of the ratio between what is advanced and what is effectively put to work.

Second, to demonstrate that the annual rate is objective and not merely a bookkeeping artefact. Marx writes that the annual rate "is therefore not something merely subjective, but a comparison produced by the actual movement of capital itself." The owner of capital A receives back his £500 with a £5,000 surplus at year's end; the owner of capital B receives back £5,000 with a £5,000 surplus; the owner of capital C (a 55-week case) has produced £5,000 surplus but has not yet had his advanced capital returned. These are real cash-flow realities, not accounting conventions.

Third, to extend the analysis from the individual capital to society as a whole. Section 3, "The Turnover of Variable Capital Considered from the Social Point of View," treats the turnover of variable capital not as the private rhythm of an individual firm but as a determinant of social reproduction. Here we find workers paid with their own value product, the drain on the market's material elements of production, the communist counterfactual, the pressure on the money market, the railway mania, the absorption and re-release of the reserve army of labour, and finally the international transmission of crises via the Anglo-Indian cotton trade.

How the Chapter Fits into the Broader Theoretical Framework

This chapter is the logical culmination of Volume II, Part Two, on the turnover of capital. In the preceding chapters, Marx treated the turnover of constant and variable circulating capital together, "as a separate theme, without regard to the fixed capital." Here he goes "one step further" and isolates variable capital, disregarding the constant circulating capital that turns over alongside it. The chapter also recapitulates and extends certain results from Volume I: the "General Formula for Capital" (Chapter 4), which established that capital value is always advanced and returns enriched with surplus-value; Chapter 13, which established the law that, with a given rate of surplus-value, the mass of surplus-value is determined by the relative magnitude of the variable capital; and Chapter 23, "Simple Reproduction," from which Marx quotes the passage on production as reproduction and on surplus-value acquiring "the form of a revenue arising out of capital."

Crucially, the chapter also stands as the direct preparation for the theory of the rate of profit in Volume III. Marx is explicit that the illusion of circulation-generated value has been interpreted "in its more complicated and concealed form (that of the annual rate of profit)." By demonstrating that the apparent anomaly is wholly a matter of turnover, Marx is clearing the ground for the later demonstration that profit rates are equalized in a way that has nothing to do with the exploitation of labour, and that the appearance of a profit-bearing productivity in capital itself is a fetishized form masking a social relation. Finally, the manuscript footnote inserted at the end — a note "for future elaboration" — signals that the social analysis points forward to the theory of over-production and realization crises, which "belongs rather to the next Part." The chapter thus functions as a hinge: backwards to the theory of surplus-value, forwards to the theory of profit and the analysis of crisis.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Architecture: The £2,500 Circulating Capital and the Split Between Capital I and Capital II

Marx begins with an apparently modest example meant to clarify the entire architecture of the argument. A circulating capital of £2,500 is advanced, distributed in the organic ratio of four-fifths constant to one-fifth variable: £2,000 constant capital (the material elements of production — raw materials, auxiliary materials) and £500 variable capital (wages). The turnover period is set at five weeks, subdividing into a working period of four weeks and a circulation period of one week.

This subdivision is not incidental; it is the mechanical hinge of the example. Because production runs for four weeks but payment for the product only arrives after a fifth, the capitalist cannot use the same capital continuously. He must split the advance. Capital I is £2,000, the portion actually at work during the four-week working period, itself divided in the 4:1 ratio into £1,600 constant and £400 variable. Capital II is the £500 that keeps the process alive during the circulation period, divided into £400 constant and £100 variable.

The arithmetic then runs as follows: each working week calls for the outlay of a capital of £500 (since capital I plus capital II, divided across the cycle, yields £500 per week). Over a year of fifty weeks, therefore, an annual product of 50 × £500 = £25,000 is produced. The £2,000 of capital I turns over 12½ times (12½ × £2,000 = £25,000), while the total circulating capital of £2,500 turns over 25,000 ÷ 2,500 = 10 times during the year. Of the £25,000 annual product, four-fifths, or £20,000, represents constant capital laid out on means of production, and one-fifth, or £5,000, represents variable capital laid out on wages.

This first set of figures establishes the reference magnitudes on which the whole chapter will operate: a turnover of ten times per year for the total circulating capital, and an annual variable capital outlay of £5,000, financed by a maximum advance of £500 at any moment in time. Note that the OCR of the printed page garbles one figure — the £2,500 total is said to "turn over" with a truncated computation — but the arithmetic is unambiguous: 25,000/2,500 = 10.

The Common Fate and the Divergence of Constant and Variable Fluid Capital in Circulation

Before isolating variable capital, Marx pauses to specify what unites and what distinguishes the two components of circulating capital in the sphere of circulation, because it is precisely this preliminary clarification that licenses the later abstraction.

The variable circulating capital consumed in production can serve again only insofar as the product in which its value is reproduced is sold — that is, transformed from commodity capital into money capital — so that it can be laid out again in wages. But the same is true of the constant circulating capital laid out on materials: its value too must reappear via the sale of the product. What these two parts of the circulating capital share, and what distinguishes them from fixed capital, is not that their transferred value circulates through the circulation of the product. A portion of the product's value — and therefore of the product itself as a commodity — always consists of the wear and tear of fixed capital, i.e. the value the fixed capital has transferred to the product during production. Fixed capital also circulates in the value-form of the product.

The genuine difference is different: the fixed capital "continues to function in the productive process in its old shape through a longer or shorter cycle of turnover periods of the circulating capital," whereas every single turnover presupposes the replacement of the entire circulating capital that enters the circulation sphere from the production sphere in the shape of commodity capital. The distinction between fixed and circulating capital is thus physico-technical, not simply a matter of values changing form.

Marx then decomposes the circulation of circulating capital into its two phases. The first phase, C′–M′ (commodity capital into money capital), is common to both fluid constant and fluid variable capital: the product is sold. In the second phase they separate. The money into which the commodity is transformed is partly converted back into a production stock (circulating constant capital), with different components purchased at different times according to the different terms of purchase. A further part of the money released by the sale remains in the form of a money reserve, to be spent bit by bit in payment for labour-power. This monetary reserve is circulating variable capital.

The essential point, and the warrant for treating both together in the previous chapter, is that "the entire replacement of one or the other part derives each time from the turnover of the capital, its transformation into a product, from product into commodity, and from commodity into money." Constant and variable fluid capital share a rhythm — the turnover rhythm — even though they differ in the role they play in valorization. This shared rhythm is what makes it permissible to abstract from constant circulating capital when we shift attention to the peculiar problem of the variable part.

The Isolation of Variable Circulating Capital and the "Absolutely Exact" Average

Having established this warrant, Marx proceeds to isolate the variable part. His proposal — one that a careless reader will mistake for a trick of bookkeeping — is to "treat the variable part of the circulating capital as if it alone formed the circulating capital, in other words we shall disregard here the constant circulating capital that turns over together with the variable capital."

The operation is then performed. The £2,500 total advanced yields an annual product of £25,000; the variable portion of that product is £5,000; and if we divide the £5,000 by the £500 advanced, we get ten, the number of turnovers. But note the crucial methodological caveat Marx immediately attaches:

"This average calculation, in which the value of the annual product is divided by the value of the capital advanced and not by the value of that part of this capital that is constantly applied in a particular working period (i.e. in this case not by 400 but by 500, not by capital I, but rather by capital I + capital II), is here, where only the production of surplus-value is at issue, absolutely exact. We shall see later on, though, that from another point of view it is inexact, just as this average calculation in general is not quite exact. It is sufficient for the practical purposes of the capitalist, but it does not adequately or precisely express all the real circumstances of the turnover."

This is a remarkable moment of methodological self-consciousness and a vital nuance. Marx distinguishes two levels of exactness. For the specific problem at hand — the production of surplus-value and the determination of the annual rate of surplus-value — the average is exact. For the later problem of the annual rate of profit (which integrates constant capital and the price of production), the same average will prove inexact. Marx is therefore not committing himself to the general truth of the vulgar average; he is marking the boundaries of its validity. This self-limitation is part of what makes the chapter rigorous, and it also anticipates the turn to Volume III.

Introducing Surplus-Value: Capital A and the Definition of the Annual Rate of Surplus-Value

Only now does surplus-value enter the frame — it has been "completely left out of account" up to this point. The example takes on substance: assume the variable capital of £100 laid out each week produces a surplus-value of 100 per cent, i.e. £100. Then the £500 laid out in the course of a five-week turnover period produces a surplus-value of £500; "i.e. half of the working day consists of surplus labour."

Now the decisive step. If a variable capital of £500 produces £500, then a variable capital of £5,000 (applied over the year) produces a surplus-value of ten times £500 = £5,000. But the variable capital advanced — the maximum that must be in hand at any moment — is only £500. And now Marx supplies the central definition:

"The ratio of the total surplus-value annually produced to the value of the variable capital advanced, we call the annual rate of surplus-value. In the present case this is 5,000s/500v = 1,000 per cent."

Analytically, the annual rate is the product of two factors: the rate of surplus-value produced in a single turnover period (100 per cent) and the number of turnovers of the variable capital in the year (ten). This is the seed of the law S′ = s′n. And the mass of surplus-value in a turnover period is determined separately: it equals the variable capital advanced during that period, multiplied by the rate of surplus-value — £500 × 100/100 = £500. If the advanced capital were £1,500, at the same rate, the mass would be £1,500.

Marx now gives this configuration a name: capital A. Its features are: £500 variable capital advanced, ten turnovers per year, £5,000 annual surplus-value, annual rate of surplus-value of 1,000 per cent. This is the "fast" capital.

Capital B and the Shock of the 900-Point Gap

Against capital A Marx now poses capital B. Capital B advances £5,000 of variable capital for the entire year — fifty weeks — and therefore turns over only once. Marx adds two stipulations that make the example maximally favourable to B: the product is paid for at the end of the year on the very day it is finished, so that the money capital into which it is transformed returns the same day, and hence the circulation period is zero; the turnover period is therefore identical with the working period, which is one year.

The terms of comparison are carefully levelled. As in the previous case, £100 is in the labour process each week, hence £5,000 over fifty weeks. The rate of surplus-value is again 100 per cent: with a working day of the same length, half consists of surplus labour. Taking five weeks as the unit, the variable capital applied is £500, the rate of surplus-value is 100 per cent, and the mass of surplus-value created in five weeks is £500. "The amount of labour-power that is exploited, and the degree of its exploitation, are exactly the same here, on the assumptions made, as in capital A."

But the annual rates diverge catastrophically. Forty-one... no — fifty weeks of £100 surplus each produce £5,000. The mass of surplus-value annually produced is thus £5,000 in both cases. But the annual rate of surplus-value differs radically: for B it is 5,000s/5,000v = 100 per cent, whereas for A it was 1,000 per cent.

Marx drives home the identity of the two cases in a single, deliberately repetitive paragraph enumerating every point of identity: same weekly expenditure of £100 variable capital; same degree of valorization; same rate of surplus-value of 100 per cent; same magnitude of variable capital; same quantity of labour-power exploited; same degree and scale of exploitation; same working days, equally divided into necessary and surplus labour; same sum of variable capital applied during the year (£5,000); same amount of labour set in motion by equal capitals; same mass of surplus-value extracted (£5,000). "Yet there is a difference of 900 per cent in the annual rate of surplus-value between A and B."

The presentation of the paradox is the pivot of the chapter. Everything before has been preparation for it; everything after is its resolution.

The Ideological Stakes: the Ricardian Collapse and the Concealed Form of the Annual Rate of Profit

Before resolving the paradox, Marx pauses to name the stakes explicitly, and this is where the chapter ceases to be merely a technical treatise and becomes a work of intellectual history and ideological critique. The phenomenon, he says, "makes it appear... as if the rate of surplus-value did not depend only on the amount of variable capital and the rate of exploitation of the labour-power set in motion by it, but also on inexplicable influences deriving from the circulation process."

The key word is inexplicable. The appearance is not a benign illusion but a scandal: the rate of exploitation — the very heart of the labour theory of value — appears to be determined by a process (circulation) whose only function is to change the form of value, not its magnitude. If circulation can raise the rate of surplus-value, then circulation creates value, and the labour theory collapses.

Marx then adds a historical charge: "Since the beginning of the 1820s, this phenomenon has led to the complete destruction of the Ricardian school." Here the reference is to Volume II's companion text, the Theories of Surplus-Value, Part III, Chapter XX. The Ricardian school had derived profit from unpaid labour, but its members — James Mill, McCulloch, and others — attempted to read the rate of profit directly off the rate of surplus-value without intervening mediation. When confronted with the empirical fact that capitals of equal size, employing equal amounts of labour, yielded rates of profitability disparate enough that no simple proportionality between labour and profit could be maintained, the school disintegrated. Some of its members abandoned the labour theory of value; others drifted into apologetics. The incoherence of the Ricardian average — you cannot explain why a £500 capital deployed ten times should yield the same annual profit as a £5,000 capital deployed once — was fatal.

And Marx adds a crucial observation about the form in which the illusion has propagated: it has been interpreted, "if not in this pure form, then at least in its more complicated and concealed form (that of the annual rate of profit)." The annual rate of surplus-value is the pure — naked — form of the paradox; the annual rate of profit is the dressed-up version, the one in which the appearance of a capital-generated profit enters practical business reasoning. This is a signal that the chapter is the launching pad for the theory of the general rate of profit.

The Resolution: Placing A and B in Identical Conditions

The resolution is announced with a sentence of astonishing economy: "However, its strangeness immediately disappears if we really do place capitals A and B in exactly the same conditions, and do not just appear to do so."

What would "really" placing them in identical conditions mean? The same conditions obtain "only if the variable capital B is wholly spent on payment of labour-power in the same interval of time as capital A." That is: if B stops dribbling out £100 a week and instead spends its entire £5,000 in the same five-week rhythm in which A spends its £500 — which means spending it at a rate of £1,000 per week, £5,000 per five-week period, and £50,000 over the year.

The arithmetic then runs to equality. Over the year, £50,000 is spent on wages. The surplus-value produced, at 100 per cent, is £50,000. The capital turned over (£50,000) divided by the capital advanced (£5,000) yields ten turnovers. The rate of surplus-value, 100 per cent, multiplied by the number of turnovers, ten, yields an annual rate of surplus-value of 1,000 per cent. A and B now have identical annual rates of 1,000 per cent.

But — and here comes the compensating asymmetry — the masses of surplus-value diverge in the ratio of the capitals: for B £50,000; for A £5,000. The ratio is 5,000:500 = 10:1. And this ratio is exactly the ratio in which B set ten times as much labour-power in motion in the same time as A. This is not an anomaly but a straightforward application of the law of surplus-value: masses are proportional to the quantities of labour actually set in motion.

The key principle, underscored by a footnote pointing back to Volume I, Chapter 13: "It is only the capital actually operating in the labour process which creates surplus-value and to which all the laws given for surplus-value apply, including the law that, with a given rate of surplus-value, the mass of surplus-value is given by the relative magnitude of the variable capital."

The Law of Surplus-Value and Labour Measured in Hours

Because everything hinges on the notion of equal quantities of labour actually set in motion, Marx pauses to specify how that is measured. The labour process itself is measured by time. The length of the working day being given (as it is by assumption, in order to isolate the difference in annual rates), the working week consists of a definite number of working days. Alternatively, one may treat each working period — here the five-week period — as a single working day. If the working day is ten hours and the week six working days, the five-week period comprises 300 hours.

Then one must multiply by the number of workers employed side by side in the same labour process. If ten workers work simultaneously, the weekly total is 60 × 10 = 600 labour-hours, and the five-week working period amounts to 600 × 5 = 3,000 labour-hours. This is a crucial clarification because it answers, in advance, a potential objection: equal variable capitals are not equal because they have the same price tag; they are equal because they set in motion equal amounts of labour-power — one labour-power of the given price multiplied by the given number of workers — in the same interval of time. The criterion for equality is not the monetary size of the advance but the quantity of living labour put to work.

The Pivotal Distinction: Capital Advanced versus Capital Applied

This is the conceptual heart of the chapter, and it is here that the paradox is finally dissolved. Marx returns to the original examples and articulates the distinction on which everything turns.

In both A and B, equal variable capitals of £100 per week are applied each week over the year. In both cases, the variable capitals actually functioning in the labour process are identical. But the variable capitals advanced are quite unequal. With A, £500 is advanced every five weeks, of which £100 is applied each week: five weeks' worth of advance sustains five weeks of application, and at the end of each five-week cycle the advance returns. With B, £5,000 has to be advanced at the outset. But out of this £5,000, only £500 (one-tenth) is actually applied in the first five weeks. In the second five-week period, £4,500 remains unapplied and idle, while only £500 is applied, and so on. In each successive period, the same disproportion recurs.

Marx then states the governing principle with great precision:

"The variable capital advanced for a certain period of time is transformed into applied, i.e. really functioning and effective, variable capital, only to the degree that it actually does enter those sections of the period of time in question that are filled by the labour process, and really does function in this labour process. In the intervening period in which a part of it is advanced for application only at a later date, this part is as good as non-existent for the labour process, and thus does not have any influence on the formation of either value or surplus-value."

To illustrate: of A's £500, one-fifth is applied in the first week; four-fifths is advanced without being applied, held in reserve to be applied in the four following weeks. The applied fraction, not the advanced total, is the effective variable capital.

This yields a general law about the determinants of surplus-value:

"The circumstances that differentiate the ratio between the advanced and the applied variable capital affect the production of surplus-value — at a given rate of profit — only in so far as they differentiate the amount of variable capital which can actually be applied in a definite period of time, e.g. in one week, five weeks, etc."

And further:

"The law of surplus-value production is that, with the same rate of surplus-value, equal amounts of functioning variable capital create equal masses of surplus-value. So if equal amounts of variable capital are applied by capitals A and B for the same space of time at the same rate of surplus-value, then they must produce equal amounts of surplus-value in this time, no matter how different may be the ratio between the variable capital applied in the time in question and the variable capital advanced during the same time, and hence how different also the ratio between the mass of surplus-value produced and the total variable capital advanced, rather than that actually applied."

The final clause is the direct refutation of the vulgar reading: the variation in the ratio between surplus-value and advanced capital does not contradict the law of surplus-value but confirms it, and is "an inescapable consequence" of it.

The First Five Weeks of B and the Two Modes of Reckoning

To make the argument concrete, Marx asks us to consider only the first five-week production period of capital B. At the end of week 5, £500 has been applied and consumed. The value produced is £1,000, and 500s/500v = 100 per cent. This is exactly the same as with capital A. The fact that A has realized its surplus-value alongside the capital advanced, whereas B has not, is "of no importance to us here, where the issue is simply the production of surplus-value and its ratio to the variable capital advanced during its production."

Now suppose one instead calculated the ratio of B's surplus-value to the entire £5,000 advanced: 500s/5,000v = 1/10 = 10 per cent. That is, 10 per cent for capital B against 100 per cent for capital A — ten times as much. If the objection is raised that this difference contradicts the law of surplus-value, "the answer is simple, and given by a mere glance at the factual relations." For A, the fraction expresses the actual rate of surplus-value, i.e. the ratio of the surplus-value produced during five weeks by a variable capital of £500 to that variable capital of £500. For B, the mode of reckoning has nothing to do with either the production of surplus-value or the determination of its rate. The £500 surplus-value is being reckoned against a capital of £5,000, "nine tenths of which — i.e. £4,500 — has nothing at all to do with the production of this surplus-value of £500," being designed to function gradually over the following forty-five weeks and not existing at all as far as the first five weeks are concerned.

The methodological lesson here is that the appearance of anomaly is generated by a misreckoning — dividing a surplus-value by a denominator that includes capital that never entered the productive consumption of the surplus-value's production. The illusion is not a fact of reality but an artefact of an illegitimate comparison. Marx will later perform the mirror-image operation on capital A, showing that A can equally be made to look anomalous if the numerator is mismatched to the denominator.

Turning the Problem Round: Capital A as the Anomaly

Marx then compares the annual rates and finds that the ratio between them is unchanged: 10 per cent : 100 per cent = 100 per cent : 1,000 per cent. The ratio is stable.

But, Marx observes, "the problem has now been turned round the other way." For capital B, the annual rate of 5,000s/5,000v = 100 per cent "does not present the slightest divergence — not even the shadow of a divergence — from the laws of surplus-value production." £5,000 was advanced over the year and productively consumed; £5,000 of surplus-value was produced; the rate of surplus-value is exactly the fraction 5,000s/5,000v = 100 per cent. The annual rate agrees with the actual rate. "This time it is not capital B that presents the anomaly to be explained, as it did last time, but rather capital A."

For A, the rate is 5,000s/500v = 1,000 per cent. But where B's £500 surplus-value (produced in five weeks) was previously illegitimately calculated against a £5,000 advance, now A's £5,000 surplus-value is being calculated against a £500 advance — only one-tenth of the variable capital actually applied in producing that £5,000 surplus. For the £5,000s is the product of a variable capital of £5,000 productively consumed over fifty weeks, not of the £500 used in a single five-week period. In the first case (B), the surplus-value of five weeks was reckoned against a capital advanced for fifty weeks, i.e. a capital ten times greater than the one actually used. In the second case (A), the surplus-value of fifty weeks is reckoned against a capital advanced for five weeks, i.e. a capital ten times smaller than the one actually used. The two errors are exact mirror images of one another.

This symmetry is a philosophical payoff: it shows that the entire phenomenon of the "dependence of surplus-value on circulation" is nothing but the reciprocal mismatch of numerators and denominators across different temporal horizons. The rate of surplus-value has no independent circulation component. What does vary — the annual rate — is a determinate function of turnover, not an irrational intrusion of the circulation sphere.

The Two Consequences of A's Five-Week Turnover

Marx now extracts two consequences of the fact that capital A's £500 is advanced for no longer than five weeks, returns at the end of that period, and repeats the same process ten times in a year.

First consequence: advanced capital is only a multiple of the applied capital. In A's case, the capital advanced is only five times greater than the portion of capital applied in any one week's production process — because the advance covers five weeks. In B's case, the capital advanced is fifty times greater than the portion that can ever be applied in a week. This means: "The turnover time thus modifies the ratio between the capital advanced for the production process during the year and the capital applied for any given production period, e.g. a week." And this is precisely what generated the illusion in the first instance — the misleading comparison in which B's five-week surplus-value was reckoned against a fifty-week advance.

Second consequence: the year comprises multiple turnover periods. A's turnover period of five weeks is one-tenth of the year; the year therefore includes ten such periods, in which the same £500 is applied afresh each time. The capital applied over the year is 500 × 10 = £5,000. The capital advanced over the year — in the sense of the maximum required at any moment, in light of the constant reflux — is 5,000 ÷ 10 = £500. Even though the £500 is "always applied afresh, never more than the same £500 is applied every five weeks."

In B's case, though only £500 is applied and advanced for any given five weeks, because the turnover period is fifty weeks the capital applied during the year is the same as the capital advanced not for every five weeks but for fifty. And the mass of surplus-value produced annually is governed, at a given rate of surplus-value, by the capital applied during the year, not by that advanced. Thus the annual surplus-value is no greater for the £5,000 turning over once than for the £500 turning over ten times — "and the only reason why it is the size it is, is that the capital that turns over once in the year is itself ten times greater than that turning over ten times."

The General Law: Annual Turned-Over Variable Capital and the Average Rate of Valorization

This leads to what might be called the generalized theorem of the section. The variable capital turned over during the year — i.e. the part of the annual product, or the annual expenditure, equal to this part — is the variable capital actually applied and productively consumed during the year. It follows that:

"if the variable capital A turned over annually and the variable capital B turned over annually are the same, and they are applied under the same conditions of valorization, the rate of surplus-value must be the same for both; and since the masses of capital applied are the same, so must be the annually reckoned rate of surplus-value, as long as it is expressed as:

mass of surplus-value annually produced / annual turnover of variable capital"

He then generalizes further: "whatever may be the relative magnitudes of the variable capitals turned over, the rate of surplus-value that they produce in the course of a year is determined by the rate of surplus-value at which the respective capitals have operated in average periods (e.g. on a weekly or even daily average)." This introduction of the average period — the weekly or daily average rate — is crucial because it provides a bridge from the idealized examples to the real world of fluctuating rates. And Marx concludes: "This is the only possible result that follows from the laws of surplus-value production and those determining the rate of surplus-value."

Formalizing the Ratio: Numerator, Denominator, and the Number of Turnovers

Marx now undertakes a precise analytical decomposition of the ratio:

annual turnover of capital / capital advanced

The quotient gives the number of turnovers of the capital advanced in one year. For capital A: £5,000 annually turned over / £500 advanced = 10. For capital B: £5,000 annually turned over / £5,000 advanced = 1.

The numerator, he explains, expresses the capital advanced multiplied by the number of turnovers — for A, 500 × 10; for B, 5,000 × 1. Equivalently, it is the capital multiplied by the reciprocal of the turnover time, reckoned on the basis of a year. A's turnover time is 1/10 year; the reciprocal is 10; and 500 × 10 = 5,000. B's turnover time is one year; the reciprocal is 1; and 5,000 × 1 = 5,000.

The denominator expresses the capital turned over multiplied by the reciprocal of the number of turnovers — for A, 5,000 × 1/10; for B, 5,000 × 1. "The respective quantities of labour (the sum of the paid and the unpaid labour) that are set in motion are the same here, since the capitals turned over are the same, and so are their rates of valorization."

Marx draws two further implications from this ratio:

(1) The ratio of the capital that must be advanced to the variable capital applied in a certain working period. If the number of turnovers is ten, and the year is fifty weeks, the turnover time is five weeks. The advance must be five times larger than the variable capital applied weekly. That is, only one-fifth of the advance can be applied in the course of a week. In B's case, where the number of turnovers is one, the turnover time is fifty weeks, and the ratio of advance to weekly application is 50:1. If B were in A's situation, B would need to apply £1,000 each week instead of £100.

(2) The tenfold disproportion of capital needed for the same labour. B applied a capital ten times as great as A — £5,000 rather than £500 — in order to set in motion the same amount of variable capital, the same quantity of labour (paid and unpaid), and thus to produce the same mass of surplus-value in the course of the year. The real rate of surplus-value, by contrast, "expresses nothing more than the ratio of the variable capital applied in a given period of time to the surplus-value produced in the same period; or the mass of unpaid labour that the variable capital applied during this time sets in motion. It has absolutely nothing to do with the portion of variable capital that is advanced during the time in which it is not applied."

Deriving S′ = s′n

Marx now states the general conclusion with full formal precision, and the derivation deserves to be transcribed in full.

Only when the capital advanced turns over exactly once in the year — so that the capital advanced equals the capital turned over — will the annual rate of surplus-value coincide with the real rate.

The derivation proceeds in two steps:

(A) The annual rate of surplus-value =

mass of surplus-value produced during the year / variable capital advanced

But the mass of surplus-value produced during the year equals the real rate of surplus-value multiplied by the variable capital applied in its production. And the capital applied for the production of the annual mass of surplus-value equals the capital advanced multiplied by the number of its turnovers, which Marx calls n.

(B) Substituting, the annual rate of surplus-value becomes:

(real rate of surplus-value × variable capital advanced × n) / variable capital advanced

The v cancels, giving:

S′ = s′n

Verification in the case of B: (100% × 5,000 × 1) / 5,000 = 100 per cent. "Only if n = 1, i.e. if the variable capital advanced turns over only once in the year, and is thus equal to the capital applied or turned over in the year, is the annual rate of surplus-value equal to the real rate of surplus-value."

Marx then consolidates the notation: let the annual rate of surplus-value be S′, the real rate of surplus-value s′, the variable capital advanced v, and the number of turnovers n. Then S′ = s′vn/v = s′n, and S′ = s′ only if n = 1.

Three general relations follow, and they constitute the chapter's terminal theorem on this point:

  • S′ = s′ if n = 1.
  • S′ > s′ if n > 1 — i.e. if the capital advanced turns over more than once in the year, so that the capital turned over is greater than that advanced.
  • S′ < s′ if n < 1 — i.e. if the capital turned over during the year is only a part of the capital advanced, and the turnover period thus lasts longer than a year.

This is one of the most elegant results in all of Capital: the entire apparent dependence of the exploitation rate on circulation is captured by a single scalar multiplier, the number of turnovers, which is itself a purely temporal magnitude.

The Case n < 1: The 55-Week Turnover and Capital C

Having established the three cases, Marx dwells at length on the previously unconsidered case n < 1, since it has not yet been exemplified. He continues all the assumptions of the earlier example but extends the turnover period to fifty-five weeks. The labour process demands £100 in variable capital each week, hence £5,500 for the turnover period, and each week it produces 100s; s′ is thus 100 per cent, as before. The number of turnovers is 50/55 = 10/11, since the turnover time is 55 weeks = 11/10 years, and 50/55 = 10/11.

Applying the formula S′ = s′n:

S′ = 100% × 10/11 = 90 10/11 per cent.

(Here the printed text is garbled — "90rr per cent" and "H years" in the source are OCR corruptions of the fractions 90 10/11 per cent and 1 1/10 years respectively — but the arithmetic is unambiguous.)

The result is less than 100 per cent. Marx explains concretely: if the annual rate of surplus-value were 100 per cent, then 5,500v would have to produce 5,500s in a year, whereas it actually takes 1 1/10 years for this. The £5,500 variable capital produces only £5,000 of surplus-value in the course of the year, giving an annual rate of 5,000s/5,500v = 10/11 = 90 10/11 per cent.

This case is important for a subtle reason. When n > 1, the annual rate inflates the real rate, and one might suspect that the annual rate is a merely "nominal" or "fictitious" magnitude, a bookkeeping exaggeration. The n < 1 case blocks that reading, because it shows the annual rate can equally deflate the real rate. The annual rate is a genuinely two-directional measure, and cannot be reduced to mere inflation. It is, as Marx will insist, an objective magnitude determined by the actual motion of capital.

The Objectivity of the Annual Rate: Advance, Reflux, and the Shilling Analogy

Marx now makes his case for the objectivity of the annual rate and its independence of mere subjectivism. The comparison between surplus-value produced and variable capital advanced (as distinct from variable capital turned over during the year) "is therefore not something merely subjective, but a comparison produced by the actual movement of capital itself."

He illustrates with the concrete fate of each capitalist:

  • The owner of capital A receives back at year's end his variable capital of £500, together with a surplus-value of £5,000. What expresses the size of the capital he has advanced is not the quantity of capital he has applied during the year, but that which periodically flows back to him. Whether the capital exists at the end partly as production stock and partly as commodity or money capital is irrelevant, as is the ratio in which it is divided between these portions.
  • The owner of capital B receives back £5,000, his advanced capital, together with £5,000 surplus-value.
  • The owner of capital C (the £5,500 case) has produced £5,000 surplus-value during the year (a £5,000 outlay at 100 per cent rate), "but his capital advanced has not yet returned to him, and so neither has the surplus-value it has produced."

This trio of concrete end-of-year positions anchors the abstract formula in the tangible reality of the capitalist's cash flow. The "advance" is not a theoretical fiction; it is the money that must be laid out and that periodically returns. And the periodicity of the return is precisely the turnover period.

Marx then returns to the deeper conceptual structure, recapitulating the meaning of "advance." S′ = s′n expresses the fact that the rate of surplus-value on the variable capital applied during a turnover period must be multiplied by the number of turnover or reproduction periods of the variable capital advanced. And he grounds this by citing Volume I, Chapter 4 ("The General Formula for Capital") and Chapter 23 ("Simple Reproduction"), where it was established that capital value is always advanced and not genuinely spent — it returns enriched with surplus-value. "The time that elapses between its point of departure and its point of return is the time for which it is advanced. The entire circuit which the capital value undergoes, measured by the time from its advance to its reflux, forms its turnover, and the duration of this turnover is a turnover period."

Once a turnover period has elapsed, the same capital value can begin the same circuit afresh, "and thus also valorize itself afresh and again produce surplus-value." So if the variable capital turns over ten times in the year, the mass of surplus-value produced is ten times that corresponding to one turnover period. And now Marx asks the deepest question of the section: "The nature of the advance must now be investigated from the standpoint of capitalist society as a whole."

The investigation proceeds: capital A, turning over ten times, is advanced ten times in the course of the year. But at the same time, all its owner ever advances during the year is the same capital value of £500, and all he ever has at his disposal is £500. Once this £500 has completed a circuit, he lets the same circuit begin anew. If the turnover needed a longer period, the £500 would be insufficient; if shorter, part of the capital would be superfluous. "It is not ten capitals of £500 that are advanced, but one capital of £500 advanced ten times in succession at different intervals of time." Hence — and this is the decisive point — the annual rate of surplus-value is not calculated on a capital of £500 advanced ten times, i.e. on £5,000, but rather on a capital of £500 advanced once.

The famous analogy follows: "just as, when a shilling circulates ten times, there is still only one shilling in circulation, even though it performs the functions of ten shillings. However, no matter in whose hand it exists for the moment, it remains as always the same identical value of one shilling." The identity of the value across its successive turnovers is what makes it the same advanced capital, not ten capital advances. Capital A shows each time it returns — including its final return at year's end — that its owner has always operated with the same capital value of £500. All he ever receives back each time is £500. The capital he advances is therefore never more than £500.

Marx now arrives at a secondary formula. Since s′ = s/v, we can substitute in s′n the equivalent of s′, giving:

S′ = sn/v

This formula is worth noting because it brings the mass of surplus-value s into direct relation with the variable capital advanced v, mediated not by the real rate but by the rate governing the annual rotation. But the meaning is unchanged: the £500 capital, by turning over ten times, performs the function of a capital ten times as great — of £5,000. 500 shilling pieces turning over ten times in the year perform the same function as 5,000 turning over only once.

Section 2: The Individual Variable Capital — Wages Consumed, Labour-Power Annihilated and Renewed

Section 2, "The Turnover of an Individual Variable Capital," shifts the analytical register from the abstract formula to a fine-grained examination of what happens to the substance of variable capital as it turns over. It opens with a substantial quotation from Volume I, Chapter 23, which sets the philosophical stage: "Whatever the social form of the production process, it has to be continuous, it must periodically repeat the same phases... When viewed, therefore, as a connected whole, and in the constant flux of its incessant renewal, every social process of production is at the same time a process of reproduction... As a periodic increment of the value of the capital, or a periodic fruit borne by capital-in-process, surplus-value acquires the form of a revenue arising out of capital."

This quotation is doing a great deal of work. It establishes that production, viewed as a connected whole, is reproduction — a periodic, rhythmic process — and that surplus-value is accordingly a periodic, rhythmic output. Surplus-value is not a one-off event but a harvest, and this is why the annual rate can be understood as a multiplication of the rate per harvest by the number of harvests.

Marx then walks carefully through the ten five-week turnover periods of capital A. In the first turnover period, £500 of variable capital is advanced; i.e. £100 is converted each week into labour-power, so that at the end of the period £500 has been spent on labour-power. Of this £500: it has ceased to be capital; it has been paid out in wages. The workers pay it out again in purchasing means of subsistence and consume them. A mass of commodities to the value of £500 is thereby annihilated (with the qualification that what the worker may save as money is also not capital). The mass of commodities is consumed unproductively as far as the worker is concerned — except insofar as he thereby maintains his labour-power in working condition, which is an indispensable instrument for the capitalist.

But, secondly, this £500 is converted, for the capitalist, into labour-power of the same value (or price). He consumes the labour-power productively in the labour process. At the end of the five weeks, a value product of £1,000 exists. Half of this, £500, is the reproduced value of the variable capital spent as wages. The other half, £500, is newly produced surplus-value.

A crucial ontological point now follows. The five weeks' labour-power — the very substance into which the variable capital was converted — is spent or consumed, even if productively. "The labour active yesterday is not the same labour as is active today." Its value, together with the surplus-value it created, now exists as the value of a thing distinct from labour-power — the product. But because the product is transformed into money, the part of its value equal to the variable capital advanced is converted once more into labour-power and hence functions afresh as variable capital. And here an important qualification: whether the same workers are re-engaged, "i.e. the same bearers of labour-power," is beside the point. New workers may be employed in the second turnover period. The identity that matters is not the identity of the human bearers but the identity of the value magnitude passing through the cycle.

Marx now draws the starkest formulation of the difference between the advance and the consumption: "In fact, therefore, in the course of the ten five-week turnover periods a capital of £5,000 is successively spent on wages, and not one of £500, these wages being spent again by the workers on means of subsistence. The capital of £5,000 advanced in this way is consumed. It no longer exists. On the other hand, it is labour-power to the value of £5,000, and not just £500, that is successively incorporated into the production process, not only reproducing its own value of £5,000, but producing in addition to this a surplus-value of £5,000."

The substance of the variable capital is destroyed; the form of capital is continuously re-established. The £500 is not preserved as capital; it is consumed as wages and replaced by a new value. And this is what reproduction means.

Section 2 Continued: Newly Produced Capital and the Divergence Between A and B in the Form of the Replacement Value

Marx now sharpens the analysis by showing that in each successive turnover period a genuinely new capital is operating.

The £500 advanced in the second turnover period is not the identical capital of £500 advanced in the first. The latter has been consumed, spent on wages. But it has been replaced by a new variable capital of £500, produced in the first turnover period in the commodity form and then transformed back into the money form. "This new money capital of £500 is therefore the money form of the mass of commodities newly produced in the first turnover period."

A decisive observation follows: the fact that an identical money sum of £500 exists once more in the hands of the capitalist — assuming we disregard the surplus-value, the same amount of money capital as he originally advanced — conceals the fact that he is operating with a newly produced capital. This is a specific instance of the fetishism of capital: the numerical identity of the money sum conceals the substantive identity-difference, the fact that this £500 is a different £500, the money-form of a different produce.

Marx hastens to add, parenthetically, that this does not hold for the constant parts of the capital: for those, the value is not newly produced — "it is only the form in which the value exists that is changed." In this parenthesis we see the deep distinction between constant and variable circulating capital: only the variable part is newly produced in value terms.

Then he takes the third turnover period: "Here it is evident that the variable capital of £500 advanced for the third time is not an old capital, but one newly produced, for it is the money form of the mass of commodities produced in the second turnover period and not in the first turnover period." The part of their value equal to the variable capital advanced was converted into new labour-power for the second turnover period and produced a new mass of commodities; this was sold, and a part of their value forms the £500 advanced in the third period.

This pattern holds for all ten turnover periods: every five weeks, newly produced masses of commodities — whose value, insofar as it replaces variable capital, is also newly produced — are thrown on the market, so that ever new labour-power can be incorporated. What is attained by the tenfold turnover is therefore NOT that the £500 can be productively consumed ten times over, nor that a variable capital sufficient for five weeks can be applied for fifty. "In fact, 10 × £500 of variable capital is applied in the fifty weeks; the capital of £500 is only ever sufficient for five weeks, and must be replaced at the end of these five weeks with a newly produced capital of £500."

So far, capital A and capital B are identical. "But now comes the difference."

At the close of the first five-week section, a variable capital of £500 has been advanced and spent in both B and A. For both, its value has been converted into labour-power and replaced by a part of the value of the newly produced product equal to that of the advanced variable capital. For both, the labour-power has not only replaced the £500 with a new value of the same amount, but added a surplus-value of the same size.

The difference is in the form in which the replacement value exists. In case B, the value product that replaces the variable capital advanced and adds the surplus-value "does not exist in the form in which it can function once again as productive capital, i.e. as variable capital." For A, it does. For B, through the end of the year, the variable capital spent in the first five weeks — and successively every five weeks — is replaced by newly produced value and surplus-value, but not in the form in which it can function as productive capital, and in particular as variable capital. Its value has certainly been replaced and thus renewed, but the form of its value — its money form — has not been renewed.

So for the second period of five weeks, and successively for every five weeks during the year, a further £500 must be on hand, just as for the first period. "If we ignore credit, then £5,000 must be on hand at the beginning of the year, and exist as latent money capital advanced, even though it is only actually spent and converted into labour-power bit by bit in the course of the year." In case A, by contrast, "since the circuit or turnover of the capital advanced has been completed, the replacement value already exists, after five weeks have elapsed, in the form in which it can set in motion new labour-power for five weeks: in its original money form."

Marx then explores the ordinary appearance of things to guard against a misunderstanding. In both cases, new labour-power is consumed in the second five-week period, and a new capital of £500 is spent in wages. The workers' means of subsistence paid for with the first £500 have disappeared; the second £500 serves to buy new labour-power and withdraw new means of subsistence from the market. "In short, a new capital of £500 is spent, not the old one." But in case A, this new capital is the money form of the newly produced replacement value for the £500 spent previously; in case B, the replacement value exists in a form in which it cannot function as variable capital. An additional capital of £500 must therefore be available in the money form — "which is here unavoidable" — to continue the production process for the next five weeks, and it must be advanced as such.

So the same quantity of variable capital is spent in fifty weeks in B as in A; the same quantity of labour-power is paid for and used. But in B this has to be paid for with a capital advance equal to its entire value, £5,000, whereas in A it is paid for successively by the ever renewed money form of the replacement value produced every five weeks for the £500 advanced for each five weeks. In A, therefore, the money capital advanced is never greater than that needed for five weeks — never greater than the £500 advanced for the first five weeks. This £500 is sufficient for the whole year.

Section 2 Continued: Latent Money Capital, Inverse Proportion, and the Immateriality of the Form of Replacement

The result: with the same degree of exploitation, i.e. the same real rate of surplus-value, the annual rates in A and B stand in inverse proportion to the magnitudes of the variable money capitals advanced to set in motion the same quantity of labour-power over the year:

  • A: 5,000s/500v = 1,000 per cent
  • B: 5,000s/5,000v = 100 per cent
  • And 500v : 5,000v = 1:10 = 100 per cent : 1,000 per cent.

The distinction arises from the divergence in the turnover periods — "i.e. the intervals at which the replacement value of the variable capital applied in a certain period of time can function afresh as capital, and therefore as new capital." With both A and B there is the same replacement value for the variable capital applied during the same period, and the same additional surplus-value. But with B, "even though every five weeks there is a replacement value of £500, plus £500 surplus-value, this replacement value does not yet form any new capital, since it does not exist in the money form." In A, by contrast, the old capital value "is not only replaced by a new one, but is re-established in its money form, and hence replaced as new capital capable of performing its function."

Marx then formulates one of the most important and philosophically charged conclusions of the chapter: "The earlier or later transformation of the replacement value into money, and hence into the form in which the variable capital is advanced, is evidently a circumstance quite immaterial to the production of surplus-value. The latter depends on the magnitude of the variable capital applied, and on the level of exploitation of labour. But the circumstance mentioned above does modify the size of the money capital that has to be advanced in order to set in motion a definite amount of labour-power in the course of the year, and in this way it does affect the annual rate of surplus-value."

This is the philosophical verdict, and its two-part structure is critical: 1. The production of surplus-value is entirely a matter of the labour process — of the magnitude of applied variable capital and the degree of exploitation. 2. The annual rate is affected by the form of the replacement value — by whether the reflux arrives in time to be applied in the next period.

The chapter thus preserves the priority of production over circulation — the production of surplus-value owes nothing to circulation — while admitting that the annual measure of surplus-value is determined by turnover, which is jointly determined by production (working period) and circulation (circulation period). This distinction between the production of a magnitude and the temporal measurement of its accumulated total is the analytic hinge of the entire chapter.

Section 3: The Social Standpoint — Workers Paid With Their Own Value Product

Section 3, "The Turnover of Variable Capital Considered from the Social Point of View," ascends from the individual firm to the totality of social reproduction. The scenario is specified with unusual concreteness: a worker costs £1 per week; the working day is ten hours; both A and B employ 100 workers throughout the year (£100 per week for 100 workers, making £500 for five weeks and £5,000 for fifty weeks); each worker works 60 hours in a six-day week. 100 workers perform 6,000 hours' labour per week and therefore 300,000 hours' labour in fifty weeks. This labour-power is requisitioned by A and B and "cannot be spent by the society on anything else." In this respect the matter is the same for both.

Moreover, in both cases each 100 workers receive a yearly wage of £5,000 — so the 200 workers together receive £10,000 — and withdraw means of subsistence to this value from the society. In this respect, too, the matter is equivalent from the social standpoint. Since the workers are paid by the week, they also withdraw means of subsistence from society each week, and each week they cast into circulation in return their money equivalent.

"But now comes the difference."

Marx states the first distinction: the money that the workers under capital A cast into circulation is not only — as for the workers under capital B — the money form of the value of their labour-power (in actual fact a means of payment for labour already performed). From the second turnover period onward, reckoning from the opening of the business, it is the money form of their own value product (= price of labour-power plus surplus-value) in the first turnover period, which pays for their labour during the second turnover period.

With capital B the position is different. Here, too, the money is a means of payment for labour already performed, but this labour is not paid for with their own value product turned into money. "This can only start to happen from the second year onwards, when the workers under capital B are paid with their own value product of the previous year, converted into money."

This is a profound structural observation. Under a short-turnover regime, the workers are effectively paid out of the produce of their own recent labour — the money in the capitalist's hand is the realized value of the product they made five weeks ago, and by the time they are paid for the current week, their previous product has already been sold. Under a long-turnover regime, they are paid out of the capitalist's original advance, and it will be a year before their own product's money form returns to pay them. They are working on the basis of a fund that is not, in its current form, the result of their own immediate labour.

Marx then draws out the general implications:

"The shorter the turnover period of the capital — and hence the shorter the intervals at which its reproduction period is repeated in the course of the year — the sooner is the variable part of the capital originally advanced by the capitalist in the money form transformed into the money form of the value product created by the worker as a replacement for this variable capital (this product also including surplus-value); the shorter, too, is the time for which the capitalist has to advance money from his own funds, and the smaller the total capital that he advances in relation to the given scale of production; the relatively greater, therefore, is the mass of surplus-value that the capitalist extracts in the course of the year, at a given rate of surplus-value, since he can buy the workers all the more often, and set their labour in motion, with the money form of their own value product."

Two further economic relations follow: - "At a given scale of production, the absolute size of the variable money capital advanced (and so of the circulating capital in general) is reduced in proportion to the brevity of the turnover period, and the annual rate of surplus-value correspondingly grows." - "With a given volume of capital advanced, the scale of production grows, and hence, with a given rate of surplus-value, the absolute mass of the surplus-value produced in one turnover period also grows, and there occurs, simultaneously with this, a rise in the annual rate of surplus-value caused by the reduction in the reproduction period."

The general result: "according to the varying magnitudes of the turnover period, money capitals of very different scale have to be advanced, in order to set in motion the same volume of productive circulating capital and the same amount of labour, given the same level of exploitation of labour."

Section 3 Continued: The Drain on the Market, the Communist Counterfactual, and post festum Rationality

Marx now articulates the second distinction, explicitly related to the first. In both cases, the workers pay for the means of subsistence they buy with the variable capital that is transformed in their hands into means of circulation. They withdraw wheat from the market and replace it with an equivalent in money. But since the money with which the workers employed by capital B pay for their means of subsistence is not the money form of their own value product cast into the market during the year — as is the case with A's workers — it follows that although they supply the seller of their means of subsistence with money, they do not supply any commodity — either means of production or means of subsistence — which he could buy with the money provided, which is the position with A.

The consequence is staggering in its systemic implications: "labour-power, means of subsistence for this labour-power, fixed capital in the form of the means of labour applied under capital B, and production materials, are all withdrawn from the market, and an equivalent in money is cast into the market to replace them with; but no product is cast into the market during the year in question to replace the material elements of productive capital withdrawn from it."

This is a profound observation about real versus monetary replacement. In B's case, the productive resources of society are being drawn down and, in the year in question, not replenished by any product. A monetary equivalent is put into circulation, but a monetary equivalent is not a product — it is a claim on products. The replacement of the material elements has been deferred.

Marx now turns to the communist counterfactual, which crystallizes the analysis. "If we were to consider a communist society in place of a capitalist one, then money capital would immediately be done away with, and so too the disguises that transactions acquire through it. The matter would be simply reduced to the fact that the society must reckon in advance how much labour, means of production and means of subsistence it can spend, without dislocation, on branches of industry which, like the building of railways, for instance, supply neither means of production nor means of subsistence, nor any kind of useful effect, for a long period, a year or more, though they certainly do withdraw labour, means of production and means of subsistence from the total annual product."

This passage is one of the great anticipations of socialist planning theory in Capital. Under communism, the same real problem exists — long-period investments withdraw real resources and supply nothing in the interim — but it is visible and can be solved by ex ante planning (reckoning in advance). Under capitalism, the same problem takes a disguised form: money mediates, and it appears as a purely financial phenomenon. "In capitalist society, on the other hand, where any kind of social rationality asserts itself only post festum, major disturbances can and must occur constantly."

Marx glosses post festum in a footnote — literally "after the feast," a favourite expression of his, in the sense of "too late to have any effect." This is one of the most concentrated statements of his theory of economic dynamics: capitalist rationality is retrospective, ex post facto, embodied in price adjustments that come too late to prevent the disruption. The market coordinates only by crashing.

The mechanism of the disturbance is then traced. On the one hand there is pressure on the money market, while conversely the absence of this pressure itself calls into being a mass of such undertakings, and therefore the precise circumstances that later provoke a pressure on the money market. Marx notes the reflexive, self-undermining character of the cycle: the very conditions that permit speculative investment create the conditions that will later trigger the crisis. The money market is under pressure because large-scale advances of money capital for long periods of time are always needed here — and this is "quite apart from the fact that industrialists and merchants throw the money capital they need for the carrying on of their businesses into railway speculations, etc., and replace it with loans from the money market." Capital is diverted from productive use into speculation and reconstituted by credit, compounding the pressure.

The other side of the coin is pressure on the society's available productive capital. Since elements of productive capital are constantly being withdrawn from the market and all that is put into the market is an equivalent in money, the effective demand rises without this in itself providing any element of supply. Hence prices rise, both for means of subsistence and for the material elements of production.

Section 3 Continued: The Railway Fever, Speculation, and the Reserve Army of Labour

The analysis of the trade cycle then unfolds with almost journalistic concreteness. During this period there are "regular business swindles, and great transfers of capital. A band of speculators, contractors, engineers, lawyers, etc. enrich themselves. These exert a strong consumer demand on the market, and wages rise as well."

As far as foodstuffs are concerned, agriculture is given a boost. But since foodstuffs cannot be suddenly increased within the year, imports grow, as well as the import of exotic foods (coffee, sugar, wine, etc.) and objects of luxury. "Hence over-supply and speculation in this part of the import trade."

On the other hand, in those branches of industry in which production can be increased more quickly — "manufacture proper, mining, etc." — the price rise leads to sudden expansion, soon followed by collapse. The same effect occurs on the labour market, drawing great numbers of the latent relative surplus population, and even workers already employed, into the new lines of business.

Then Marx gives a memorable illustration of the specificity of labour demand. "Undertakings of this kind, such as railways, generally withdraw from the labour market on a large scale a certain quantity of force, which can derive only from branches such as agriculture, etc., where only strong lads are needed." This is a concrete, physiologically-grounded observation about the kind of labour-power that large infrastructural projects require. This still occurs even after the new undertakings have already become an established branch of industry and the migrant working class needed for them has already been formed — e.g. when railway construction is temporarily pursued on a scale greater than the average.

A part of the reserve army of workers whose pressure keeps wages down is absorbed. Wages generally rise, even in the formerly well-employed sections of the labour market. "This lasts until, with the inevitable crash, the reserve army of workers is again released and wages are pressed down once more to their minimum and below it."

Marx then inserts (in a manuscript footnote, "for future elaboration") one of his most celebrated statements of the contradictions of the capitalist mode of production:

"Contradiction in the capitalist mode of production. The workers are important for the market as buyers of commodities. But as sellers of their commodity — labour-power — capitalist society has the tendency to restrict them to their minimum price. Further contradiction: the periods in which capitalist production exerts all its forces regularly show themselves to be periods of over-production; because the limit to the application of the productive powers is not simply the production of value, but also its realization. However the sale of commodities, the realization of commodity capital, and thus of surplus-value as well, is restricted not by the consumer needs of society in general, but by the consumer needs of a society in which the great majority are always poor and must always remain poor. This however belongs rather to the next Part."

This footnote is a perfect encapsulation of the tensions the section has developed. It identifies two "contradictions": 1. The buyer/seller contradiction: workers must buy, but their wages are pressed to a minimum; consumption capacity is limited by the wage relation. 2. The realization contradiction: expansions of production capacity, by their very energy, produce over-production — not over-production relative to "consumer needs of society in general," but over-production relative to a capitalistically distributed social need, in which the great majority are "always poor and must always remain poor."

The chapter thus closes its social section by gesturing beyond itself, to the theory of crises "belonging to the next Part."

The Determinants of the Length of the Turnover Period

The concluding pages of the chapter provide a systematic taxonomy of the causes of turnover-length variation, which in turn determine the magnitude of the divergence between S′ and s′. Marx identifies three sources, each with its own logic.

(1) The working period in the strict sense — "the period needed to prepare the product for the market" — depends on "the material conditions of production in the various spheres of capital investment, as these are given at the time." Crucially, Marx differentiates by branch: "In agriculture these have more the character of natural conditions of production; in manufacture, and for the most part in the extractive industries too, they change with the social development of the productive process itself." There is thus a historical element: the working period is more malleable, more subject to technical transformation, in industry than in agriculture. This grounds the turnover in a material-technical base while admitting historicity.

(2) The size of deliveries — the quantitative scale on which the product is thrown onto the market — introduces a conventional element: "this has a conventional character." Marx immediately guards against the conclusion that the turnover length is therefore arbitrary: "But the convention itself has as its material basis the scale of production, and is therefore accidental only if considered in isolation." Conventions then are not free-floating; they are grounded in material scale, and appear arbitrary only under the abstracting gaze. This is a methodological caveat that pre-empts the objection that the entire analysis rests on conventional magnitudes.

(3) The length of the circulation period — which is partly conditioned by the constant change in market conditions, the greater or lesser ease of selling, and the necessity of casting the product partly on nearer and partly on more distant markets. "Apart from the scale of demand in general, the movement of prices plays a major role here. Sales are deliberately restricted when prices are falling, while production goes ahead; and the converse occurs when prices are rising, when production and sale keep in step, or selling even takes place in advance." Note here a striking observation about speculative timing: production and sale need not move in step, and the deliberate desynchronization of sale and production is a normal business strategy under price uncertainty. And "the actual distance of the place of production from the market outlet should be considered as a specific material basis."

The India Trade: Credit, Distance, and the International Transmission of Crisis

Marx then illustrates the third determinant with the extended example of the Anglo-Indian cotton trade — a case study of how a lengthy circulation period, mediated by credit, generates financial pressure and international crisis transmission. This is one of the most developed concrete analyses in all of Volume II.

The mechanism is set out in stages. English cotton cloth or yarn is sold to India. The export merchant pays the English cotton manufacturer. But — a parenthetical of importance — "He does this willingly only when the situation on the money market is favourable. As soon as the manufacturer himself replaces his money capital by credit operations, things start to go wrong." The exporter then sells the cotton goods on the Indian market, from where the capital he advanced is remitted. "Until this reflux, the situation is just the same as one in which the length of the working period requires a new advance of money capital in order to keep the production process going on the same scale."

Two crucial consequences follow:

(a) The manufacturer's wage fund is not the money form of his own product. "The money capital with which the manufacturer pays his workers and replaces the other elements of his circulating capital is not the money form of the yarn that he produced. This can only be the case after the value of this yarn has returned to England in money or products. It is additional money capital, as before." The distinction, however, is that "instead of the manufacturer it is the merchant who advances it, and he may well have obtained it himself by credit operations." Marx thus identifies an institutional locus of advance — the merchant, and behind the merchant, the credit system — that substitutes for the manufacturer in performing the function of carrying the long circulation period.

(b) The drain on the English market. "Until this money has been cast into the market, no additional product has been put on the English market that could be bought with this money and enter the sphere of production or individual consumption. If this condition sets in for a long while and on a large scale, then it must lead to the same results as the prolonged working period did previously." The analysis directly mirrors the earlier analysis of long-period investment: exported goods have withdrawn real product from the domestic market, and no replacement product has appeared.

Marx then shows how the phenomenon acquires additional complexity through the credit chain. The yarn may be sold on credit in India itself; with this credit, products are bought in India and sent as a return shipment to England, or else drafts are remitted to this amount. "If this process is delayed, then pressure builds up on the Indian money market, which may react on England to produce a crisis here. This crisis, in its turn, even if it is combined with the export of precious metals to India, provokes a new crisis in that country, on account of the bankruptcy of English firms and their Indian branches, who were given credit by Indian banks. Thus a simultaneous crisis arises both on the market for which the trade balance is unfavourable, and on that for which it is favourable."

The last clause is remarkable: crises can strike both partners simultaneously, regardless of the direction of the trade balance. "This phenomenon can be still more complicated. England may have sent silver bullion to India, but India's English creditors now press their demands here, and in a short while India will have to send its silver back to England." The bullion flows reverse — the metal goes to India, then must return to satisfy English demands — illustrating the turbulence of the credit-driven adjustment.

Marx then describes the deceptive balance of trade. It can happen that the export trade to India and the import trade from India are in approximate balance, "even though the size of the latter (with the exception of special circumstances such as an increase in cotton prices, etc.) is determined by the former, and stimulated by it." The trade balance between England and India may appear in equilibrium, or exhibit only weak fluctuations. "But once the crisis breaks out in England, it becomes clear that unsold cotton goods are being stored up in India (goods which have therefore not been transformed from commodity capital into money capital — over-production on this side) and that on the other hand there are not only unsold stocks of Indian products in England, but a major part of stocks sold and consumed have not yet been paid for."

The conclusion is the theoretical payoff of the section:

"Thus what appears as a crisis on the money market in actual fact expresses anomalies in the production and reproduction process itself."

This is a direct application of the framework developed throughout the chapter: the money market is the phenomenal form; the production and reproduction process is the essence. The crisis is not a purely financial event, but a financial expression of the underlying disequilibrium in production and reproduction. The merchant credit that masks the long circulation period eventually exposes, in the form of a financial crisis, the fact that products have been produced and shipped but not realized — that commodity capital has not been transformed into money capital.

The Final Point: Self-Supply Out of Its Own Product

The chapter's final paragraph presents one more precise consequence of turnover-length: "Thirdly, in relation to the actual circulating capital applied (both variable and constant), the length of the turnover period, in so far as it derives from the length of the working period, leads to the distinction that, with a greater number of turnovers in the course of the year, an element of the variable or constant circulating capital can be supplied by way of its own product, as with the production of coal, of ready-made clothes, etc. In other situations this is not the case, at least not within the year."

The point is elegant and often overlooked. In branches whose own outputs can serve as inputs to their own reproduction — coal mining (whose product can be burned to power further mining) and ready-made clothing manufacture (whose output can clothe the workforce directly) — a rapid turnover allows the branch to supply part of its own circulating capital out of its own product. Short turnover periods therefore have a material advantage independent of the money-form question: the branch can become partially self-reproducing in physical inputs, reducing the drain on the rest of the economy. This marks an important boundary condition of the whole analysis and shows that the concrete advantages of rapid turnover are not merely financial.

This final observation also connects the whole chapter back to the materialist foundations of Marx's method. Circulation, credit, and money are all real; the annual rate of surplus-value is a real magnitude; the money market pressure is real. But the fundamental determinants are always material and social: the physical conditions of production, the length of the working period, the quantity of living labour set in motion, the social distribution of consumption and purchasing power. That is the ultimate lesson of the chapter.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles Revealed by the Chapter

The first and central principle is the law of the annual rate of surplus-value, S′ = s′n, and the three-case theorem that follows: S′ = s′ when n = 1, S′ > s′ when n > 1, and S′ < s′ when n < 1. This law dissolves the entire apparent dependence of the rate of surplus-value on the circulation process. It demonstrates that the rate of surplus-value can appear to diverge wildly between capitals employing equal amounts of labour only because of a mismatch between the numerator (surplus-value produced in a period) and the denominator (variable capital advanced over a different period). The divergence is an artefact of the ratio between advanced and applied variable capital — which is itself a function of turnover — and not a real generative power of circulation.

The second fundamental principle is the ontological priority of production over circulation. Surplus-value is created only in the labour process, only by living labour, and only to the extent that the variable capital is actually functioning there. Capital advanced but not applied — B's £4,500 "as good as non-existent for the labour process" — contributes nothing to value or surplus-value. This is not merely a quantitative law but an ontological one: it establishes that the productive power of capital is the productive power of the labour it sets in motion. Everything else — the money form, the reflux, the credit system, the annual measurement — is derivative.

The third principle is the objectivity of the annual rate. The comparison between surplus-value produced and capital advanced is "not something merely subjective, but a comparison produced by the actual movement of capital itself." This claim is philosophically important because it resists both horns of a false dilemma. The annual rate is not the "real rate" (which would collapse the distinction), but neither is it an arbitrary accounting construct. It is a real, determinate magnitude indexed to the actual motion of capital — to the advance and the reflux. Marx anchors this objectivity in the concrete positions of capitalists A, B, and C at the end of the year: each has composed a determinate ratio of returned capital to surplus-value realized. The formula is a description of these positions, not an imposition upon them.

The fourth principle is the materiality of turnover determinants. The length of the turnover period — which governs the magnitude of S′ relative to s′ — is determined by material conditions of production (working-period length), by the conventionally-mediated quantitative scale of deliveries, and by the market- and distance-conditions that govern the circulation period. Marx's taxonomy shows that turnover is not a contingent factor but a material-technical-social variable, and its analysis is therefore susceptible to determinate historical specification.

Broader Theoretical Implications

The most direct theoretical implication is that the chapter prepares the theory of the rate of profit for Volume III. The annual rate of surplus-value is the "pure form" of the paradox; the annual rate of profit is its "more complicated and concealed form." By showing that the annual rate of surplus-value is determinable by the law S′ = s′n, Marx eliminates one source of apparent anomaly in the profit rate and shows that whatever remains cannot be attributed to the circulation process. When profit rates are equalized across branches with different organic compositions and different turnover periods, the equalization cannot be attributed to any labour-magnitude — it must be attributed to the competitive formation of prices of production. This chapter clears away the red herring of turnover from the theory of exploitation.

The second theoretical implication is that the chapter's analysis of the social standpoint prepares the analysis of realization crises. The distinction between A and B is not merely an accounting distinction; it has real consequences for the material supply of the market. In B's case, productive resources are withdrawn and not replaced within the year — a structural condition that, when generalized across many branches, generates the kind of disequilibrium that expresses itself as money-market pressure. The railway speculations, the pressure on the money market, the drain on productive capital, the rise of prices, the speculative overexpansion of some branches and the collapse of others, the absorption and re-release of the reserve army of labour — these form a coherent cyclical mechanism, and the chapter presents it in a compact, almost model-like form. This anticipates the fuller treatment of crisis in the following part and in Volume III.

The third theoretical implication is the critique of fetishism in the form of the reflux. The fact that the same monetary sum returns to the capitalist at the end of each turnover conceals the fact that the substance of the variable capital has been consumed and replaced. The £500 that comes back is not the £500 that went out; it is the money form of a newly produced commodity. The chapter's insistence on this point is a specific application of the theory of commodity fetishism: the form of value obscures the process of value creation. Similarly, the entire mediated structure of merchant credit, drafts, and Indian trade bills obscures the real condition — that products are produced, shipped, and not realized — behind the appearances of the money market.

Broader Practical and Philosophical Implications

For revolutionary politics and the working class, the most striking implication is the passage on workers being paid with their own value product (in A's case) versus out of the capitalist's advance (in B's case). Under conditions of rapid turnover, the working class is, in a functional sense, already being paid with the money of their own recent labour. This is a concrete material anticipation of the associationist and socialist proposals of Marx's time: the workers' product is their own, and under a rational social order it could be distributed to them directly. In the communist counterfactual, "money capital would immediately be done away with, and so too the disguises that transactions acquire through it" — this is a claim that under a just social order, the real problem of long-term investment would become visible and tractable, and the "disguises" of money would no longer produce an appearance of a purely financial problem.

For the theory of the trade cycle, the chapter provides a coherent and sophisticated model. The cycle is not driven by arbitrary psychological factors or by government policy alone; it is driven by the inherent temporal structures of capital — specifically, by the mismatch between the long working periods and circulation periods of certain branches (railways, agriculture) and the shorter turnover periods of others. The credit system postpones but does not solve the mismatch; when the postponement reaches its limits, the crisis erupts. The reserve army of labour acts as the regulating mechanism: absorbed during the boom, released during the crash, driving wages down to and below their customary minimum. The crisis is "post festum rationality" in action — the moment at which the market finally coordinates, too late.

For the critique of political economy, the chapter's most important lesson is method. When an appearance contradicts a law, the task is not to reconcile the law with the appearance by diluting the law, but to discover the deeper logic of the appearance through sharper conceptual distinctions. Marx's distinction between capital advanced and capital applied is a model of this procedure: it takes an apparent refutation of the labour theory of value (the 900-point gap between A's and B's annual rates) and shows that it is a rigorous consequence of the theory, once the distinction is made. This is one of the great methodological achievements of Capital: a dialectical analysis that preserves the fundamental law intact by clarifying the conditions of its application.

Finally, for the theory of the planned economy — a question never far from Marx's mind even where he does not fully elaborate it — the chapter offers one of the most striking prefigurations. The communist counterfactual states the core insight clearly: under a planned order, the task is to reckon in advance how much labour, means of production, and means of subsistence can be devoted to long-gestation projects (railways) without dislocation. The identical real problem exists in both capitalist and communist societies — long investment periods will always withdraw resources from current production — but under capitalism the problem is solved only through periodic crises. This is an extraordinary anticipation of the socialist planning debates of the twentieth century, and it grounds them not in an ethical argument but in a structural analysis of turnover time.

The Chapter as a Whole

In sum, Chapter 16 accomplishes what few chapters in Capital accomplish with such economy: it simultaneously (1) dissolves an apparent empirical anomaly that had discredited the Ricardian school, (2) establishes a formal law of the annual rate of surplus-value S′ = s′n, (3) articulates the crucial distinction between capital advanced and capital applied, (4) develops a fine-grained theory of the consumption and reproduction of variable capital, (5) ascends to the social standpoint to analyze the temporal dimensions of reproduction as a whole, (6) sketches a theory of long-period investment and its cyclical consequences, (7) provides an extended case study of international trade, credit, and crisis transmission, and (8) prepares the way for the theory of the rate of profit and the analysis of realization crises in subsequent parts. Its most potent lesson remains its greatest paradox: the appearance that circulation generates value is a superstructural illusion resting on the real, material, temporal rhythm of production — a rhythm whose analysis requires neither abandoning the labour theory of value nor dissolving its conceptual distinctions into blurred averages, but rather sharpening those distinctions until the apparent anomaly reveals itself as an inessential consequence of the law it seemed to refute.
