Volume 2 • Chapter 19: Former Presentations of the Subject

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem

Chapter 19 of Marx's Theories of Surplus-Value (the second volume of the Theories, and evidently the opening of Manuscript VIII of the 1861–63 Economics Manuscript) is the historic-critical hinge of Marx's entire theory of reproduction. Its core objective is deceptively modest in formulation but revolutionary in implication: to demonstrate that every major economist before Marx — from Quesnay through Adam Smith and down to John Stuart Mill — failed to correctly conceptualize how the constant capital component of the annual social product is reproduced. The chapter arises at a moment when Marx has already, in his own analytical work, discovered the two-departmental structure of social reproduction (Department I, means of production; Department II, means of consumption), and now turns to examine how his predecessors handled — or mishandled — the same terrain. The purpose is doubly polemical: to rescue what is correct in the Physiocrats and Smith, and to destroy the "dogma" that commodity value resolves entirely into wages plus surplus-value (v + s).

The chapter's organizing thesis can be stated in one proposition: Adam Smith's theory that the price of every commodity — and hence the value of the annual social product — "resolves itself" into wages, profit, and rent (that is, into v + s) is false, because it omits the constant capital component (c), which is not produced in the current year but merely reappears in a new natural form. This omission flows from two deeper confusions: (1) the identification of the value of the annual product with the annual value product, and (2) the failure to distinguish the twofold character of labour as value-creating and use-value-creating. Everything else in the chapter — the treatment of Quesnay, the dissolution of Smith's "gross and net revenue" distinction, the demolition of Smith's corn-horse-Pebble proofs, the survey of Ricardo, Say, Storch, Sismondi, Barton, Ramsay, Cherbuliez, and Mill — is a working out of the consequences of this fundamental diagnosis.

The Intellectual Objective

The intellectual objective is not merely negative. Marx is doing something constructive at the same time. He is establishing, against the tradition, that: 1. The annual social product must be analyzed in two departments, because the material form in which value exists (means of production vs. means of consumption) determines whether that value can function as revenue or must function as capital. 2. Even on the basis of simple reproduction — the supposedly simplest case — the analysis of value must include the constant capital component. 3. The categories of "revenue" and "capital" are not originally constitutive of commodity value; rather, commodity value is the precondition of revenue. Smith inverted this relationship, making revenues the "original sources of all exchangeable value," and thereby opened the door to vulgar economics.

Placement in the Broader Theoretical Framework

This chapter is the culminating critical complement to the reproduction schemas of Capital Volume 2, Part 3. In the published Capital, Marx presents his own positive solution in the famous formulas:

Department I: c₁ + v₁ + s₁ = total product of means of production Department II: c₂ + v₂ + s₂ = total product of means of consumption

with the equilibrium conditions c₂ = v₁ + s₁ (Department II's constant capital must equal Department I's wage-and-surplus bill) and c₁ + v₁ + s₁ = c₁ + c₂ (total means of production output must equal the constant capital used across both departments). Chapter 19 is the negative, historical-historiographical mirror of this positive construction. Before Marx can show how reproduction works, he must show why everyone else thought it didn't require a c-term at all. The chapter thus functions as a clearing of the ground — an act of intellectual hygiene that removes the rubbish of Smith's dogma from the path of the scientific analysis.

It also matters that Marx here is operating at a specific moment in his own development. The chapter begins with the note "This is the start of Manuscript VIII," and the section on "Later Writers" is an addition from Manuscript II. This tells us the text is a working document — a place where Marx thinks aloud, refines his historical judgments, and anticipates his mature schemas. The frequent appeals to "what was said above" refer not to this chapter but to the analytical arguments in the preceding parts of the Theories, where Marx has already worked out the distinction between constant and variable capital and the theory of surplus-value.

The Polemical Stakes

Why does this matter so much? Because the stakes are nothing less than the possibility of an economic science of capitalism. If Smith is right that commodity value = v + s, then the entire annual product is consumable as revenue and no portion is permanently tied up in capital replacement. This is, in fact, what Jean-Baptiste Say concluded — and Marx notes with characteristic irony that "it is never the original thinkers who draw the absurd conclusions. They rather leave this for the Says and MacCullochs." The error, if not detected, collapses the distinction between gross and net product into a subjective illusion, dissolves the reality of capital replacement, and literally makes it possible to think that a nation can consume its entire annual product without disinvestment. The chapter is, in effect, a war over whether capital has any independent existence in the theory of value and distribution at all. Marx's answer is emphatic: it does, and the failure to see this is the mark of vulgar economics.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Physiocrats: Quesnay's Tableau and the Feudal Signboard

Marx opens Chapter 19 not with an attack on Smith but with an appreciative, though critical, reconstruction of Quesnay's Tableau économique. This choice is significant. Marx is establishing that the correct instinct — the instinct to treat reproduction as a social, multi-class process — originates with the Physiocrats, and that Smith represents a retrogression from their insight rather than an advance. The chapter's opening sentence sets the frame: "Quesnay's Tableau économique shows in a few broad lines how the annual result of national production, defined in terms of value, is distributed by circulation in such a way that, with other circumstances remaining the same, simple reproduction can take place, i.e. reproduction on the same scale."

Two features of this formulation deserve emphasis. First, the Tableau operates in terms of value — it tracks the distribution of the annual product's value, not just its physical dispersion. Second, it demonstrates the possibility of simple reproduction — reproduction "on the same scale" — through the mechanism of circulation. This is crucial because it shows that the Physiocrats, unlike the vulgar economists who came later, understood that the social product must pass through a definite set of channels before it can serve either as capital replacement or as revenue.

The material starting-point of this process, Marx notes, is "the previous year's harvest." The annual production cycle does not begin from nothing; it begins from a stock of use-values inherited from the previous period. The "numberless individual acts of circulation" are thereby "immediately grouped together in their characteristic social movement as a mass circulation between major economic classes of society that are defined by their functions." This is what makes the Tableau revolutionary from a methodological standpoint: it substitutes class-functional analysis for the individualistic analysis of atomized exchange. Instead of tracing contracts between individuals, Quesnay traces flows between classes whose economic roles are structurally defined.

The specific insight Marx wants to rescue is this: "one part of the total product — which as an object of use, just like any other part, is the fresh result of the past year's labour — is also just a bearer of old capital value reappearing in the same natural form. It does not circulate, but rather remains in the hands of its producers, the class of farmers, to begin its service as capital there once again." This is the germ of the entire reproduction problem. The annual product contains value that is not newly produced during the year — it is old capital value in new (or the same) physical shape. And this value does not enter circulation as revenue; it stays put, immediately resuming its function as capital. In agricultural terms: seed grain, draught animals, and the like. Quesnay, Marx acknowledges, "in fact includes in this constant capital part of the annual product, elements that do not belong there," but he "comes to grips with the main question, thanks to the very narrowness of his mental horizon." The narrowness — the belief that agriculture alone produces surplus-value — turns out to be methodologically productive, because in agriculture "the process of economic reproduction ... is always intertwined with a process of natural reproduction." The natural cycle makes the necessity of capital replacement visible: you can see the seed going back into the ground, the cattle breeding themselves. This visible natural cycle "illuminate[s] those of the former, and keep[s] at bay those confusions which are only introduced by the illusions of circulation."

Here we encounter Marx's first important definitional distinction, embedded in the argument: the difference between economic reproduction and natural reproduction. Economic reproduction is the social process by which value — as opposed to physical material — is maintained and expanded through production and exchange. Natural reproduction is the biological/physical process by which living things (crops, livestock) reproduce themselves. In agriculture the two are palpably intertwined, and this intertwining makes it hard to pretend that the entire annual product is net revenue. The illusions of circulation, by contrast, arise when circulation is studied in isolation from production, and one comes to believe (as the vulgar economists do) that money begets money without the need for material capital to be physically replaced.

Now Marx turns to the "label" problem. "The label of a system of ideas is distinguished from that of other articles, among other things, by the fact that it deceives not only the buyer, but often the seller as well." This is a characteristically Marxian jab: the Physiocrats themselves — Quesnay and his disciples — "believed in their feudal signboard." They thought they were defending feudal landed property; in fact they were the theorists of capitalist production. "In point of fact, however, the Physiocratic system is the first systematic conception of capitalist production." This is a key thesis of the chapter: the actual content of Physiocracy, beneath the ideological form, is capitalist. How so? "The representatives of industrial capital — the class of farmers — lead the whole economic movement." The farmers are the "direct exploiter of 'productive' labour, i.e. the producer of surplus-value," distinguished from the "mere appropriator of this surplus-value," the landlord.

Marx is careful in his footnotes here. There is a footnote distinguishing "industrial capital" (as Marx defines the term: capital that produces surplus-value through the employment of wage-labour, as opposed to merchant's capital, which merely appropriates it) from merchant's capital. And he notes that the Physiocratic celebration of the capitalist farmer is: "Agriculture is pursued on a capitalist footing, i.e. as the large-scale undertaking of the capitalist farmer; the immediate tillers of the soil are wage-labourers." Three classes, three functions: capitalist farmers exploit; wage-labourers produce surplus-value; landlords appropriate it. This three-class division — "the direct exploiter of 'productive' labour ... the producer of surplus-value, the capitalist farmer, is distinguished from the mere appropriator of this surplus-value" (the landlord) — establishes the template that Smith will later obscure with his v + s formula.

Marx notes that the capitalist character of Physiocracy "already provoked opposition during its heyday." He names Linguet and Mably on the one hand, and the "defenders of free small-scale property in land" on the other. He footnotes Linguet's Théories des lois civiles (1767), noting that Linguet used the Physiocratic presentation as the basis for a critique of the working class's position in bourgeois society — but a reactionary critique, one that contrasted modern exploitation with an idealized pre-capitalist past. This is a small but telling moment in Marx's historical method: he never welcomes a critique simply because it is critical; the reactionary critique of capitalism from the standpoint of the feudal past is no less false than the capitalist apologetic, even if it is aimed at the same target.

The real enemy, however, is not Linguet but Adam Smith. Marx's transition is abrupt: "The retrogression in Adam Smith's analysis of the reproduction process is all the more striking, in that he not only elaborated on Quesnay's correct analyses ... but in places fell back completely into the errors of the Physiocrats." The phrase "retrogression" (Rückschritt) is deliberately chosen. Smith did make genuine progress: he generalized Quesnay's avances primitives and avances annuelles into "fixed" and "circulating" capital, thereby freeing the concept of capital from the specifically agricultural sphere. Marx footnotes that Turgot had prepared the ground for this generalization, extending the term "capital" beyond avances and identifying the capitaux of manufacturers with those of farmers. But the progress is undermined by what Marx calls the "retrogression": Smith accepted and perpetuated "the concepts 'fixed' and 'circulating' as the decisive distinctions" — and this taxonomic choice, as we shall see, obscured the really decisive distinction between constant and variable capital.

Marx then quotes Smith's remarkable passage (Book Two, Chapter V) in which Smith argues that agriculture produces more value than manufacturing because "nature labours along with man." Smith writes: "No equal capital puts into motion a greater quantity of productive labour than that of the farmer. Not only his labouring servants, but his labouring cattle, are productive labourers." Marx's parenthetical remark — "(A charming compliment for the labouring servants!)" — is one of the great sarcasms of the Theories. Smith goes on: "A field overgrown with briars and brambles may frequently produce as great a quantity of vegetables as the best cultivated vineyard or corn field." The point Smith is driving at is that the rent of the landlord is the product of "those powers of nature, the use of which the landlord lends to the farmer" — a "work of nature which remains after deducting or compensating everything which can be regarded as the work of man. It is seldom less than a fourth, and frequently more than a third of the whole produce."

Marx does not comment extensively on this passage here, beyond the initial sarcasm, but its relevance to the chapter's argument is clear. Smith, so eager to show agriculture's special productivity, resurrects the Physiocratic error that surplus-value originates in nature's bounty rather than in the exploitation of wage-labour. This is the substantive Physiocratic error, and Smith falls back into it. The passage also illustrates the "exoteric" Smith — the one who deals in surface appearances and conventional categories.

The next piece of evidence against Smith is his treatment of seed. In Book Two, Chapter I, Smith says: "The whole value of the seed, too, is properly a fixed capital." Marx glosses: "Here, therefore, capital = capital value; this exists in a 'fixed' form." Smith continues: "Though it goes backwards and forwards between the ground and the granary, it never changes masters, and therefore does not properly circulate. The farmer makes his profit, not by its sale, but by its increase." Marx's diagnosis: "The narrowness of this conception lies in Smith's failure to see what Quesnay had already seen, namely the reappearance of the value of constant capital in a renewed form. Instead, he saw here only a further illustration, and moreover a false one, of his distinction between fixed and circulating capital; hence he missed an important aspect of the reproduction process." This is the key point. Quesnay sees seed as constant capital value reappearing. Smith sees it as an illustration of his fixed/circulating distinction. The first insight is about reproduction; the second is about taxonomy. Smith's taxonomic obsession blocks the substantive insight. And so the transition to the next section is set up: "The progress in Smith's translation of 'avances primitives' and 'avances annuelles' into 'fixed capital' and 'circulating capital' consists in the word 'capital,' this concept being generalized and freed from its special reference to the Physiocratic 'agricultural' sphere of application; the retrogression consists in the acceptance and the perpetuation of the concepts 'fixed' and 'circulating' as the decisive distinctions."

This is the frame for the entire chapter's engagement with Smith. The rest of Section 2 can be read as a detailed demonstration of how Smith's taxonomic mistake — the fixed/circulating mistake — produces a cascade of substantive errors in his theory of reproduction and value.

Smith's "General Perspectives": The Dogma and Its Smuggled Correction

The second major section of the chapter begins with a seemingly innocent quotation from Smith's Wealth of Nations, Book One, Chapter VI (p. 153):

"In every society the price of every commodity finally resolves itself into some one or other, or all of those three parts" (wages, profit, and rent), "and in every improved society, all the three enter more or less, as component parts, into the price of the far greater part of commodities."

Smith elaborates with two examples: sea fish (rent does not enter, only wages and profit) and Scotch Pebbles (only wages enter — "In some parts of Scotland a few poor people make a trade of gathering, along the sea-shore, those little variegated stones commonly known by the name of Scotch Pebbles. The price which is paid to them by the stone cutter is altogether the wages of their labour; neither rent nor profit make any part of it"). These examples are crucial as evidenced below, because Marx will later use the Scotch Pebble case to expose the fraudulent logic of Smith's "proof."

The doctrine is then extended from the individual commodity to the total social product. Smith says (Book Two, Chapter II): "Since this is the case, it has been observed, with regard to every particular commodity, taken separately, it must be so with regard to all the commodities which compose the whole annual produce of the land and labour of every country, taken complexly. The whole price of exchangeable value of that annual produce must resolve itself into the same three parts, and be parcelled out among the different inhabitants of the country, either as the wages of their labour, the profits of their stock, or the rent of their land."

Marx pauses to note the logical structure: "After Adam Smith has thus resolved both the price of all commodities taken separately and 'the whole price or exchangeable value... of the annual produce of the land and labour of every country' into three sources of revenue, for wage-labourer, capitalist and landlord, into wages, profit and rent, he has to smuggle in a fourth element by an indirect route, namely the element of capital." This "smuggling" is accomplished via the gross/net revenue distinction. Smith writes (p. 382): "The gross revenue of all the inhabitants of a great country comprehends the whole annual produce of their land and labour; the net revenue, what remains free to them after deducting the expense of maintaining — first, their fixed, and, secondly, their circulating capital, or what, without encroaching upon their capital, they can place in their stock reserved for immediate consumption, or spend upon their subsistence, conveniences, and amusements. Their real wealth, too, is in proportion, not to their gross, but to their net revenue."

Marx draws three critical implications. First, "Adam Smith is expressly dealing only with simple reproduction, not with reproduction on an expanded scale, or with accumulation; he speaks only of the expense of 'maintaining' the functioning capital." This is important because it means the difficulty does not arise from accumulation (where new investment creates extra complications) but already at the level of simple reproduction — the supposedly easy case. The "net" revenue is that part of the annual product that can enter the consumption fund "without encroaching upon capital"; hence some portion of the product is neither wages nor profit nor rent but capital itself. "One part of the value of both individual and social product, therefore, is neither resolved into wages nor into profit or rent, but into capital."

Second — and this is the core of Marx's critique — Smith escapes his own theory "by means of a play on words, the distinction between 'gross' and 'net' 'revenue.'" The English word "revenue" (from Old French revenue, past participle of revenir, "to return") literally means "return." Both the individual capitalist and the "nation" receive, in place of the capital used up in production, a commodity product whose value on the one hand replaces the capital value consumed and on the other hand comprises value components "parcelled out" as wages, profit, and rent. So the whole product value is "revenue" in one sense (it's a return), yet part of it is capital income (return of capital), while another part is revenue in the everyday sense (disposable income). Marx writes: "Thus, what was eliminated when the value of commodities was analysed into its component parts is introduced again through the back door by the ambiguity of this word 'revenue.' But only the components of the product's value that already exist in the product can be 'taken in.' If capital is to income in as revenue, then capital must previously have been spent."

The third implication concerns the trap that Smith's terminology puts him in. Smith says (Book One, Chapter IX): "The lowest ordinary rate of profit must always be something more than what is sufficient to compensate the occasional losses to which every employment of stock is exposed. It is this surplus only which is net or clear profit." Marx's parenthetical: "(What capitalist sees profit as including necessary outlays of capital?)" — a jab at Smith's conflation of profit with gross proceeds. Smith continues: "What is called gross profit comprehends frequently, not only this surplus, but what is retained for compensating such extraordinary losses." Marx's reply: "But this means no more than that a part of the surplus-value, treated here as part of the gross profit, has to form an insurance fund for production. This insurance fund is created by a part of the surplus labour, which thereby directly produces capital, i.e. the fund set aside for reproduction." So the insurance fund is not a distinct "element" of value — it is a portion of surplus-value differentiated only by its social function. Similarly for the "maintenance" of fixed capital: "the replacement of the fixed capital consumed by new capital in no way constitutes a new investment of capital, but is simply a replacement of the old capital value in new form." And for repairs: "its cost forms part of the price of the capital advanced. If the capitalist invests this only gradually and according to need ... this in no way changes the source of this profit. The component of value from which it derives simply indicates that the workers provide surplus labour for the repair fund as well as for the insurance fund."

Marx then summarizes what Smith's definition of fixed capital "actually boils down to": "it is the part of the industrial capital advanced that is fixed in the production process," corresponding to Smith's own phrases "affords a revenue or profit without circulating or changing masters" (p. 377) and "either remains in his possession, or continues in the same shape" (p. 374). So far so good — but the technical definition is unstable.

Smith then makes a substantive claim: not only the whole fixed capital but also "that entire part of the circulating capital required to maintain, repair and replace the fixed capital" must be excluded from net revenue — "in point of fact all capital that does not exist in a natural form destined for the consumption fund." He writes (Book Two, Chapter II): "The whole expense of maintaining the fixed capital must evidently be excluded from the net revenue of the society. Neither the materials necessary for supporting their useful machines and instruments of trade, their profitable buildings, etc., nor the produce of the labour necessary for fashioning those materials into the proper form, can ever make any part of it. The price of that labour may indeed make a part of it; as the workmen so employed may place the whole value of their wages in their stock reserved for immediate consumption. But in other sorts of labour, both the price" (i.e., the wage paid for this labour) "and the produce" (in which this labour is embodied) "go to this stock, the price to that of the workmen, the produce to that of other people, whose subsistence, conveniences, and amusements, are augmented by the labour of those workmen."

This is a pivotal passage, and Marx treats it as such. Despite its confused phrasing, Smith "has stumbled upon a very important distinction, between the workers involved in the production of means of production, and those involved in the direct production of means of consumption." Marx now formalizes the distinction:

  • The workers producing means of production (Department I, in Marx's later terminology) receive wages equal in value to a component of their product. That wage is revenue for them, but the product they create is not consumable — it is means of production. Hence the value-component of the product equal to their wages exists physically as an aliquot part of means of production, and is not part of the consumption fund.
  • Smith forgets that what holds for wages also holds for the surplus-value (profit and rent) in this department. The surplus-value also exists initially in means of production. Only after conversion into money can it withdraw an equivalent amount of consumption goods produced in Department II, transferring them into the individual consumption fund of the surplus-receivers.
  • All the more should Smith have seen that the portion of the annually produced means of production used up in the production of means of production — i.e., "the means of production with which means of production are made" — is "absolutely excluded, not only by the natural form in which it exists, but also by its capital function, from being any component of value that constitutes revenue."

Marx also corrects Smith on the second type of worker (Department II). Smith says that for such workers "both the price of labour and the product 'go to' the direct consumption fund: 'the price' (i.e., the money received as wages) 'to that of the workmen, the product to that of other people.'" But, Marx objects, "the worker cannot live off the 'price' of his labour, the money in which his wages are paid; he realizes his money by using it to buy means of consumption, and these may consist partly of types of commodities which he has himself produced. However, his own product may be one that is only consumed by the exploiters of labour." This is a small but important correction: the material form of the wage and the material form of the worker's personal consumption need not coincide, since the wage is money and money is universally convertible.

Smith then returns to circulating capital, this time to argue that circulating capital (unlike fixed) is not wholly excluded from net revenue. He lists the "four parts" of circulating capital — money, provisions, materials, and finished work — and observes that three of the four (provisions, materials, finished work) are regularly withdrawn and placed either in fixed capital or in the stock reserved for immediate consumption. "Whatever portion of those consumable goods is not employed in maintaining the former" (the fixed capital) "goes all to the latter" (the stock reserved for immediate consumption) "and makes a part of the net revenue of the society." Marx's comment: "All this is simply the tautology that the part of circulating capital that does not serve for the production of means of production goes into the production of means of consumption, i.e. into the part of the annual product that is destined to form the society's consumption fund." In other words, Smith has restated the Department II / Department I division, but under the misleading label "circulating capital."

What is important is the immediately following passage, where Smith distinguishes individual from social circulating capital: "The circulating capital of a society is in this respect different from that of an individual. That of an individual is totally excluded from making any part of his net revenue, which must consist altogether in his profits. But though the circulating capital of every individual makes a part of that of the society to which he belongs, it is not upon that account totally excluded from making a part likewise of their net revenue. Though the whole goods in a merchant's shop must by no means be placed in his own stock reserved for immediate consumption, they may [be placed] in that of other people, who, from a revenue derived from other funds, may regularly replace their value to him, together with its profits, without occasioning any diminution either of his capital or of theirs."

Marx extracts three lessons from this. First, "Just like the fixed capital, and that circulating capital needed for its reproduction and maintenance (he forgets its functioning), so the circulating capital of each individual capitalist involved in the production of means of consumption is also totally excluded from his net revenue, which can consist only of his profits." Second, "The circulating capital of each individual capitalist forms part of the circulating capital of the society, just as with each individual fixed capital." Third — and this is the crux — "The circulating capital of the society, even though it is only the sum of the individual circulating capitals, possesses a character distinct from the circulating capital of an individual capitalist." Smith has accidentally discovered that aggregation changes the significance of a category: what is capital for one capitalist may be revenue-accessible for the class as a whole.

But Smith misses the significance of his own observation because he has chosen the wrong example. "Instead of selecting as his example the goods in the retail shop, Adam Smith should have chosen the stocks amassed in the storehouses of the industrial capitalists." The retail shop example obscures the crucial point: the relevant "social consumption fund" is not the stock of commodities sitting on retail shelves awaiting purchase; it is the annual mass of means of consumption flowing out of Department II's production. Retail stocks are a distributional intermediary, not the substantive social category.

Marx then performs a constructive thought-experiment: "If Adam Smith had now, in discussing what he calls circulating capital, fitted together the fragmentary ideas that impressed themselves on him previously, while he was contemplating the reproduction of what he calls fixed capital, he would have arrived at the following result." This "result" is Marx's own two-department schema, here presented as the logical consequence of Smith's scattered insights:

  1. The annual social product consists of two departments: the first comprises means of production, the second comprises means of consumption; "the two have to be treated separately."
  2. The total value of the Department I product divides into three parts: (a) the value of the means of production consumed in creating these means of production — this is "only the capital value reappearing in a new form"; (b) a part equal to the value of the capital laid out on labour-power (wages); (c) a part forming the source of profits and rent (surplus-value).
  3. The Department II capitalists — "the direct producers of means of consumption" — replace the capital used up by the Department I capitalists in producing means of consumption (excepting the portion converted into wages for Department II workers). Meanwhile the used-up capital, now existing in the hands of the producing capitalists (Department I) in the form of means of consumption, "forms for its part — from the social standpoint — the consumption fund in which the capitalists and workers in the first department realize their revenue."

This is the complete, if still informal, two-department schema. Marx is showing that Smith had all the materials for it — that "he almost hit the nail on the head" — but failed to assemble them because the fixed/circulating distinction kept displacing the constant/variable and means-of-production/means-of-consumption distinctions.

Two implications are drawn "from the investigation so far."

First: "even though the social capital is simply the sum of the individual capitals, and therefore the annual commodity product (or commodity capital) of the society is equal to the sum of the commodity products of these individual capitals; and although, therefore, the same analysis of the commodity value into its components that holds for each individual commodity capital must also hold for the society as a whole and in the last analysis actually does hold, yet the form of appearance which these components assume in the overall process of social reproduction is a different one." This is a subtle point. At the level of the individual commodity, value = c + v + s in a straightforward way. But at the level of the total social product, the components — c, v, s — appear in different material forms and distributional patterns. In particular, some c-components of individual products re-appear as revenue-accessible goods at the social level, while some v + s components exist as non-consumable means of production. The sum of individual capitals does not behave like one big individual capital.

Second: "even on the basis of simple reproduction, we find not only the production of wages (variable capital) and surplus-value, but also the direct production of new constant capital value; this is true even though the working day consists of only two parts, one in which the worker replaces the variable capital, and the other in which he produces surplus-value (profit, rent, etc.). In other words, the daily labour that is spent in reproducing the means of production — whose value breaks down into wages and surplus-value — is realized in new means of production which also replace the constant capital component expended in the production of the means of consumption."

This second point is the analytic heart of the whole chapter. The worker's working day has only two parts (necessary labour and surplus labour). Yet the annual product contains three value components (c, v, s). How? Because the labour that creates new value is at the same time the labour that preserves and transfers old value — via its concrete, useful character. The labour spent in Department I produces new means of production whose value (v + s) is only two parts, but whose use-value replaces the constant capital consumed in Department II. In this way, c is reproduced without any additional labour-time being expended on it. This is what Marx elsewhere calls the "twofold character of labour" — as abstract value-creating labour and as concrete use-value-creating labour. Smith fails to make this distinction, and hence cannot explain where c comes from.

The section closes with a historical note: "The main difficulties arise not in the treatment of accumulation, but already in that of simple reproduction, although the greater part of these have already been resolved in what was said above. This is why both Adam Smith (Book Two) and before him Quesnay (Tableau économique) started with simple reproduction, whenever they had to deal with the movement of the annual social product and its reproduction by way of circulation." Marx is signaling a crucial methodological point: simple reproduction is the analytically hard case, not accumulation. Accumulation adds complications (the surplus must be reinvested in specific proportions), but the fundamental puzzle — how constant capital is reproduced — is already present at the level of simple reproduction. Even the conservative Smith, operating in Book Two with the simplest possible case, couldn't get it right.

Smith's Resolution of Exchange-Value into v + s: The Dogma Stated and Defended

Having established the substantive problem, Marx now attacks Smith's formula at the level of the individual commodity. "Adam Smith's dogma that the price or 'exchangeable value' of every single commodity — and thus of all the commodities comprising the annual social product (he correctly assumes capitalist production everywhere) — is composed of, or 'resolves itself into,' the three 'component parts' wages, profit and rent, can be reduced to the thesis that commodity value = v + s, i.e. the value of the variable capital advanced, plus the surplus-value."

The reduction is legitimate because Smith himself permits us to treat profit and rent as common forms of surplus-value. The quotations Marx provides show Smith saying as much: in manufacture, "The value which the workmen add to the materials ... resolves itself ... into two parts, of which the one pays their wages, the other the profits of their employer upon the whole stock of materials and wages which he advanced" (Book One, Chapter VI, p. 151). And: "Though the manufacturer (i.e., the manufacturing worker) has his wages advanced to him by his master, he, in reality, costs him no expense, the value of those wages being generally restored, together with a profit, in the improved value of the subject upon which his labour is bestowed" (Book Two, Chapter III, p. 430). This second quote is significant: it makes plain that wages are not truly an expense to the capitalist, because the worker reproduces them plus a surplus. The capitalist only appears to advance wages.

Smith also says (Book Two, Chapter III, p. 432): "The part of 'stock' that is laid out 'in maintaining productive hands,' 'after having served in the function of a capital to him' (the employer) ... 'constitutes a revenue to them' (the workers)." And (p. 431): "the whole annual produce of the land and labour of every country ... naturally divides itself into two parts. One of them, and frequently the largest, is, in the first place, destined for replacing a capital, or for renewing the provisions, materials, and finished work, which had been withdrawn from a capital; the other for constituting a revenue either to the owner of this capital, as the profit of his stock, or to some other person, as the rent of his land."

Marx pauses on this: "Only one part of the capital, as Adam Smith has just told us, also forms revenue for someone, namely the part invested in the purchase of productive labour. This part — the variable capital — firstly performs 'the function of a capital' in the hands of the employer, and for him, and then it 'constitutes a revenue' for the productive worker himself." Marx then makes a subtle criticism of Smith's phrasing: "The capitalist transforms one part of his capital value into labour-power, and precisely in this way into variable capital; but as a result of this transformation it is not only this part of the capital, but his entire capital, that functions as industrial capital." This is a precision-point: the transformation of one part of capital into labour-power makes the whole function as industrial capital. And — crucially — "The worker — the seller of labour-power — receives its value in the form of his wage. In his hands, labour-power is simply a saleable commodity, the commodity from whose sale he lives, and which therefore forms the only source of his revenue; it is only in the hands of its buyer, the capitalist, that the labour-power functions as variable capital, and in fact the capitalist only appears to advance the purchase price of the labour-power, for its value has already been supplied to him by the worker."

This is one of those dense passages where Marx packs multiple theoretical claims. The commodity labour-power is sold by the worker (a sale, like any other commodity sale) and bought by the capitalist. In the seller's hands it is a commodity and a source of revenue; in the buyer's hands it is capital (variable capital). Smith and his successors constantly blur these two moments, treating the wage both as capital (from the capitalist's standpoint) and as revenue (from the worker's standpoint) without keeping them logically separate.

Marx then turns to the agricultural case, where Smith says the workers "besides 'the reproduction of a value equal to their own consumption, or to the' (variable) 'capital which employs them, together with its owners' profits' — 'over and above the capital of the farmer and all its profits, they regularly occasion the reproduction of the rent of the landlord.'" Marx's comment is important: "The fact that the rent goes into the hands of the landlord is quite beside the point for the question we are dealing with here. Before it passes into his hands, it must first exist in the hands of the farmer, i.e., of the industrial capitalist. It must form a value component of the product, before it becomes revenue for anyone. Thus for Smith rent and profit are both only components of the surplus-value that the productive worker constantly reproduces together with his own wages, i.e., with the value of the variable capital. Rent and profit are parts of the surplus-value s, and in this way Smith resolves the price of all commodities into v + s."

The formal statement of the dogma: "The dogma that the prices of all commodities (and therefore of the annual commodity product) can be resolved into wages plus profit plus rent of land, assumes the form, even in the intermittent esoteric part of Smith's work, that the value of any commodity, and thus of the society's annual commodity product, = v + s, i.e., the capital value laid out on labour-power and constantly reproduced by the workers, plus the surplus-value added by the workers through their labour."

What this reveals, Marx says, is the source of Smith's one-sidedness: "For the fact that these components also form the various sources of revenue for classes with different functions in production has nothing to do with the quantitative determination of each of these components, and the limits of their total values." In other words, Smith confused the distributional question (who gets what) with the constitutive question (how is value itself determined). The components' role as revenues led Smith to treat them as the constitutive "sources" of value.

Marx then examines Smith's famous statement: "Wages, profit, and rent, are the three original sources of all revenue as well as of all exchangeable value. All other revenue is ultimately derived from some one or other of these" (Book One, Chapter VI, p. 155). He identifies a double confusion.

Confusion #1: "All members of society who do not figure directly in the reproduction process, whether as workers or not, can receive their share of the annual commodity product — i.e., their means of consumption — in the first instance only from the hands of those classes to whom this product firstly accrues — productive workers, industrial capitalists and landlords. To this extent, their revenues are, in a material sense, derived from wages ... profit, and ground-rent, and hence appear, in contrast to these original revenues, as derivative." Marx gives a memorable list of the "derivative" revenue-receivers: "king, priest, professor, prostitute, mercenary, etc." Their revenues are derived because the goods they consume must first pass through the hands of the productive classes. But — and here is a dialectical twist — these derivative receivers "draw them by way of their social functions ... and they can therefore view these functions of theirs as the original source of their revenue." Ideologically, the professor thinks his salary is the "product" of his professing; analytically, it is a derivative claim on the surplus produced by others.

Confusion #2 — "this is the high point of Smith's stupid blunder": Smith begins by correctly defining value components, then shows how they form sources of revenue, and then reverses himself, "making these revenues, instead of just 'component parts,' into 'original sources of all exchangeable value,' thereby throwing the doors wide open to vulgar economics. (Cf. our Roscher.)" Marx footnotes Roscher as a German vulgar economist and founder of the historical school. The reference is barbed: Smith's confusion is the fons et origo of a whole tradition of apologetic economics that treats the distributive categories as primary and the value substance as derivative. This is the logical fallacy Marx calls the quid pro quo — the substitution of the revenue-categories for the value-substance.

The Constant Capital Component: How Smith Conjures "c" Out of Existence

The next subsection, (c), is the analytic core of the chapter. Marx sets up the question: "We shall now see how Adam Smith tries to conjure the constant part of capital out of commodity value."

The relevant quotation is from Book One, Chapter VI, p. 153, where Smith discusses the price of corn: "In the price of corn, for example, one part pays the rent of the landlord ... Another [part] pays the wages or maintenance of the labourers ('and labouring cattle,' he adds) employed in producing it, and the third pays the profit of the farmer. These three parts seem (they 'seem' indeed) either immediately or ultimately to make up the whole price of corn."

Marx pounces on the word "seem": "The origin of this component of value has as little to do with the circumstance that it is paid to the landlord and forms revenue for him in the form of rent, as the origin of the other value components has with the fact that they form sources of revenue as profit and wages." And, on the quantitative point: "This whole price, i.e., its quantitative determination, is absolutely independent of its distribution among three kinds of person." In other words, whether the total is distributed as rent/wages/profit has no bearing on the magnitude of the total. Distribution is a separate question from constitution.

Smith then tries to head off the obvious objection — "A fourth part, it may perhaps be thought, is necessary for replacing the stock of the farmer, or for compensating the wear and tear of his labouring cattle and other instruments of husbandry." His answer: "But it must be considered that the price of any instrument of husbandry, such as a labouring horse, is itself made up of the same three parts; the rent of the land upon which he is reared, the labour of tending and rearing him, and the profits of the farmer who advances both the rent of this land, and the wages of this labour. Though the price of the corn, therefore, may pay the price as well as the maintenance of the horse, the whole price still resolves itself either immediately or ultimately into the same three parts of rent, labour" (he means wages) "and profit."

Marx's devastating comment: "This is literally all that Adam Smith has to say in support of his astonishing doctrine. His proof consists simply in the repetition of the same assertion." This is a maximally uncharitable but accurate characterization. Smith, faced with the obvious counter-example (a farmer's capital outlays — horses, tools, etc. — that are neither rent nor wages nor profit), simply asserts that "ultimately" the price of the horse itself decomposes into the same three categories. But this is question-begging: it doesn't show that the horse's price is constituted out of v + s; it just asserts so, and pushes the problem one stage back.

Marx states the logical requirement: "The statement that the entire price of commodities is either 'immediately' or 'ultimately' resolvable into v + s would only cease to be an empty subterfuge if Smith could demonstrate that the commodity products whose price is immediately resolved into c (the price of the means of production consumed) + v + s are finally compensated for by commodity products which entirely replace these 'consumed means of production,' and which are for their part produced simply by the outlay of variable capital, i.e., capital laid out on labour-power. The price of these latter commodities would then immediately be v + s. And in this way the price of the former, too, c + v + s, where c stands for the component of constant capital, would be ultimately resolvable into v + s." But Smith provides no such demonstration. His Scotch Pebble example is useless as a proof because the Pebble collectors "according to him (1) do not supply any kind of surplus-value, but simply produce their own wages; (2) do not employ any means of production. (Though in fact they do, in the form of baskets, sacks and other containers for carting off the pebbles.)" The second point is a delicious reductio: even the Scotch Pebble example, which Smith adduces to show that some commodity prices consist only of wages, in fact involves means of production (baskets, sacks) that Smith has ignored.

The section now deepens the diagnosis. "We have already seen above how Adam Smith refutes his own theory without even becoming aware of its contradictions. The source of these, however, is to be sought precisely in his own scientific premises." Marx is being dialectical: he does not simply say Smith is stupid; he says Smith's correct premises generate the contradiction. Smith correctly sees that "The capital converted into labour produces a value greater than its own. How? Because, says Smith, the workers impart to the things on which they work, during the production process, a value that, besides the equivalent for their own purchase price, forms a surplus-value accruing to their employers (profit and rent)." This is correct as far as it goes. But Smith does not then ask: what about the means of production the workers transform? These already had value before the workers touched them, and that value is preserved, not created, in the product. Smith's analysis of the labour-process (adding) and his analysis of the labour-process's material conditions (already-existing means of production) are never coordinated.

Marx's sharpest formulation: "The society's total annual value product, therefore, can be broken down only into v + s, into an equivalent with which the workers replace the capital value spent on their own purchase price, and the additional value that they have to provide for their employers over and above this. ... How can there be any other component of value, if the annual value product contains no other elements besides v + s? Here we are concerned with simple reproduction. Since the total annual labour is resolved into labour needed for the reproduction of the capital value laid out on labour-power, and labour needed for the creation of a surplus-value, where can still more labour come from to produce a capital value not laid out on labour-power?"

This is the reductio in its purest form. If (as is true) the working day has only two parts, and if (as Smith claims) all commodity value reduces to v + s, then there is no labour-time left to produce c. Yet c clearly exists — the factory, the raw materials, the seed corn, the horses. Where does its value come from? The answer, which Smith cannot see, is: it does not need to be produced by the current year's labour at all. It was already there, produced in previous years, and is merely preserved and transferred into the new product by the useful character of living labour. This requires understanding the twofold character of labour — a distinction Smith lacks.

Marx now lays out the argument in numbered steps.

Step 1: "Adam Smith defines the value of a commodity by the amount of labour that the wage-labourer 'adds' to the object of labour." Note the specific word "adds" — one of Smith's own. "The value that the worker adds to a thing (and this 'adds' is our Adam's own expression) is quite independent of whether the object to which the value is added already has value before this addition, or whether it does not." This is important. The new value added is a magnitude determined by the labour-time expended, regardless of the value of the object worked upon. A worker making a jewel worth £1000 from raw material worth £999 adds the same amount of value as one making a jewel worth £1000 from raw material worth £0 (assuming equal labour-time). The pre-existing value of the means of production does not affect the magnitude of the new value created; it only affects the total value of the resulting product. "One part of this total value is characterized as being equal to the value of the wage, i.e., an equivalent for wages. The second part, therefore, the surplus-value, is necessarily characterized, by the same token, as being equal to the total value of the product minus the value component that is the equivalent for the wage."

Marx is being precise: the total value of the commodity has more than two parts, but the new value added has exactly two parts. Smith identifies the two — the value of the product and the value added — and so loses the constant component.

Step 2: "What holds for a commodity produced in a single capitalist business by some individual worker holds also for the annual product of all branches of industry taken together." The annual product of the working class "fixes" (Smith's expression) a total value determined by the amount of annual labour; this breaks down into wage-equivalent + surplus-value. The wage forms the revenue of the working class; the surplus-value the revenue of the capitalist class; the two represent the relative shares in the annual consumption fund. "There is therefore no room left for the constant capital value, for the reproduction of the capital functioning in the form of means of production."

Marx now cites Smith's own claims from the Introduction to make clear that Smith intended this conclusion: "To explain in what has consisted the revenue of the great body of the people, or what has been the nature of those funds which ... have supplied their annual consumption, is the object of these Four first Books" (p. 106). And: "The annual labour of every nation is the fund which originally supplies it with all the necessaries and conveniences of life which it annually consumes, and which consists always either in the immediate produce of that labour, or in what is purchased with that produce from other nations" (p. 104).

"This, then," Marx concludes, "Smith's first error is to equate the value of the annual product with the annual value product." The annual product is the total mass of commodities produced during the year, whose value includes: (i) the new value produced this year (v + s) and (ii) the value of means of production created in previous years but transferred to the current product (c). The annual value product is only the new value created this year — i.e., v + s. Smith confuses the two. "The latter is simply the product of the current year's labour; the former includes, on top of this, all those elements of value that were used in the production of this annual product, but which were produced in the previous year and partly in still earlier years: means of production whose value only re-appears — and which, as far as their value is concerned, have been neither produced nor reproduced by the labour spent during the current year."

The confusion is "itself based on a further error in his fundamental conception. He does not distinguish the two-fold character of labour itself: labour that creates value, by the expenditure of labour-power, and labour that creates objects of use (use-values), as concrete useful labour." This is the crucial textual moment. Marx claims that Smith's failure to distinguish the twofold character of labour is the root of the confusion. Let us unpack this.

Labour as value-creating is abstract labour — the expenditure of human labour-power in general, in abstraction from any particular useful form. Its magnitude is measured in units of socially necessary labour-time. This labour produces the new value added (v + s).

Labour as use-value-creating is concrete labour — the specific, useful, purposive labour that transforms specific materials into specific products (weaving, spinning, carpentering, etc.). This labour, by its concrete character, transfers the value of the means of production into the new product and preserves it there. The carpenter's labour as concrete activity makes a table, and in so doing retains the value of the wood he uses; the spinner's labour as concrete activity makes yarn, retaining the value of the cotton.

Smith treats labour only as value-creating (abstract). He therefore cannot explain how the value of the means of production gets into the product. He can only see labour as adding new value, not as also transferring old value. Whence, for him, does the value of the wood come from, if not from the carpenter's labour? Either from the carpenter (in which case it should be in v + s) or from nowhere (impossible). He lacks the third alternative: it was already there, brought into the product by the concrete action of the labourer.

Marx continues: "The total sum of commodities annually produced, i.e., the total annual product, is the product of the useful labour operating in the current year; it is only by the social application of labour in an intricate system of varieties of useful labour that all these commodities have come into being; it is only in this way that the value of the means of production used up in their production is retained in their total value, and reappears in a new natural form. The total annual product is thus the result of the useful labour expended during the year; but only one part of the value of this product has been created during the year; this part is the annual value product, which represents the amount of labour actually performed during the year itself."

Two distinctions are now in play: (i) value of annual product vs. annual value product; (ii) labour as useful (concrete) vs. labour as value-forming (abstract). Marx ties them together: Smith equates (i) because he fails to distinguish (ii).

The section closes with a quotation from Smith's Introduction — "The annual labour of every nation is the fund which originally supplies it with all the necessaries and conveniences of life which it annually consumes, etc." — and Marx's comment: "He one-sidedly adopts the standpoint of useful labour. It is this, to be sure, that has brought all these means of subsistence into their consumable form. He forgets here, however, that this would have been impossible without the collaboration of means of labour and objects of labour handed down from earlier years, and that the 'annual labour,' therefore, in as much as it forms value, has in no way created the entire value of the products prepared by it; that the value produced is smaller than the value of the product."

This phrase — "the value produced is smaller than the value of the product" — is a compact restatement of the entire critique. In any given year, the newly produced value (v + s) is strictly less than the total value of the annual product (c + v + s), because the difference (c) is transferred, not produced. Smith denies this by conflating value produced with value of product. "If we cannot reproach Adam Smith for going no further in this analysis than his successors (even if an approach to the correct conception was already available with the Physiocrats), we can say that he fell increasingly into confusion, and the principal reason for this was that his 'esoteric' conception of commodity value was constantly being thwarted by the exoteric ideas which on the whole prevail in his work, even though his scientific instinct did permit the esoteric standpoint to reappear from time to time."

This is one of Marx's central historiographical categories: the esoteric vs. exoteric Smith. The "esoteric" Smith is the one who sees, from time to time, that commodity value = c + v + s, that rent and profit are forms of surplus-value, that value is determined by labour-time. The "exoteric" Smith is the one who, for public consumption, presents the three revenues as the "original sources" of all exchangeable value. The esoteric view is scientifically better; the exoteric is more systematically consistent with the surface appearances of bourgeois society. Because the exoteric view dominates Smith's text, the esoteric insights "reappear from time to time" but never become the organizing principle. Marx's own scientific approach inverts this: he makes the esoteric view the systematic foundation and treats the exoteric view as a set of illusions to be explained.

Capital and Revenue in Adam Smith: The Deepest Analysis

Subsection (d) is the most philosophically dense portion of the chapter. Its aim is to dissect the category of "revenue" itself and to show how the conceptual confusion between revenue and capital — not just an accounting quibble — is at the heart of Smith's error.

Marx begins with the mechanics of variable capital. When a capitalist "advances" variable capital, he may pay the worker in money before the product is sold, after it is sold, out of already-received money, or on credit. In all these cases, "the capitalist spends variable capital which accrues to the workers as money, and he possesses in return the equivalent of this capital value in that portion of his commodities' value with which the worker newly produces the share in the total value of these that accrues to him; with which, in other words, he has produced the value of his own wages." The worker, for his part, "spends the value or price of the labour-power he has sold on means of subsistence, on the means to reproduce his labour-power. A sum of money equal to the variable capital forms his income, in other words his revenue, which he receives only so long as he can sell his labour-power to the capitalist."

Marx now sets forth the key structural claim: "The wage-labourer's commodity — his own labour-power — functions as a commodity only in so far as it is incorporated into the capitalist's capital, and functions as capital; on the other hand, the capital spent as money capital on the purchase of labour-power functions as revenue in the hands of the seller of labour-power, the wage-labourer."

The same sum of money has two functional identities: from the capitalist's standpoint it is variable capital (a value advanced for valorization); from the worker's standpoint it is revenue (spent on means of subsistence). Smith conflates the two. Marx insists on their separation. He breaks down the whole circuit into three distinct stages.

First stage — circulation. "The worker sells his commodity — labour-power — to the capitalist; the money with which the capitalist buys it is for him money invested for the purpose of valorization, i.e., money capital; it is not spent, but merely advanced." Marx adds a crucial clarification: "(This is the real sense of the 'advance' — the Physiocrats' avance — quite independently of where the capitalist acquires the money itself. What is advanced, for the capitalist, is every value that he pays for the purpose of the production process, whether this is done in advance or after the event; it is advanced to the production process itself.)" This is an important point. The concept of "advance" does not depend on temporal priority (payment before the product exists). It depends on functional orientation — value paid for the sake of the production process, whether the money comes from the capitalist's pocket, from previous sales, or from credit. This exposes Smith's (and the Physiocrats') temporal notion of "advance" as superficial.

Second stage — production. "The labour-power that has been bought now forms part of the functioning capital, and the worker himself functions here simply as a special natural form of this capital, as distinct from the elements of capital that exist in the natural form of means of production." This is uncompromising. In production, the worker is a component of capital — not a subject, but an instrument. "During the process, the worker adds value to the means of production that he transforms into a product by spending his labour-power to the value of this labour-power (leaving aside surplus-value). In this way, he reproduces for the capitalist, in the commodity form, the part of the former's capital advanced to him in wages, or due to be advanced; produces for him an equivalent for this capital. He produces for the capitalist, in other words, the capital that the capitalist can 'advance' afresh in the purchase of labour-power."

Third stage — sale. "With the sale of the commodity, therefore, a part of its sale price replaces for the capitalist the variable capital that he advanced, and therefore enables him to buy labour-power afresh, just as it enables the worker to sell it again."

Now comes the fundamental methodological point. "With all commodity purchases and sales — in as much as these transactions are simply considered by themselves — it is completely immaterial what happens in the hands of the seller to the money received for his commodity, and what happens in the hands of the buyer to the item of use that he purchased. It is also completely immaterial, therefore, in so far as it is simply the circulation process that is under consideration, that the labour-power bought by the capitalist reproduces capital value for him, while the money paid as the purchase price of this labour-power forms revenue for the worker. The value of the worker's item of trade, his labour-power, is affected neither by the fact that this forms 'revenue' for him, nor by the fact that the use of his commodity by the buyer reproduces capital value for the buyer."

This is a subtle but vital claim. In a simple commodity transaction, we do not care what the seller does with the money or what the buyer does with the thing bought. Circulation as such abstracts from all that. So the fact that labour-power, once bought, further produces value for the buyer, and that the wage further serves as revenue for the seller, is extraneous to the circulation act. The value of labour-power is determined independently — by the labour needed to reproduce it (i.e., the labour embodied in the worker's necessary means of subsistence). "Because the value of labour-power — i.e., the adequate sale price of this commodity — is determined by the amount of labour needed to reproduce it, and this amount of labour is itself determined by that needed to produce the means of subsistence that the worker needs, i.e., the amount of labour needed to maintain his life, the wage becomes the revenue from which the worker has to live."

Marx now moves to a direct attack on Smith's claim: "Adam Smith is completely wrong to say (p. 432) that the part of capital employed 'in maintaining productive hands,' 'after having served in the function of a capital to him' (the capitalist), 'constitutes a revenue for them' (the workers)." Why wrong? Because "The money with which the capitalist pays for the labour-power that he buys 'serves in the function of a capital to him,' in as much as he thereby incorporates labour-power with the material components of his capital and hence for the first time puts his capital in a position in which it can function as productive capital." The point is that the same money is capital from the capitalist's standpoint and is revenue from the worker's standpoint — but this duality is not the result of the money functioning twice; it is the result of labour-power functioning twice. "The difference is that labour-power is a commodity in the hands of the worker, and not capital, and it constitutes a revenue for him in so far as he can regularly repeat its sale; it functions as capital after the sale, in the hands of the capitalist, during the production process itself. What serves two purposes here is labour-power. In the hands of the worker, it is a commodity sold at its value; in the hands of the capitalist who has bought it, it is a power producing both value and use-value."

Marx then makes a temporal point of considerable importance. "The money, however, that the worker receives from the capitalist, he receives only after he has given him the use of his labour-power, after this has already been realized in the value of the product of labour. The capitalist has this value in his hand before he pays for it. So it is not the money that functions twice: first as money form of the variable capital, then as wages. It is rather the labour-power that has functioned twice; first as a commodity, when the labour-power is sold (when the wage to be paid is stipulated, the money operates merely as an ideal measure of value, and it in no way needs to be in the capitalist's hands yet); secondly in the production process, where it functions as capital, i.e., as an element creating both use-value and value in the hands of the capitalist. It has already supplied the equivalent to be paid to the worker, in the commodity form, before the capitalist pays this to the worker in the money form. The worker therefore himself creates the payment fund from which the capitalist pays him."

This is a compressed and decisive argument. Note the sequence: (1) the worker sells labour-power; the wage is stipulated; the money operates "merely as an ideal measure of value" — the capitalist does not yet need to have it. (2) The worker labors; the value equivalent to his wage is already produced and embodied in the product. (3) The capitalist sells the product; the money is realized. (4) Only now does the capitalist pay the worker. So the worker has, in effect, financed his own wage. This is the material basis of the illusion, noted earlier, that the capitalist "advances" wages.

Marx expands on this with another key thought: "But this is not all. The worker spends the money he receives on maintaining his labour-power, and thus — if we consider the capitalist class and the working class as a whole — on maintaining for the capitalist the only instrument by means of which he can remain a capitalist." The worker's consumption is not merely his own private matter; it is the reproduction of labour-power as a commodity, and therefore the reproduction of the fundamental condition of capitalist production. This is the deep sense in which the "revenue" of the worker is functional for capital.

Here Marx offers a dialectical summary of the two-sided process: "On the one hand, therefore, the constant purchase and sale of labour-power perpetuates the position of labour-power as an element of capital, and in this way capital appears as the creator of commodities, articles of use that have a value; this is also how the portion of capital that buys labour-power is regularly restored by the product of labour-power itself, so that the worker himself constantly creates the capital fund out of which he is paid. On the other hand, the constant sale of labour-power becomes a source of maintaining the worker's life that he perpetually has to repeat, and his labour-power thus appears as the means by which he draws the revenue from which he lives."

The category of revenue, in the worker's case, means "nothing more than the appropriation of values effected by the constantly repeated sale of a commodity (labour-power), and in this connection these values themselves serve only towards the constant reproduction of the commodity to be sold." So the worker's "revenue" is not a share in a social fund on the same footing as the capitalist's profit; it is the price of a commodity he must perpetually re-sell.

Marx now offers a careful analogy to make his point about the independence of value determination: "To this extent, Adam Smith is correct to say that the value component of the product created by the worker for which the capitalist pays an equivalent in the form of the wage becomes a source of revenue for the worker. But this affects the nature and the size of this portion of commodity value just as little as the value of the means of production is affected by the fact that they function as capital values, or the nature and length of a straight line by the fact that it functions as the base of a triangle or the axis of an ellipse. The value of labour-power remains just as independently determined as that of any means of production."

This is a strong philosophical claim. The function a value serves — as capital, as revenue, as base of a triangle — does not affect its magnitude or its nature as value. The base of a triangle is still a straight line of a given length; the fact that it serves as a base does not make it longer or shorter, nor does it cease to be a line. This anticipates the later metaphor of drawing a straight line and dividing it into segments, which Marx will use to illustrate the difference between composition and resolution of value.

The consequence for Smith: "This component of commodity value does not consist of revenue as an autonomous factor constituting it, nor does this component of value resolve itself into revenue. This new value constantly reproduced by the worker forms a source of revenue for him, but it does not follow from this, inversely, that his revenue forms a component of the new value he has produced. What determines the volume of his revenue is the share in the new value he creates that is paid to him, and not the other way round." Marx illustrates with a homely example: "If my income is 10 shillings per week, then the circumstances of this weekly income affect neither the nature of the 10 shillings' value nor its magnitude." And: "Just as with any other commodity, so in the case of labour-power, too, its value is determined by the amount of labour needed to reproduce it; ... this is a characteristic of this particular commodity (labour-power), but it is no more peculiar to it than the fact that the value of draught cattle is determined by the means of subsistence needed for their maintenance ... is peculiar to these draught cattle."

This last point — the draught-cattle analogy — is pointed. It mocks Smith's own comparison of workers to "labouring cattle." If the value of cattle is determined by the labour needed to feed them, why should the value of labour-power be determined otherwise? The "revenue" of the worker is no more mysterious, from a value-theoretic standpoint, than the "feed" of a cow: it is the input required to maintain the commodity. The worker's revenue is a cost of production of labour-power, not a component of value.

Marx now names the root cause: "The category of 'revenue' is the root cause of Smith's whole trouble. For him the different types of revenue form the 'component parts' of the commodity value annually produced, newly created, whereas in actual fact it is the other way round; it is the two parts into which this commodity value is divided for the capitalist — the equivalent for the variable capital that he advanced in the money form for the purchase of labour, and the other portion of value that belongs to him although it has cost him nothing, the surplus-value — that form sources of revenue."

This is a clean statement of the correct causal ordering: value is prior to revenue; revenue is a distribution of value. Smith reverses this, making revenue prior to value. "The equivalent of the variable capital is advanced once again in labour-power, and to this extent forms a revenue for the worker in the form of his wage; the other part — the surplus-value — since it does not have to replace for the capitalist any of the capital advanced, can be spent by him on means of consumption (both necessities and luxuries), can be consumed as revenue, instead of forming capital of any kind. The precondition for this revenue is the commodity value itself, and its components are distinguished for the capitalist only to the extent that they form either an equivalent for, or a surplus over, the variable capital that he has advanced."

Then a crucial positive statement: "Both of these consist of nothing but labour-power spent in the course of commodity production, set flowing as labour. They do not consist of income or revenue, but rather of expenditure — expenditure of labour." This is Marx's materialist first principle. Value is congealed labour; revenue is a social right to consume value. Revenue does not "consist of" anything but value; it is not a substance but a claim.

Marx now formulates the quid pro quo: "After this quid pro quo, in which revenue is made into the source of commodity value, instead of this commodity value being the source of revenue, commodity value now appears to be 'composed' of the various kinds of revenues; these are determined independently of one another, and the total value of the commodity is determined by adding together the values of these revenues."

But this generates an impossible problem: "how can we determine the value of each of these revenues from which the commodity value is supposed to derive? In the case of wages, this can in fact be done, because wages are the value of the commodity labour-power, and the latter can be determined (like the value of any other commodity) by the labour needed for its reproduction. But as for surplus-value, or in Adam Smith's case its two forms, profit and rent, how are these to be determined? Here we are left with empty prattle."

This is the withering conclusion. Once revenues are treated as primary, wages can be determined (because labour-power is a commodity with a determinate value), but profit and rent have no independent determinant. Smith can only say they are what remains. And so the theory is not even internally consistent as an explanation of the sources of value.

Marx now introduces his famous geometric metaphor to distinguish composition from resolution: "At some points Adam Smith depicts wages and surplus-value (or wages and profit) as components out of which the commodity value or price is put together, while at others, and often almost in the same breath, he depicts them as parts into which the commodity value 'resolves itself'; the latter, however, means that the commodity value is given first and that various parts of this given value accrue to different persons involved in the production process in the form of different revenues. This is in no way identical with the composition of value from these three components."

He elaborates: "If I define the lengths of three straight lines independently, and then make these three lines 'components' of a fourth straight line equal in length to their sum, this is in no way the same procedure as if I start with a given straight line and divide this for some purpose or other — 'resolve' it, so to speak — into three parts. The length of the line in the first case invariably changes with the length of the three lines whose sum it forms; in the latter case the length of the three segments is limited from the beginning by their forming parts of a line of a given size."

This is a decisive logical point. Composition (adding up) makes the total dependent on the parts; resolution (dividing up) makes the parts dependent on the total. Smith's claim that commodity value "resolves itself" into wages, profit, rent, if taken strictly, would mean the total is prior and the parts derive from it — a correct procedure as far as it goes. But Smith almost immediately shifts to treating the parts as components whose independent magnitudes determine the total — an incorrect procedure, since it leaves the total underdetermined (either it doesn't add up, or we have no independent determinants for profit and rent). Marx's point is that Smith runs these two incompatible procedures together.

Marx now sums up the correct kernel of Smith's doctrine: "if we stick to what is correct in Smith's presentation, i.e., that that value contained in the society's annual commodity product which is newly created by the year's labour is equal (just as with any single commodity or the product of a day, week, etc.) to the sum of the variable capital advanced (i.e., to that value component destined to serve again for the purchase of labour-power), plus the surplus-value ... if we bear in mind moreover that Smith lumps together labour in its value-creating capacity, as expenditure of labour-power, with labour in its capacity of creating use-value, i.e., labour spent in useful, purposive form — then the whole conception boils down to this: The value of any commodity is the product of labour; so too, therefore, is the value of the product of a year's labour or the value of the society's annual commodity product. Since however all labour can be resolved into (1) necessary labour-time, in which the worker simply reproduces an equivalent for the capital advanced in purchasing his labour-power, and (2) surplus labour, by which he supplies the capitalist with a value for which the latter pays no equivalent, i.e., a surplus-value, all commodity value can then be resolved simply into these two different components, and in the last analysis it forms, as wages, the revenue of the working class, and as surplus-value, that of the capitalist class."

Now the crucial critical move: "As far as the constant capital value is concerned, however, i.e., the value of the means of production consumed in the production of the annual product, there is no way of telling how this value comes into the value of the new product (except in the mere phrase that the capitalist charges it to the buyer when he sells his commodity), but 'ultimately,' since the means of production are themselves the product of labour, this portion of value can in turn consist only of an equivalent for the variable capital and the surplus-value; that is, it can consist only of the product of necessary labour and surplus labour." Marx then makes his final critical move against the "ultimately" argument: "If the values of these means of production function in the hands of their employers as capital values, this does not hinder them from being divisible 'originally' (and if one goes to the root of it, in other people's hands, even though earlier) into the same two portions of value, i.e., into two different sources of revenue."

But this is sophistical — as Marx has shown — because it simply pushes the problem one step backward ad infinitum. What Smith needed to show was not that a previous generation's products had once been v + s, but that the current year's product replaces the c it consumes without itself requiring c. This requires a real theory of the coexistence of value-transfer (by concrete labour) with value-creation (by abstract labour), which Smith does not have.

The subsection closes with an important concession to Smith: "A correct point in all this is that in the movement of the social capital — i.e., of the totality of individual capitals — things look different from the way they do when each individual capital is taken separately, i.e., from the standpoint of each individual capitalist. For the latter, commodity value can be resolved into (1) a constant element (a fourth element, as Smith says) and (2) the sum of wages and surplus-value, i.e., wages, profit and rent. From the social standpoint, however, Smith's fourth element, the constant capital value, simply vanishes."

This is a moment of remarkable dialectical generosity. Marx acknowledges that Smith's theory is not simply wrong; there is a perspective from which the constant element "vanishes." At the individual-capitalist level, c is a real fourth element. At the social level, c is a portion of the annual product — specifically, the portion consisting of means of production — that is never available as revenue for anyone, but is always functioning as capital. It does not "vanish" in the sense of ceasing to exist; it "vanishes" in the sense of not entering the revenue fund. Smith's confusion is precisely that he treats the social standpoint as if only the individual standpoint counted — as if the sum of consumable revenues exhausted the annual product.

Summary: The Deeper Foundation of Smith's Error

Subsection (e) recapitulates and deepens the critique. "The absurd formula that the three revenues of wages, profit and rent form three 'components' of commodity value, derives in Smith's case from the more plausible formula that commodity value 'resolves itself' into these three components. This too is false, even on the assumption that commodity value can be divided simply into the equivalent for the labour-power used up and the surplus-value created by the latter."

Note the fine logical discrimination. There are two distinct claims: - (A) Commodity value = the sum of wages + profit + rent (composition). This is absurd. - (B) Commodity value resolves itself into wages + profit + rent (resolution). This is more plausible but still false, because it omits c even as a residual category.

Yet, Marx says, even (B)'s error "is based in turn on a deeper and true foundation." This deeper foundation is the reality of capitalist production itself: "Capitalist production rests on the fact that the productive worker sells his own labour-power as a commodity to the capitalist, in whose hands it then functions simply as an element of his productive capital. This transaction (the sale and purchase of labour-power) does not just introduce the production process, but implicitly determines its specific character."

Marx then recalls the fundamental structure of the analysis from earlier volumes: "The production of a use-value, and even of a commodity (something that can also be undertaken by independent productive workers), is here only a means for the production of absolute and relative surplus-value for the capitalist. In analysing the production process, therefore, we saw how the production of absolute and relative surplus-value determines (1) the duration of the daily labour process, and (2) the whole social and technical shape of capitalist production."

Within this process, three distinct functions must be distinguished: "(a) the mere maintenance of value (of the constant capital value), (b) the actual reproduction of value advanced (the equivalent for labour-power), and (c) the production of surplus-value, i.e., of value for which the capitalist neither advanced a previous equivalent, nor advances one after the event."

The appropriative act — "the appropriation of surplus-value (of a value over and above the equivalent of the value advanced by the capitalist)" — even though it is preceded by the purchase and sale of labour-power, "is an act performed within the production process itself, and forms an essential moment of the latter." This is the crucial point: surplus-value is not appropriated in circulation (as if the capitalist bought cheap and sold dear); it is appropriated in production, from the unpaid labour-time of the worker.

Marx adds a foundational point about the presuppositions of the whole system: "The introductory act of circulation, the purchase and sale of labour-power, itself depends in turn on a distribution of the social elements of production which is the presupposition and premise of the distribution of social products, viz., the separation between labour-power as a commodity for the worker, and the means of production as the property of non-workers."

This is one of the most important sentences in the chapter. The historical presupposition of the entire capitalist process is the separation of the producer from the means of production. This is a precondition that cannot be generated within the process itself; it must be historically established. Once established, the process reproduces it every time it runs (the worker must sell labour-power to eat; the capitalist must buy it to profit).

Yet, having said all this, Marx insists on a further distinction: "At the same time, however, this appropriation of surplus-value, the separation of value production into the reproduction of the value advanced and the production of a new value that does not replace any equivalent (a surplus-value), in no way affects the substance of value itself and the nature of value production. The substance of value is and remains nothing more than expended labour-power — labour independent of its particular useful character — and value production is nothing but the process of this expenditure."

This is essential. The social form of value production (capitalist appropriation) does not affect the substance of value (expended labour-power). The specific appropriation relations are separable from the value-substance. This is what allows Marx to compare capitalist value production with other historically specific forms: "If the feudal serf, for example, expends his labour-power for six days of the week, i.e., works for these six days, it makes no difference to the fact of this expenditure of labour-power as such that he may work for three of these days on his own field and three others on his lord's field and for his lord. Both his voluntary labour for himself and his forced labour for his lord are equally labour; in as much as this is considered as labour in relation to the values — or even the useful products — created by it, there is no distinction between these six days' labour. The distinction is solely related to the different situations which have given rise to the expenditure of his labour-power in the two halves of the six-day working period. It is just the same with the necessary and surplus labour of the wage-labourers."

The feudal serf analogy is illuminating. The serf's six days constitute the same quantity of abstract labour regardless of who benefits. The distinction between "own work" and "corvée" is not a distinction within the labour; it is a distinction in the social conditions under which the labour is performed. Similarly with the wage-labourer: necessary labour and surplus labour are the same abstract labour; they differ only in the social relations under which the products are appropriated. This is the deep truth that Smith's category of revenue obscures — by treating revenues as the "elements" of value, Smith makes it appear as if the social distribution is constitutive of the value itself.

Marx now delivers the key exposition of the finished commodity and its value-structure. "The process of production disappears in the finished commodity. The fact that labour-power was expended to create it now appears in the form that the commodity has the following concrete property: it possesses value. The magnitude of this value is measured by the amount of labour expended; the commodity value cannot be resolved into anything further, and consists of nothing more."

He then gives a second geometric metaphor, richer than the first: "If I draw a straight line of a certain length, I have, in the first place, 'produced' a straight line (only symbolically, of course, as I am aware from the start) by my manner of drawing, practised in accordance with certain rules (laws) that are independent of me. If I divide this line into three segments (which may be required for a certain problem), then each of these three pieces remains a straight line, as before, and this division does not resolve the line whose parts they are into anything other than a straight line, e.g., a curve of some kind. Just as little can I divide this line of a given length in such a way that the sum of these parts is greater than the undivided line itself; the length of the undivided line in other words is not determined by the lengths of the segments into which it is divided. It is rather the relative lengths of the latter that are limited in advance by the limits of the line of which they are parts."

Several points are packed into this metaphor. (1) The line's existence as a line is owed to the act of drawing, which follows rules independent of the drawer's will. Analogously, value exists by virtue of labour-time, under laws of socially necessary labour-time not subject to the individual's caprice. (2) Dividing the line into parts does not change its nature. So dividing value into c + v + s does not change the nature of value as congealed labour. (3) The parts cannot sum to more than the whole. So profit and rent cannot be independent determinants that "add up" beyond the total value. (4) The parts' relative magnitudes are determined by the limits of the whole. So the distribution of value is bound by the total, not the reverse.

"In this respect, the commodity produced by the capitalist is in no way distinguished from a commodity produced by an independent worker, by a community of workers, or by slaves. In our case, however, the entire product of labour and its entire value belongs to the capitalist. Just like any other producer, he has first to transform the commodity into money by selling it, before he can manipulate it any further; he must convert it into the form of the universal equivalent."

Marx now analyzes the finished product's value into its parts, in a systematic sequence.

First, the constant capital component. "A part of this value is merely the value of the means of production used up to produce the commodity, which reappears in a new form. This value has not been produced in the process of producing the commodity, for the means of production already possessed this value before the production process in question and independently of it. They entered this process as the bearers of this value, and all that has been replaced and changed is simply its form of appearance. For the capitalist, this part of the commodity value forms an equivalent for the portion of the constant capital value he advanced that has been consumed in the commodity's production." The value now exists as a component of the new commodity; it will be converted back into means of production when the commodity is sold. "The value character of a commodity is in no way changed by the function of this value as capital." Again, the function does not change the nature.

Second, the variable capital component. "A second portion of the commodity's value is the value of the labour-power that the worker sells to the capitalist. This is determined, just like the value of the means of production, independently of the production process which the labour-power is to enter, and is fixed in an act of circulation, the purchase and sale of labour-power, before this goes into the production process. In the course of his function — the expenditure of his labour-power — the wage-labourer produces a commodity value equal to the value that the capitalist has to pay him for the use of his labour-power. He gives the capitalist this value in commodities, and the capitalist pays him the same in money. If this part of the commodity value is for the capitalist only an equivalent for the variable capital that he has to advance in wages, this makes absolutely no difference to the fact that it is a commodity value newly created during the production process, consisting exactly like the surplus-value of past expenditure of labour-power."

Third, the surplus-value component. "Over and above them both, there is still the surplus-value. This has in common with the value component that replaces the variable capital advanced in wages that it is a value newly created by the workers — congealed labour. It is just that it does not cost anything to the owner of the total product, the capitalist. This circumstance is what enables the capitalist to consume it completely as revenue, as long as he does not have to deduct parts of it for others with a stake in it — e.g., rent for the landlords — in which case these portions then constitute the revenues of third parties of this kind. This same circumstance was also the driving motive for our capitalist to concern himself with commodity production at all. But neither his original benevolent intention to hunt out surplus-value, nor the subsequent spending of this as revenue by himself and others, affects the surplus-value as such. It in no way alters the fact that it is unpaid congealed labour, nor does it alter its size, which is determined by quite other factors."

Marx then delivers a sharply ironic observation about Smith's logic: "Given that Adam Smith already wanted to concern himself in dealing with commodity value with the roles that the various parts of this play in the overall process of reproduction, it should have been evident that, if certain portions function as revenue, others must just as constantly function as capital — and on his logic, this means that these should also have been described as constituent parts of the commodity value, or parts into which it is resolved." In other words, if Smith is going to explain value by its functions (as revenue), then he must also explain it by its other function (as capital) — which would force him to include c. His own method should have forced him to correct himself.

Marx now diagnoses Smith's methodological error at the deepest level: "Adam Smith identifies commodity production in general with capitalist commodity production; the means of production are 'capital' from the beginning, labour is wage-labour." Smith says in the Introduction (p. 105): "The number of useful and productive labourers ... is everywhere in proportion to the quantity of capital stock which is employed in setting them to work." Marx comments: "In short, the various factors of the labour process — objective and personal — appear from the start in the character masks of the era of capitalist production. The analysis of commodity value therefore directly coincides with the question as to how far this value is, on the one hand, simply the equivalent for the capital laid out, and how far, on the other hand, it constitutes 'free' value that does not replace any capital value advanced, i.e., is surplus-value."

The point is that Smith's analysis of commodity value is already the analysis of capitalist commodity value. He doesn't have a prior, abstract analysis of value in general on which to fall back. He jumps directly into the comparison of value to "capital laid out" vs. "free" surplus. This makes him prone to reading the specific forms of capitalist value distribution back into the general concept of value. "The portions of commodity value compared from this standpoint are thereby transformed surreptitiously into its independent 'component parts,' and ultimately into the 'sources of all value.'"

And so his final error is total: "A further conclusion is that commodity value is composed of revenues of various kinds, or alternatively is 'resolved into' these revenues, so that it is not the revenues that consist of commodity value, but rather the commodity value that consists of 'revenues.'" This is the inversion in its pure form. Value does not consist of revenues; revenues consist of value. Smith has turned the world upside down.

"But just as it scarcely affects the nature of a commodity value as commodity value, or of money as money, whether it functions as capital value or not, so equally a commodity value is scarcely altered by the fact that it goes on to function as revenue for this person or that." Statement of the correct method: value is prior to all its social functions.

Marx's prescription: "The commodities Smith is dealing with here are commodity capital from the start (and therefore include surplus-value as well as the capital value consumed in their production), i.e., they are commodities produced in the capitalist manner, the result of the capitalist production process. This last should therefore have been the object of prior analysis, together with the process of valorization and value formation that it involves. And since the very presupposition of this is in turn commodity circulation, its presentation also requires, therefore, an independent and prior analysis of the commodity."

This is a direct statement of Marx's own method in Capital Volume 1: begin with the analysis of the commodity and money (Part One), then move to the capitalist production process. The footnote attached to this passage (numbered 7 n. in the text) explicitly notes: "This is of course Marx's own procedure in Capital, Volume 1, of which commences with the analysis of 'Commodities and Money' (Part One), and continues with the all-round analysis of the capitalist production process. The separate text on 'The Results of the Immediate Process of Production,' published in the present edition as an appendix to Volume 1, analyses commodities produced in the capitalist manner as this 'result' and this lays the basis for the analysis of capital circulation in Volume 2."

The conclusion of this section: "Even when Smith in his 'esoteric' aspect occasionally comes up with something correct, he takes account of value formation only in connection with the analysis of commodities, i.e., the analysis of commodity capital." So Smith never reaches the level of abstraction at which value in general can be analyzed independently of its capitalist form. This is the methodological failure from which all others flow.

Later Writers: The Persistence and Degeneration of the Dogma

The final section of the chapter surveys Smith's successors. It is compact but highly damning. The section is an addition from Manuscript II, and represents Marx's ongoing effort to establish the dogma's historical transmission.

Ricardo "reproduces Adam Smith's theory almost verbatim." Marx quotes from Ricardo's Principles (p. 169, note): "It must be understood that all the productions of a country are consumed; but it makes the greatest difference imaginable whether they are consumed by those who reproduce, or by those who do not reproduce another value. When we say that revenue is saved, and added to capital, what we mean is, that the portion of revenue, so said to be added to capital, is consumed by productive instead of unproductive labourers." Marx notes: "In point of fact Ricardo completely accepted Smith's theory of the resolution of commodity price into wages and surplus-value (or variable capital and surplus-value)."

Yet Marx grants Ricardo two important departures from Smith. "(1) the components of surplus-value: he eliminates ground-rent as a necessary element of it; (2) Ricardo breaks down commodity price into these components. The magnitude of value thus comes first. He takes the sum of the components as a given magnitude, rather than deriving the magnitude of value of the commodity after the event by adding together its components, as Smith often does, even against his own better judgement."

The second point is important. Ricardo avoids Smith's "composition" fallacy. He does not say "value = the sum of the revenues"; he says "value is a given magnitude, which is then divided into the revenues." This is closer to Marx's procedure. But Ricardo still inherits Smith's omission of c. He never questions the v + s framework itself; he merely gets the internal logic of distribution right. This is why Marx elsewhere calls Ricardo "the last great representative of classical political economy" but also criticizes him for accepting Smith's framework.

Ramsay is then quoted, because he is one of the few who breaks with Smith on the substantive point. In his An Essay on the Distribution of Wealth (Edinburgh, 1836), p. 174: "He [Ricardo] seems always to consider the whole produce as divided between wages and profits, forgetting the part necessary for replacing fixed capital." Marx notes that Ramsay understands by "fixed capital" what Marx calls constant capital, and quotes Ramsay's definition: "Fixed capital exists in a form in which, though assisting to raise the future commodity, it does not maintain labourers" (p. 59). The definition is imprecise (it identifies fixed capital by its failure to maintain labourers, rather than by its material role in production), but the substantive point — that a portion of the produce must replace fixed capital and cannot be divided as wages and profits — is correct. This will reappear as one of the few genuine advances on Smith.

Marx now turns to the logical degeneration of Smith's doctrine. "Adam Smith baulked at the logical conclusion of his resolution of commodity value, and thus of the value of the annual social product, into wages and surplus-value, i.e., simply into revenue: the conclusion that the total annual product could then be entirely consumed. It is never the original thinkers who draw the absurd conclusions. They rather leave this for the Says and MacCullochs."

Jean-Baptiste Say "certainly has an easy time of it. What is for one person an advance of capital is for the other revenue and net product, or was so at least; the distinction between gross and net product is purely subjective, and 'thus the entire value of all products has been distributed in society as revenue' (Traité d'économie politique, 1817, II, p. 64)." Say continues: "The total value of any product is composed of the profits of the landlords, the capitalists and the artisans" (Marx's parenthetical: "thus wages figure here as profits des industrieux [artisans' profits]!") "who have contributed to its production. This means that the society's revenue is equal to the gross value produced, and not as the sect of economists" (the Physiocrats) "believed, only equal to the net product of the soil" (p. 63).

Say's conclusion is precisely the absurd one Smith had refused to draw: that the entire value of all products is revenue, and (consequently) that a society can consume its entire annual product without disinvestment. Marx notes: "This discovery of Say's was taken over by Proudhon, among others." The sarcasm is blunt: Say's "discovery" is the logical consequence of Smith's error, but Smith had been prudent enough not to state it. Say lacks even that prudence.

Storch accepts Smith's doctrine "in principle" but finds Say's application "untenable." His critique: "If it is held that the revenue of a nation is equal to its gross product, i.e., that no capital (he should say no constant capital) needs to be deducted, it must also be conceded that this nation can consume the entire value of its annual product unproductively, without making the least inroad on its future revenue.... The products that compose the (constant) capital of a nation are not consumable" (Considérations sur la nature du revenu national, Paris, 1842, pp. 147, 150).

Marx points out the inconsistency in Storch's position: "Storch has forgotten to tell us how the existence of this constant portion of capital fits in with the analysis of prices that he has taken over from Smith, in which commodity value contains only wages and surplus-value, but no portion of constant capital." This is the crucial move. Storch sees the constant portion of capital (empirically), but his price theory (taken from Smith) cannot accommodate it. "It is only through Say that he realizes that this price analysis leads to absurd results, and his own last word on it is 'that it is impossible to resolve the necessary price into its simplest elements' (Cours d'économie politique, Petersburg, 1815, II, p. 141)." Storch ends with an implicit retraction of Smith's price analysis, but he cannot correct it — he just declares the problem insoluble. This is a fitting image of the state of the theory before Marx: those who see the difficulty cannot resolve it within Smith's framework.

Sismondi, who "deals particularly with the relation between capital and revenue, and in actual fact makes his particular conception of this relationship the differentia specifica of his Nouveaux Principes, did not utter a single scientific word or contribute one jot or tittle to the explanation of the problem." This is a scathing verdict on the most important critic of capitalism in the generation after Smith. Sismondi saw the problem (the relation between capital and revenue) but could not solve it because he remained within Smith's framework.

Barton, Ramsay, and Cherbuliez "make attempts to go beyond Smith's conception. They fail because they pose the problem one-sidedly from the start, by not clearly separating the distinction between constant and variable capital from the distinction between fixed and circulating capital." This is a key diagnosis. The fixed/circulating distinction (Smith's) and the constant/variable distinction (Marx's) are different axes. Fixed/circulating concerns the velocity of turnover (how quickly value is returned through the sale of the product). Constant/variable concerns the source of surplus-value (constant capital doesn't produce surplus-value; variable capital does). Conflating them — as Ramsay, Barton, and Cherbuliez do — prevents any solution: they think that because some capital is "fixed" it doesn't produce surplus-value (or because some is "circulating" it does), which is the wrong criterion.

John Stuart Mill "reproduces the doctrine handed down by Adam Smith to his successors, with his customary air of self-importance." The sarcasm is brief and complete. Mill, the most technically accomplished economist of the mid-century, simply perpetuates the error.

The chapter's concluding sentence: "The result is that Smith's confusion persists to this day, and his dogma forms an article of orthodox belief in political economy."

This is the final verdict. Despite a century of critique — Physiocratic, Ricardian, Sismondian, Storch's, Ramsay's — the fundamental error stands. It is the doxa of the discipline — orthodox, unquestioned, reproduced in textbooks.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles Revealed

Principle 1: Value is prior to revenue. The most important conceptual lesson of the chapter is the ordering of value and revenue. Smith inverted it, treating revenues (wages, profit, rent) as the constituent elements of value. Marx restores the correct order: value is the substance (congealed labour); revenues are the uses to which portions of value are put. As Marx puts it: "It is not the revenues that consist of commodity value, but rather the commodity value that consists of 'revenues'" — this is Smith's error in its pure form, and it is precisely the inversion Marx corrects. The consequences of this correction are enormous. Once revenues are seen as derivatives, the idea that additional revenue-receivers (bankers, professors, prostitutes, mercenaries) produce value evaporates; their incomes are derivative claims on value produced elsewhere. This is the deep truth that Smith's exoteric confusion concealed.

Principle 2: Function does not affect substance. A closely related principle is dropped in repeatedly through the chapter's geometric analogies. Whether 10 shillings is spent as capital or as revenue, its value is 10 shillings. Whether a line serves as the base of a triangle or an axis of an ellipse, its length is unchanged. The function a value serves (capital vs. revenue) is a matter of social form; it does not alter the substance of value (congealed labour) or its magnitude (determined by labour-time). This is what allows Marx to insist on the independence of the value of labour-power from the wage's function as revenue. It also grounds the critique of Smith: by treating function (revenue) as constitutive of value, Smith commits a category error — a confusion of the substance with its social functions.

Principle 3: The twofold character of labour. This is the technical key that unlocks the whole problem. The labour spent in production is (a) abstract, value-creating labour, which adds new value (v + s); and (b) concrete, useful labour, which preserves and transfers the value of the consumed means of production (c) into the new product. Because Smith does not distinguish these two aspects, he cannot explain where c comes from — unless he "dissolves" it by an infinite regress (always pushing c back to previous productions, and so on). Once the distinction is in place, the answer is elegant: c does not have to be created in the current year; it only has to be preserved. Concrete labour does not manufacture the value of the wood it transforms into a table; it merely retains it. Hence the annual value product (v + s) is strictly less than the value of the annual product (c + v + s), without any paradox.

Principle 4: The division of the annual product into two departments. Marx's constructive conclusion in the "General Perspectives" section is that Smith's scattered insights imply a two-departmental analysis: Department I produces means of production; Department II produces means of consumption. The value components of Department I's product — including the portion that represents wages and surplus-value — are not consumable in material form; they must be exchanged with Department II to acquire consumable goods. Conversely, Department II's product, while consisting of consumption goods, includes a value component (c₂) that is not available as revenue for anyone — it must be exchanged with Department I for replacement means of production. This two-department schema is the seed of the reproduction schemas in Capital Vol. 2, Part 3.

Principle 5: The relation between circulation and production. The chapter also teaches a crucial methodological lesson about the separation of analytical levels. Acts of circulation (buying, selling, receiving money, spending it) — when "simply considered by themselves" — are indifferent to what happens to the money received or the things bought. The circular-flow aspects of wages and profits, examined purely as circulation, are separate from the production-process aspects (value creation, surplus-value appropriation). Smith conflates these; Marx separates them. This methodological lesson grounds the distinction between the analysis of commodity circulation (Capital Vol. 1, Part 1), the analysis of production (Capital Vol. 1, Parts 2-7), and the analysis of the circulation of capital (Capital Vol. 2).

Broader Practical and Theoretical Implications

For the theory of distribution. The chapter's most direct implication is that any theory of distribution that treats wages, profit, and rent as additive components of price is logically incoherent. Such theories cannot account for the total (the parts don't add up to the whole), and they cannot independently determine the magnitudes of profit and rent (except by residual). Ricardo's "given magnitude, then distributed" approach is logically superior to Smith's "components added up" approach — but even Ricardo's approach omits c. Marx's correct approach: value (c + v + s) is determined by labour-time; then it is distributed among capitalists (profit), landlords (rent), and workers (wages). The distribution is constrained by the total.

For the theory of crisis. The two-department schema has enormous implications for crisis theory (which Marx develops elsewhere). If Department I and Department II must exchange in specific proportions — c₂ = v₁ + s₁ and c₁ + c₂ = total means of production — then any disproportion between the departments can produce a crisis. The reproduction schemas allow Marx to model the conditions of balanced growth under simple and expanded reproduction, and to locate sources of imbalance. The critique of Smith's v + s framework clears the way for this.

For the critique of vulgar economics. The chapter ends with a flourish about the "orthodox belief" of political economy. The point is that vulgar economics — the tradition descending from Say, Bastiat, Roscher, and (in Marx's view) Mill — is built on the Smithian confusion of revenues with value components. The critique of Smith's constant-capital blind spot is thus also a critique of the entire apologetic tradition that treats the surface categories of distribution as the deep structure of production.

For the critique of the Physiocrats. The chapter opens with an appreciative but critical reconstruction of Quesnay. Marx's treatment complicates the standard line that Physiocracy was a mere error. He insists that beneath the "feudal signboard" (the ideological self-presentation as defenders of landed property) lies the first systematic theory of capitalist production. The Physiocrats' method — the Tableau as a flow analysis between classes — anticipates Marx's own two-departmental analysis. Their error (agriculture as the sole source of surplus-value) is compensated by their insight (reproduction as a social, class-structured process). The lesson is methodological: a theory's ideological self-understanding may be at odds with its substantive content; the critic's task is to extract the content from the form, not to reject the whole on the basis of its ideological wrapping.

For the theory of exploitation. Marx's careful argument about the creation of the payment fund by the worker himself has important implications. "The worker therefore himself creates the payment fund from which the capitalist pays him." This is the material basis for the appearance that the capitalist "advances" wages. It also grounds the argument — developed more fully in Capital Vol. 1 — that wages are a form of the value of labour-power and not a share of the product determined by the capitalist's goodwill. Furthermore, the worker's spending of his wage reproduces for the capitalist "the only instrument by means of which he can remain a capitalist." The reproduction of labour-power is thus not just a private affair; it is the reproduction of the fundamental condition of capitalist production.

For the concept of value itself. The chapter clarifies Marx's concept of value in a way that is often lost in summary. Value is "expended labour-power — labour independent of its particular useful character." It is real, definite, and measurable. It is not a mere accounting convention; it is a social substance that can be transferred (by concrete labour) or created (by abstract labour). It is prior to and independent of the social forms in which it appears (as capital, as revenue, as wealth). The geometric metaphors (straight line) underscore this realist commitment: just as a line's length is real and unchanged by its function, value's magnitude is real and unchanged by its social role.

For the methodology of the history of ideas. The chapter demonstrates Marx's characteristic historiographical method: he reads the tradition dialectically, looking for the rational kernel inside the mystical shell, and treats even "errors" as products of deeper truths. Smith's error — treating revenues as components of value — "is based in turn on a deeper and true foundation," namely the reality of capitalist production relations. And the Physiocrats' error — the narrowness of seeing only agriculture as productive — "comes to grips with the main question" because natural reproduction visibly requires capital replacement. Marx's history of economics is not a tale of fools; it is a tale of partial insights whose limits are historically explicable. This is why Marx's critiques of Smith, Ricardo, and even Say are not merely dismissive but precisely diagnostic: he identifies the specific source of each error in the theoretical framework, and shows why it was necessary given the analytical instruments available.

For the relationship between Marx and his predecessors. The chapter makes plain that Marx's own analysis is not a refutation of classical political economy from outside, but a resolution of its internal contradictions. Smith and Ricardo developed all the essential categories (value, surplus-value, capital, reproduction) but could not assemble them coherently because of the residual fixed/circulating (rather than constant/variable) distinction and the confusion of value with its distributive forms. Marx's achievement is to complete the classical project by introducing the distinction (constant/variable) and the framework (two departments) that the classics lacked. The chapter is thus not merely historical but self-clarifying: by showing where Smith went wrong, Marx is showing where his own solution necessarily goes.

Philosophical Implications

The dialectical structure of the argument. Throughout the chapter, Marx proceeds dialectically. He shows that Smith saw the correct phenomena (the workers producing means of production vs. consumption goods; the "circulating capital of society" with distinct properties from individual circulating capital; the constant capital as a "fourth element" at the level of the individual capitalist) but could not organize them into a coherent whole. The reason is that his categories (fixed/circulating) were not adequate to the phenomena; he needed different categories (constant/variable; means of production/means of consumption). The dialectical progression is from surface confusions to underlying distinctions to a reconstructed system. This is the method that Marx's own Capital enacts: start from the commodity (surface), move to value (substance), then to surplus-value (process), then to reproduction (whole).

The concept of "revenue" and its ideological function. The chapter's deep theme is the ideological function of the category of revenue. By treating revenues as original "sources" of value, Smith opens the door for a whole apologetic tradition that presents the existing distribution of the product as natural, necessary, and original. Marx's counter-argument is that revenue is a distribution of value created in production; it is derivative, not original; and the only "original" source is labour. The critique is not just logical; it is ideological-critical. It exposes how the surface categories of economic science can serve to legitimate exploitation by erasing its traces.

Historical materialism implicit in the argument. The chapter's insistence on the specific historical presuppositions of capitalist production — "the separation between labour-power as a commodity for the worker, and the means of production as the property of non-workers" — points to the historical-materialist framework. Capitalist production is not a natural form of economic life; it requires historical preconditions. Once established, it reproduces those preconditions internally. The critique of Smith is therefore also a critique of the attempt to treat the categories of capitalist production as natural and universal.

The philosophical realism of the value-form. Finally, the chapter defends a kind of realism about value: value is real, not merely conventional or perceptual; it has substance and magnitude; it is independent of the forms (capital, revenue) in which it appears. This is a philosophical position that runs counter both to the empiricist reduction of economics to surface transactions and to the idealist reduction of value to subjective utility. It is, in its own register, a materialist realism: value is congealed labour-power, and the analysis of value is the analysis of this material substance.

Concluding Reflection

Chapter 19 is, in the end, a chapter about how to see correctly. The Physiocrats saw correctly, through a glass darkly; Smith saw truly esoteric things but kept losing them in exoteric formulations; Ricardo got the distribution right but accepted the wrong framework; Say drew the absurd conclusion; Storch saw the difficulty and gave up; Ramsay saw part of the problem and posed it one-sidedly; Sismondi saw the question and produced nothing; Mill repeated the tradition with self-satisfaction. The result: an entire discipline built on a mistake. Marx's contribution is to diagnose the mistake, extract the correct kernel from the tradition, and build a system in which — as he was soon to show in the reproduction schemas — the entire problem is solved. The chapter's final sentence, "The result is that Smith's confusion persists to this day, and his dogma forms an article of orthodox belief in political economy," is not merely a lament. It is a challenge: to see through the confusion, to break with the dogma, and to build a science in which value is prior to revenue, production is prior to distribution, and capital's reproduction is understood in its specific, historical, class-structured reality.
