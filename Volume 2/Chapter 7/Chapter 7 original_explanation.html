Volume 2 • Chapter 7: The Turnover Time and the Number of Turnovers

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 7, titled "Turnover Time and Number of Turnovers," is the opening chapter of Part Two of Capital, Volume 2—the section devoted to The Turnover of Capital. Its deceptively modest title conceals a pivotal conceptual operation: the transformation of the circuit of capital (the single, isolated path traced by a capital value through its successive forms) into the turnover of capital (that same circuit understood not as a one-off act but as a periodic, endlessly repeated process). This distinction is the chapter's central intellectual contribution. A circuit, the author insists, is "only a section that is constantly repeated, i.e. a period." Once the circuit is grasped as repetition, capital acquires a genuinely new property that it did not possess as a mere circuit: it acquires a tempo, a periodicity, a measurable rhythm—what the text names its turnover time.

The chapter's core thesis is threefold. First, turnover time is quantitatively identical to the sum of production time and circulation time; it is nothing more than the total interval elapsing "from the moment that the capital value is advanced in a particular form until the return of the capital value in process in the same form." Second, this interval is measurable against a natural unit—just as the working day is the natural measuring unit for the function of labour-power, the year is the natural measuring unit for the turnover of capital, grounded in the biological fact that the staple food crops of the temperate zone (the historical birthplace of capitalist production) are annual products. Third, from these two determinations flows a compact but consequential formula: if the year is designated U, the turnover time of a given capital u, and the number of turnovers n, then n = U/u. This formula converts the qualitative drama of capital's metamorphoses into a single calculable ratio of speed.

But the chapter is not merely arithmetic. Before it can define turnover, it must settle a prior, more delicate question of form: which of capital's three circuits—M...M' (money capital), P...P (productive capital), or C'...C' (commodity capital)—is the appropriate frame for analyzing turnover? The chapter answers that turnover analysis must begin from the advance of capital value and must require its return in the form advanced, which disqualifies circuit III (C'...C') and mandates the use of circuits I and II, with circuit I (M...M') serving as the primary lens for questions of surplus-value formation and circuit II (P...P) for questions of product formation. This is a methodological purification: the chapter clears the conceptual ground so that the phenomena of speed, repetition, and periodicity can be studied cleanly. It closes by flagging that two new forms—forms capital acquires "as a result of the circulation process"—must be examined before turnover's effects on production and valorization can be traced, a deferral that opens the door to the analysis of fixed and circulating capital.

Within the broader architecture of Volume 2, then, this chapter functions as the threshold: it completes the analysis of the circuit (Part One) by showing how the circuit, when taken as periodic, yields the category of turnover, and thereby lays the foundation for Part Two's investigation of how turnover speed governs the annual rate of surplus-value, the scale of accumulation, and ultimately the reproduction of the total social capital.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

7.1 The Operational Definition of Turnover Time and the Valorization Imperative

The chapter opens by recalling and consolidating a definition from the preceding analysis rather than introducing a new one: "the overall time of circulation of a given capital is the sum of its circulation time proper and its production time." These two components must be kept rigorously distinct. Production time is the time during which capital is actually functioning in the sphere of production—being transformed, worked upon, combined with labour-power—while circulation time proper is the time during which capital is suspended between its successive forms, undergoing purchase and sale, lying in the market, or in transit. The chapter's definitional formula is that turnover time measures "the period of time that elapses from the moment that the capital value is advanced in a particular form until the return of the capital value in process in the same form." Everything hinges on the phrase in the same form. The capital must return not merely as some value, and not merely as more value, but specifically in the identical formal shape in which it set out. This strict "same-form" requirement is what makes the advance-and-return cycle a closed loop, and it is precisely this closure that is repeatable, and therefore periodic.

The author immediately grounds this in the purpose of capitalist production: "The specific purpose of capitalist production is always the valorization of the value advanced." The point deserves emphasis because it explains why the cycle cannot simply stop at recovery. It is not enough that the value come back; it must come back valorized—increased by surplus-value. And this valorization must be possible regardless of the initial form: whether the value is "advanced in its independent form, i.e. the money form, or in commodities, in which case its value form only possesses an ideal independence in the price of the commodities advanced." Here a subtle technical concept appears: ideal independence. When capital starts as commodities rather than money, those commodities' value does not stand before the capitalist as tangible coin; it exists only as a price—an ideal (thought, accounted) magnitude attached to goods. The underlying value is real, but its formal existence is, so to speak, notional until sale converts it into money. This matters because it prefigures the chapter's later argument about the capitalist's bookkeeping consciousness.

In all cases, the text stresses, "this capital value passes through different forms of existence in the course of its circuit. Its identity with itself is established in the capitalist's ledger, or in the form of money of account." This is a profound claim about how capital's continuity is represented: the transformations (money→commodities→production→new commodities→money) are heterogeneous, qualitative swerves, but the capitalist tracks them as one and the same value through the device of money of account—the abstract unit of reckoning in which debits and credits are recorded. The unity of capital is thus not something directly visible in any single form; it is an accounting fact, an identity maintained across qualitatively distinct embodiments. This observation quietly sets up the later critique of economists who mistake one circuit for the whole truth.

7.2 What Circuits M...M' and P...P Have in Common

The chapter now isolates what the two principal circuits share, asking us to treat the first two circuits—M...M' and P...P—as a pair before differentiating them from the third. Here the notations require glossing. M denotes capital value advanced in money form; the prime (M') denotes the valorized money, i.e., M plus surplus-value; the ellipsis ... denotes the intervening transformations (purchase, production, sale). P denotes capital in its productive form—the elements of production, i.e., means of production and labour-power—and P...P consequently denotes the circuit that starts and ends in the productive form. The author asserts that both forms include two facts: "(1) the value advanced functions as capital value and is valorized; (2) after describing its process, it returns to the form in which this process began."

The first fact establishes that in both circuits the starting quantity behaves as capital—it grows through the extraction of surplus-value—rather than as mere hoarded wealth or mere consumption fund. The second fact establishes the formal closure: the endpoint reoccupies the starting shape. The text then shows that these two facts, though obvious in M...M', are equally true of P...P—a point requiring care because P...P can appear, superficially, to be a purely "productive" affair with no obvious money bookends. The argument runs: "the starting-point of P is the presence of the elements of production, commodities of a given value. This form includes the valorization of this value (C and M') and its return to its original form, since in the second P the value advanced once again possesses the form of the elements of production in which it was originally advanced." In other words, even though P...P never begins with money, its internal trajectory passes through the commodity form (C, the product) and the money form (M', the sale proceeds), and it closes by reconstituting itself as a fresh batch of production elements—the "second P." So valorization and same-form return are interior to P...P just as they are exterior in M...M'.

To cement this, the chapter invokes the authority of its own earlier analysis, quoting Volume 1, Chapter 23: "If production has a capitalist form, so too will reproduction. Just as in the capitalist mode of production the labour process appears only as a means towards the process of valorization, so in the case of reproduction it appears only as a means of reproducing the value advanced as capital, i.e. as self-valorizing value." The import is that reproduction under capitalism is not neutral replenishment—it is the renewal of a self-expanding value. The worker does not merely reproduce the means of subsistence; the process is structurally bent toward reproducing the capital relationship. This grounds the claim that every circuit, regardless of its starting form, is fundamentally a valorization loop.

7.3 The Three Circuits Differentiated: M...M', P...P, and C'...C'

Having established the commonality of the two main circuits, the chapter turns to differentiation. The three circuits "are distinguished in the following ways." The first distinction concerns whether repetition is expressed as reality or mere possibility. "In form II (P...P) the repetition of the process, the process of reproduction, is expressed as a reality, whereas in form I it is only a possibility." The reasoning is elegant. Because P...P begins with production elements (machinery, raw materials, labour-power), its very starting point presupposes that a previous production process generated those elements—reproduction is built into its premise. By contrast, M...M' begins from money, which could equally well fund consumption or hoarding; that it should flow back into a new production cycle is contingent, a possibility rather than a certainty. Money does not announce its own origin in production the way production elements do; hence repetition is "only a possibility" in circuit I. This is a crucial nuance: the form of the starting point encodes information about the necessity of repetition.

The second distinction separates circuits I and II from circuit III (C'...C'). In circuits I and II, "the capital value advanced—whether as money, or in the shape of the material elements of production—forms the starting-point and hence also the point of return." This is the decisive structural criterion for turnover: the cycle is anchored in an advanced capital value that must be recovered. Circuit III begins differently—not with an advance but with an already-valorized result. This asymmetry between advance and prior result is the hinge on which the whole chapter—and indeed the choice of analytical frame—turns.

7.4 The Disqualification of C'...C' from Turnover Analysis

The chapter now subjects circuit III to close scrutiny in order to exclude it from turnover analysis. In M...M', "the return is M' = M + m." Here m is the surplus-value—the increment generated over and above the advanced M. The text notes that "if the process is repeated on the same scale, then M again forms the starting-point; m does not enter into it, but simply shows us that although M has been valorized as capital and thus created a surplus-value, it has cast this surplus-value off." This is a statement about simple reproduction: when the capitalist consumes the surplus rather than reinvesting it, the next advance is again M, and the surplus-value m is expelled from the circuit's starting point. The money cycle thus visibly spits out its surplus—a transparency that makes circuit I analytically convenient.

In P...P, "the capital value P advanced in the form of the elements of production forms the point of departure." This form includes valorization. In simple reproduction, "it is the same capital value that begins its process again in the same form." In accumulation, however, something changes: "P' (possessing a value of M' or C') now starts the process as an increased capital value. But the process still begins with capital value advanced in the original form, even if with a greater value than previously." The crucial phrase is in the original form. Even under accumulation, where the cycle restarts at a higher magnitude, the formal starting point remains the advance of capital value. The form of the beginning is invariant even as its size grows.

Circuit III is qualitatively different: "the capital value does not begin the process as capital value advanced, but as capital value already valorized, as the total wealth existing in the form of commodities, of which the capital value advanced forms only a part." This is the decisive defect. C' (the prime again signaling valorization) represents everything the production cycle threw off—not merely the original capital but the surplus and, indeed, the totality of commodity wealth. Its starting point is a result, not an advance. The text acknowledges that this very feature makes circuit III "important for Part Three of the present volume, where the movement of individual capitals will be dealt with in its relationship with the movement of the total social capital." That is, when analyzing society-wide reproduction—where the question is how all commodity products flow among all capitals—the C' starting point is the natural one, since society's starting datum is indeed the mass of finished commodities. But "it cannot be used for the turnover of capital, which always begins with the advance of capital value... and always requires the return of the circling capital value in the form in which it was advanced." The same-form return criterion is violated by C'...C', because C' does not return in the form it "started" (it started as a result) and because the requirement of recovering the advanced value cannot be traced when the advance is invisible. Thus circuit III is set aside—not because it is false, but because it answers a different question.

7.5 The Methodological Choice Between Circuit I and Circuit II

The chapter's resolution is to use both surviving circuits, each for a distinct analytical purpose. "Out of circuits I and II, the former will be adhered to in so far as the influence of the turnover on the formation of surplus-value is the main thing under consideration; the latter in so far as its influence on the formation of the product is concerned." This is a deliberate division of labour. Circuit I (M...M') foregrounds money and surplus-value, so it is the ideal instrument for studying valorization dynamics—how fast surplus-value is generated, realized, and reinvested. Circuit II (P...P) foregrounds the elements of production and their product, so it is the instrument for studying physical product-formation—how the turnover of machinery, materials, and labour shapes the flow of output. Rather than forcing one frame onto all problems, the author keeps two frames in reserve, matching each to the question it best illuminates. This dual-track method is a hallmark of the whole volume.

7.6 The Economists' Confusion and Its Documentary Evidence

Having settled the correct analytical terrain, the chapter turns polemically against earlier economists: "Just as the economists have rarely distinguished between the different forms of the circuit, so too they have rarely considered these separately in connection with the turnover of capital." The charge is a failure of formal discrimination—a tendency to collapse the distinct circuits into one another and thereby obscure the specific determinants of turnover.

Two examples are marshalled. The dominant error is fixation on M...M': economists "have generally concentrated on the form M...M' because it is this that dominates the individual capitalist and is used by him in his calculations, even if money forms the starting-point only in the shape of money of account." Here the earlier observation about the ledger returns with critical force: because the individual capitalist thinks in money terms—because his books are kept in money of account—his practical standpoint systematically biases him (and the economists who adopt his standpoint) toward the money circuit, even when actual money transcends into commodities that are merely priced. The accountants' viewpoint is thus revealed as a partial one, valid for the capitalist's calculations but not exhaustive of capital's forms.

A second, opposite error is then exposed. "Certain others proceed from outlays in the form of elements of production, finishing with the receipt of returns, without even mentioning the form of these returns, whether they are in commodities or money." This is the error of starting from P and ending vaguely—recognizing the productive outlay but leaving the formal character of the return unspecified. To illustrate, the chapter quotes S. P. Newman, Elements of Political Economy (Andover and New York, p. 81): "The Economic Cycle... [is] the whole course of production, from the time that outlays are made till returns are received. In agriculture, seed-time is its commencement, and harvesting its ending." Newman's formulation captures the temporal span but remains formally indeterminate about what kind of thing the "returns" are—a conceptual looseness the chapter implicitly faults. The agricultural metaphor (seed-time to harvest) is telling, however, because it anticipates the chapter's own use of annual harvests as the natural temporal anchor for turnover.

The third and most confused tendency is to start from C' (circuit III). The text cites Thomas Chalmers, On Political Economy (2nd edn, Glasgow, 1832, p. 85), who describes the "economic cycle" as beginning from the point "at which the capitalist has obtained those returns by which his capital is replaced to him," and then tracing the workmen, wages, finished articles, the market, and finally a sale that "terminates the orbit of one set of movements." Chalmers's cycle explicitly starts from returns already obtained, i.e., from a valorized result—precisely the C' standpoint the chapter has disqualified for turnover purposes. A footnote amplifies the critique with heavy irony: Chalmers (1780–1847) "is described by Marx in Theories of Surplus-Value (Part I, p. 290) as 'one of the most fanatical Malthusians.' Like Malthus, he was himself a cleric, and in fact Professor of Divinity at Glasgow University." The polemical sting is that this clerical Malthusianism produces a formal muddle—a cycle that begins at the end—mirroring a broader theoretical carelessness.

7.7 The Circuit as Periodic Process: Turnover Formally Defined

With the critical ground cleared, the chapter delivers its central definition. "When the entire capital value that the individual capitalist invests in one branch of production or other has described its cyclical movement, it exists once again in its original form and can then repeat the same process. It has to repeat it, if the value is to be perpetuated and valorized as capital value." Two modalities of necessity appear: the capitalist can repeat (formal possibility established by closure) and has to repeat (the imperative of self-valorization). The deontic "has to" is important—capital that stops turning ceases to be capital; its value must be continuously valorized or it degrades into hoard.

Then the decisive conceptual leap: "In the life of the capital, the individual circuit forms only a section that is constantly repeated, i.e. a period." The circuit becomes a period—a unit in a recurring series. The text traces this for each surviving circuit: "At the close of the period M...M', the capital exists again in the form of money capital and passes once more through the series of changes of form that constitute its process of reproduction and valorization." And "At the close of the period P...P, the capital exists again in the form of the elements of production which constitute the premise of its repeated circuit." From here, the definition crystallizes: "The circuit of capital, when this is taken not as an isolated act but as a periodic process, is called its turnover. The duration of this turnover is given by the sum of its production time and its circulation time. This period of time forms the capital's turnover time. It thus measures the interval between one cyclical period of the total capital value and the next; the periodicity in the capital's life-process, or, if you like, the time required for the renewal and repetition of the valorization and production process of the same capital value."

Three things are packed into this passage. First, turnover is defined as the circuit qua periodic process—the same event, differently regarded. Second, its duration is the sum of production and circulation time—tying the new category back to the definition with which the chapter opened. Third, turnover time measures interval (between one cycle and the next), periodicity (the rhythm of capital's "life"), and renewal time (how long before the same value must be re-advanced). The phrase "renewal and repetition of the valorization and production process" emphasizes that turnover involves both valorization and production—i.e., both the value-magnifying and the physically-producing faces of capital, echoing the earlier decision to employ circuits I and II respectively.

7.8 The Year as the Natural Measuring Unit

The chapter now confronts the problem of measurement: turnover time is a duration, but durations need units. The text approaches this by analogy: "As the working day forms the natural measuring unit for the function of labour-power, so the year forms the natural measuring unit for the turnovers of capital in process." The parallel is exact and instructive: labour-power, when functioning, is naturally reckoned in working days; capital, when turning over, is naturally reckoned in years. Both units are grounded in nature rather than convention.

The chapter supplies the material basis for this choice: "The natural basis for this measurement is that the most important food crops in the temperate zone, the native ground of capitalist production, are annual products." This is a materialist—indeed agro-biological—justification. Capitalist production arose in the temperate zone, and its agrarian base depended on crops whose full vegetative cycle occupies exactly one year. The annual rhythm of sowing and harvest thus imposed a natural one-year periodicity on the reproduction of the most fundamental means of subsistence, and by extension on the economic rhythm built atop it. The year is therefore not an arbitrary bookkeeping convenience but is derived from the physical conditions of production itself. (Note the resonance with Newman's seed-time-to-harvest imagery from section 7.6: the quotation was not accidental.)

7.9 The Turnover Formula: n = U/u and Its Worked Examples

With the unit fixed, the chapter formalizes the measure. "If we call the year, as measurement unit of the turnover time, U, the turnover time of a particular capital u, and the number of its turnovers n, then n = U/u." The variables are now fully specified:

  • U = the standard measuring unit (normally the year, treated as 12 months).
  • u = the turnover time of the particular capital in question (expressed in the same units as U).
  • n = the number of turnovers the capital completes within the measuring unit.

The formula is a ratio of available time to cycle length, yielding the count of completed cycles. The text immediately supplies worked examples to fix the intuition.

Example one (sub-annual turnover): "If the turnover time u is three months, for example, then n = 12/3 = 4; the capital completes four turnovers in a year, or turns over four times." A capital whose production-and-circulation cycle lasts only a quarter completes four full valorization episodes per year—rapid rotation.

Example two (super-annual turnover): "If u = 18 months, then n = 12/18 = 2/3; the capital only gets through two thirds of its turnover time in one year." Here the cycle is longer than the measuring unit, so the resulting count is a fraction—less than one full turnover per year. The number of turnovers need not be a whole number; it can express a partial completion, capturing the fact that capital in slow sectors (e.g., forestry, heavy construction) may take more than a year to go round once.

Example three (multi-year turnover): "If the turnover time amounts to several years, then it is reckoned in terms of multiples of a year." When u spans several years, turnover is expressed as a multiple—e.g., a five-year cycle yields n = 1/5 turnover per year. So the formula scales gracefully across extremely fast, ordinary, and glacially slow capitals, always reducing them to a common yardstick of annual turnover frequency.

A methodological caveat precedes the examples: "If we disregard the individual occurrences that may accelerate or shorten the turnover time of an individual capital, the turnover times of capitals differ according to their different spheres of investment." This is essential. Individual accidents—a lucky quick sale, a transport delay—can perturb any single capital's turnover. Abstracting from these idiosyncrasies, the systematic determinant of turnover time is the sphere of investment: different branches of production (bakeries vs. shipyards vs. vineyards) have structurally different production and circulation times, hence structurally different turnover rates. This is the seed of the entire subsequent analysis, in which turnover differences across branches will drive differences in the annual rate of surplus-value.

7.10 The Capitalist's Practical Viewpoint and the Chapter's Transition

The chapter closes by translating the abstract category into the capitalist's lived experience: "For the capitalist, the turnover time of his capital is the time for which he has to advance his capital in order for this to be valorized and for him to receive it back in its original shape." This restatement emphasizes advance duration—the length of the interval during which capital is tied up, unavailable, "in the hands of" the process. For the practical capitalist, turnover time is fundamentally a matter of how long his money is locked away before returning amplified. This subjective-practical register complements the objective-formal register of the definition and prepares the reader to think about turnover in terms of tie-up and release of capital.

Finally, the chapter announces its own incompleteness. "Before we investigate more closely the influence of turnover on the production and valorization process, we have to consider two new forms which capital obtains as a result of the circulation process, and which affect the form of its turnover." This is the hinge into the following chapter. The two "new forms" arise from the circulation process itself—they are not mere repetitions of the M, P, C distinctions already established, but genuinely new determinations of how capital is parcelled and cycled. (They will turn out to be the distinction between fixed capital and circulating capital, whose differing turnover forms—fixed capital turning over only piecemeal through wear-and-tear, circulating capital turning over wholly in each cycle—reshape everything the formula n = U/u can mean in practice.) The sentence is a deliberate cliffhanger: the arithmetic machinery of turnover has been constructed, but before it can be applied to valorization, the internal composition of capital—how it divides into slow-turning and fast-turning portions—must be analyzed. Thus the chapter hands off precisely at the point where its statistical abstraction must give way to material differentiation.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter yields several fundamental principles. First, form determines what can be measured. Before turnover can be quantified, the analyst must choose the correct circuit (M...M' or P...P, not C'...C'), because only circuits anchored in an advance and closed by a same-form return can be treated as repeatable periods. This is a general epistemological lesson: the categories you deploy constrain the phenomena you can see. Circuit III is not false—it is essential for social-capital analysis—but it is incommensurate with turnover analysis. Marx thus models a disciplined pluralism of abstractions, matching frame to question rather than imposing one schema universally.

Second, capital is defined by motion, not by substance. The chapter's insistence that capital "has to repeat" its circuit shows that capital is not a stock of things but a process that must continuously valorize or perish. Turnover time, the periodicity of that process, is therefore not an incidental property but an essential dimension of capital's existence: a capital is partly characterized by how fast it turns. Speed enters the definition of the thing itself.

Third, the natural world underwrites economic categories. The choice of the year as measuring unit is grounded in the annual crop cycle of the temperate zone—the "native ground of capitalist production." This is a striking instance of historical materialism's method: economic measurement conventions are traced back to material-biological facts. The abstract ratio n = U/u rests, at bottom, on seeds and seasons.

Fourth, abstraction is a tool of purification, not evasion. By "disregarding individual occurrences," the chapter isolates the sphere of investment as the systematic determinant of turnover. This sets up the entire subsequent argument: because different branches have different structural turnover rates, and because surplus-value is generated in production but realized in circulation, the speed of turnover will govern how much surplus-value a given capital extracts per year. The "annual rate of surplus-value"—a category Marx will develop at length in later chapters—is the direct payoff of the formula constructed here. A capital that turns over four times a year can realize its surplus four times over, in principle, within the same calendar span; a capital locked into an 18-month cycle realizes only two-thirds of one turnover. Turnover speed thus becomes a material lever on profitability, independent of the rate of exploitation itself—a vital point for understanding why capitalists constantly strive to accelerate circulation (through credit, transport, marketing) and why crises can be triggered by turnovers unexpectedly slowing.

Fifth, and more subtly, the chapter exposes a standpoint bias built into accounting itself. Because the individual capitalist keeps books in money of account, he perceives capital through the money circuit—and economists, adopting his practical consciousness, have reproduced his blind spot. This is a methodological warning of enduring value: the categories most natural to economic actors may be the least adequate for theory. Money of account, an evidently "real" datum in the ledger, is shown to be an ideal representation of value, capable of masking the productive and commodity forms on which it depends.

Finally, the chapter's deliberate incompleteness is itself instructive. By ending on the announcement of "two new forms," it demonstrates that analytical progress is staged: one cannot simply apply turnover arithmetic to real capitals until one has grasped fixed and circulating capital. The implication is that the rate n = U/u is only a scaffold; the true richness of turnover theory lies in the differentiated turnover of capital's parts—machines versus materials—and their divergent rhythms. In sum, Chapter 7 transforms the circuit from a linear narrative of metamorphoses into a tempo, infusing capital with a temporal life, and thereby converts economic analysis from a static anatomy of value into a dynamics of speed—speed that will prove decisive for the rate of surplus-value, the rhythm of accumulation, and the stability of the entire capitalist system.
