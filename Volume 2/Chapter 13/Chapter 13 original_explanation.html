Volume 2 • Chapter 13: The Production Time

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS:

Chapter 13 of Capital Volume 2 opens with a deceptively simple proposition that organizes the whole of Part II's analysis of turnover: "Working time is always production time… But it is not true, conversely, that the entire time for which capital exists in the production process is necessarily therefore working time." This sentence contains the chapter's entire thesis. The chapter's intellectual objective is to dismantle the tacit assumption — carried over from the analysis of the working period in the preceding chapter — that the duration of capital's confinement to the production sphere is equivalent to the duration of the labour actually applied to the product. Marx demonstrates that these two magnitudes can diverge, often enormously, and that this divergence is not a peripheral curiosity but a systemic determinant of turnover time, of the volume of capital that must be advanced, of the valuation and durability of fixed capital, of the price of the product, and even of the social form of whole branches of production (agriculture, forestry, cattle-raising).

The central problem is thus: what happens to capital during the interval when it is, by the nature of the product, still in the production process but no longer in the labour process? Marx's answer is that capital is held hostage by natural processes — physical, chemical, or physiological — that require time independent of human labour (fermentation, drying, bleaching, ripening, seasoning, the growth of timber). During these intervals the labour process is "either completely or partially suspended," yet the capital cannot leave the production sphere because the product is unfinished and thus not yet convertible into commodity capital. This means the production period is longer than the working period, and this excess directly extends the turnover period.

The chapter's placement within the broader theoretical architecture of Volume 2 is crucial. Part II ("The Turnover of Capital") is building a complete theory of how capital circulates and returns to its starting point. Chapter 12 established the working period as the first determinant of turnover. Chapter 13 introduces the production period as the second, more complex determinant, showing that the working period is merely a segment of the production period. This distinction will prove indispensable for everything that follows in Part II: the calculation of turnover, the annual rate of surplus-value, the distinction between fixed and circulating capital, and the analysis of the "formation of stock." Furthermore, the chapter plants a flag for Volume 3, announcing that MacCulloch's and James Mill's "nonsensical theories" — which tried to identify production time with working time — will be dismantled there, since those errors spring from "an incorrect application of the theory of value." So the chapter is not merely descriptive: it is a polemical staging ground, defending the labour theory of value against the attempt to treat nature's time as if it were labour time.

Finally, the chapter carries a deeply social argument that runs beneath its technical surface. The natural divergence of production time and working time becomes, under capitalist production, the material basis for the subordination of rural labour to merchant capital, the separation of manufacture from agriculture, and the immiseration of the rural worker. Marx's chilling line — "As far as capital is concerned… all these differences in the turnover balance out. Not so for the worker" — signals that this is a chapter about political economy, not merely about scheduling.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS:

2.1 The Founding Distinction and the Nature of the Interruption

The chapter begins by drawing the line that will govern everything else. Working time is time during which capital is actively engaged in the labour process — labour is being applied to the object of labour. Production time is the total time during which capital is confined to the production sphere, whether or not labour is being applied. The first is always a subset of the second; the second is not always coextensive with the first.

Marx is immediately careful to demarcate the kind of interruption he means. He explicitly excludes interruptions "conditioned by the natural limits of labour-power itself" — that is, the simple fatigue of workers, the need for sleep, the daily and weekly pauses of the labour process. Those interruptions were already discussed in Volume 1, Chapter 10 (§4), where Marx noted that the fact that fixed capital — factory buildings, machinery — "lies idle during the pauses in the labour process became one of the motives for the unnatural extension of the labour process, and for working day and night." So the idleness of capital during human pauses is an old theme, and it has a specific consequence: it drives capital to intensify the working day.

What is at issue here is a different species of interruption: one "independent of the length of the labour process," "conditioned by the nature of the product and its production." During this interruption the object of labour is subjected to natural processes of shorter or longer duration and undergoes "physical, chemical or physiological changes" while the labour process is "either completely or partially suspended." The critical conceptual move is that this interruption is not a failure of labour supply, nor a market event, nor a technical accident. It is immanent to the material nature of the product and its production. This makes it a production-sphere phenomenon in the strictest sense, rooted in physics, chemistry, or biology rather than in exchange. This grounding is what will later allow Marx to classify certain elements of turnover as arising from the production sphere rather than the circulation sphere — a distinction he treats as analytically decisive.

2.2 Natural Processes as Suspensions of the Labour Process: The Illustrative Cascade

Marx builds the concept through a rapid cascade of examples, each chosen to display a different aspect of the phenomenon.

Wine. "After grapes have been pressed… the wine must go through a period of fermentation, and then also rest for a while before it reaches a certain degree of readiness." Here capital is in the production process (the wine exists as an unfinished product) but no labour is being applied — the chemical transformation happens on its own. Marx will later return to wine as the pure case of capital remaining in production time after the labour process has been performed.

Drying and chemical transformation. "In many branches of industry the product must undergo a process of drying, as in pottery, or else be exposed to certain conditions in order to change its chemical properties, as with bleaching." Drying is a physical process; bleaching is a chemical one. Both require elapsed time, not labour.

Winter corn. "Winter corn needs nine months or so to ripen. Between seed-time and harvest, the labour process is almost completely interrupted." Here we see the extreme case: the labour process is not merely paused but almost completely suspended for the better part of a year. This is the case that will dominate the rest of the chapter, because agriculture combines a long working period with a massive gap between working time and production time.

Timber. "In the raising of timber, once planting and the preliminary work connected with this is completed, the seed may need 100 years to be transformed into a finished product; during this whole time, only a relatively very insignificant intervention of labour is needed." This is the limiting case: production time measured in centuries, working time almost negligible. The point of putting timber last in the cascade is rhetorical — Marx is stretching the reader's sense of how far the divergence can go before he formalizes it.

The unifying observation is stated immediately afterward: "In all these cases, additional labour is added only occasionally for a large part of the production time." Marx contrasts this with the situation of the preceding chapter, where "additional capital and labour has to be added to capital already tied up in the production process." In the previous chapter (on the working period), the continuation of production required continuous injections of labour and capital. Here, by contrast, those injections occur "only with interruptions of a greater or lesser extent." This contrast is the bridge between the two chapters: the working period is a period of continuous outlay; the production period may be a period of intermittent outlay even though capital remains tied up throughout.

2.3 The Two-Period Structure of Production Time and the Extension of Turnover

Marx now formalizes the structure. The production time of the capital advanced consists of two periods:

  1. A period in which the capital exists in the labour process (working time).
  2. A period in which its form of existence — that of an unfinished product — is "handed over to the sway of natural processes, without being involved in the labour process."

He notes that this structure "is not altered if the two periods of time occasionally cut across one another or are interspersed." This is an important analytical caveat: in some processes the two periods are interleaved (labour applied, then nature works, then labour applied again, then nature works again) rather than strictly sequential. But whether the periods are sequential or interleaved, the aggregate relation holds.

The core quantitative relation can be written as:

Production period (P) = Working period (W) + Natural-process period (N), where N ≥ 0.

When N > 0, P > W, and "here the working period and the production period do not coincide." The consequences flow directly:

  • "It is only after the production period has been left behind that the product is finished and mature, and can thus be transformed from the form of productive capital into that of commodity capital." So the value realization waits on the completion of P, not merely on the completion of W.
  • "The turnover period is then extended according to the length of that part of the production time that does not consist of working time." In other words, the excess N is added to the turnover period.

This yields the chapter's central formula in words: Turnover is extended by N = P − W. The whole subsequent analysis is an elaboration of the consequences of this extension for the volume, timing, and valuation of capital advances.

2.4 Artificial Shortening of Production Time: Tanning, Bleaching, Iron

Marx immediately introduces a critical qualification. The natural-process period is not always a fixed, unalterable datum. "In so far as this time of production over and above the labour time is not determined by natural laws given once and for all, as with the ripening of corn, the growth of an oak, etc., the turnover period can often be shortened to a greater or lesser extent by the artificial shortening of the production time."

This introduces a crucial distinction between naturally fixed production times (biological rhythms of ripening and growth) and technically reducible ones (chemical and physical processes subject to industrial intervention). Marx gives a series of escalating examples:

Bleaching. "The introduction of chemical in place of open-air bleaching." The open-air method required the product to be exposed to sunlight and air for long periods; chemical bleaching compresses the process dramatically.

Drying. "More effective drying apparatus in the drying processes." The introduction of machinery to accelerate evaporation.

Tanning. "In tanning, the penetration of tannic acid into the skins, which used to take between six and eighteen months with the old method, only takes one and a half to two months with the new method involving the use of the air-pump." Marx cites J. D. Courcelle-Seneuil's Traité théorique et pratique des entreprises industrielles (Paris, 1857, 2nd edn, p. 49). Note the magnitude of compression: from 6–18 months down to 1.5–2 months, i.e., a reduction of roughly 70–90% of the natural-process period.

Iron production. "The most far-reaching example of artificial shortening of a production time made up exclusively of natural processes is given by the history of iron production over the last 100 years, from the invention of puddling in 1780 to the modern Bessemer process and the latest procedures introduced since then." Marx's verdict: "The production time has been enormously curtailed, but the application of fixed capital has also increased to the same extent."

This last sentence carries a theoretical payload. It establishes a substitution relation: shortening natural-process time requires massive fixed capital investment. The turnover gain is purchased with a change in the organic and technical composition of capital. This connection anticipates the discussion of fixed capital later in the chapter and is one of the earliest hints in this Part of the way technical change reshapes turnover.

2.5 The Shoe-Last Example: Intra-Capital Divergence of Turnover

Marx now introduces what he calls "a peculiar example of the divergence between production time and working time": "the American manufacture of shoe-lasts."

Here "a significant part of the expense arises from the wood having to dry out in store for up to eighteen months, so that the finished lasts do not warp. During this time, the wood does not undergo any other labour process." The consequence: "The turnover period of the capital applied is therefore not only determined by the time required to produce the lasts themselves, but also by the time for which the capital has to lie idle in the shape of wood which is being dried out. The capital exists in the production process for eighteen months before it can enter the labour process proper."

This example introduces two new analytical points:

  1. Capital can be in production time before the labour process proper. This is the mirror image of the wine case (where capital is in production time after the labour process). In shoe-last manufacture, the drying-out happens prior to shaping the wood into lasts. So the natural-process period can precede, follow, or interleave with the labour process.

  2. Different parts of the total circulating capital can have different turnover times, determined by the production sphere. This is the "peculiar" element. Marx states explicitly: "This example also shows how the turnover times of various parts of the total circulating capital may differ as a result of circumstances that arise from the production sphere and not from the circulation sphere." This is a crucial methodological point: not all turnover differentiation is to be explained by market and circulation phenomena. Some of it is materially rooted in the nature of production itself.

2.6 Agriculture as the Paradigmatic Case

"The distinction between production time and working time is particularly important in agriculture." Marx now pivots to the branch where the divergence is largest and most consequential.

He lays out the agricultural case in temperate climates: "the land brings forth grain once a year. The shortening or lengthening of the production period (an average of nine months for winter sowing) is itself dependent on the alternation of good and bad years, and hence cannot be precisely determined in advance and controlled, as in industry proper."

Two features distinguish agriculture from industry proper:

  • Annual biological rhythm. Grain is harvested once per year; the production period is ~9 months for winter sowing.
  • Ineliminable uncertainty. Because the production period depends on weather, it cannot be "precisely determined in advance and controlled." In industry proper, by contrast, production time is largely technically controllable. Only "subsidiary products such as milk, cheese, etc. can be produced and sold continuously in short periods."

Marx then documents the working period in agriculture with a quantitative extract from F. Kirchhof's Handbuch der landwirtschaftlichen Betriebslehre (Dessau, 1852, p. 160). The number of working days across the three main working periods in different German districts is:

  • Spring (mid-March/early April to mid-May): 50–60 days
  • Summer (early June to late August): 65–80 days
  • Autumn (early September to end of October/mid-to-late November): 55–75 days
  • Winter: only "work suited to that period, such as haulage of fertilizer, wood, goods for market, building materials, etc."

Summing the three main periods, working time amounts to roughly 170–215 days per year, leaving a substantial winter period in which agricultural labour is largely absent. The gap between the production period (~365 days for the annual crop cycle) and the working period (~170–215 days) is thus enormous. This is the empirical substance of the theoretical divergence.

2.7 The Russian Case and the Genesis of Rural Subsidiary Industry

The more extreme the climate, the more compressed the working period and the wider the gap between working time and production time. Marx quotes the Reports by H.M. Secretaries of Embassy and Legation (no. 8, 1865, pp. 86–87):

"In some of the northern districts, field labour is only possible during from 130 to 150 days in the course of the year, and it may be imagined what a loss Russia would sustain, if out of 65,000,000 of her European population, 50,000,000 remained unoccupied during six or eight months of winter, when all agricultural labour is at a standstill."

So in northern Russia the working period shrinks to 130–150 days, leaving 6–8 months of enforced idleness for a peasant population of 50 million out of 65 million. Marx's concern here is not merely technical. This material condition — the seasonal idleness of a vast rural population — is the natural basis for the rise of rural subsidiary industries:

"There are villages, for instance, in Russia in which all the peasants have been for generations either weavers, tanners, shoemakers, locksmiths, cutlers, etc.," particularly in the gubernias of Moscow, Vladimir, Kaluga, Kostroma and St Petersburg. "These cottage industries, incidentally, are already being pressed more and more into the service of capitalist production; for example, merchants supply the weavers with warps and weft, either directly, or by intermediate agents."

This is one of the chapter's most theoretically loaded passages. Marx derives a social consequence from a natural fact:

"We see here how the distinction between production period and working period, with the latter forming only a part of the former, constitutes the natural basis for the unification of agriculture with rural subsidiary industries, just as these, in turn, are points of vantage for the capitalist, who first intrudes in his capacity as merchant."

The causal chain is: natural divergence → seasonal idleness → subsidiary industries → merchant capital enters → rural labour subordinated. And then the historical sequel: "In so far as capitalist production later manages to complete the separation between manufacture and agriculture, the rural worker becomes ever more dependent on merely accidental subsidiary employments and his condition thereby worsens."

Finally, the devastating symmetry that closes this movement:

"As far as capital is concerned, as we shall see later on, all these differences in the turnover balance out. Not so for the worker."

This is the class-theoretic pivot of the chapter. The natural unevenness of agricultural production time is indifferent to capital in the long run (the turnover differences average out across the capitalist class and across the cycle), but it is existentially determining for the worker, who must endure the seasonal dependency it generates.

2.8 Unevenness of Circulating Capital Outlay and the Fixed Capital Problem

Marx now contrasts the smooth reproduction of industry proper with the lumpy reproduction of branches characterized by a production period longer than the working period.

"In most branches of industry proper, as well as in mining, transport, etc., production proceeds evenly and the same working time is worked year in year out; apart from fluctuations of price, disturbances of business, etc. and abnormal interruptions, the outlay of capital going into the daily circulation process is evenly distributed. While market conditions remain the same, therefore, the reflux or renewal of circulating capital is distributed over the whole year in equal portions."

By contrast:

"However, in those investments of capital where working time forms only one part of the production time, there is great unevenness in the outlay of circulating capital in the course of the different periods of the year, in as much as the reflux only follows, at one stroke, at a time prescribed by natural conditions."

This produces a specific quantitative consequence for capital advances. "On a given scale of business, therefore, i.e. with the same volume of circulating capital advanced, this must be advanced in larger amounts at once, and for a longer time, than in those businesses with continuous working periods."

More formally: if the reflux is concentrated at a single point (harvest) rather than spread evenly across the year, then the same annual volume of circulating capital requires a larger front-loaded advance and a longer period of advance before reflux. The capital is tied up longer with no compensating gradual return. This is the material root of the agricultural credit problem, and it is the same structural fact that makes the agricultural producer chronically dependent on those who can extend credit — precisely the mechanism Hodgskin will describe.

Marx then notes that "the life of fixed capital is significantly different here from the time in which it actually functions productively." Because the fixed capital (draught cattle, implements, machines) is idle for long stretches, its chronological lifespan and its productive-functioning time diverge. This introduces the next movement of the chapter.

2.9 Fixed Capital Idle Time, Depreciation, and the Dearness of the Product

"With this difference between working time and production time, the time during which the fixed capital is utilized is of course constantly interrupted for a longer or shorter interval; in agriculture for instance, with the use of draught cattle, implements and machines."

Marx distinguishes two cases of idle fixed capital:

  1. Draught animals (a "living" fixed capital). "In so far as this fixed capital consists of draught animals, it continues to require the same or almost the same outlay on fodder, etc. as during the time in which it operates." So the maintenance cost is not suspended even though the productive use is. Feed is consumed during idleness — pure cost.

  2. Dead means of labour. "In the case of dead means of labour, non-use also gives rise to a certain depreciation." Machinery rusts, buildings decay, materials degrade. Here the loss is not an outlay but a value loss.

The consequence for price: "The product thus always becomes dearer, since the transfer of value to the product is not calculated according to the time for which the fixed capital functions, but rather according to the time in which it loses value."

This is a subtle and important point about value transfer. The value that fixed capital transfers to the product is proportional to the time over which it depreciates (its lifetime), not the time over which it functions (its operational time). If a machine functions only half the year but depreciates all year, then its annual depreciation cannot be charged solely against the goods produced during the functioning half; the depreciation cost is spread over fewer units of output, raising the unit cost. Equivalently: the value loss is continuous while the use is intermittent, so the value transferred per unit is inflated.

Marx then makes a striking analytical generalization: "In these branches of production it is a condition of normal use that fixed capital should lie idle, whether or not this still involves running costs, just as in spinning a condition of normal use is the loss of a certain quantity of cotton; and in the same way, in every labour process, the labour-power expended unproductively, but unavoidably so under normal technical conditions, counts just as much as the productive."

This is a crucial conceptual move. Idle fixed capital in agriculture is not an anomaly to be eliminated — it is a normal technical condition of production, exactly analogous to the inevitable waste of cotton in spinning or the inevitably unproductive expenditure of labour-power under normal technical conditions. The idleness is a cost of production like any other. And so the general rule follows: "Every improvement that reduces the unproductive expenditure of means of labour, raw materials and labour-power also reduces the value of the product." So the earlier iron-production example is not isolated: technical improvements in any of these dimensions lower unit values, whether by shortening N or by reducing idle-time costs.

2.10 Hodgskin on Agricultural Dependence

Marx now turns to a passage from Thomas Hodgskin's Popular Political Economy (London, 1827, p. 147, note) that captures the social consequence of agricultural production time:

"The difference of time (although he does not differentiate here between working time and production time) required to complete the products of agriculture, and of other species of labour" is "the main cause of the great dependence of the agriculturists. They cannot bring their commodities to market in less time than a year. For that whole period they are obliged to borrow of the shoemaker, the tailor, the smith, the wheelwright, and the various other labourers, whose products they cannot dispense with, but which are completed in a few days or weeks. Owing to this natural circumstance, and owing to the more rapid increase of the wealth produced by other labour than that of agriculture, the monopolizers of all the land, though they have also monopolized legislation, have not been able to save themselves and their servants, the farmers, from becoming the most dependent class of men in the community."

Marx's editorial note is important: Hodgskin "does not differentiate here between working time and production time." Hodgskin has seen the difference of time as the cause of dependence, but he conflates the two categories that Marx has just distinguished. This is not a mere pedantic correction: it shows that the social effect (dependence) can be perceived even when the analytical distinction (production vs. working time) has not yet been drawn. Marx's contribution is to reveal the precise mechanism — the extension of turnover by N — that generates the dependence Hodgskin observed.

The substance of Hodgskin's argument is a self-sufficiency deficit: the agricultural producer cannot bring output to market for at least a year, but needs the products of shoemakers, tailors, smiths, and wheelwrights, which are completed in days or weeks. The temporal asymmetry forces the agriculturist into debt — hence dependence. And even the monopolizers of land and legislation cannot escape the structural dependence this creates for "themselves and their servants, the farmers."

2.11 Intensification: Crop Rotation, Undersowing, and Increased Circulating Capital

Marx now notes a dialectical counter-tendency. "All methods in agriculture which on the one hand distribute expenditure on wages and means of labour more evenly over the whole year, and on the other hand shorten the turnover, by diversifying the products and thus making different crops possible during the year, require an increase in the circulating capital laid out on production, on wages, fertilizer, seed, etc."

So there is a trade-off: evening out and shortening turnover requires more circulating capital. Marx gives two historical illustrations:

  1. Transition from the three-field system (with fallow) to crop rotation without fallow. Abolishing the fallow year means land is productive every year, shortening the rotation cycle — but it requires more intensive inputs.

  2. The cultures dérobées (undersowing system) in Flanders. "The root crops are planted by undersowing; the same field first bears corn, flax or rape-seed for human requirements, and then after the harvest root crops are sown for the maintenance of cattle. This system, which enables the horned cattle to remain permanently in the stall, produces a considerable amount of manure and is thus the cornerstone of crop rotation. More than a third of the cultivated area in the sandy districts is undersown in this way; it is just as if the cultivated land had been extended by a third."

The undersowing system's logic is an elegant intensification: by planting a second (fodder) crop beneath the main crop, the same land produces twice within a single year, feeding cattle in-stall year-round, generating manure, and thereby closing the nutrient cycle. The quantified result: more than a third of the cultivated area is undersown, effectively extending the arable surface "by a third."

The capital consequence is stated via Émile de Laveleye's Essais sur l'économie rurale de la Belgique (Brussels, 1863, pp. 59–60, 63): "Agriculture, being thus carried to the point at which it is transformed into horticulture, understandably requires a relatively considerable capital investment. In England the sum reckoned with is 250 francs of investment capital to the hectare. In Flanders our farmers will probably find an investment capital of 500 francs per hectare far too low." So the intensification of production time management doubles (at least) the capital requirement per hectare — from 250 francs in England to over 500 francs in Flanders.

2.12 Timber-Raising: Production Time Beyond a Human Life

Marx now returns to timber-raising, quoting Kirchhof (p. 58) at length:

"The production of timber is fundamentally different from most others in that here natural forces work independently, and the power of men or capital is not required for natural growth. Even where forests are artificially cultivated, the amount of human and capital power expended in comparison with the action of natural forces is only slight. Furthermore, forests will thrive in types of soil and places where grain cannot grow, or where it no longer pays to produce it. Forest culture, however, requires a greater surface area than the cultivation of grain, if it is to be conducted on a regular commercial basis. Since small plots do not allow proper forestry methods, the secondary uses are abandoned, and forest protection is made more difficult, etc. The production process is also tied to such a long period of time that it extends beyond the plans of a private undertaking, and sometimes even beyond a single human life. Capital invested in the acquisition of forest land (in communal production this capital disappears and the question is simply how much land the community can withdraw from arable and grazing land for timber production) only bears fruit after a comparatively long period of time, and turns over only partially, taking up to 150 years in the case of many types of wood. Moreover, effective timber production actually requires a reserve stock of growing timber amounting to between ten and forty times the annual yield. Thus someone who does not have other income or possess substantial areas of forest cannot pursue regular forestry."

Marx draws the theoretical conclusion: "The long production time (which includes a relatively slight amount of working time), and the consequent length of the turnover period, make forest culture a line of business unsuited to private and hence to capitalist production, the latter being fundamentally a private operation, even when the associated capitalist takes the place of the individual."

This is a major structural claim. Capitalist production has a horizon limit imposed by its private form. When production time exceeds a human lifespan (up to 150 years for many woods) or the planning horizon of a private undertaking, it falls outside the scope of capitalist organization. The parenthesis Marx inserts — "(in communal production this capital disappears and the question is simply how much land the community can withdraw from arable and grazing land for timber production)" — is telling: the transactions by which forest land is acquired are an artifact of private ownership, and under communal production the question becomes a planning question, not a capital question.

The reserve-stock ratio is one of the chapter's few quantitative laws: effective timber production requires growing stock amounting to 10–40× the annual yield, which means the turnover of the total capital takes 10 to 40 years and more. Marx emphasizes this by repeating the Kirchhof passage: "Thus the turnover takes from ten years up to forty and more."

And he adds a historical coda that underlines the destructiveness of the civilizational dynamic: "The development of civilization and industry in general has always shown itself so active in the destruction of forests that everything that has been done for their conservation and production is completely insignificant in comparison." So the branch that is least suited to capitalist production is also the branch that capitalist civilization most rapidly destroys — a contradiction worth pondering.

2.13 Cattle-Raising and the Category of Stock

Marx now turns to cattle-raising as an intermediate case. "Part of the herd (cattle stock) remains in the production process, while another part is sold as the annual product. Here only one part of the capital turns over each year, just as in the case of the fixed capital — machinery, draught cattle, etc."

Two points are made here:

  1. Partial turnover. Only the part sold each year turns over; the breeding herd remains as ongoing stock. So the annual turnover fraction is less than 1 in relation to the standing herd.

  2. But it is not fixed capital "in the categorical sense." Marx is explicit: "Even though this capital is fixed for a longer time in the production process, and thus lengthens the turnover of the total capital, it does not constitute fixed capital in the categorical sense." So cattle stock (and timber stock) is a distinct analytical category — permanently tied-up productive capital that is neither fixed capital in the strict sense nor ordinary circulating capital.

Marx then generalizes the category of stock: "What is referred to here as a stock — a definite quantity of growing wood or cattle — exists partially in the production process (both as means of labour and material of labour); depending on the natural conditions of its reproduction, a significant part must always exist in this form in the case of regular cultivation."

Note the pregnant phrase: the stock exists "both as means of labour and material of labour." The breeding herd is both the instrument of production (it produces calves) and the raw material (its offspring are the product). Likewise, growing timber is both the standing means of production and the maturing product. This dual character is what makes the category of stock irreducible to either fixed or circulating capital.

2.14 Productive Stock, Replacement Difficulty, and the Circulation Sphere

Marx then introduces a second kind of stock: "a stock that forms only potential productive capital, but has to be accumulated in larger or smaller amounts as a result of the nature of agriculture, and must be advanced to production for a relatively long time even though it enters the active production process only bit by bit. This includes manure, for example, before it is carted to the field, as well as corn, hay, etc. and any stocks of feed that go into the production of cattle."

This is the category of potential productive capital — means of production that have been advanced (purchased/withdrawn from the fund) but have not yet entered the active production process. They sit in reserve, and their reserve status is imposed by the discontinuous nature of agricultural production. Marx connects this to his earlier discussion (pp. 215–20) of the formation of stock, where he established that "a greater or lesser quantity of potential productive capital is required, i.e. a quantity of means of production destined for production, which has to be held in reserve in a greater or lesser amount in order to go into the production process bit by bit."

He then states the determinants of stock size: "with a capital investment of a given scale, the size of this production stock depends on the greater or lesser difficulty of its replacement, its relative proximity to the supplying markets, the development of means of transport and communication, etc. All these circumstances affect the minimum capital that must exist in the form of productive stock, and thus the period of time for which advances of capital have to be made, and the volume of capital that has to be advanced at once."

So the minimum productive stock is a function of replacement difficulty, market proximity, and transport/communication development. And this volume "also has an effect on the turnover" because it is "determined by the longer or shorter time for which circulating capital is tied up in the form of productive stock." Then comes a crucial analytic bifurcation:

"On the other hand, in so far as the extent of this stagnation depends on the greater or lesser possibility of rapid replacement, on market conditions, etc., it itself arises from the circulation time, from circumstances that pertain to the circulation sphere."

So Marx here carefully separates two sources of turnover determination: the production sphere (natural-process time, the nature of the product) and the circulation sphere (market proximity, transport, replacement ease). The shoe-last turnover divergence arose "from the production sphere"; the required volume of productive stock is partly determined "from the circulation sphere." This two-sphere classification is fundamental to the entire Part II architecture.

2.15 Kirchhof on Inventory and the Timing of Purchases

Marx adds another Kirchhof extract (p. 301) on the stock of implements and accessories:

"Moreover, a stock of all these implements or accessories, working tools, sieves, baskets, ropes, axle grease, nails, etc. is all the more necessary for replacement at any moment, the less opportunity there is of procuring them quickly in the vicinity. Finally, the entire inventory should be carefully inspected each winter, and the necessary additions and repairs immediately put in hand. Whether a larger or smaller stock of implements is generally needed is principally determined by local conditions. Where there are no craftsmen or shops in the vicinity, a greater stock must be kept than where these are to be found in the locality or very close by. If the requisite stocks are procured in greater quantities at once, under otherwise similar conditions, the advantage in cheap purchase is generally obtained, provided that a suitable point of time has been chosen; but of course a greater sum is then withdrawn at once from the current capital, which cannot always be dispensed with."

This passage reinforces the replacement-difficulty determinant of stock size and adds a further market consideration: bulk purchasing yields a price advantage but withdraws a larger sum at once from current capital, which may strain the business. So the optimal stock policy is a trade-off between price (bulk discount) and liquidity (current capital constraint). This is a concrete illustration of the abstract principle that the volume of productive stock is determined by both production-sphere and circulation-sphere conditions.

Two earlier Kirchhof passages (pp. 292, 300) had already laid out the management implications of stock: the need for painstaking attention to barns, fodder and grain lofts and cellars; storage places "kept well closed, and also kept clean, ventilated, etc."; grain turned over periodically; potatoes and beets protected against "frost, water and rot." And on feed: "In calculating one's own requirements, particularly for cattle… attention must be paid not only to covering requirements, but also to having a sufficient stock left over for unforeseen contingencies. As soon as it appears that the need cannot be fully met by one's own production, it is necessary to take into consideration whether this lack cannot be covered by other products (substitutes), or whether these cannot be procured more cheaply in place of the missing products. If there should be a lack of hay, for example, this can be made up by root crops with added straw. In general, the material value and market price of the different products must be constantly borne in mind, and consumption regulated accordingly; if oats are dearer, for instance, while peas and rye are relatively cheap, then it will be advantageous to replace some of the oats for the horses with peas and rye, and sell the superfluous oats."

These passages are not mere practical advice. They illustrate the rational calculation of stock under uncertainty — a necessary feature of production where the production period exceeds the working period, because the producer must cover a long non-productive interval from a stock accumulated during the productive interval, with substitution flexibility to absorb price shocks.

2.16 Recapitulation of the Range of Possibilities

Marx now provides a systematic recapitulation of the range of temporal patterns the divergence permits:

"The difference between production time and working time, as we have seen, permits a wide range of possibilities."

  1. "The circulating capital can be in its production time before it enters the actual labour process (manufacture of lasts)."
  2. "It may still be in production time after it has undergone the actual labour process (wine, seed-corn)."
  3. "The production time may be occasionally interrupted by labour time (field crops, timber)."
  4. "Or a large part of the product in a condition ready for circulation may remain incorporated in the active production process, while a much smaller part enters into the annual circulation (timber and cattle-growing)."
  5. "The greater or lesser length of time, thus the greater or lesser measure in which circulating capital has to be laid out all at once in the form of potential productive capital, partly arises from the kind of production process (agriculture) and partly depends on the proximity of markets, etc., in short, on circumstances that belong to the circulation sphere."

This recapitulation is the chapter's analytic synthesis: it maps the full topology of the divergence and reaffirms the two-source structure (production sphere vs. circulation sphere) of turnover determination.

2.17 The Mill/MacCulloch Polemic and the Theory of Value

Marx closes the substantive argument with a forward reference: "We shall see later on (in Volume 3) what nonsensical theories MacCulloch, James Mill, etc. were led to in their attempts to identify this production time diverging from working time with working time, attempts which in their turn arose from an incorrect application of the theory of value."

This is the chapter's most compressed but theoretically crucial passage. The target is the attempt — by MacCulloch and James Mill (the Utilitarian philosopher and Ricardo disciple whose Elements of Political Economy appeared in 1821) — to identify production time with working time, i.e., to claim that the interval during which capital is in the production process but not in the labour process is nonetheless "working time" in the value-theoretic sense. Marx calls this "nonsensical." Why? Because if production time is identified with working time, then nature's time would be credited with creating value — which contradicts the labour theory of value. The erroneous identification arises from "an incorrect application of the theory of value": because value is created only by labour, and because production time ≠ working time, those who wish to explain the valuation of goods whose production requires long natural intervals are tempted to pretend that the natural interval is labour time, so that the labour theory can accommodate the observed prices. Marx's point is that this evasion is unnecessary: the labour theory can accommodate the divergence by recognizing that production time extends turnover (thereby affecting capital advances, profitability, and price of production) without creating value. Value comes from labour; the natural interval affects the turnover through which value is realized and the capital that must be advanced — but it does not itself create value.

The footnote elaborates: "On MacCulloch see above, p. 94." And it notes: "It is not in Volume 3 of Capital, as published, but rather in Theories of Surplus-Value, that Marx's discussion of the elder Mill's 'futile attempts to resolve the contradictions of the Ricardian system' is to be found (Part III, pp. 84ff.), a discussion of MacCulloch coming later on in the same chapter, 'The Disintegration of the Ricardian School' (pp. 168ff.)." So the polemic is not merely a footnote to Chapter 13 but a hinge between Volume 2 and Volume 3, tying the analysis of turnover to the critique of Ricardian value theory.

2.18 The Rotation Cycle: Lease Length and the Compounding of Turnover

The final movement of the chapter returns to the concept of the turnover cycle as a function of the durability of fixed capital. "The turnover cycle that we considered previously is a function of the durability of the fixed capital advanced to the production process. Since this encompasses a greater or smaller number of years, it also encompasses a series of turnovers of the fixed capital, repeated either yearly or within a year."

In agriculture, this kind of cycle arises from the system of crop rotation. Marx quotes Kirchhof (pp. 117–118) at length:

"The duration of the lease must in any case not be shorter than the time taken to complete the system of crop rotation that is introduced, and hence with the three-field system it is always reckoned in terms of 3, 6, 9, etc. If we assume the three-field system with complete fallow, the field is cultivated only four times in six years, and in the years cultivated, with both winter and summer grain; the properties of the soil also require or permit this to be alternated between wheat and rye, barley and oats. Each kind of grain grows on the same soil better or worse than the others, each has a different value and is also sold at a different price. The yield of the land thus varies with the years of cultivation; it is different in the first half of the cycle (in the first three years) and in the second. Even the average yield over the whole cycle is not the same in one case as in the other, since fertility does not just depend on the quality of the soil, but also on the year's weather, the price also depending on many different conditions. If the yield is calculated on the basis of the average years of the entire cycle of six years, and of the average prices in these years, then the total yield for one year can be arrived at for either period of the rotation. But this is not the case if the yield is only calculated for half of the rotation, i.e. for three years, since then the total yields would not be the same. It is evident from this that the duration of the lease in the three-field system must be fixed at at least six years. It is always far more desirable for both landlord and tenant that the lease should run for a multiple of the lease (sic: F.E.) and thus instead of six years in the case of the three-field system, it should be twelve or eighteen years or more, and in the case of the seven-field system not seven but fourteen or twenty-eight."

The argument is a statistical one, and it deserves careful unpacking. In the three-field system with complete fallow, the field is cultivated only four times in six years (two cultivation years, each with winter and summer grain, in a six-year cycle — the other two years are fallow). The yields differ across the years of the cycle (first half vs. second half differ; soil quality interacts with crop type; weather varies year to year; prices vary). Therefore:

  • A three-year lease is too short because the yields over any three-year half-cycle are systematically different from a full-cycle average.
  • A six-year lease captures one complete rotation, allowing the calculation of a representative average across the full cycle. This is the minimum lease length.
  • Longer leases (multiples of the cycle) are preferable because they further smooth out year-to-year variations, giving a more representative average, and reducing the risk that the tenant's tenure happens to fall in an unfavorable sub-period.

The final judgment: leases should be at least 6 years for the three-field system (ideally 12, 18, or more), and at least 7 years for the seven-field system (ideally 14 or 28). The rotation cycle thus functions, in agriculture, analogously to the durability period of fixed capital in industry: both define the minimum horizon over which capital can be productively evaluated and recouped.

The chapter closes with a manuscript note: "(The manuscript states here: 'The English system of crop rotation. A note on this to be given.' — F.E.)" — an editorial signal that Marx intended to extend this analysis but left the note unfinished.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS:

The chapter yields several fundamental principles that together constitute a substantial addition to the theory of turnover.

First, the law of the two-fold composition of production time. Every production process has two potentially distinct temporal components: the time labour is applied (working time) and the time natural processes act independently (natural-process time). The production period is their sum; the turnover period is extended by the natural-process excess. This is a material law — rooted in physics, chemistry, and biology — not a conventional or institutional one. It establishes that turnover time has a substratum in the physical constitution of the product and its production, irreducible to market rhythms or human volition.

Second, the concept of unproductive but necessary capital. Idle fixed capital, depreciation during non-use, fodder consumed by idle draught animals, and the "unavoidable" waste of materials and labour-power under normal technical conditions are all conditions of normal production, not accidents. They enter the unit value of the product as costs exactly as productive expenditures do. This dissolves the naive notion that idle capital is "wasted" capital and shows that value transfer follows depreciation time, not functioning time — a subtle but consequential point about how fixed capital contributes to the value of the product.

Third, the category of potential productive capital and the two-sphere determination of turnover. The chapter distinguishes: - Production-sphere determinants of turnover (natural-process time, the nature of the product, the material form of capital). - Circulation-sphere determinants of turnover (replacement difficulty, market proximity, transport and communication development, price conditions).

This two-sphere framework becomes foundational for the rest of Part II. It allows Marx to trace precisely why different capitals turn over at different rates, and to show that this differentiation is not uniformly reducible to market phenomena.

Fourth, the class asymmetry of natural time. The natural unevenness of agricultural production — long production periods, seasonal working intervals, lumpy reflux, dependence on weather — is indifferent to capital in the aggregate ("all these differences in the turnover balance out") but destructive to the worker, who bears the seasonal unemployment, the subsidiary-industry dependency, and the ever-worsening condition produced by the separation of manufacture from agriculture. This is the chapter's political core: the natural basis of agricultural labor is transformed by capitalist relations into a structure of subordination and immiseration.

Fifth, the limits of capitalist production as a private form. The timber case demonstrates that branches requiring production times longer than a private planning horizon (up to 150 years, with reserve requirements of 10–40× annual yield) "cannot" be sustained under private and hence capitalist production. This is a structural — not incidental — limit. The parenthesis about communal production suggests that the limit is a function of the private form of capital, not of production as such.

Sixth, the staging of the Mill/MacCulloch critique. By announcing that the attempt to identify production time with working time is "nonsensical" and arises from "an incorrect application of the theory of value," Marx positions this chapter as a defense of the labour theory of value. Natural time does not create value; it extends turnover, forces larger and longer capital advances, and shapes the price of production through the mechanism of capital turnover — but value remains the product of labour alone. The distinction between production time and working time is thus not merely a technical refinement; it is the analytical firewall that prevents the conflation of nature's time with labor's time, and thereby preserves the coherence of the value theory.

Broader implications. For economic theory, the chapter shows that any account of capital turnover that treats it as a function solely of the labour process will systematically misestimate the capital requirements, profitability, and credit structure of branches with long natural-process intervals — agriculture, forestry, tanning, brewing, and iron historically foremost among them. For the theory of ground rent and agrarian credit, the chapter provides the material foundations: the seasonal idleness of the working population, the front-loaded capital advance, the dependence on merchants and lenders, the lease-length determination by rotation cycles. For the critique of political economy, it supplies one of the most rigorous demonstrations that value is a social relation, not a natural fact: the same physical interval — eight months between sowing and reaping — creates value through labour and extends turnover through nature, and the distinction between the two is invisible to those who identify production time with working time. Finally, for the broader Marxian project, the chapter reveals the deep material roots of rural underdevelopment and the subordination of agriculture to industrial capital: the natural rhythm of agricultural production is the substrate on which merchant capital first intrudes, manufacture is later separated from agriculture, and the rural worker becomes "ever more dependent on merely accidental subsidiary employments" — a structure of dependence that begins in the soil and ends in the wage relation.
