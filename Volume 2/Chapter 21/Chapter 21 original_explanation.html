Volume 2 • Chapter 21: Accumulation and Reproduction on an Expanded Scale

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS:

Chapter 21 of Capital Volume 2 performs the decisive intellectual move in Marx's theory of social reproduction: it lifts the analysis from the closed, static equilibrium of "simple reproduction" (where the annual product is consumed entirely and capital is merely replaced on the same scale) up to the dynamic, disequilibrating reality of "reproduction on an expanded scale" — the reproduction of the total social capital as an accumulating system. The chapter is drawn, as the editorial note at the top of page 1 explicitly tells us, "From here to the end, Manuscript VIII," which is Marx's own 1870s manuscript rather than Engels' synthetic reconstruction. This matters enormously: the chapter displays Marx's workshop as he struggles with a problem he never fully resolved — how the smooth ballet of numerical exchange rates between the two great departments of production (I. Means of Production, II. Means of Consumption) can be reconciled with the violent, crisis-prone, hoard-driven reality of capitalist accumulation.

The central problem the chapter poses is deceptively simple. In Volume 1 Marx showed how the individual capitalist accumulates: he realizes surplus value in money, then converts that money into additional elements of productive capital (means of production and labour-power), and next year produces on a larger scale. But a sum of individual capitals is not automatically a coherent social whole. When every capitalist tries to hoard surplus value simultaneously — selling commodities to acquire money but deferring the purchase of new commodities — the circulation process appears to break down. Where do the buyers come from if everyone wants to sell? Marx's answer contains the chapter's first major thesis: expanded reproduction does not require any addition to social wealth in the form of money. Money is withdrawn from circulation and stored as a hoard, but it does not cease to exist; it simply changes function — from means of circulation to hoard, to potential (or "virtual") money capital. And crucially, the surplus product whose money-form is being hoarded already exists as a physical stock of means of production. What is required for accumulation is not the creation of new wealth but the rearrangement of the elements of the annual product. This is Marx's second great thesis: "the material substratum for expanded reproduction is produced in the course of simple reproduction" (p. 573). Expanded reproduction is not a matter of the absolute size of the product but of the qualitative character of its elements, of "a different arrangement or a different determination of the functions of the various elements of the given product" (p. 575).

The third foundational thesis concerns Department II. Once Department I (the producers of means of production) retains a portion of its surplus product as additional constant capital instead of exchanging it against Department II's means of consumption, a portion of Department II's constant capital (IIc) becomes unsaleable. The condition for expanded reproduction thus cannot be the simple-reproduction equality I(v+s) = IIc. Instead Marx derives the fundamental unequal-exchange condition: I(v+s) > IIc. Department I must produce more means of production than Department II can absorb in the mere replacement of its constant capital, because Department I is now also supplying the material elements for Department II's own accumulation, while Department II in turn must supply Department I's additional workers with their means of subsistence. This is the dialectic of mutual provisioning that the chapter formalises in the schemas of Section III.

The chapter's intellectual objective is thus threefold. First, to demonstrate, in the money-form, how the hoards that appear in individual capitalist accounting also appear (and are reconciled) at the level of annual social reproduction. Second, to specify the value and material conditions under which the annual product can be converted back into productive capital on a larger scale — producing the famous reproduction schemas (Schema a: I. 4,000c+1,000v+1,000s, II. 1,500c+376v+376s; and the second example: I. 5,000c+1,000v+1,000s, II. 1,430c+285v+285s). Third, to draw out the dangerous consequences: the necessary inequality of I(v+s) and IIc, the perpetual tendency of the process to break down in crisis, the impossibility of simple reproduction as a stable capitalist state, and the role of the credit system in mobilising the "dead weight" of hoarded money capital.

How does this fit into the broader architecture? Chapter 21 is the culminating chapter of Capital Volume 2's Part III, "The Reproduction and Circulation of the Total Social Capital," and it is the bridge between Volume 2's abstract analysis of circulation and Volume 3's analysis of the credit system, the profit rate, and crisis. It is also, historically, the seedbed of the twentieth-century "market question" debate (Tugan-Baranovsky, Lenin, Rosa Luxemburg, Bukharin, Otto Bauer) and the disputed heritage of underconsumptionist and disproportionality theories of crisis. Within Volume 2 as a whole, this chapter proves that capital is not just a sum of things but a self-expanding process whose conditions of existence are internal to its own reproduction — an argument that has no equivalent in classical political economy. Adam Smith's attempt to reduce the annual product to a sum of revenues (the notorious "v+s dogma") is, we now see, only the mirror image of an error Marx is here at pains to demonstrate: capital is not consumed as revenue, and the productive consumption of a portion of the surplus product is the material precondition of accumulation.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS:

Opening: From the Individual Capitalist to the Social Whole

The chapter opens with the retrospective gesture of recalling Volume 1's account of individual accumulation. Marx's argument is that the form of individual accumulation determines the form of social accumulation. In Volume 1 the individual capitalist realizes his commodity capital (C′) as money; the portion of that money representing surplus value (M′) is converted back into additional elements of productive capital. "The surplus-value that is transformed into money in this way is then transformed back by the capitalist into additional natural elements of his productive capital. In the next production circuit, the increased capital supplies an increased product." The method is analogical: what occurs in the individual case must also occur in the overall annual reproduction, just as the successive precipitation of a worn-out fixed component as money (hoarding) at the individual level was shown, in Chapter 20, to have its parallel in social reproduction.

The numerical apparatus is set up immediately. A capital of 400c+100v, with annual surplus value 100, produces a commodity product of 400c+100v+100s = 600. This 600 is realized as money. Of that money, 400c is reconverted into the natural (use-value) form of constant capital; 100v is used to purchase labour-power; and 100s — if the entire surplus value is to be accumulated — is transformed into additional constant capital. Here the chapter inserts an editorial footnote (from Engels, presumably) noting that Marx "leaves this out of consideration for the time being" the fact that accumulation must also involve an increased outlay for variable capital (p. 575). This is important for the reader: the initial presentation looks only at additional constant capital, and only later (Section I(c)) does Marx turn to the additional variable capital. This is a textual strategy, not a substantive neglect.

Two assumptions are specified, both of which carry the weight of the chapter. (1) The sum of surplus value hoarded is assumed sufficient, under the given technical conditions, either to extend the already-functioning constant capital or to install an entirely new industrial business. Marx immediately concedes that this may require hoarding money "for a much longer time before this process takes place, i.e. before real accumulation, an expansion of production, can occur." Thus the quantitative threshold of accumulation is conditioned by the technical scale of modern industry; below it, the hoarded surplus value is idle. (2) More importantly, "it is presupposed that there has in fact already been reproduction on an expanded scale." Why? Because in order to transform money into the elements of productive capital, "these elements must be available on the market as commodities." It makes no difference if they are not bought ready-made but produced to order: "in any case only after real reproduction on an expanded scale, the extension of what was formerly normal production, has already taken place as far as they are concerned." The money calls expanded reproduction into being on the other side "only because the possibility of this already exists without the money; for money in itself is not an element of real reproduction." This last clause is the chapter's first theoretical hammer-blow: it is a direct refutation of any monetary or circulationist theory of accumulation.

The Puzzle of Universal Hoarding and Its Solution

The next movement of the text (pp. 577–579) sets out the apparent aporia of universal hoarding. Consider capitalist A, who sells his surplus product — the bearer of his surplus value — year after year, converting it bit by bit into money and storing it away. He is forming "potential new money capital," potential "on account of its capacity and its destiny, which is to be converted into elements of productive capital." But in fact, at this stage, A is merely performing "simple hoard formation." His activity "consists first of all simply in the successive withdrawal of circulating money from the circulation sphere."

Three critical points follow. First, this hoard is not an addition to social wealth — no more than money spent on means of consumption would be. The money withdrawn from circulation "may before that have either been already stored up once as a similar hoard, may have been the money form of wages, may have realized means of production or other commodities, or may have circulated constant capital components or revenue for some capitalist or other." Money no more constitutes new wealth by virtue of its manifold turnover than money in simple commodity circulation is "the bearer, not just of its actual value, but of ten times its value, simply because it has turned over ten times in a day." The commodities still exist without it; the money itself deteriorates through wear and tear. Only in gold production — in so far as the gold product contains or is a bearer of surplus value — is new wealth (in the form of potential money) actually created. This is a foundational qualification: the metallic money supply is the only substance of potential new money capital, and even then, only when the new gold product actually steps into circulation.

Second, and yet: "even though it is not additional new social wealth, this surplus-value hoarded up in the money form does represent new potential money capital, on account of the function for which it is stored." Function, not substance, makes the hoard into capital. This distinction between potential money capital and actual productive capital will later underwrite the analysis of the credit system (Volume 3), where banks concentrate these dispersed, idle hoards into "loanable capital."

Third, the aporia: "If this operation is conceived as taking place on all sides, it seems impossible to explain where the buyers are to come from." Everyone wants to sell in order to hoard; no one wants to buy. The circulation between the various parts of annual reproduction cannot be conceived rectilinearly, since "with few exceptions, it always consists of mutually opposing movements." If it were rectilinear, we would have to begin with the gold producer, "who buys without selling," and assume all others sell to him. But then the gold producer's surplus product would have to absorb the entire annual surplus product of society, and his surplus value would be the sole fund from which all other capitalists draw to realize their own surplus products — an absurdity. "These assumptions are so absurd that they are only helpful towards explaining the possibility of a general simultaneous hoard formation, and do not take reproduction itself, except that of the gold producers, a single step forwards."

Marx now signals the analytical division he will follow: accumulation in Department I must be distinguished from accumulation in Department II, and he will begin with Department I.

Section I — Accumulation in Department I

(a) Hoard Formation in Department I

The first substantive move (pp. 579–581) is to show that the apparent aporia dissolves once we recognise that the hoard-forming activity of some capitalists is bought by the productive investment of others. All the branches within Department I, and all the individual capitals within those branches, are "to be found at different stages in the process of their successive transformation of surplus-value into potential money capital." Some are still hoarding; others have already assembled enough hoarded value to convert it into productive capital, "buying means of production, additional elements of constant capital." The two categories "relate to one another as buyers and sellers respectively, and each of the two in this exclusive role."

Note Marx's methodological caution: what A does in this transaction is simple hoard formation, which "is in no case production, and thus from the start not an increment to production." The action consists of "simply withdrawing from circulation the money he obtained by selling his surplus product, holding on to it and impounding it." And this operation is performed "at numerous points on the circulation surface" by other capitalists A′, A″ etc., who all "work equally zealously at this kind of hoard formation." These multiple points at which money is withdrawn from circulation "appear as an equal number of obstacles to circulation, because they immobilize the money and deprive it of its capacity for circulation for a longer or shorter time."

There is a historical caveat here that Marx inserts deliberately: hoard formation is not a phenomenon original to capitalism. In simple commodity circulation — long before capitalism — there are already hoards; "the quantity of money present in the society is always greater than the part of this that is in active circulation, even if the latter rises and falls according to circumstances." What the capitalist mode does is to transform this pre-existing phenomenon into "an immanent moment of the capitalist production process." From this, Marx remarks ironically: "It is easy to understand the satisfaction evinced when the credit system concentrates all these potential capitals in the hands of banks, etc., makes them into disposable capital — 'loanable capital' — i.e. money capital, no longer passive and, as it were, a castle in the air, but active, usurious, proliferating capital." This is a preview of the dialectic: the hoard appears as passivity, but the credit system gives it activity — while also, as we will see in Section III, giving it the capacity to mistake itself for real accumulation.

The precondition for A's hoard formation is "thus his successive production of surplus product — the repository of his surplus-value that is to be realized." This "successive" character is crucial: the surplus product is produced continuously, but the money cannot be invested until the hoard reaches the threshold specified at the outset. The natural form of A's surplus product, in the case considered, is "the natural form of an element of constant capital in department I, i.e. it belongs to the category of means of production of means of production." Thus, when A sells to B (still within Department I), the commodity cast into circulation is destined to enter B's constant capital "as a fixed or fluid element."

But the key insight is that although A withdraws money from circulation for his surplus value, "he casts commodities into circulation, on the other hand, without withdrawing other commodities for these; this enables B, B′, B″, etc. for their part simply to cast money into circulation and withdraw commodities." The apparent impossibility of universal hoarding dissolves into a complementarity of one-sided movements: A sells without buying (hoards); B buys without selling (invests). In the present case — exchange internal to Department I — the commodity drawn out is a means of production that will enter B's capital. Thus each is doing what the other cannot, and the total process moves forward.

There follows a compressed reprise (pp. 581–583) of the fixed-capital complication from Chapter 20. Marx reminds the reader of the earlier schema's conclusion: the periodic renewal of the fixed capital portion of IIc presupposes "on the one hand a one-sided purchase of that fixed part of IIC which is transformed back from the money form into the natural form and to which corresponds a one-sided sale of Is; on the other hand it presupposes a one-sided sale on the part of IIC, the sale of that fixed value component (wear and tear) which is precipitated out in money and to which corresponds a one-sided purchase of Is." The balance condition for the exchange to take place "normally" is that "the one-sided purchase by IIC is equal in value to its one-sided sale," and similarly that I's one-sided sale to one section of II is equal to its one-sided purchase from another. Otherwise, "simple reproduction would be disrupted." Marx now generalises this: in the case of expanded reproduction, "it has similarly to be assumed that the one-sided sales by the hoard-forming section of Is, A, A′, A″, balance the one-sided purchases by section B, B′, B″ of Is, which transform their hoards into elements of additional productive capital."

This is the chapter's first crisis-theoretic proposition. The balance is not guaranteed. "The fact that the production of commodities is the general form of capitalist production already implies that money plays a role, not just as means of circulation, but also as money capital within the circulation sphere, and gives rise to certain conditions for normal exchange that are peculiar to this mode of production." These conditions for normal reproduction on either scale "turn into an equal number of conditions for an abnormal course, possibilities of crisis, since, on the basis of the spontaneous pattern of this production, this balance is itself an accident." The phrase "the balance is itself an accident" is one of the most concentrated statements of Marx's theory of crisis in the whole of Volume 2.

Marx then extends the analysis to the circulation between the two departments. In the exchange of Iv for a corresponding value of IIc, commodities II are ultimately replaced for IIc by the same value of commodities I. But "in this mutual conversion of their reciprocal commodities, there is no direct exchange between capitalists I and II." Instead, the circulation involves several interlocking one-sided transactions. Capitalist II sells his commodities IIc to the working class of Department I, facing them one-sidedly as a seller while they face him as buyers of commodities. With the money received, IIc one-sidedly faces the collective capitalist I as a buyer of commodities, and I faces him as a seller of commodities to the value of Iv. "It is only by this sale of commodities that department I finally reproduces its variable capital in the form of money capital."

The full circulation is composed of three independent but intertwined processes: (1) the continuous supply of labour-power on the part of Department I's working class; (2) the transformation of one part of Department I's commodity capital back into the money form of variable capital; (3) the replacement of a part of Department II's commodity capital by natural elements of constant capital IIc. "These necessary preconditions all mutually require one another, but they are mediated by a very complicated process which involves three processes of circulation that proceed independently, even if they are intertwined with one another." The sentence that closes this subsection is the refrain of the whole chapter: "The very complexity of the process provides many occasions for it to take an abnormal course."

(b) The Additional Constant Capital

This is the core theoretical subsection. It begins with a striking claim: "The surplus product, the repository of surplus-value, does not cost anything to its appropriators, here the capitalists in department I. They do not have to advance either money or commodities, in any form, in order to receive it." This is a quietly devastating point. The Physiocrats' concept of avance (advance) signified "simply the general form of value realized in the elements of productive capital." What the capitalist advances is nothing but constant and variable capital. The worker, however, "does not merely maintain their constant capital for them by way of his labour, and replace their variable capital by way of a corresponding portion of value newly created in the form of commodities; he also supplies them, by his surplus labour, with a surplus-value existing in the form of a surplus product."

The "in the case considered here" — the case examined throughout this chapter — the surplus product "consists from the start of means of production of means of production." Thus, "it is only in the hands of B, B′, B″ etc. (department I) that this surplus product functions as additional constant capital; but it is already virtually this, even before it is sold, in the hands of the hoard formers A, A′, A″."

Now comes the crucial conservation-of-value argument. "If we simply consider the level of reproduction on the part of department I in value terms, then we still find ourselves within the limits of simple reproduction, for no additional capital has been set in motion in order to create this virtual excess of constant capital (the surplus product), and no more surplus labour than was performed on the basis of simple reproduction." What has changed is only the use-form of the surplus labour applied — "the concrete character of its particular useful mode." In the case of simple reproduction the surplus labour was assumed to have been spent producing means of production for IIc. Now it is "spent on means of production for Ic instead of IIC, on means of production for means of production instead of on means of production for means of consumption."

Therefore: "in order to make the transition from simple reproduction to expanded reproduction, production in department I must be in a position to produce fewer elements of constant capital for department II, but all the more for department I." The transition "can never be achieved without difficulty" and is "made easier by the fact that a number of the products of department I can serve as means of production in both departments." Here is the fundamental thesis of the chapter: "It follows therefore that — simply considering the values involved — the material substratum for expanded reproduction is produced in the course of simple reproduction."

From this, Marx draws two analytic consequences that he will need. First: "The formation of virtual additional money capital on the part of A, A′, A″ (department I) — by the subsequent sale of their surplus product, which has been formed without any monetary expenditure by the capitalists involved — is thus here simply the money form of extra production of means of production in department I." In other words, the money-form of the hoard is nothing but the shadow of a use-value transformation that has already occurred. Second: "the large-scale production of additional virtual money capital — at numerous points on the surface of circulation — is therefore nothing more than the result and expression of the many-sided production of virtual additional productive capital, whose genesis does not itself presuppose any additional monetary expenditure on the part of the industrial capitalists."

The successive transformation of virtual additional productive capital into virtual money capital (the hoard) on the part of A, A′, A″ etc. is "conditioned by the successive sale of their surplus product — i.e. by the repeated one-sided sale of commodities without a complementary purchase." Two consequences follow: (i) there is a repeated withdrawal of money from circulation and a corresponding hoard formation; (ii) hoard formation "in no way implies additional wealth in precious metals, but only a different function for the money that was already in circulation previously. It formerly functioned as a means of circulation, and now it functions as a hoard, as virtual new money capital in the course of formation." Hence: "The formation of additional money capital and the quantity of precious metal existing in a country thus do not stand in any causal connection with one another." This is a decisive methodological break from the quantity theory of money.

Marx now states the growth principles of hoarding. The greater the productive capital already functioning (including the labour-power incorporated into it), and the more developed the productive power of labour, and hence the greater the mass of surplus product in both value and use-value terms — then the greater is: (1) the additional virtual productive capital in the form of surplus product in the hands of A, A′, A″ etc.; and (2) the quantity of this surplus product transformed into money, i.e. the quantity of additional virtual money capital in the hands of A, A′, A″. Thus, "if Fullarton, for instance, does not want to recognize over-production in the customary sense, but does recognize the over-production of capital, in particular of money capital, this proves once again how utterly unable even the best bourgeois economists are to understand the mechanism of their system" (p. 574, footnote). Fullarton, an English opponent of the quantity theory of money, is here criticised for recognising only money-capital over-production, not real over-production — a symptom of the inability to see that money capital is the mere form of a surplus product that is materially already there.

Marx then sharpens the point: "If the surplus product directly produced and appropriated by the capitalists A, A′, A″ (department I) is the real basis for capital accumulation, i.e. for expanded reproduction, even though it actually functions in this capacity only in the hands of B, B′, B″ etc. (department I), it is however absolutely unproductive in its monetary metamorphosis — as a hoard and as virtual money capital that is formed bit by bit. In this form it runs parallel with the production process but lies outside it. It is a 'dead weight' on capitalist production." The credit system and the "papers" through which it operates are the attempt to set this dead weight in motion as active capital — and Marx here prepares us for the later analysis of fictitious capital.

There follows the segmentation argument. "The surplus product converted into virtual money capital becomes quantitatively greater, the greater the total sum of capital already functioning, from the functioning of which it emerged." This absolute increase makes segmentation easier: money capital can be "completely separated from its parent capital, in order to be invested as new money capital in an independent business." Inheritances, family fortunes, new independent ventures — all are ways in which the aggregate hoard can be split and deployed.

The next section (pp. 575–577) raises the question of the origin of the money that B, B′, B″ must use to buy A's surplus product. Marx's assumption so far has been that A, A′, A″ themselves have already produced the surplus product without monetary expenditure. But to come into the hands of B, B′, B″, "an act of circulation is required; they have to buy the surplus product." Two issues arise: (1) a major part of the surplus product produced by A in the current year "can actually function only in the hands of B, B′, B″ (department I) as industrial capital in the following year or even later"; (2) where does the money for this circulation come from?

Marx's answer is that we already know, from the analysis of simple reproduction, that "a certain quantity of money must exist in the hands of the capitalists in departments I and II so that they may exchange their surplus product." There, the money whose only use was to be spent as revenue on means of consumption returned to the capitalists to the extent they advanced it. Here "the same money similarly reappears, but with its function changed." The A's and B's (Department I) "supply one another with the money for transforming their surplus products into additional virtual money capital, and alternately cast the newly formed money capital into the circulation sphere as a means of purchase."

The only presupposition required is that "the quantity of money existing in the country (the velocity of circulation etc. taken as constant) is sufficient both for active circulation and for the reserve hoards" — the same condition Marx established for simple commodity circulation — with the important qualification that the required quantity must be larger in the case of capitalism, for four reasons: (1) all products are produced as commodities in capitalist production and must therefore undergo a metamorphosis into money; (2) the mass of commodity capital and its value is not only absolutely greater but grows incomparably faster; (3) the variable capital that has to be converted into money capital is ever more extensive; (4) as production expands, the formation of new money capital keeps in step, and so the material for its hoarded form also has to be present.

Marx then notes the most developed phase of the credit system: metallic circulation remains its basis. "The extra production of precious metals, according to whether this makes them abundant or scarce, can now exert a disturbing influence on the price of commodities, not only in the long term, but also within very short periods." Conversely, "the whole credit mechanism must constantly be engaged in restricting the actual circulation of metal by all kinds of operations, methods, technical devices, to what is relatively an ever decreasing minimum — though this also increases in the same proportion the artificial character of the entire machinery and the chances of its normal course being disturbed." Another compressed statement of the dialectic of the credit system: rationalisation and fragility go hand in hand.

The subsection closes with a note on method: "It is important above all, however, to start by assuming metal circulation in its most simple original form, since in this way the flux or reflux, settlement of balances, in short all those aspects that appear in the credit system as consciously regulated processes, present themselves as existing independently of the credit system, and the thing appears in its spontaneous form, instead of the form of subsequent reflection." This is a methodological tour de force: the credit system is the secondary reflection of processes that originate in the spontaneous metallic circulation; to see the true mechanism we must strip away the credit system's mediation.

(c) The Additional Variable Capital

This is one of the shortest subsections in the chapter, and its brevity is deliberate. Marx refers back to Volume 1 for the demonstration "how, on the basis of capitalist production, labour-power is always on hand, and how, if necessary, more labour can be extracted without an increase in the number of workers employed, or the mass of labour-power." He then states: "We do not have to go into this any further here, therefore, but can simply assume that the portion of the newly formed money capital that is convertible into variable capital always finds available the labour-power into which it is to be transformed."

Marx also invokes Volume 1's demonstration that a given capital can within certain limits expand its scale of production without accumulation (e.g. through longer working hours or greater intensity). But "what we are dealing with now, however, is capital accumulation in the specific sense, where the expansion of production is conditioned by the transformation of surplus-value into extra capital, and therefore by the expanded capital basis of production." This is an important conceptual distinction: "accumulation" in the strict sense is not mere expansion of production; it is expansion grounded in the transformation of surplus value into new capital.

The gold producer is treated as a special case, because he can accumulate a part of his golden surplus value as virtual money capital; once it reaches the necessary level, he can convert it directly into new variable capital, and also into elements of constant capital. But he must find the material elements of constant capital available — whether producers work to fill stocks or work to order. "The real expansion of production, i.e. the surplus product, is presupposed in both cases, once as actually present, the other time as virtually present, capable of being supplied."

Section II — Accumulation in Department II

This section turns from the internal circulation of Department I to the circulation between the two departments — and Marx here introduces the conflict that structures the remainder of the chapter. Consider the case in which A (Department I) sells his surplus product to a B belonging to Department II. "This can only happen if A (I), after he has sold means of production to B (II), does not go on to buy means of consumption; i.e. only by a unilateral sale on his part."

The consequence is immediate and severe. If the conversion of IIc back into the natural form of productive constant capital normally involves the exchange of Iv plus at least a part of Is for part of IIc, and A now realizes Is in money without undertaking that exchange, "then although the formation of additional virtual money capital takes place on A's part, on the other side an equal part of B (II)'s constant capital is tied up in the form of commodity capital, incapable of conversion into the natural form of productive, constant capital." In other words, "a part of B (II)'s commodities, and at first sight a part without selling which he cannot transform his constant capital completely back into the productive form, has become unsaleable; in this respect there is over-production, which also inhibits B (II)'s reproduction — even on the same scale."

Marx generalises. "A (I), by selling its surplus product to B (II), has supplied the latter with a corresponding portion of constant capital in the natural form, but at the same time made an equal portion of B (II)'s commodity value unsaleable." Considered in aggregate, the transformation of A(I)'s surplus product into virtual money capital "expresses the non-transformability of a portion of commodity capital equal to this in value back into productive (constant) capital; i.e. not virtual production on an expanded scale, but rather a restriction of simple reproduction, i.e. a shortfall in simple reproduction."

The passage that follows is one of the most theoretically loaded in the chapter. Since the formation and sale of A(I)'s surplus product are themselves normal phenomena of simple reproduction, we have even on the basis of simple reproduction the following "mutually conditioning phenomena": "formation of virtual extra money capital in department I (hence under-consumption from department II's standpoint); piling up of commodity stocks in department II which cannot be transformed back into productive capital (i.e. relative over-production in department II); surplus money capital in department I and a shortfall in reproduction in department II."

But Marx's analytical verdict is sharp. This coordinated set of phenomena does not yet constitute expanded reproduction. It is a disturbance of simple reproduction. Expanded reproduction requires something more: an actual modification of the arrangement of the elements of the annual product. And here Marx makes the crucial clarification: "Without delaying any longer on this point, we simply note that it was presupposed in our presentation of simple reproduction that the entire surplus-value in departments I and II was spent as revenue. In point of fact, however, one portion of surplus-value is spent as revenue, and another portion transformed into capital. Only with this precondition does real accumulation take place."

The next sentence is a searing critique of an entire theory: "But the idea that accumulation is achieved at the expense of consumption — considered in this general way — is an illusion that contradicts the essence of capitalist production, in as much as it assumes that the purpose and driving motive of this is consumption, and not the grabbing of surplus-value and its capitalization, i.e. accumulation." This is Marx's rejection of underconsumptionism — not in the sense that workers' consumption is irrelevant, but in the sense that treating accumulation as constrained by the need to feed consumption misconstrues the purpose of the capitalist process. Capital does not accumulate in order to consume; it consumes (productively) in order to accumulate.

Marx now considers accumulation in Department II "somewhat more closely." He begins from the previous schema (1,000v + 1,000s)I exchanged for 2,000 IIc. If half of Is (500) is now reincorporated into Department I as constant capital, then "this part of the surplus product that is retained in department I cannot replace any part of IIc." Instead of being converted into means of consumption, it "is to serve as additional means of production in department I itself." And "it cannot perform this function simultaneously in both department I and department II." The upshot: instead of 2,000 I(v+s), only 1,500 is available for conversion into 2,000 IIc; and so 500 IIc "can in fact not be transformed from its commodity form into productive (constant) capital II."

"Thus an overproduction in department II, corresponding in value precisely to the expansion of production that took place in department I." And this can have a feedback effect: the over-production in II "might in fact react so strongly on department I that even the reflux of the 1,000 spent by the department I workers on means of consumption II would take place only partially." This means the 1,000 spent on means of consumption II would not return to the hands of the Department I capitalists in the form of variable money capital. Hence: "The latter would thus be inhibited even in their reproduction on the same scale, and inhibited, moreover, by the very attempt to expand it." Marx then adds an important clarification: "It should also be mentioned in this connection that all that has taken place in department I is in fact simple reproduction, the elements merely being grouped together differently from the above schema, in accordance with the needs of future expansion, say in the coming year."

Marx then anticipates and critiques a possible escape from the difficulty. One might argue that the 500 IIc lying in the capitalists' stores is not over-production but a necessary element of reproduction which we have up to now neglected. The argument: because money is piled up at several points, and must be withdrawn from circulation partly to make possible the formation of new money capital in Department I itself, and partly to maintain the value of the fixed capital being gradually consumed, commodity stocks must accumulate. Since the schema assumes all money and commodities are in the hands of the capitalists I and II, with no merchants, money-dealers, bankers, or non-producing consumer classes, "the constant formation of commodity stocks is indispensable, in the hands of their respective producers themselves, in order to keep the machinery of reproduction going." Thus the 500 IIc in the stores of Department II "represents the commodity stock in means of consumption that ensures the continuity of the consumption process involved in reproduction, and therefore the transition from one year to the other." This stock cannot sink to zero on the last day of the year and reappear on the first day of the next. Capitalist producers must hold a money reserve capital, and they must also, in the schema's simplified world, supply themselves with the money capital that in reality exists in the hands of merchants.

Marx concedes the reality of these stock-effects but subjects them to three objections. (1) The argument holds for all capitalists in both departments; "a stock of commodities in department II implies a previous stock of commodities in department I. If we ignore this stock on one side, we must also ignore it on the other. But if we bring both sides into consideration, the problem is in no way changed." (2) The symmetry must be extended temporally. Just as the current year concludes with a commodity stock for the next, so it began with a commodity stock left over from the previous year. "In analysing the annual reproduction — reduced to its most abstract expression — we must thus cancel out the stock on both sides." If we leave the year with the commodity stock transferred to the next, we must deduct the stock received from the year before, leaving us with the total product of an average year as the object of analysis. (3) Most importantly: the fact that we did not encounter this difficulty in simple reproduction "shows that we are dealing here with a specific phenomenon that is due merely to the different arrangement of the elements of department I (as far as reproduction is concerned), an arrangement without which there could be no reproduction on an expanded scale at all." This is the dialectical point: the difficulty is the solution. The rearrangement of Department I's product that causes the difficulty in exchange is precisely what constitutes the material precondition for expansion.

Section III — The Schematic Presentation of Accumulation

The section opens with Schema (a):

I. 4,000c + 1,000v + 1,000s = 6,000 II. 1,500c + 376v + 376s = 2,252

Total annual social product = 8,252. This is smaller than the 9,000 of the original schema. Marx's explanation: "We might just as well take a much larger sum, multiply it by ten, say, for all the difference it makes." The point is pedagogical. The smaller total is chosen "precisely to draw attention to the fact that reproduction on an expanded scale (which is conceived here simply as production pursued with a greater investment of capital) has nothing to do with the absolute size of the product, that for a given volume of commodities it simply assumes a different arrangement or a different determination of the functions of the various elements of the given product, and is thus in the first instance only simple reproduction, as far as its value goes." Then the crucial sentence: "It is not the quantity, but the qualitative character of the given elements of simple reproduction that is changed, and this change is the material precondition for the ensuing reproduction on an expanded scale."

Schema (b) is then introduced for contrast:

I. 4,000c + 875v + 875s = 5,750 II. 1,750c + 376v + 376s = 2,502

In both schemas, the annual product is the same value (8,252). But in (b) it is arranged such that reproduction begins again on the same scale: (875v+875s)I = 1,750 I(v+s) is exchanged without a surplus for 1,750 IIc. In (a), by contrast, (1,000v+1,000s)I = 2,000 I(v+s) leaves behind, when exchanged for 1,500 IIc, a surplus of 500 Is for accumulation in Department I. Thus Schema (b) is a simple-reproduction schema of the same total value as (a), and the difference is entirely in the arrangement of the elements.

Marx then analyses Schema (a) more closely. He assumes toward this end that "both in department I and department II half the surplus-value, instead of being spent as revenue, is accumulated." Half of Is = 500. Since this 500 is accumulated (i.e. transformed into extra productive capital), "it follows that only (1,000v+500s)I is spent as revenue. Hence the normal size of IIc is now only 1,500." The exchange between 1,500 and 1,500 IIc requires no further investigation — it is already a process of simple reproduction.

What remains to be investigated are: the 500 Is and (376v+376s)II — "which involves both the internal relations in the two departments and the movement between them." Since it is assumed that half of Department II's surplus value (i.e. 188) is to be accumulated, "one quarter of this or 47 into variable capital, say 48 for the sake of a round number, leaving 140 to be transformed into constant capital." (Note: the relation is organic — Department II's own c:v ratio, from 1,500c+750v, is 2:1; so the split of new investment into v and c follows this ratio. If 188 is accumulated, v: c = 1:4 because the earlier total (1,500c+376v+376s = 2,252, of which c = 1,500, v = 376, ratio ~4:1). Actually looking at the schema: 1,500c + 376v means c:v = 3.99:1 ≈ 4:1, so of the 188 accumulated, 1/5 = 37.6 → 48 as v, 4/5 = 150.4 → 140 as c. Marx admits 48 is a "round" replacement for 47.)

Here Marx explicitly signals the new theoretical problem: "The 140 IIS can be transformed into productive capital only by being replaced by a portion of commodities Is to the same value. It is self-evident that the part of the Is that is to be exchanged for IIS must consist of means of production, able to go either into the production of both departments, or exclusively into that of department II." And then: "This exchange can take place only by a unilateral purchase on the part of department II, since the surplus product 500 Is, which has still to be considered, is destined to serve for accumulation within department I, and cannot therefore be exchanged for commodities II. In other words, department I cannot at the same time both accumulate and consume the surplus product." And so: "Department II must therefore buy 140s for cash, without this money flowing back to it by the subsequent sale of its commodities to department I. And this is moreover a constant and repeated process for each new year's production, in so far as this is reproduction on an expanded scale. Where then in department II is the source of money for this?"

This question is then answered by a series of careful exclusions. First, the 376 IIv does not help: "The money capital of 376, advanced for labour-power, constantly returns to the department II capitalists as variable capital in the money form, with the purchase of department II's commodities. This constantly repeated removal from and return to the starting-point — the capitalist's pocket — in no way increases the amount of money driving round this circuit." It is not a source of monetary accumulation; nor can the money be withdrawn from this circuit and hoarded. Second, Marx considers the possibility that Department II might exploit its peculiar advantage of selling commodities back to its own workers. He sets out two variants: (1) simply reduce wages below their normal average level, freeing part of the money functioning as variable capital, which, if the process is constantly repeated, becomes "a normal source of hoard formation"; (2) exploit the truck system and falsification of the circulating medium. Both are rejected. In the case of (1): "the normal wage which is actually paid (and which determines the size of the variable capital, other things being equal) is in no way paid out of the good will of the capitalists, but is what has to be paid under the given conditions. This mode of explanation is thereby dispensed with." In the case of (2): "we are talking about the real wage, not that nominally paid."

Marx interjects a characteristic meta-commentary: "We see here that in an objective analysis of the capitalist mechanism, certain blemishes that still stick to it, and with extraordinary tenacity, cannot be used as subterfuges for getting round theoretical difficulties. But strange though this seems, the great majority of my bourgeois critics complain that I do the capitalist an injustice by assuming — in Volume 1 of Capital, for example — that he pays the real value of labour-power, which in most cases he does not! (Here I might quote Schäffle, with the magnanimity he attributes to me.)" This is a deliciously ironic passage: Marx is accused of being too kind to capitalists for assuming they pay the value of labour-power, yet his critics then demand he invoke underpayment as an escape from a theoretical puzzle.

Third, the 376 IIs: "Here it is only capitalists in the same department who confront one another, selling and buying from each other the means of consumption that they have produced. The money needed for this exchange functions simply as a means of circulation, and in the normal course it must flow back to the parties involved in the same degree to which they first advanced it to circulation, so it can tread the same path once again." Withdrawal of this money from circulation for hoard formation is possible only in two ways. (i) One section of Department II capitalists might swindle the others and rob them of their money. "The fact that the money can be stolen and that the formation of additional money capital by one section of the capitalists in department II can therefore be combined with a positive loss of money by another section has absolutely no bearing on the matter. The defrauded section of department II capitalists would have to live a little less extravagantly, but that would be all." (ii) Alternatively, "a portion of IIS that represents necessary means of subsistence is directly transformed into new variable capital in department II. How this happens will be investigated at the close of the present chapter (section 4)." Marx postpones the resolution to the last section of the chapter.

(a) First Example

Marx now presents the fully worked-out numerical illustration, beginning with the two schemas of departure.

(A) Schema of simple reproduction: I. 4,000c + 1,000v + 1,000s = 6,000 II. 2,000c + 500v + 500s = 3,000 Total = 9,000

(B) Initial schema for reproduction on an expanded scale: I. 4,000c + 1,000v + 1,000s = 6,000 II. 1,500c + 750v + 750s = 3,000 Total = 9,000

Assume half of Is is accumulated, i.e. 500. Then (1,000v+500s)I = 1,500 I(v+s) is replaced by 1,500 IIc. There then remains in Department I: 4,000c + 500s, the latter to be accumulated. The replacement of (1,000v+500s)I by 1,500 IIc "is a process of simple reproduction and has already been examined in connection with the latter."

Marx then assumes that 400 of the 500 Is to be accumulated has to be transformed into constant capital, and 100 into variable capital. (Notice: this ratio 4:1 corresponds to Department I's own composition, 4,000c:1,000v = 4:1.) "The exchange within department I of the 400s that has to be capitalized has already been explained; so this can be annexed to Ic without any more ado, and we then get the following capitalization for department I: I. 4,400c + 1,000v [in money] + 100s (to be converted into 100v)."

Department II now buys from Department I, for the purpose of accumulation, the 100 Is (existing as means of production), which now forms extra constant capital for Department II. The 100 in money it pays is transformed into the money form of Department I's extra variable capital. Hence Department I's capital: 4,400c + 1,100v (the latter in money) = 5,500.

Department II now has 1,600c in constant capital; "a further 50v has to be added for the purchase of new labour-power for working this up, and so its variable capital grows from 750 to 800." (Notice again: 100c added, so 50v added, ratio 2:1, matching Department II's original c:v = 1,500:750 = 2:1.) This extension of both constant and variable capital in Department II is met out of its own surplus value. Of the 750 IIs, therefore, there only remains 600s as a consumption fund for Department II's capitalists: II. 1,600c + 800v + 600s (consumption fund) = 3,000.

The 150s produced as means of consumption that is converted here into (100c+50v)II goes completely into the workers' consumption in its natural form: 100 is consumed by Department I's workers (100v) and 50 by Department II's workers (50v), as elaborated above. This is a key point: the physical form of the value transferred (150 IIs) is means of consumption that must be consumed by workers; the arrangement is such that the additional constant and variable capital of Department II correspond to a larger portion of its product being produced as necessary means of consumption rather than luxury/consumption-fund goods.

The rearrangement after accumulation: I. 4,400c + 1,100v + 500 (capitalists' consumption fund) = 6,000 II. 1,600c + 800v + 600 (capitalists' consumption fund) = 3,000 Total = 9,000.

Capital after rearrangement: I. 4,400c + 1,100v (money) = 5,500 II. 1,600c + 800v (money) = 2,400 Total capital = 7,900.

Whereas production began with: I. 4,000c + 1,000v = 5,000 II. 1,500c + 750v = 2,250 Total = 7,250.

If real accumulation proceeds on this basis (i.e. if production actually takes place with this increased capital), then at the end of the following year: I. 4,400c + 1,100v + 1,100s = 6,600 II. 1,600c + 800v + 800s = 3,200 Total = 9,800.

Marx then continues the process. If accumulation continues in Department I in the same proportions, i.e. 550s spent as revenue and 550s accumulated, then to start with 1,100 Iv is replaced by 1,100 IIc. And 550 Is remains to be realized in an equal amount of commodities II, i.e. altogether 1,650 I(v+s). But IIc = 1,600, so "the remaining 50 must be supplemented from the 800 IIs." If we abstract from the money initially: I. 4,400c + 550s (to be capitalized); as well as 1,650(v+s) in the consumption fund for capitalists and workers, realized in commodities IIc. II. 1,650c (with 50 added from IIs) + 800v + 750s (capitalists' consumption fund).

Now if the former ratio of v to c in Department II remains unchanged, a further 25v must be laid out for 50c (ratio 2:1). This comes out of the 750s: II. 1,650c + 825v + 725s.

In Department I, 550s has to be capitalized. If the earlier ratio remains unchanged, 440 forms constant capital and 110 variable capital (ratio 4:1). This 110 is ultimately obtained from the 725 IIs. Means of consumption to the value of 110 are consumed by Department I's workers instead of by Department II's capitalists, "the latter being forced to capitalize this 110s instead of consuming it." This leaves 615 IIs out of 725 IIs. But if Department II transforms this 110 into additional constant capital, it needs a further additional variable capital of 55 (ratio 2:1). This comes out of the 615 IIs, leaving 560 for consumption of Department II's capitalists. After all "actual and potential transfers," we get: I. (4,400c+440c) + (1,100v+110v) = 4,840c + 1,210v = 6,050 II. (1,600c+50c+110c) + (800v+25v+55v) = 1,760c + 880v = 2,640 Total capital = 8,690.

The crucial general observation: "If things are to proceed normally, accumulation in department II must take place quicker than in department I, since the part of I(v+s) that has to be exchanged for commodities IIc would otherwise grow more quickly than IIc, which is all that it can be exchanged for." This is the structural asymmetry of expanded reproduction: Department II must accelerate its own accumulation in order to absorb the growing flow of I(v+s) that requires IIc as its counterpart.

The process continues. At the end of the following year, with reproduction continuing on the new basis: I. 4,840c + 1,210v + 1,210s = 7,260 II. 1,760c + 880v + 880s = 3,520 Total = 10,780.

Marx then works through the same fractions again. Department I has 1,210v plus half of s (=605) to spend as revenue, a total of 1,815. This consumption fund is 55 greater than IIc (which is 1,760). The 55 has to be deducted from the 880s. The transformation of 55 IIs into Ic presupposes a further deduction from IIs for a corresponding variable capital of 27½; there remains 797½ IIs to be consumed. Department I: 605s to be capitalized, of which 484 for constant, 121 for variable. The 121 is deducted from the 797½, leaving 676½. Department II transforms a further 121 into constant capital and needs for this purpose a further variable capital of 60½; this comes out of the 676½, leaving 616 for consumption. Capital: I. Constant 4,840 + 484 = 5,324; Variable 1,210 + 121 = 1,331. II. Constant 1,760 + 55 + 121 = 1,936; Variable 880 + 27½ + 60½ = 968. So: I. 5,324c + 1,331v = 6,655; II. 1,936c + 968v = 2,904.

Product at the end of the year: I. 5,324c + 1,331v + 1,331s = 7,986 II. 1,936c + 968v + 968s = 3,872 Total = 11,858.

Repeating the calculation, rounding off fractions, at the end of the following year: I. 5,856c + 1,464v + 1,464s = 8,784 II. 2,129c + 1,065v + 1,065s = 4,259 Total = 13,043.

At the close of the year after that: I. 6,442c + 1,610v + 1,610s = 9,662 II. 2,342c + 1,172v + 1,172s = 4,686 Total = 14,348.

Summary statistics: In the course of five years of reproduction on an expanded scale, total capital of departments I and II has risen from 5,500c + 1,750v = 7,250 to 8,784c + 2,782v = 11,566, a ratio of 100:160. Total surplus value originally 1,750, now 2,782. Surplus value consumed was originally 500 for Department I and 600 for Department II, a total of 1,100; in the final year 732 for Department I and 745 for Department II, altogether 1,477. It has grown in the ratio of 100:134.

Note the accent in Marx's summary: even while the total mass of surplus value has grown substantially, the consumed portion has grown too — from 1,100 to 1,477. This is the numerical demonstration that accumulation does not require a sacrifice of consumption (at the aggregate level); on the contrary, accumulation raises the consumption fund of the capitalist class (and, indirectly, the variable capital, hence the workers' means of consumption). This is the numerical refutation of the underconsumptionist illusion.

(b) Second Example

Marx now considers a second example designed to illustrate a different organic composition. He assumes an annual product of 9,000 existing entirely as commodity capital in the hands of the industrial capitalist class, "in a form in which the general average ratio of variable to constant capital [in both departments] is 1:5." This fact itself presupposes "a significant development of capitalist production and, accordingly, of the productivity of social labour as well; a significant prior expansion of the scale of production; and finally a development of all the circumstances that produce in the working class a relative surplus population." The annual product:

I. 5,000c + 1,000v + 1,000s = 7,000 II. 1,430c + 285v + 285s = 2,000 Total = 9,000.

(An editorial footnote notes: "The division of the social product between departments I and II here is an arbitrary ratio, selected presumably for arithmetical convenience.")

Assume the capitalist class in Department I consumes half its surplus value, 500, and accumulates the other half. In this case, (1,000v + 500s)I = 1,500 needs to be exchanged with 1,500 IIc. But IIc = 1,430. Therefore, 70 of surplus value has to be added — 70 taken from IIs. This, deducted from the 285 IIs, leaves 215 IIs. So: I. 5,000c + 500s (to be capitalized) + 1,500(v+s) in the consumption fund for capitalists and workers. II. 1,430c + 70s (to be capitalized) + 285v + 215s.

Since 70 IIs has been directly annexed to IIc, a variable capital of 1/5 × 70 = 14 is required to set this extra constant capital in motion, given the general average composition 1:5. This 14 comes out of the 215 IIs, leaving 201 IIs, and we have: II. (1,430c + 70c) + (285v + 14v) + 201s.

The exchange of 1,500 against 1,500 IIc is again simple reproduction, "and nothing further need be said about it." Marx then states the crucial specificity of accumulating reproduction: "In so far as there are still some peculiarities to be noted here, these stem from the fact that with accumulating reproduction, Iv is not just replaced by IIc alone, but rather by IIc plus a part of IIs."

Marx then articulates the general principle of interdepartmental provisioning: "It is self-evident that, on the assumption of accumulation, I(v+s) is greater than IIc, and not equal to it as in simple reproduction; since (1) department I incorporates a part of its surplus product into its own productive capital and transforms five sixths of this into constant capital, so that it cannot simultaneously exchange this five sixths for means of consumption II; and (2) department I has to supply the material for the constant capital needed for accumulation within department II out of its surplus product, just as department II has to supply department I with the material for the variable capital that is to set in motion the portion of its surplus product that department I itself applies as extra constant capital."

This is one of the most important conceptual passages in the chapter: the reciprocity between the departments is material, not merely value-formal. Department I provides Department II with the material of its additional constant capital (means of production). Department II provides Department I with the material of its additional variable capital — i.e. means of subsistence for the additional workers. Marx adds the crucial clarification: "We know that variable capital actually consists of labour-power, and so too therefore does this additional capital. It is not the capitalists in department I who buy or store up means of subsistence from department II for the additional labour-power that they need to employ, as the slave-owners had to do. It is the workers themselves who deal with department II. But this does not prevent the means of consumption for this additional labour-power from being viewed by the capitalist as only so many means of production and maintenance for his potential additional labour-power." Thus for the capitalist, additional variable capital appears materially as means of production-and-maintenance of labour-power, even though the actual purchase of means of consumption is performed by the workers themselves. "Once he has incorporated this labour-power, the money becomes for the workers a means of purchase of commodities II, and they must therefore find their means of consumption to hand."

Here Marx inserts a long polemical digression (pp. 587–589), triggered by observations about "rational consumption." He writes: "Incidentally, Mr Capitalist, as well as his press, is frequently discontented with the way in which labour-power spends its money, and with the commodities II in which it realizes this. On this occasion he philosophizes, waxes cultural and philanthropizes, as for example Mr Drummond, the British Secretary of Embassy in Washington." He quotes at length from The Nation (October 1879): "The working-people have not kept up in culture with the growth of invention, and they have had things showered on them which they do not know how to use, and thus make no market for." And: "There is no reason why the working man should not desire as many comforts as the minister, lawyer, and doctor, who is earning the same amount as himself." And: "The problem remains, how to raise him as a consumer by rational and healthful processes" — the demagogues rather incite the worker to shorten his hours than to raise his condition "by the improvement of his mental and moral powers."

Marx's riposte: "Long hours of labour seem to be the secret of these 'rational and healthful processes,' which are to raise the condition of the worker by improving his 'mental and moral powers' and making a rational consumer out of him. In order to become a rational consumer of the capitalists' commodities, he must before all else — but the demagogues prevent him — begin by letting his own labour-power be consumed irrationally and in a way contrary to his own health, by the capitalist who employs him." He then cites Drummond's further account of the Lowell and Lawrence model cotton factories, where boarding houses belong to the same joint-stock company as the factory; "no girl may return home after 10 p.m."; a special police force patrols the area to enforce this; "no girl may lodge anywhere but on the company's land, and each house brings in some 10 dollars a week in rent." The "ever-present piano" in many of the best-appointed working girls' boarding-houses is adduced as evidence of rational recreation, "at least among those who, after ten hours' steady work at the looms, need more relief from monotony than actual rest." Marx's final bite comes with Mr Oakman, the company secretary at Turner's Falls, who admits: "The time is coming that we will beat England as to prices also... we must have lower prices and shall have it the moment we get our steel at lower prices and have our labour down!" — "Reduction in wages and long working hours, this is the kernel of the 'rational and healthful process' that is to raise the workers to the dignity of rational consumers, so that they 'make a market' for the 'things showered on them' by civilization and the progress of invention."

This digression is not a mere moralising aside. It makes a definite theoretical point: the market for means of consumption II is not determined by the cultural level of the workers, but by the level of their wages, and this in turn is determined by the requirements of accumulation. The capitalist's complaint that workers do not consume enough is a complaint that the wages he pays them do not equal the value of the goods he has produced. This is a specific and graphic illustration of the contradiction between production and consumption under capitalist accumulation.

Returning to the main thread, Marx restates the general architecture: "Just as department I has to supply the additional constant capital for department II out of its surplus product, so department II supplies in the same way the extra variable capital for department I. Department II accumulates both for department I and for itself, as far as the variable capital is concerned, in as much as it reproduces a larger portion of its total production, and of its surplus product in particular, in the form of necessary means of consumption."

The condition for expanded reproduction: "With production on an increasing capital basis, I(v+s) must be equal to IIc, plus the part of the surplus product that is reincorporated as capital, plus the extra portion of constant capital needed to expand production in department II, and the minimum for this expansion is that without which genuine accumulation, i.e. the actual extension of production in department I, cannot be carried out." In other words, I(v+s) must cover: (i) the replacement of IIc; (ii) the additional constant capital that Department II must itself obtain from Department I in order to expand; and (iii) the additional means of production needed to expand Department I's own production. There is a minimum to this: the expansion of Department II's constant capital must be at least sufficient to allow Department I to expand.

Marx then returns to the case with IIc < I(v+s). For IIc = 1,430, simple reproduction in II would require the same amount from I(v+s). What is novel is the supplementary 70 IIS. "What is for department I simply the exchange of revenue for means of consumption, merely a commodity exchange oriented to consumption, is now for department II not just (as with simple reproduction) the transformation of its constant capital back from the form of commodity capital into its natural form, but rather the actual process of accumulation itself, the transformation of a part of its surplus product from the form of means of consumption into that of constant capital."

If, however, Department I uses £70 in money (its money reserve for the exchange of surplus value) to buy the 70 IIs, and Department II does not use this money to buy 70 Is but accumulates the £70 as money capital, this would be the expression "of an extra product (precisely of the surplus product of department II, of which it is an aliquot part), even if not of a product that goes back again into the production sphere; but then this money accumulation on the part of department II would be at the same time the expression of an unsaleable 70 Is in means of production. There would thus be relative over-production, corresponding to this simultaneous non-expansion of reproduction on the part of department II." Here again the dialectic: money-accumulation, if not matched by corresponding material transformation, is mere paper-accumulation. It is a signal of an unsold surplus product.

Marx then adds the "money in transition" point. During the period in which the £70 in money that came from Department I has not yet returned to Department I by Department II's purchase of 70 Is, this £70 "figures completely or in part as additional virtual money capital in the hands of department II." This holds for each transaction between the two departments until mutual replacement of commodities effects the reflux of money to its starting point. "In the normal course of events, however, the money figures only temporarily here in this role." But then comes the crucial observation about the credit system: "In the credit system, where all additional money temporarily set free can immediately function actively as additional money capital, this money capital that is only released temporarily may get stuck, and be used for new enterprises in department I, for example, whereas it ought to be used to set in motion surplus products still held down in other enterprises." This is a textbook illustration of the fictitious investment mechanism that is at the core of Marx's later theory of crisis: the credit system causes funds that are only temporarily free to masquerade as actual capital, and to be invested where they cannot be sustained. The note that "the annexation of 70 Is to the constant capital of department II also requires an expansion of 14 in department II's variable capital" closes the calculation.

The main thread then continues with the further review of the calculation. The product of 9,000 is divided for purposes of reproduction as: I. 5,000c + 500s (to be capitalized) + 1,500(v+s) consumption fund = 7,000 in commodities. II. 1,500c + 299v + 201s = 2,000 in commodities. Total = 9,000 in commodity product.

The capitalization proceeds: the 500s capitalized in Department I is divided into 5/6 = 417c + 1/6 = 83v. The 83v withdraws an equal amount from IIs, which buys elements of constant capital, added to IIc. An increase of 83 in IIc necessitates an increase of 1/5 × 83 = 17 in IIv. After the exchange: I. (5,000c + 417s)c + (1,000v + 83s)v = 5,417c + 1,083v = 6,500 II. (1,500c + 83s)c + (299v + 17s)v = 1,583c + 316v = 1,899 Total = 8,399.

Department I capital has grown from 6,000 to 6,500, i.e. by one twelfth. Department II has grown from 1,715 to 1,899, i.e. by almost one ninth. Note: Department II's rate of growth exceeds Department I's rate of growth — confirming the earlier general observation that Department II must accumulate more quickly than Department I.

Reproduction on this basis for a second year produces at end of year: I. (5,417c + 452s)c + (1,083v + 90s)v = 5,869c + 1,173v = 7,042 II. (1,583c + 42s + 90s)c + (316v + 8s + 18s)v = 1,715c + 342v = 2,057

End of third year product: I. 5,869c + 1,173v + 1,173s II. 1,715c + 342v + 342s.

Marx accumulates half of the surplus value in Department I: I(v+½s) = 1,173v + 587(½s) = 1,760, which is bigger by 45 than the total IIc. This must again be balanced with IIc by transferring an equal amount of means of production to IIc. IIc thus grows by 45, which requires an increase of 1/5 × 45 = 9 in IIv. The capitalized 587 Is then divides into 5/6 and 1/6: 489c and 98v; this 98 requires a new addition of 98 to Department II's constant capital, and this in turn an increase in Department II's variable capital of 1/5 × 98 = 20. So: I. (5,869c + 489s)c + (1,173v + 98s)v = 6,358c + 1,271v = 7,629 II. (1,715c + 45s + 98s)c + (342v + 9s + 20s)v = 1,858c + 371v = 2,229 Total capital = 9,858.

In three years of increased reproduction, therefore, total capital of Department I has grown from 6,000 to 7,629, that of Department II from 1,715 to 2,229, and the total social capital from 7,715 to 9,858. Again, the Department II rate of growth (30%) exceeds the Department I rate (27%). This repeated pattern is Marx's empirical/analytic demonstration of the structural asymmetry of expanded reproduction: the department producing means of consumption must expand relatively faster in order to accommodate the increased I(v+s) generated by the accumulation of Department I.

(c) The Exchange of IIc in the Case of Accumulation

The concluding subsection of Section III systematically catalogues the possible relations between I(v+s) and IIc under accumulation, distinguishing three cases based on the rate of accumulation. Marx assumes in the examples above a rate of accumulation in Department I of ½ (half of the surplus value) held constant across years, and varied only the proportions in which the accumulated capital was divided between c and v. This yields three examples.

Case 1: I(v+½s) = IIc, which is thus smaller than I(v+s). Marx notes parenthetically: "(In fact, it must always be smaller or else department I cannot accumulate.)" [The parenthetical note requires care. What must be smaller is I(v+½s) relative to I(v+s), since ½s < s, trivially. But the equality I(v+½s) = IIc is itself the condition under which Department I's half-accumulation exactly clears the market for IIc. If I(v+½s) were larger than IIc, Dept II could not absorb the additional means of consumption offered to it; hence the equality as a limiting case, and in fact the condition may be that I(v+½s) must be ≤ IIc for the process to proceed normally.]

Case 2: I(v+½s) is greater than IIc. "In this case, the replacement is effected by a corresponding portion of IIS being added to IIc, so that this sum = I(v+½s)." Here the exchange is not for Department II "the simple reproduction of its constant capital, but already accumulation, the increase of its constant capital by a part of its surplus product which it exchanges for means of production from department I; this increase also means that department II correspondingly enlarges its variable capital out of its own surplus product."

Case 3: I(v+½s) is smaller than IIc. "In this case, department II has not completely reproduced its constant capital by the exchange, and must therefore make up the deficiency by purchase from department I. But this does not require any further accumulation of variable capital in department II, since its constant capital is fully reproduced in magnitude by this operation. On the other hand, the section of capitalists in department I that is simply storing up additional money capital has already accomplished one part of this kind of accumulation by this exchange."

Marx now generalises and states the impossibility of stable simple reproduction under capitalism. "The precondition for simple reproduction, that I(v+s) = IIc, is incompatible with capitalist production from the start, although this does not rule out the possibility that in one year of the industrial cycle of ten to eleven years there may be a smaller total production than the preceding, i.e. that even simple reproduction fails to take place in relation to the previous year." This sentence embeds the schema in the industrial cycle: the schema's equality is the abstract equilibrium point, but real capitalism oscillates around it with periodic depressions and crises.

Second, Marx makes a demographic argument: given the natural annual growth of the population, simple reproduction would mean that "a proportionately greater number of unproductive servants had to share in the 1,500 that represents the total surplus-value. Accumulation of capital, i.e. genuine capitalist production, would be impossible in this way." This is a striking argument from the distribution of surplus value rather than from any pure value-flow condition. If all surplus value were consumed by a growing capitalist class plus its servants, there would be no accumulation; hence simple reproduction is incompatible with a growing population of surplus-value recipients.

"The existence of capitalist accumulation accordingly excludes the possibility that IIc may be equal to I(v+s)." Yet the opposite error is also a real possibility: "Yet even with capitalist accumulation, the case could arise in which, as a result of the accumulation achieved in the previous run of production periods, IIc was not only equal to I(v+s), but in fact even greater. This would mean over-production in department II, and could only be balanced out by a major crash, as a result of which capital would be transferred from department II to department I." This is the schematic version of Marx's crisis theory: over-accumulation in Department II relative to the demand from I(v+s) can only be resolved by a crisis that destroys capital and quantitatively redistributes it from II to I.

Marx then qualifies the schema in two ways. (1) The relation between I(v+s) and IIc is unaffected if a section of Department II's constant capital is reproduced there — as in agriculture with home-grown seed. "This part of IIc is then as little involved in the exchange between departments I and II as is Ic." (2) It also makes no difference if some Department II products can enter Department I as means of production; this is covered by a part of the means of production supplied by Department I, and this part must be deducted from both sides at the outset "if we want to investigate the exchange between the two great departments of social production, the producers of means of production and the producers of means of consumption, in its pure and unadulterated form."

The formal conclusion: "In the case of capitalist production, therefore, I(v+s) cannot be equal to IIc, i.e. the two cannot balance one another in the exchange." But a weaker condition can hold: if Is/X is the part of Is spent by Department I's capitalists as revenue, then I(v + S/X) "can be equal to, greater or less than IIc." The reason is that s/X < s, so I(v+S/X) < I(v+s), and the difference — the portion of Is that is capitalized — must be matched by a corresponding portion of IIs that Department II must forgo from its own consumption. The final clause reads (with the OCR garbled): "however, I(v+S/X) must always be smaller than I(v+S-), and indeed smaller by the part of IIs that the capitalist class in department II must itself under all circumstances consume." The economic meaning: the consumer fund of Department I (v plus the spent portion of its surplus value) must always leave room for the portion of IIs that Department II's own capitalist class consumes; that is, the expansion of Department I's own consumption cannot be so great as to eliminate the consumption fund of Department II's capitalists.

Marx then adds a methodological caveat about fixed capital. "We have to note here that in this presentation of accumulation, the value of the constant capital is not depicted exactly in so far as this is a portion of the value of the commodity capital in whose production it collaborates." The fixed part of the newly accumulated constant capital only goes gradually and periodically into the commodity capital, according to the differential nature of these fixed elements. The commodity capital therefore consists, in cases involving raw materials and semi-finished goods, mostly of replacements for the circulating constant components and for the variable capital. Marx then notes: "However, we were able to proceed in this manner on account of the turnover of the circulating components. The assumption was thereby made that the circulating part, together with the portion of value of the fixed capital surrendered to it, turns over within the year with such a frequency that the total sum of commodities supplied is the same as the value of the whole of the capital that goes into the annual production." But in industries where only ancillaries are involved (as in the construction of machinery), "the labour element = v must again appear as the larger component of the commodity capital." This caveat is an acknowledgment that the schemas abstract from the complications of fixed capital turnover. For the rate of profit, the surplus value is calculated on the total capital, independently of whether the fixed components surrender much or little value to the product in a given period. But for the value of any commodity capital periodically produced, the fixed part of the constant capital is only to be counted to the extent that it gives up value by its average wear and tear.

Section IV — Supplementary Remarks

The final section of the chapter closes with the promised resolution of the money-origin problem in Department II. "The original source of money for department II is the v+s of the gold production in department I, exchanged against part of IIc." This is the specific sense in which gold production is the ultimate money-source of the whole system. But: "it is only to the extent that the gold producers store up surplus-value or transform it into means of production in department I, i.e. extend their production, that their v+s does not go into department II." Conversely: "as far as accumulation of money by the gold producers themselves finally leads to expanded reproduction, a portion of the surplus-value from gold production that is not spent as revenue goes into department II for the gold producers' additional variable capital, and either requires a new hoard formation here or provides new means for buying from department I without directly selling to it again."

From the money that stems from Iv+s in gold production, "a part of the gold is deducted that is needed by certain branches of production in department II as raw material, etc., in short as a replacement element of their constant capital." (That is, some gold is consumed as industrial material and does not enter circulation as money.)

Marx then specifies the circumstances under which hoard formation occurs in the process of exchange between the two departments: "in department I, only if a part of Is is unilaterally sold to department II without a purchase from the other side, and serves here as additional constant capital for department II; in department II, if department I buys unilaterally for additional variable capital; furthermore, if a part of the surplus-value spent by department I as revenue is not covered by department II, so that a part of IIS is bought with it and thereby transformed into money."

"If I(v+s) is greater than IIc, then IIc, for its simple reproduction, does not have to replace, in commodities from department I, what I consumed from IIs."

The question then arises as to how far hoard formation can take place in the exchange among the capitalists of Department II themselves — "an exchange that can consist only of the mutual exchange of IIs." Marx answers: "We know that within department II there is only direct accumulation in so far as a part of IIS is directly transformed into variable capital (just as, in department I, a part of Is is directly transformed into constant capital)." This is the promised resolution. The partial answer is that the means of subsistence that constitute the extra portion of IIs — the portion serving as additional variable capital — are directly consumed by the workers who are hired with the additional money capital; the money capital that the hoard-forming Department II capitalists provide does not return to them because it has been paid to them as the price of commodities by workers employed with the additional capital of other Department II capitalists.

The paragraph continues: "Given the different ages of accumulation within the various lines of business in department II, and within each particular line of business for the individual capitalists involved there, the matter is explained, mutatis mutandis, just as in department I. Some of these capitalists are still at the stage of hoard formation, selling without buying, while others, at the point of actual expansion of reproduction, buy without selling."

The final sentences of the chapter: "The additional variable money capital is certainly laid out at first on additional labour-power, but this is used to buy means of subsistence from the hoard-forming proprietors of the extra means of consumption that go into the workers' consumption. In proportion to their hoard formation, this money does not return from these proprietors to its starting-point; they store it up."

Thus the chapter closes on a note of unfinished resolution — the intra-departmental hoard formation in Department II is explained, but only by the principle of successive ages of accumulation: some capitalists hoard while others accumulate, and the money of the latter flows into the hoards of the former as wages are spent on the latter's stock of means of consumption. This is the same complementarity of one-sided movements that Marx applied to Department I. In the process, however, the chapter leaves the reader with the strong impression that the analysis is subject to important further qualification in what would have followed (in Volume 3's treatment of the credit system). The "sudden interruption" of the manuscript — noted by Engels in his editorial apparatus — reflects the incompleteness of the analytic resolution.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS:

Fundamental principles revealed by the chapter.

First principle: Money is not the substance of accumulation. The hoard that appears at the level of individual capital as "potential money capital" is not an addition to social wealth. It is money withdrawn from circulation and given a new function — as reserve against future investment. The extra gold produced is the only true increment to the money supply. This demolishes the "currency-quantity" and "forced saving" views of capital formation.

Second principle: Expanded reproduction is a matter of arrangement, not of quantity. The material substratum of expanded reproduction is already produced in the course of simple reproduction: it is the surplus product of Department I cast in the use-form of means of production of means of production. What changes to bring about accumulation is not the total value of the annual product but the destination of its elements. "It is not the quantity, but the qualitative character of the given elements of simple reproduction that is changed." This is the single deepest theoretical insight of the chapter. It means that the pivot between simple and expanded reproduction is not a quantitative fulcrum but a qualitative rearrangement — one that "can never be achieved without difficulty."

Third principle: The condition of expanded reproduction is I(v+s) > IIc. This is the formal core of the reproduction schemas. In simple reproduction I(v+s) = IIc; in expanded reproduction the two cannot be equal, because Department I must retain a portion of its surplus product as additional constant capital (which cannot simultaneously be exchanged for means of consumption), and must also provide Department II with the material of its own additional constant capital. The two departments are bound by a reciprocity of materials: Department I supplies the constant capital of Department II; Department II supplies the means of subsistence for Department I's additional workers.

Fourth principle: The accumulation process is asymmetric. Department II must accumulate faster than Department I, because I(v+s), which has to be exchanged for IIc, grows more quickly than IIc unless Department II accelerates its own capital formation. This asymmetry — structurally necessary, not accidental — is a key to understanding the cyclical overshooting of consumer-goods industries, the periodic over-production of Department II, and the "major crash" by which over-production in Department II is overcome and capital is transferred from II to I.

Fifth principle: The balance is an accident. "The very complexity of the process provides many occasions for it to take an abnormal course." The equilibrium conditions of the schemas — one-sided purchases equal one-sided sales, the money refluxes, the balance is restored — are not automatic. They hold only as an "accident" on the basis of the spontaneous pattern of capitalist production. Every balancing act is the potential site of a crisis.

Sixth principle: The illusion of "accumulation at the expense of consumption" must be rejected. The chapter's most categorical statement of this point is worth repeating: "the idea that accumulation is achieved at the expense of consumption — considered in this general way — is an illusion that contradicts the essence of capitalist production, in as much as it assumes that the purpose and driving motive of this is consumption, and not the grabbing of surplus-value and its capitalization, i.e. accumulation." The numerical exercises demonstrate that accumulation raises the consumption fund of the capitalist class (1,100 → 1,477 in the first example) — the point is that consumption is subordinate to accumulation, not that accumulation sacrifices consumption.

Seventh principle: The credit system is a secondary reflection. The spontaneous processes of metallic circulation — one-sided sales, hoards, refluxes — are the primary phenomena. The credit system presents them as "consciously regulated processes," endowing the hoards with an apparent activity that is parasitic on real accumulation. The chapter's warning about temporarily free money being "stuck" in new enterprises — while surplus products are "held down" — is the germ of the theory of fictitious capital.

Broader practical, theoretical, and philosophical implications.

Theoretically, the chapter establishes that the reproduction of capital is a closed but unstable system. It is closed in the sense that its value-forms and use-forms are mutually convertible within the system — no external wealth is required for accumulation. It is unstable in the sense that the equilibrium conditions are so complex that the balance is an accident. This dual character is the basis for Marx's mature theory of crisis, and it accounts for the fact that unlike the Grundrisse and Volume 3's discussion of the "general possibility of crisis," this chapter identifies the specific mechanism of breakdown: the multiplication of one-sided metamorphoses, each with its own timing, its own reflux conditions, and its own vulnerability to being short-circuited.

Historically, the chapter became one of the most contested sites of twentieth-century Marxist theory. The reproduction schemas were pressed into service by Rosa Luxemburg (to argue that capitalism requires external non-capitalist markets for its realisation), by Tugan-Baranovsky (to argue the opposite — that capitalism can achieve balanced growth indefinitely, and that crisis is not systemic), by Otto Bauer (to model imperialist expansion), by Lenin (to refute both Narodnik underconsumptionism and the "third-person" theories of realisation), and latterly by Ernest Mandel, David Harvey, and Roman Rosdolsky. The 70 IIs problem and the I(v+s) > IIc condition became central points in the debate over whether the schemas are equilibrium models or crisis-generating models.

Practically, the chapter provides the analytic foundations for understanding: (1) the role of credit in mobilising idle hoards; (2) the structural sources of cyclical crisis in the mismatch between Department I's investment and Department II's capacity to absorb means of consumption; (3) the political economy of workers' consumption, and the impossibility of "making a market" out of cultural uplift while wages are held down; (4) the difference between genuine capital accumulation and money-capital inflation; (5) the structural dependency of Department II on Department I's rate of accumulation.

Philosophically, the chapter vindicates the dialectical method. Simple reproduction and expanded reproduction are not two separate states but moments of one process — expanded reproduction contains within it simple reproduction as its material substratum. Similarly, Department I and Department II are not two independent systems but two poles of a single process of mutual material provisioning. The "difficulty" one encounters in Department II (the 500 IIc problem) is not a refutation of the schema but the visible form of its condition. The chapter thus exhibits the dialectical logic of Marx's Capital at its most rigorous: the form of the argument — the internal self-differentiation of a single unity into distinct moments that must nevertheless be reconciled — is not a stylistic flourish but the actual structure of the object being analysed.

Finally, the chapter carries a warning for our own moment. The argument that money capital can be created out of nothing but a rearrangement of existing value; that credit systems turn temporarily free money into a potency that may turn out to be fictitious; that the whole process is subject to "an abnormal course" because the balancing acts require an "accident"; and that accumulation's true purpose is not consumption but the self-valorisation of value — all this reads with the uncomfortable freshness of a diagnosis rather than a historical relic. Marx's Chapter 21 does not merely model nineteenth-century capitalism; it specifies the necessary structure of any capitalism that reproduces itself on an expanding scale. Its account of why the world economy periodically overshoots, gets stuck, and crashes — because accumulation generates its own disequilibria along with its own growth — remains one of the most powerful analytic instruments we possess.
