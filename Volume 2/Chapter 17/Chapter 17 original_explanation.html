Volume 2 • Chapter 17: The Circulation of Surplus-Value

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem and Object of Chapter 17

Chapter 17 of Capital, Volume II — "The Circulation of Surplus-Value" — is, at first glance, a technical appendix to the preceding chapter on the turnover of variable capital. In reality it is one of the most philosophically pregnant and theoretically innovative chapters in the entire three volumes, because it pivots the analysis of capital away from the production of surplus-value and toward the monetary machinery by which surplus-value is realized, spent, hoarded, and re-capitalized. Having established in Chapter 16 that a variation in the turnover period produces a variation in the annual rate of surplus-value even when the mass of surplus-value produced annually is unchanged, Marx opens Chapter 17 by announcing that there is a further necessary variation — this time in the capitalization of surplus-value (in accumulation) and consequently in the mass of surplus-value produced during the year even when the rate of surplus-value remains the same. The chapter therefore has a double object: first, to demonstrate how the turnover period also modifies accumulation behaviour, and second, to resolve the far more difficult question of money — where the money comes from that converts surplus-value into money.

The chapter is thus situated at a strategic intersection. On one side, it completes the "turnover" half of Volume II (Parts I–II and the first three chapters of Part III). On the other, it prepares the ground for the reproduction schemes of Chapters 18–20, since the question "where does the money come from to realize surplus-value?" is the monetary counterpart of the question "how do the departments of social production balance?" It also arguably prepares for Volume III's theory of credit, since the chapter's climactic section deals with latent money capital, bank deposits, government papers, and shares.

The Twin Theses of the Chapter

Thesis one: the general problem of the money needed to realize surplus-value is a false special problem. The capitalist class appears to be the unique starting point of money circulation, so it seems there must be a special capitalist money that converts surplus-value into money. Marx dissolves this appearance. Building on the laws of simple commodity circulation developed in Volume I, Chapter 3, he demonstrates that the quantity of money required to circulate a mass of commodities is independent of whether that value contains surplus-value, of whether production is capitalist, and of how the value is distributed between wages and profit. The general answer to the question "where does the money come from?" is that the capitalist class itself casts the money into circulation that realizes its own surplus-value — but not as capital, only as means of purchase for individual consumption (i.e. as revenue). The one genuine exception — the exception that makes the whole system physically possible — is the special case of gold and silver production, where surplus-value emerges already in the money form and is cast into circulation from production itself, not from exchange.

Thesis two: accumulation is not a simple, unilinear process of converting surplus-value into productive capital; it also requires the formation of latent money capital (hoards), and this hoarding is inherently partial, temporary, and institutionally mediated by credit. There is no general, all-at-once hoarding by the capitalist class as a whole; hoarding at one pole is always mirrored by real expansion of production or real consumption at another pole. The latent money capital of the individual capitalist is not stored coin at all, but bank deposits, government securities, and shares — i.e. legal claims on the social product. And here Marx draws on William Thompson's 1824 treatise to make a startling claim: the "accumulated wealth" that strikes the eye is, in its greater part, nominal — mere demands on the future annual productive powers of society.

The Intellectual Objectives

The chapter pursues four interlocking objectives:

  1. To extend the theory of turnover into the theory of accumulation by showing that the turnover period determines not just the annual rate of surplus-value but the manner and rhythm in which surplus-value can be capitalized — including the crucial distinction between a repair-and-maintenance fund created out of realized surplus-value (case A) and a repair-and-maintenance fund that must be originally advanced (case B).

  2. To resolve the monetary paradox of capitalist circulation posed by an opponent of Thomas Tooke (the "where does the money come from?" question), and to do so by using, but deeply transforming, the simple circulation laws of Volume I.

  3. To demolish the "wages-cause-prices" fallacy, the capitalist apologetic that any wage increase must be passed on in higher prices. Marx identifies three real facts that are used as pretexts for this fallacy and shows that none of them actually licenses the inference.

  4. To theorize latent money capital and the credit system's material basis, thus laying groundwork for the later analysis of interest-bearing capital in Volume III.

Contextual Placement

Within the architecture of Volume II, Chapter 17 is the monetary keystone. Parts I and II analyzed the circuit and the turnover of individual capital; Chapter 16 integrated turnover with the annual rate of surplus-value. Chapter 17 now asks: what is the social and monetary meaning of the surplus-value whose production and timing we have already established? The answer requires the analysis of the total social money stock, hoard formation, gold production, and the credit system.

Within Marx's broader project, the chapter resolves a contradiction left unresolved in Volume I — namely, how the capitalist "always withdraws more money from circulation than he casts into it" (the M–C–M' paradox). Marx's answer is that this paradox cannot be resolved by looking at the formation of surplus-value (which is presupposed) but only by looking at the circulation of money, where the total commodity value, surplus-value included, is circulated by a quantity of money that the capitalist class originally injected — partly in the form of a consumption fund that returns as the money form of surplus-value.

The chapter is also a sustained polemic. It engages Thomas Tooke, the Physiocrats (Quesnay and Turgot), Adam Smith, William Thompson, and "bourgeois economics" and "economic sycophants" in general. Each engagement serves to mark off Marx's position from both the currency school and the apologetic defenders of profit.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

Beat 1 — Opening: From Turnover to Capitalization (the A/B Contrast and the Maintenance Fund)

Marx opens by recalling the central result of Chapter 16: a variation in the turnover period produces a variation in the annual rate of surplus-value even with the same annual mass of surplus-value. He then announces the chapter's new movement: there is a further necessary variation, this time in the capitalization of surplus-value — in accumulation — and thus also in the mass of surplus-value produced during the year even if the rate of surplus-value remains the same. This sets the conceptual agenda: turnover affects not only how much surplus-value is realized per year but also how much can be turned back into capital. The chapter's true subject is therefore the rhythm of accumulation as mediated by the rhythm of monetary realization.

The opening contrast is between two capitalists, A and B (carried over from the preceding chapter's example). Capitalist A has a steady periodic revenue and, except for the initial turnover period, meets his own consumption out of his own surplus-value production; he does not need to advance anything from his original funds for this. Capitalist B produces the same amount of surplus-value in the same time as A, but the surplus-value is not realized in B's case and therefore can be consumed neither individually nor productively. So far as individual consumption is concerned, B must anticipate surplus-value; funds for this must be advanced. Here Marx introduces the key terminological distinction that will structure the entire chapter: the distinction between realized surplus-value (surplus-value that has returned in the money form and can therefore function as revenue or as latent capital) and produced but unrealized surplus-value (surplus-value that exists only in the commodity form and is therefore unavailable for either consumption or accumulation). Turnover, in other words, is not just a statistic of timing; it determines whether a capitalist has money at his disposal now or must borrow, anticipate, or wait.

From this abstract contrast, Marx extracts an important but easily overlooked phenomenon: a part of the productive capital that cannot be easily categorized — the extra capital needed for the repair and maintenance of fixed capital — now presents itself in a new light. The footnote refers back to Volume II, p. 255, where this topic was developed. In case A, this portion of capital is either not advanced at the start of production or is advanced only to a small extent; it does not need to be available, let alone actually present. It arises from the business itself, by the direct transformation of surplus-value into capital, i.e. by its direct application as capital. In other words, a portion of the surplus-value that is not only produced periodically in the course of the year, but also realized, can cover the expenses necessary for repairs. Therefore a part of the capital needed to conduct the business on its original scale is produced by the business itself, in the course of business, through the capitalization of a part of the surplus-value. This is impossible for capitalist B. In B's case, the portion of capital in question must form part of the capital originally advanced.

The deeper point here is that in both cases the amount appears on the capitalist's books as "capital advanced," and indeed it is productive capital necessary for the business at the given scale. But it makes a great difference whose funds it is advanced out of. In case B, it is an actual part of the capital originally advanced or kept available. In case A, it is a portion of surplus-value applied as capital. Consequently, Marx derives a striking result: "not only the capital accumulated, but also a part of the capital originally advanced, can be simply capitalized surplus-value." This is a significant theoretical claim — it means the line between "original capital" and "accumulated capital" is porous in the case of a capitalist whose turnover is rapid enough to generate repair funds out of current surplus-value. The appearance that the capitalist began with a fully formed capital stock is partly illusory; the business manufactures part of its own capital out of its own surplus.

Beat 2 — Credit Complicates the Relation: A, Banker C, and Capitalists D, E, F

Marx immediately deepens the analysis by introducing the credit system. Once credit develops, the relation between capital originally advanced and capitalized surplus-value becomes still more intricate. In his example, capitalist A may borrow part of the productive capital with which he begins or carries on business from banker C. At the start, therefore, A lacks sufficient capital of his own to conduct the business. Banker C lends him a sum that simply consists of surplus-value deposited with him by industrialists D, E, F, etc. From A's standpoint, this sum is still not accumulated capital. But in point of fact, for D, E, F, etc., A is no more than an agent who capitalizes the surplus-value that they have appropriated.

This is a crucial conceptual move. It shows that credit is not an exogenous force that adds to capital; it is a mechanism by which one capitalist's hoarded surplus-value is transferred to another capitalist for productive use. The capitalist who borrows appears, from his own standpoint, to be starting with capital; but the social content of the loan is a reallocation of already-produced surplus-value. The identity of the borrower as "capitalist" is thus partly an article of credit. This prepares the way for the chapter's later, more systematic discussion of latent money capital and bank deposits, in which the same insight is generalized: what appears as stored-up money capital is, on inspection, the surplus-value of other capitalists circulating through financial intermediaries.

Marx then links back to Volume I, Chapter 24, where "the real content of accumulation" was defined as the transformation of surplus-value into capital, i.e. reproduction on an expanded scale, whether this expansion expresses itself extensively (addition of new factories) or intensively (enlargement of the former scale of operations). This backward reference matters because it fixes the meaning of "accumulation" for the rest of the chapter: accumulation is not mere hoarding of money but actual expansion of productive capital. The money-form of accumulation is secondary and transient; the real content is expanded reproduction.

Marx then enumerates three ways in which expansion can proceed in relatively small doses:

  1. Improvements that either simply raise the productive power of the labour applied, or allow labour to be more intensively exploited simultaneously — i.e. technical/organizational accumulation.
  2. Additional outlay of circulating capital (production materials and wages) when the working day is not restricted by law; this expands production without any increase in fixed capital, because the fixed capital's time of use is prolonged and its turnover period correspondingly shortened.
  3. Speculation in raw materials under favourable market conjunctures — operations for which the capital originally advanced would have been insufficient.

The nuance here is that accumulation does not require a new factory; it can be squeezed out of the existing plant through intensification, extension of working time, and speculative manoeuvring with inputs. This is itself an important theoretical insight: the turnover period can be shortened without new fixed capital investment, which is precisely why turnover analysis is bound up with accumulation analysis.

But Marx immediately introduces a countervailing limit. Where a relatively large number of turnover periods brings about more frequent realization of surplus-value, periods nevertheless occur in which the working day cannot be extended and individual improvements cannot be brought about. Meanwhile, extension of the whole business on a proportional scale — expanding the entire plant, buildings, agricultural labour fund, etc. — is possible only within certain limits and requires a volume of additional capital that can only be supplied by several years' accumulation of surplus-value. Thus the chapter sets up a distinction between small-dose, high-frequency accumulation (which turnover reinforces) and lumpy, large-scale accumulation (which turnover cannot replace, because it requires the aggregation of surplus-value over years).

Beat 3 — Real Accumulation vs. Latent Money Capital; the Money Market

From the previous distinction Marx derives the second fundamental structural category of the chapter: latent money capital. Besides real accumulation (the transformation of surplus-value into productive capital, and correspondingly reproduction on an expanded scale), there is also the accumulation of money, the scraping together of a part of the surplus-value as latent money capital, which is only to function as additional active capital later on, when it has attained a certain volume.

The term "latent money capital" is crucial and needs careful definition. It denotes surplus-value that has been realized in money but is not yet applied to production. It is capital because it is destined to become productive capital; it is latent because it is not yet functioning as such. It is the intermediate state between realized surplus-value and expanded reproduction. Its existence is a structural necessity of capitalist production, not an accident, because large-scale expansion requires the accumulation of a critical mass. This is the first of the chapter's several decisive distinctions: surplus-value as revenue (consumed individually) vs. surplus-value as latent money capital (awaiting conversion into productive capital) vs. surplus-value as productive capital (actually functioning in production).

Marx then notes that this is how the matter appears from the standpoint of the individual capitalist. With the development of capitalist production, however, the credit system develops simultaneously. The money capital the capitalist cannot yet apply in his own business is employed by others, from whom he receives interest. It functions for him as money capital in the specific sense — a kind of capital distinct from productive capital. But it is in someone else's hands that it actually operates as capital. Here the term "money capital in the specific sense" is defined: capital that earns interest precisely by being loaned out rather than being directly invested in production. With more frequent realization of surplus-value and rising scale of production, a growing proportion of new money capital, or money as capital, is placed on the money market, and at least a large part of it is again absorbed from the money market for the expansion of production.

The "money market" therefore enters the analysis as the institutional locus where latent money capital is pooled, priced (via interest), and reallocated. This is the origin, within Volume II, of what Volume III will call the credit system. The vocabulary here is instructive: Marx speaks of money "placed on" the money market and "absorbed from" it, i.e. treated as a supply (latent capital seeking employment) and demand (productive capital seeking capital). The seed of the supply-and-demand-of-loanable-funds analysis is planted, though Marx does not yet develop it.

Beat 4 — Hoard, Claims, and Thompson's Nominal Wealth

Marx now reduces latent money capital to its simplest form: the hoard. The hoard may consist of additional gold or silver received directly or indirectly in exchange with the metal-producing countries — this is the only way the money hoard within a country grows in absolute terms. Alternatively (and this is the majority case), the hoard is nothing more than money withdrawn from domestic circulation and hoarded by individual capitalists. It may also consist of value tokens (credit money being left out of account here) or of mere claims (titles) of the capitalist on third parties established by legal documents.

In all these cases, whatever the form of existence of the extra money capital, in so far as it is prospective capital it represents no more than extra legal titles to the future additional production of society that the capitalists hold in reserve. This sentence is the analytical hinge for the William Thompson quotation that follows. Marx has translated the question of latent money capital into a question about claims: what is stored up is not primarily physical coin but legal entitlements to future production. This is a fundamentally important claim about the nature of wealth in a capitalist economy, and it is expressly attributed by Marx to a pre-Marxian socialist critic of capitalism.

The long block quotation from William Thompson's An Inquiry into the Principles of the Distribution of Wealth (London, 1850; originally 1824) drives the point home in polemical terms. Thompson argues:

  • The mass of real accumulated wealth is "utterly insignificant" compared with the powers of production of the same society, or even with the actual consumption of a few years. Legislators and political economists should therefore direct attention to productive powers and their future free development, not to the "mere accumulated wealth that strikes the eye."
  • "Of what is called accumulated wealth, by far the greater part is only nominal, consisting not of any real things, ships, houses, cottons, improvements on land, but of mere demands on the future annual productive powers of society, engendered and perpetuated by the expedients or institutions of insecurity." This is the crown jewel of Thompson's argument as Marx appropriates it. The "nominal" character of wealth means that most wealth is a legal claim, not a thing. The only thing that makes such claims valuable is the ongoing productive power of society. Hence "the use of such articles ... as a mere means of appropriating to their possessors the wealth to be created by the future productive powers of society" is the mechanism by which the minority appropriates the future.
  • Thompson then illustrates with numerical estimates: annual consumption of Britain and Ireland (20 million people at ~£20 each) is about £400 million; the whole accumulated capital is estimated at no more than £1,200 million, or three times the year's labour — sixty pounds per head. The interest on this capital stock would support the whole population in comfort for about two months; the whole accumulated capital itself would maintain them in idleness for three years, after which, without houses, clothes or food, they must starve or become slaves. So the ratio of accumulated capital to productive powers is about three years to forty years — i.e. a mere moment compared to a generation's productive capacity.
  • And crucially: "About one third part of the annual products of the labour of these countries is now abstracted from the producers, under the name of public burdens, and unproductively consumed by those who give no equivalent." So a full third of annual output is appropriated by the state (taxes) and consumed unproductively.
  • Thompson's dialectic of visibility: annual consumption is like "the eternal and incalculable waves of a mighty river [that] roll on and are lost in the forgotten ocean of consumption" — the eye is struck by the durable monuments (furniture, machinery, buildings) but not by the recurring annual product on which the whole human race depends for existence. Hence the vulgar eye is misled.
  • Thompson's framing of the intellectual-political contrast: "Actual accumulations and distributions have been always considered ... in reference, and subordinate, to the power of producing. In almost all other systems, the power of producing has been considered in reference, and subordinate, to actual accumulations, and to the perpetuating of the existing modes of distribution." This is a direct critique of the apologetic political economy that Marx has been attacking throughout the chapter.
  • The polemical conclusion: "To perpetuate the results of force, fraud, and chance, has been called security; and to the support of this spurious security, have all the productive powers of the human race been unrelentingly sacrificed."

Marx's use of this quotation is strategic. On the surface it seems only to illustrate a rhetorical point about nominal wealth, but it actually accomplishes three analytical tasks. First, it historicizes the legal claims that make up latent money capital. Second, it establishes that the total social wealth is overwhelmingly a flow (annual production) rather than a stock (accumulation), which is the theoretical premise of the entire reproduction-schemes analysis that follows in Volume II. Third, it voices a proto-socialist critique that Marx incorporates into his own analysis, marking the chapter's anti-apologetic stance.

Beat 5 — The Two Normal Cases: Simple Reproduction vs. Accumulation

At this point Marx lays out the analytical framework for the rest of the chapter. As far as reproduction is concerned, only two normal cases are possible, leaving aside disturbances which inhibit reproduction even on the existing scale:

  1. Simple reproduction — the surplus-value periodically produced and realized (annually or by several turnovers within the year) is consumed individually, i.e. unproductively, by its owners, the capitalists.
  2. Capitalization of surplus-value — i.e. accumulation.

The chapter now proceeds through these two cases in order: Section I on simple reproduction (the bulk of the chapter, most of pp. 6–25), and Section II on accumulation and expanded reproduction (pp. 25–31).

Section I: Simple Reproduction — setup. In simple reproduction, the surplus-value is consumed unproductively. But the fact that product value consists partly of surplus-value and partly of the reproduced variable capital and consumed constant capital does not change in the least either the volume or the value of the total product which enters circulation as commodity capital and is similarly withdrawn to go into productive or individual consumption. Leaving aside constant capital, only the distribution of the annual product between workers and capitalists is affected.

Marx then sets out the methodological reduction: to simplify, we take the problem in its simplest form — exclusive circulation of metallic money, money that is a real equivalent. This methodological move matters: by ruling out credit money and bank money for the moment, Marx can give a rigorous first-pass answer. Credit complications are re-introduced later in the chapter (and in Volume III).

Then Marx restates the laws of simple commodity circulation from Volume I, Ch. 3: - The mass of metallic money in a country must be sufficient not only to circulate commodities but to cope with fluctuations. - These fluctuations arise from (a) fluctuations in the speed of circulation, (b) changes in commodity prices, and (c) the changing proportions in which money functions as means of payment vs. means of circulation proper. - The ratio in which the existing money mass is divided into hoard and money in circulation constantly changes, but the total mass is always equal to hoard + circulating money. - This quantity of precious metal is a social hoard, accumulated bit by bit. - Since a portion is worn away in circulation, it must be replaced each year — which happens through direct or indirect exchange of a part of the annual product with the product of the gold- and silver-producing countries.

Marx then performs another methodological simplification: since the international character of this transaction conceals its simple course, he assumes gold and silver production takes place within the country itself, so that money-metal production is a part of total social production. This is one of the most important simplifying assumptions in the chapter, and it will be relaxed later (pp. 17–19, where Marx notes that the situation is unchanged if gold production is transferred abroad).

Marx then establishes the minimum annual production of precious metals: ignoring gold/silver produced for luxury articles, it must equal the wear and tear of the money metals occasioned by annual monetary circulation. If the value of the commodities in circulation grows, the annual production of gold and silver must also grow, unless compensated by greater velocity of circulation or more comprehensive use of money as means of payment (i.e. more mutual settlement of purchases and sales without actual money). Consequently, a part of social labour-power and a part of social means of production must be spent each year in the production of gold and silver.

This is a crucial structural point: gold production is a real burden on social production, a "faux frais" (overhead cost) of the commodity-circulation mode of production — a point Marx will develop later (p. 27).

Beat 6 — The Gold Producer's Circuit: M–C...P...M' Analyzed in Detail

Marx now analyzes the specific circuit of capital invested in precious-metal production, written M–C...P...M'. This notation must be unpacked:

  • M = money capital advanced (the purchase of labour-power and means of production).
  • C = commodity capital purchased — i.e. labour-power and means of production (including fixed capital).
  • ...P... = the production process.
  • M' = money product — but here, importantly, M' is the natural form of the product itself. Unlike other branches, where the product emerges as a commodity that must be sold for money, the gold producer's product is money from the start.

In so far as the C in M–C does not consist only of labour-power and means of production, but also of fixed capital, only a part of whose value is used up in P, it is evident that M' – the product – is a sum of money equal to the variable capital laid out on wages, plus the circulating constant capital laid out on means of production, plus the portion of value of the fixed capital used up, plus the surplus-value. If the sum were smaller, with the general value of gold unchanged, then the mines in question would be unprofitable, or — if this is generally the case — the value of gold would in future rise compared with commodities whose value was unchanged; i.e. commodity prices would fall, so that the sum of money laid out in M–C would in future be less. This is a subtle argument about the flexibility of the value of gold as a standard: an unprofitable gold industry, if general, cheapens commodities by raising the value of money.

Marx then considers the circulating part of the capital advanced in M. A certain sum of money is advanced and cast into circulation in payment for labour-power and to purchase materials of production. In ordinary commodity production, this money would be withdrawn again from circulation by the sale of the product. But here the money is not withdrawn again from circulation by the circuit of this capital. The product, in its natural form, is already money. It moves from the production process into the circulation sphere not as commodity capital that has to be transformed back into money capital, but rather as money capital that has to be transformed back into productive capital — i.e. has to buy new labour-power and materials of production. The money form of the circulating capital is replaced not by the sale of the product, but by the natural form of the product itself — not by withdrawing its value again from circulation in the money form, but rather by adding money newly produced.

Marx then gives a concrete numerical example. Let the circulating capital be £500, the turnover period five weeks (a four-week working period, one-week circulation period). Right from the start, money must be advanced for five weeks in a production stock and held to pay wages bit by bit. At the beginning of the sixth week, £400 has returned and £100 has been set free. This is continually repeated. Here, as before, £100 always exists in the released form for a certain section of the turnover — but this consists of additional money newly produced, just like the other £400. There are ten turnovers in the year, and the annual product is £5,000 in gold. Note carefully: the circulation period here does not arise from the time taken to transform commodities into money, but rather that taken to transform money into elements of production. This is one of the distinctive features of gold production: its "circulation" is not a sale of product for money but a purchase of inputs with money.

For any other capital of £500 turning over under the same conditions, the constantly renewed money form is the changed form of the commodity capital produced (cast into circulation every four weeks, receiving its money form afresh by its sale — i.e. by the periodic withdrawal of the sum of money in the shape of which it originally entered the process). Here, on the contrary, in every turnover period a new additional sum of £500 in money is cast into circulation by the production process, so as to keep withdrawing materials of production and labour-power from circulation. The money thus cast into the circulation sphere is not withdrawn from it again by the circuit of this capital, but rather by the increased quantity of new gold that is constantly produced. This is the fundamental structural difference between gold-producing capital and all other capitals: gold-producing capital is a net source of money into circulation, whereas all other capitals are net withdrawers of money from circulation (insofar as they have to sell their product before they can buy inputs).

Marx then analyzes the variable part of this circulating capital. Taking it to be £100: in ordinary commodity production, £100 would be sufficient to pay labour-power through a ten-fold turnover. Here, in money production, the same sum is sufficient; but the five-weekly reflux of £100 with which labour-power is paid is not the changed form of its product, but rather a part of its ever-new product itself. The gold producer pays his workers directly with a part of the gold they have themselves produced. Consequently, the £1,000 laid out each year on labour-power and thrown into circulation by the workers does not return via circulation to its starting-point. This is the seed of a later point: the capitalist may appear to "advance" wages, but in gold production it is transparent that the workers are being paid out of their own product.

For fixed capital: the initial establishment of the business requires the expenditure of a relatively large money capital, cast into circulation. Like all fixed capital, this returns only bit by bit over a number of years. But it flows back as a direct fragment of the product, the gold, not by the sale of the product and its conversion into money. Thus it does not receive its money form by a withdrawal of money from circulation, but rather by the accumulation of a corresponding part of the product. The money capital thus re-established is not a sum of money gradually withdrawn from circulation to balance the sum originally cast in for fixed capital. It is an additional quantity of money. This is a decisive point: fixed capital in gold production is reproduced in the money form directly, from the physical product, without any prior transaction.

For surplus-value: this is also equal to a part of the new gold product, cast into circulation in each turnover period, to be spent unproductively, and paid out for means of subsistence and luxury articles.

Marx then makes the crucial aggregate assertion: this entire annual gold production ... only replaces the money worn out during the year, and thus simply keeps intact the social money stock, which always exists in the two forms of hoard and money in circulation, though in varying proportions. So, on the assumption that gold production just covers wear and tear, the net money stock is unchanged; what has happened is that money has been cast in by gold producers and cast out (in equivalent commodity value) by everyone else. The aggregate social money stock is stable; only its division between hoard and circulating money shifts.

This sentence prepares the general answer to the Tooke puzzle: the gold producers cast pure money into circulation without having to first sell a commodity; the rest of the capitalist class casts a commodity value containing surplus-value into circulation without the money to realize it. The two flows are complementary. This is the answer in germ.

Beat 7 — The General Commodity-Circulation Answer to the "Where Does the Money Come From?" Question

Marx restates the general law. According to the law of commodity circulation, the total quantity of money must equal the quantity of money required for circulation plus a sum in hoard form which increases or decreases according to the contraction or expansion of circulation, and serves especially for the formation of the reserve fund of means of payment. What has to be paid in money — insofar as there is no direct balancing of accounts — is the value of the commodities. The fact that part of this value consists of surplus-value — i.e. has cost the seller of the commodity nothing — does not change this situation in any way.

Marx then performs a thought experiment to drive this home: imagine all producers possessed their means of production independently, and there were circulation between the direct producers themselves. Ignoring the constant part of their capital, we could divide their annual surplus product, by analogy with capitalism, into:

  • Part (a): replacement of necessary means of subsistence (analogue of variable capital).
  • Part (b): luxury consumption and application to expansion of production (analogue of surplus-value).

But this division would have no effect on the quantity of money required to circulate their total product. With circumstances otherwise the same, the value of the mass of commodities in circulation would be the same, and so would the quantity of money required by it. They would need the same money reserves as before, given a similar division of the turnover period — i.e. the same part of their capital would always have to be in the money form. Thus the circumstance that a part of the commodity value consists of surplus-value does not alter in the least the quantity of money needed to carry on the business.

This is the general answer: the question "where does the money come from to realize surplus-value?" presupposes that there is something special about realizing surplus-value, but there isn't — realization is simply sale, and sale is governed by the quantity of money required for the circulation of a given commodity value, irrespective of whether that value contains surplus-value. The problem dissolves.

Marx then brings in the Tooke polemic explicitly. An opponent of Tooke, who supports the form M–C–M', asked him how the capitalist always manages to withdraw more money from circulation than he cast into it. Marx points out that the question is not about the formation of surplus-value (which is "the only real secret," presupposed by the capitalists), but about the money into which surplus-value is turned. The question is not: where does surplus-value come from? But rather: where does the money come from which it is turned into?

Marx notes that bourgeois economics takes the existence of surplus-value to be given, and takes it as given at the same time that a part of the mass of commodities cast into circulation consists of surplus product — a value the capitalist did not cast into circulation with his capital. So the capitalist casts into circulation an excess over and above his capital, and withdraws this excess from it again. This is, as it happens, true — but the trick is to see where the money came from.

The commodity capital the capitalist casts into circulation is of greater value than the productive capital he has withdrawn in labour-power and means of production — a fact that, from the capitalist's standpoint, is not explained or understood, but is a fact for all that. On this assumption, it is clear why not only capitalist A but also B, C, D, etc. can always withdraw more value from circulation (by exchanging their commodities) than the value of their original capital. They always cast a greater commodity value into circulation in the form of commodity capital than they withdraw in the form of productive capital. They always have a value sum to share equal to the value of the productive capitals they advanced, plus a value sum which they cast into circulation as surplus-of-commodity-value over and above the value of their commodity's elements of production.

But — and this is the crux — before the commodity capital is transformed back into productive capital and the surplus-value contained in it is spent, it must be turned into money. Where does the money for this come from? This question appears difficult at first glance, and "neither Tooke nor anyone else has yet answered it." So Marx has defined a specific unresolved problem within the general circulation laws.

Beat 8 — The "£500 Becomes £600" Puzzle

Marx sharpens the paradox with a specific aggregate example. Assume that the circulating capital of £500 advanced in money form, whatever its turnover period, is the total circulating capital of society, i.e. of the capitalist class. The surplus-value is £100. How then can the entire capitalist class continue extracting £600 from the circulation sphere, if it only ever puts £500 into it?

The mechanics: once the £500 money capital is transformed into productive capital, this is transformed within the production process into a commodity value of £600. Now there exists in circulation not only a commodity value of £500 (equal to the money capital originally advanced) but also a newly produced surplus-value of £100. This extra surplus-value of £100 is cast into circulation in the commodity form. There is no doubt about that. But the extra money needed for the circulation of this additional commodity value is not provided by the same operation.

The paradox is now stated with full force: the additional commodity value is real, but the additional money to circulate it is not automatically supplied by the sale of the commodity — because sales only succeed one another; they do not spontaneously generate additional money.

Beat 9 — Marx Refutes Four "Subterfuges"

Marx now explicitly rules out four plausible ways of "solving" the paradox, each of which he shows is merely a redescription of the problem:

Subterfuge 1 — The circulating constant capital is not all laid out at once. On this view, while A sells his commodities and his capital assumes the money form, buyer B's capital, present in money form, is assuming the form of means of production — and it is A who produces these. By the same act through which A gives back its money form to the commodity capital he has produced, B gives his capital back its productive form. The same sum of money functions in the two-way process, just as in every simple sale. And when A transforms money back into means of production, he buys from C, who pays B, etc.

Marx's rebuttal: "However, none of the laws put forward with respect to the quantity of money circulating for the purpose of commodity circulation are in any way altered by the capitalist character of the production process." So when it is said that the circulating capital advanced by society in the money form amounts to £500, it has already been taken into account that this is not only the sum advanced at the same time, but a sum that also sets more productive capital than £500 in motion, since it serves alternately as the money fund for different productive capitals. This mode of explanation already presupposes that the money exists, whereas it is precisely its existence that is to be explained. In other words, the circulation of a single coin through several hands presupposes that the coin exists; it does not explain how the additional value of £600 is realized. The subterfuge confuses the circulation of a given money stock with the creation of the money needed to circulate an increased commodity value.

Subterfuge 2 — A produces articles B consumes; B's money turns A's commodity capital into money. Marx: this presupposes the solution even more directly. Where does B get this money to meet his revenue? How did he himself manage to convert into money this part of his product's surplus-value? Here the objection is circularity: B's money is itself the realization of B's own surplus-value, so the question is merely relocated.

Subterfuge 3 — The variable capital A advances to his workers flows back from circulation; between outlay and reflux, the money paid in wages can serve to convert surplus-value into money. Marx grants the timing point but notes: first, the greater the interval, the greater the money reserve A must constantly retain. Second, if the workers pay the money out and buy commodities, the surplus-value contained in these commodities is proportionately converted into money. Thus the same money advanced as variable capital also serves to convert surplus-value into money. But — crucial twist — without going any deeper into the question here, it is at least clear that the consumption of the entire capitalist class and the unproductive persons dependent on it keeps even pace with that of the working class; thus, on top of the money cast into circulation by the workers, money must be cast into circulation by the capitalists, if they are to spend their surplus-value as revenue; and so money for this must be withdrawn from circulation. Therefore this explanation only reduces the quantity needed, and does not obviate the need. In other words, even granting all the timing efficiencies, the additional money must come from somewhere — either freshly produced or withdrawn from hoards.

Subterfuge 4 — The initial investment of fixed capital casts a large sum into circulation, withdrawn only gradually, and this may suffice to convert surplus-value. Marx: the sum of £500 (including hoard formation) may already include the investment as fixed capital, if not by the person who cast it in, then by someone else. Besides, it is already presupposed that the surplus-value in the commodities serving as fixed capital is paid for — and the question precisely arises: where does this money come from?

Beat 10 — The General Answer: Money Quantity is Independent of Surplus-Value

Marx now states the resolution. The general answer has already been given: if a mass of commodities of x times £1,000 is to circulate, it in no way affects the quantity of money needed for this circulation whether the value of this commodity mass contains surplus-value or not, or whether the mass of commodities is produced under capitalist conditions or not. Thus the problem itself does not exist. With conditions otherwise given (velocity of circulation, etc.), a definite sum of money is required to circulate the commodity value of x times £1,000, quite irrespective of how much or how little of this value accrues to the direct producers. In as much as a problem does exist here, it coincides with the general problem: where does the sum of money needed in a country for the circulation of commodities come from?

So the general answer to the paradox is: it does not exist. The question is a false special problem.

Beat 11 — The Semblance of a Special Capitalist Problem and the "Revenue" Solution

But Marx immediately acknowledges — however, there does exist, from the standpoint of capitalist production, the semblance of a special problem. Here, the capitalist, the man who casts the money into circulation, appears as the point of departure.

The two starting-points of money circulation are: - The capitalist — who initially casts money into circulation for constant and variable capital. - The worker — but the worker's money is already the second circulation of the variable capital originally cast in by the capitalist. - All third parties either receive money from these two classes for services, or — insofar as they receive money without services in return — are co-proprietors of surplus-value in the form of rent, interest, etc.

Marx notes that the division of surplus-value among industrial capitalist, landlord, financier, etc., has nothing to do with the question at issue. What was asked is how the capitalist converts his surplus-value into money, not how the money obtained for it is then divided up. So Marx can, for the present, consider the capitalist as the sole owner of surplus-value. Workers are only a secondary point of departure; the capitalist is primary — because the money paid as wages was already advanced by the capitalist as variable capital. The money the worker spends is performing its second circulation when he spends it.

Hence: the capitalist class remains the sole starting-point of the money circulation. If it needs £400 for means of production and £100 for labour-power, it casts £500 into circulation. But the surplus-value contained in the product, at 100% rate of surplus-value, makes up a value of £100. How can the capitalist class continue to extract £600 from circulation, if it only ever puts in £500? Out of nothing, nothing comes. The conclusion seems inescapable.

Marx then dismisses a numerical objection: he concedes that the sum of £400 may suffice, given a ten-fold turnover, to circulate means of production to a value of £4,000 and labour to a value of £1,000, while £100 may suffice for the circulation of £1,000 surplus-value. But this ratio contributes nothing to the matter in hand. Whether the money needed is £1,000 or £100, the question is where it comes from. In either case, it is additional money capital cast into circulation.

Beat 12 — The Actual Solution: The Capitalist Class Casts in the Money, but as Revenue, Not as Capital

And now the resolution. "In point of fact, paradoxical as it may seem at first glance, the capitalist class itself casts into circulation the money that serves towards the realization of the surplus-value contained in its commodities. But note well: it does not cast this in as money advanced, and therefore not as capital. It spends it as means of purchase for its individual consumption. Thus the money is not advanced by the capitalist class, even though this class is the starting-point of its circulation."

The critical distinction: advancing money as capital vs. spending money as revenue. When the capitalist advances money as capital (constant + variable), he expects it to flow back. When he spends money as revenue (individual consumption), he does not expect it to flow back as money-invested — but he does, in fact, get it back as the money form of the surplus-value he produces. This is the elegant dialectic of the chapter.

Beat 13 — The Farmer Example (First Year vs. Subsequent Years)

Marx works this out in a detailed numerical illustration. Consider a farmer who begins business. During the first year:

  • Advances £5,000 money capital — £4,000 for means of production, £1,000 for labour-power.
  • Rate of surplus-value = 100%, so surplus-value = £1,000.
  • The farmer must also live. Say his consumption = £1,000. He must have this in hand.

He says he "advances" £1,000 for the first year. But this is an advance only in the subjective sense — it means nothing more than that he has to cover his individual consumption for the first year out of his own pocket, instead of using the product produced for nothing by his workers. He does not advance this money as capital. He spends it — pays it out for an equivalent in means of subsistence, which he then consumes. He has ceased to stand in any relationship to its value. The money with which he pays for it exists as a component of the circulating money stock, but he has withdrawn the value of this money from circulation in products, and the value of these products is destroyed together with the products.

At the end of the year, he throws into circulation a commodity value of £6,000 and sells this. There returns to him:

  1. £5,000 — the money capital he advanced.
  2. £1,000 — his converted surplus-value.

So he advanced £5,000 as capital and withdraws £6,000 — of which £5,000 as capital, £1,000 as surplus-value. The final £1,000 is converted into money with the money that he threw into circulation not as capitalist but as consumer, i.e. did not advance but actually spent. It now returns to him as the money form of the surplus-value produced by him.

From the second year on, the operation repeats annually: the £1,000 he spends is always the changed form, the money form, of the surplus-value he produced. He spends this annually, and it returns to him at the same interval.

Marx then adds a nuance: if his capital were to turn over several times in the course of the year, this would not change things in any way, though it would affect the length of time for which he must cast this sum into circulation, and hence the magnitude of the sum involved.

And a normative remark: "This money is not cast into circulation by the capitalist as capital. However, it certainly pertains to the character of the capitalist that he should be capable of living off the means of subsistence in his possession until the reflux of his surplus-value." So the capacity to live off a subsistence fund while waiting for the reflux of capital is itself a class attribute — a presupposition of capitalist status.

Marx then flags a crucial caveat: it was assumed in this case that the sum of money the capitalist casts into circulation to cover his consumption equals the surplus-value he produces. This is obviously an arbitrary assumption in relation to the individual capitalist. But it must be correct for the capitalist class as a whole, on the assumption of simple reproduction. It simply expresses what the simple reproduction assumption implies: that the entire surplus-value is unproductively consumed — but no more than this, i.e. no fraction of the original capital stock.

So the mechanism: the capitalist class, in spending its revenue, casts into circulation the money that will return to it as its surplus-value. The "paradox" is resolved by the recognition that the money advanced as revenue (not capital) is exactly the money needed to convert the surplus-value into money.

Beat 14 — The Special Role of Gold Production in Realization

Marx recapitulates the gold production assumption: the entire annual production of precious metals (taken as £500) was just sufficient to replace the wear and tear of the money. The gold-producing capitalists possess their entire product in gold — including the part replacing constant capital, the part replacing variable capital, and the surplus-value. So one part of society's surplus-value consists of gold from the start and is cast into circulation in order to withdraw products from it. The same applies to wages (variable capital) and to the replacement of constant capital advanced.

So the answer to the paradox has a second, complementary structure:

  • The non-gold-producing capitalists cast into circulation a commodity value greater (by the surplus-value) than the money capital they advanced.
  • The gold-producing capitalists cast into circulation a greater money value (greater by the surplus-value) than the commodity value they withdraw for the production of gold.

"If one group of capitalists constantly pump more money out of the circulation sphere than they put into it, the gold-producing group constantly pump more money in than they withdraw from it in means of production."

Even though a part of the £500 gold product is surplus-value for the gold producers, the entire sum is still simply determined by the replacement of the money needed for the circulation of commodities; how much converts surplus-value into money, and how much converts other value components, is immaterial.

If gold production is transferred abroad, this does not change the situation. Country A exports linen worth £500 to Country B to buy gold. This product of A is represented by £500 in gold and comes into circulation in country A only as money. The part of social surplus-value contained in this product exists directly in money, and, as far as country A goes, never in any other form. Although for the gold-producing capitalists only part of their product is surplus-value while another part replaces capital, the question of how much of the gold replaces variable capital and how much represents surplus-value depends exclusively on the respective ratios of wages and surplus-value to the value of the commodities in circulation. The surplus-value portion is divided among the various members of the capitalist class. A part of social surplus-value exists in the form of money in the capitalists' pockets, in changing portions, just as a part of the workers' wages remains in their pockets as money for at least part of the week.

Two important conclusions follow. Marx states them explicitly:

Firstly, the surplus-value spent by capitalists in money, as well as the variable and other productive capital which they advance in money, is in fact the product of the workers — in particular of those workers occupied in gold production. These workers produce afresh both the part of the gold product "advanced" to them as wages, and the part in which the surplus-value of the gold-producing capitalist is directly represented. Even the part of the gold product that simply replaces constant capital value: it reappears in gold form only as a result of the annual labour of the workers. At the start of the business it was originally given out by the capitalist in money that was not newly produced but formed part of the existing social quantity of money. But in so far as it is replaced by a new product, additional gold, it is the annual product of the workers. The advance on the part of the capitalist appears here too only as a form deriving from the fact that the worker is neither the proprietor of his own means of production nor has at his disposal during production the means of subsistence produced by other workers.

This is a striking restatement, in monetary terms, of the fundamental thesis of Volume I: the worker's apparent dependence on the capitalist's "advance" is an artifact of the property relations. In gold production, this is transparently visible: the capitalist appears to advance wages, but the wages are physically a part of the gold the workers themselves have just mined.

Secondly, as regards the quantity of money that exists independently of this annual replacement (£500), partly as hoard, partly as circulating money, the same must apply to it — the same must originally have applied, as still applies to this annual £500. Marx promises to return to this point at the conclusion of the section, and he does so at the chapter's end (see Beat 19).

Beat 15 — Turnover, Money Elasticity, and the Wage-Rise Fallacy

Marx now takes a digression that connects the chapter to his theory of money and wages. First, a point on turnover: with circumstances otherwise the same, changes in the length of turnover periods make different amounts of capital necessary in order to continue production on the same scale. The monetary circulation must thus be elastic enough to adapt to this alternate expansion and contraction.

Then a key claim: assume other circumstances remain the same (no change in size, intensity, or productivity of the working day), but there is an altered division of value product between wages and surplus-value — either the former rises and the latter falls, or vice versa. Then the quantity of money in circulation is not affected. This change can come about without any kind of expansion or contraction in the quantity of money in circulation.

Consider a general rise in wages (with a consequent general fall in the rate of surplus-value). There would be no change in the value of the mass of commodities in circulation. The money capital that has to be advanced as variable capital would grow, and so would the quantity of money serving this function. But surplus-value would decline by the same amount, and thus so would the quantity of money needed for its realization. The quantity of money needed to realize the commodity value is therefore no more affected than is this commodity value itself. The cost price of the commodities rises for the individual capitalist, but their social price of production remains unaltered. What changes is the ratio in which, leaving aside the constant portion of value, the production price of the commodities is divided between wages and profit.

This is a foundational point of Marx's theory of distribution: a general change in the wage rate, holding productivity and working conditions fixed, redistributes value but does not change total value or the money needed to circulate it.

Marx then addresses and refutes two common capitalistic arguments against this:

Capitalistic argument 1 (demand-push): A greater outlay of variable money capital means a correspondingly greater quantity of monetary means in the workers' hands. The workers therefore have greater demand for commodities, which raises commodity prices. Therefore a general rise in wages leads to a rise in prices, requiring more money to circulate.

Marx's reply: As a result of rising wages, the demand of workers for necessary means of subsistence will grow. Their demand for luxury articles will increase to a smaller degree, or else a demand will arise for articles that previously did not enter their consumption. The sudden rise in demand for necessary means of subsistence will temporarily raise their prices. This causes a greater part of social capital to be applied to production of necessaries and a smaller part to luxury goods, since the latter will have fallen in price on account of the decline in surplus-value and the resulting diminished demand from the capitalists. To the extent that workers themselves buy luxury goods, the rise in wages does not raise the prices of necessaries but simply displaces the buyers of luxury goods: more luxury goods consumed by workers, relatively fewer by capitalists. That is all. After a few oscillations the mass of commodities in circulation has the same value as before. And as for these temporary oscillations, they can have no other result than to cast into domestic circulation, as unoccupied money capital, capital which formerly sought employment in speculative undertakings on the stock exchange or abroad.

Capitalistic argument 2 (price-push): If wages rise, capitalists will simply raise the prices of their commodities, so a greater quantity of money is needed to circulate.

Marx's reply: If it were within the capacity of the capitalist producers to increase the prices of their commodities at will, then they could and would do so even without any rise in wages. Nor would wages rise with a fall in commodity prices. And the capitalist class would never oppose trade unions, since they would always be able to use any increase in wages as a pretext to raise prices to a far higher degree and thus tuck away a greater profit.

The contention that capitalists can raise the prices of luxury articles because the demand for these declines would be "an extremely original application of the law of supply and demand." Insofar as there is not just a shift in buyers — where workers replace capitalists, the workers' additional wages spent on luxury articles cannot be spent on necessaries — the prices of luxury goods fall as a consequence of the reduced demand. Capital is withdrawn from luxury production until supply is reduced to match their changed role in social production. With this reduction, prices rise again to normal, given unchanged values. During this contraction or balancing process, the same amount of additional capital is supplied for necessaries production, whose prices are rising, as is withdrawn from luxury production, until demand is satisfied. There is then once again an equilibrium, and the conclusion of the whole process is that the social capital, and hence also the money capital, is divided between the production of necessary means of subsistence and that of luxury goods in changed proportions. Thus "the entire objection is a red herring brought in by the capitalists and their economic sycophants."

Beat 16 — Three Facts Underlying the Wage-Rise Fallacy

Marx distinguishes three "facts" that provide the pretext for the fallacy:

(1) The general law of monetary circulation. If the sum of prices of goods in circulation rises (whether for the same volume or for an increased volume), with other circumstances the same, the quantity of money in circulation grows. The effect is then taken for the cause. However, wages rise (even if seldom, and proportionately only in exceptional cases) with the increased price of the necessary means of subsistence. Their rise is the result of the rise in commodity prices, and not the cause of this. This is a classic critique of post hoc reasoning: the observed correlation between wage rises and price rises is usually because both movements are consequences of prior changes (e.g. currency depreciation, harvest failures, changes in the value of gold), not because wages cause prices.

(2) Partial or local wage rises. A local rise in wages in a few branches of production can result in a local rise in prices for that branch's products. But even this depends on many circumstances. For example, that wages were not abnormally depressed there (and hence the rate of profit not abnormally high), that the market for these commodities was not constricted by a rise in price (and thus that a rise in their prices does not depend on a preceding contraction in their supply), etc. In other words, the ability of a single branch to pass on a wage increase depends on its local market conditions and the pre-existing level of profit — not on a universal law.

(3) General rise in wages. With a general rise in wages, the price of goods produced in branches of industry in which variable capital is predominant rises, whereas prices fall in those branches in which constant or fixed capital predominates. This is a sketch of a theory of price formation under a general wage rise depending on the organic composition of capital — clearly foreshadowing Volume III's analysis of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall.

Beat 17 — The Reflux of Money vs. the Circulation of Money

Marx now introduces one of the most conceptually rich distinctions of the chapter: the circuit of money (its return to its starting-point) versus the circulation of money (its constant removal from its starting-point through a series of hands).

He first cites Volume I, Ch. 3: simple commodity circulation shows that even if the money form is transient in the circulation of a particular quantity of commodities, the money transiently in one person's hands in commodity metamorphosis still necessarily finds its way into another's hands — so that not only are commodities exchanged on all sides, replacing each other, but this replacement is also mediated and accompanied by a precipitation of money on all sides. Marx quotes himself: "When one commodity replaces another, the money commodity always sticks to the hands of some third person. Circulation sweats money from every pore." (Volume I, p. 208.)

The very same fact is expressed on the basis of capitalist commodity production by the constant retention of a part of capital in the form of money capital, and the constant presence of a part of the surplus-value similarly in the money form in the hands of its proprietor.

Marx then carefully distinguishes: the circuit of money — the return of the money to its starting-point, insofar as this forms a moment of turnover of capital — is a phenomenon completely different from and even opposed to the circulation of money, which expresses its constant removal from its starting-point through a series of hands (Volume I, p. 210). However, an accelerated turnover involves by its very nature an accelerated circulation.

In the footnote, Marx credits the Physiocrats with first stressing reflux as essential: Quesnay in the Tableau économique ("the productive class gives out the money with which the other classes come to buy its products, and these return this money to it the following year ... The sole cycle that you see here is therefore that of expenditure followed by reproduction, and reproduction followed by expenditure") and Turgot in the Réflexions ("It is this continuous advance and return of capitals that must be called the circulation of money, this useful and fertile circulation which animates all the works of society, sustaining the movement and life of the body politic in a way that may well be compared with the circulation of blood in the animal body"). Marx's engagement with the Physiocrats signals that his own reflux concept is a materialist transformation of the physiocratic insight.

Marx then works through the variable capital example with numbers. If a money capital of £500 turns over ten times a year as variable capital, this aliquot part of the quantity of money in circulation circulates ten times its value sum. The worker is paid — and himself pays — ten times in the year with the same aliquot part. If instead the variable capital turned over once a year with the same scale of production, there would only be one circulation of £5,000.

Similarly for constant circulating capital: if it is £1,000 and turns over ten times, then the capitalist sells his commodity ten times and thus the constant circulating part of its value with it. The same aliquot part of the money quantity (£1,000) passes ten times from its owner's hands to the capitalist's — ten changes of place. Secondly, the capitalist buys means of production ten times, again ten circulations of money. With £1,000 of money, commodities of £10,000 are sold by the industrial capitalist, and other commodities of £10,000 are bought. By a twenty-fold circulation of the £1,000 of money, a commodity stock of £20,000 is circulated. Finally, accelerated turnover also leads to a quicker circulation of the portion of money that realizes surplus-value.

But — and here's the crucial asymmetry — conversely, a more rapid monetary circulation does not necessarily involve a more rapid turnover of capital, and hence also of money, i.e. there is not necessarily a shortening and more rapid renewal of the reproduction process. More rapid monetary circulation takes place whenever a greater volume of transactions is completed with the same quantity of money. This can also be the case without a change in the reproduction period of the capital, as a result of changed technical arrangements for monetary circulation. Further, the volume of transactions can increase without real replacement of commodities (speculation in futures on the stock exchange, etc.). On the other hand, certain monetary circulations can completely disappear: e.g. no circulation between farmer and landlord when the agriculturist is his own landlord; no circulation between industrial capitalist and creditor when the industrial capitalist owns his own capital. This means the apparent money velocity is not a reliable indicator of the underlying reproduction process — a methodological warning.

Beat 18 — The Historical Precondition of Capitalist Production: A Sufficient Supply of Precious Metals

Marx then addresses the question of the original formation of a money hoard in a country and its appropriation by a few people — but he says it is not necessary to go into this in detail here. Instead he draws out a historical conclusion.

The capitalist mode of production — since its basis is wage-labour, and therefore also the payment of the worker in money and the general transformation of services in kind into money payments — can develop on a large scale and penetrate deeply only when there is a quantity of money in the country in question sufficient for circulation and for the hoard formation conditioned by this circulation. This is a historical precondition, even if we should not conceive it in such a way that a sufficient hoard has first to be formed before capitalist production can begin. The latter rather develops simultaneously with the development of its preconditions, and one of these preconditions is a sufficient supply of precious metals. Hence the increased supply of precious metals from the sixteenth century onwards was a decisive moment in the historical development of capitalist production.

This is one of the few passages in Capital where Marx states a material-historical precondition of the capitalist mode of production in monetary-metallurgical terms — a claim that would later be picked up by historical sociologists and world-systems theorists.

As for the further supply of money material needed on the basis of the capitalist mode of production: on the one hand surplus-value is cast into circulation in the product without the money for its conversion, while on the other hand surplus-value in gold is cast into circulation without its previous transformation from product into money. The additional commodities that have to be transformed into money find the sums of money needed available because on the other hand additional gold (and silver) is cast into circulation by production itself, not by exchange, and has to be transformed into commodities. This is the complementary unity of the two flows: commodity-surplus and gold-surplus.

Beat 19 — Section II: Accumulation and Expanded Reproduction

Marx now turns to the case in which accumulation takes place — reproduction on an expanded scale. This clearly does not offer any new problems with respect to money circulation.

As far as the additional money capital is concerned — that required for the function of the increased productive capital — this is supplied by the portion of realized surplus-value cast into circulation by the capitalists as money capital, instead of as money form of revenue. The money is already in the hands of the capitalists. It is simply its application that differs. Here the analytical distinction between revenue-spending and capital-advance reappears: the same money can perform either function, but the destination differs, and hence the return differs.

Now, however, as a result of the addition to the productive capital, an additional mass of commodities is cast into circulation as its product. Together with this extra mass of commodities, a part of the extra money needed for their realization is also cast in, to the extent that the value of this mass of commodities contains the value of the productive capital consumed in their production. This additional quantity of money is advanced precisely as additional money capital, and hence returns to the capitalist with the turnover of his capital. Here the same question arises again as before. Where does the extra money come from to realize the extra surplus-value that now exists in the commodity form?

The general reply is again the same. The total price of the mass of commodities in circulation has increased, not because the price of a given mass of commodities has risen, but rather because the mass of commodities is greater than that of the commodities circulating earlier, without this having been balanced by any fall in prices. The additional money required must be created either by:

  • A more economic use of the quantity of money in circulation — whether by directly balancing payments, etc., or by means that accelerate circulation of the same pieces of money.
  • Or alternatively by the transformation of money from the hoard form into the circulating form.

This does not just imply that idle money capital begins to function as means of purchase or payment, or that money capital already functioning as a reserve fund, while continuing to perform the reserve-fund function for its owners, circulates actively for the society (as with deposits in banks, which are constantly lent out), and thus performs a double function. It also means that stagnant reserves of coin are used more economically.

Here Marx quotes his own Zur Kritik der politischen Ökonomie (1859, pp. 105–6): "So that money as coin may flow continuously, coin must continuously congeal into money. The continual movement of coin implies its perpetual stagnation in larger or smaller amounts in reserve funds of coin which arise everywhere within the framework of circulation and which are at the same time a condition of circulation. The formation, distribution, dissolution and re-formation of these funds constantly changes; existing funds disappear continuously and their disappearance is a continuous fact." And: "money is in fact only suspended coin and the various component parts of the coinage in circulation appear, constantly changing, now in one form, now in another. The first transformation of the medium of circulation into money constitutes therefore merely a technical aspect of the circulation of money." So the hoard is not the opposite of circulating money but its presupposition and complement: hoards are the technical reserve funds of circulation.

If all these means together are not enough, there must be additional production of gold, or, what comes to the same thing, a part of additional product must be exchanged directly or indirectly for gold — the product of precious-metal-producing countries.

Beat 20 — Faux Frais of Circulation and the Pointless Question of Credit

Marx next makes a normative-dynamic point. The sum of labour-power and social means of production spent in the annual production of gold and silver as instruments of circulation forms a heavy item of faux frais for the capitalist mode of production, or, more generally, for a mode of production based on commodity circulation. It withdraws from social use a corresponding sum of possible additional means of production and consumption — i.e. of real wealth.

To the extent that the costs of this expensive machinery of circulation are reduced, with the scale of production remaining the same, i.e. at a given level of its extension, the productive forces of social labour are correspondingly heightened. Thus to the extent that the auxiliary means developed with credit have this effect, they directly increase capitalist wealth, whether because a greater part of the social production and labour process is accomplished without the intervention of real money, or because the capacity of the actually functioning quantity of money to fulfil its function is thereby increased.

This yields the conclusion that "this also disposes of the pointless question of whether capitalist production on its present scale would be possible without credit (even considered from this standpoint alone), i.e. with a merely metallic circulation. It would clearly not be possible. It would come up against the limited scale of precious-metal production."

But — and here Marx inserts a warning against the opposite error — "we should not get any mystical ideas about the productive power of the credit system, just because this makes money capital available or fluid." The development of this point belongs in Volume III.

Beat 21 — Latent Money Capital and the Impossibility of General Hoarding

Marx now considers the case where there is not actual accumulation — no direct expansion of production — but a part of realized surplus-value is stored up over a longer or shorter time as a monetary reserve fund, later to be transformed into productive capital.

If the money thus accumulated is extra money, this can only be a part of the additional gold supplied by the gold-producing countries. Note: the domestic products in return for which this gold is imported no longer exist in the country in question. They have been dispensed abroad in exchange for gold.

If, on the other hand, the same quantity of money is in the country as before, then the money stored away has flowed in from circulation; it is simply its function that has changed — transformed from circulating money into gradually formed latent money capital. The money stored up here is the money form of commodities that have been sold, and moreover of that portion of their value that represents surplus-value for their owner (with credit assumed non-existent). The capitalist who stores up money has to that extent sold without buying.

As a partial phenomenon, there is nothing mysterious: one group of capitalists keeps back part of the money obtained from sales, instead of using it to withdraw products from the market; another group transforms money into products, except for the money capital needed to carry on production. A part of the product thrown onto the market as bearer of surplus-value consists of means of production, or of real elements of variable capital (means of subsistence). It can therefore immediately serve to expand production. This doesn't require one group to accumulate money while the other fully consumes — only that one group accumulates in the money form while the others actually expand production. The quantity of money present remains sufficient even if it is alternately one group that stores up money and the other that expands production, and vice versa. The storing up of money on one side can proceed even without cash, simply through the piling up of credit notes.

But — and here Marx identifies the crucial difficulty — difficulties start to arise when we assume not partial accumulation of money capital but general accumulation within the capitalist class. Outside this class, on the assumption of universal and exclusive domination of capitalist production, there is no other class except the working class. The total purchases of the working class equal the sum of their wages, i.e. the sum of the variable capital advanced by the entire capitalist class. This money flows back to the capitalist class through the sale of product to the working class; their variable capital thereby receives its money form.

If the sum of variable capital is x times £100 (note the analytic notation: x = number of turnovers or units), this is not the total variable capital advanced in the year, but only that applied — whether it is advanced with more or less money during the year, according to turnover speed, doesn't affect the current question. With this capital, the capitalist class buys a certain quantity of labour-power (first transaction). The workers buy a certain value of commodities from the capitalists, and the sum of x times £100 returns to the capitalists (second transaction). The process is constantly repeated. The sum of x times £100 can therefore never enable the working class to buy the part of the product containing the constant capital, let alone the surplus-value that belongs to the capitalists. The workers can buy with x times £100 only a portion of the value of the social product equal to the portion of value representing the variable capital advanced.

This is an extremely important structural point — essentially an anticipation of the reproduction schemes' demonstration that workers' wages cannot buy back the full social product. It also has major implications for the critique of "underconsumption" and "Say's Law" positions alike: the working class as a class does not buy back the total product; the capitalist class must purchase the surplus product and the constant capital for the system to close. The general accumulation of money by the capitalist class requires not merely the circulation of the existing money but the existence of a demand from somewhere else.

Apart from the case where this all-round monetary accumulation simply expresses the division between various individual capitalists of additional precious metal brought in, how else is the entire capitalist class to accumulate money? They would all have to have sold a part of their product without buying again. It is nothing mysterious that they all possess a certain money fund which they cast into the circulation sphere as means of circulation for their consumption, and of which each receives a part back again. But this monetary fund is then precisely a circulation fund, acquired by the conversion into money of surplus-value, and does not consist at all of latent money capital.

So general hoarding is impossible within the closed capitalist class: money received by one capitalist is spent by another; any fund is circulation fund not hoard. This destroys the illusion that all capitalists can simultaneously and permanently "store up" their surplus-value as money.

Beat 22 — Forms of Latent Money Capital in Real Life: Bank Deposits, Government Papers, Shares

Marx then considers how latent money capital appears in real life (introducing credit after all):

(1) Bank deposits. The money the banks really dispose of is a relatively small sum. Here it is only nominally stored up as money capital. What is really stored up are monetary claims which are only convertible (to the extent that they ever are converted) because there is a balance between money drawn out and money put in. The money in the hands of the bank is relatively only a small sum. This is a strikingly modern observation on fractional-reserve banking.

(2) Government papers. These are not capital at all, but simply outstanding claims on the nation's annual product.

(3) Shares. Leaving aside the fraudulent ones, these are titles of ownership to a real capital belonging to a corporate body, and drafts on the surplus-value that flows in from this each year. Note: shares do represent real capital, unlike government paper. This distinction is a rudimentary but important contribution to the theory of fictitious capital.

In all these cases, there is no storage of money, and what appears on the one hand as storage of money capital appears on the other hand as the continuous real expenditure of money. Whether the money is spent by the person it belongs to or by people in debt to him does not affect the situation.

On the basis of capitalist production, the formation of a hoard as such is never a purpose, but rather a result — a result either of a stagnation in circulation (greater quantities of money than usual assume the hoard form) or of the storage required by the turnover. The hoard can also be simply a formation of money capital, in the latent form for the time being, but destined to function as productive capital.

If on the one hand, therefore, a part of the surplus-value realized in money is withdrawn from circulation and stored up as a hoard, at the same time a further part of the surplus-value is always transformed into productive capital. With the exception of the division of additional precious metal among the capitalist class, storage in the money form never occurs simultaneously at all points.

So the system as a whole never hoards. Individual hoarding is always counterbalanced by others' investment or consumption. This conclusion is the systematic end-point of the chapter: the notion of a universally hoarding capitalist class is incoherent.

Marx's final paragraph notes a broader feature of the analysis: "The same applies to that part of the annual product which represents surplus-value in the commodity form, as applies to the rest of the annual product. A certain sum of money is required for its circulation. This sum of money belongs just as much to the capitalist class as does the annually produced mass of commodities that represents surplus-value. It was originally cast into circulation by the capitalist class itself. It is continuously divided among them afresh by circulation. Just as with the circulation of coin in general, a part of this monetary surplus-value is held up, at ever changing points, while a further part is always circulating. Whether some of this storage is deliberate, in order to form money capital, does not affect the situation in any way."

Finally, the chapter concludes with a methodological caveat: "Here we have disregarded the vicissitudes of circulation, in which one capitalist seizes for himself a piece of another's surplus-value, and even of his capital, and there is therefore a one-sided accumulation and centralization of both money capital and productive capital. A part of the extorted surplus-value that A stores up as money capital may thus be a fragment of B's surplus-value that has failed to return to him." This final observation gestures toward Volume III: circulation is not only a mechanism of realization but of redistribution, seizure, and centralization. Some capitalists' hoards are literally others' losses.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Fundamental Principles Revealed

Principle 1: The independence of the money-quantity requirement from the surplus-value content of commodities. The chapter's central technical result — that a given commodity value requires a determinate quantity of circulating money irrespective of how it divides into constant capital, variable capital, and surplus-value — dissolves the supposedly special problem of "where the money comes from to realize surplus-value." This is a profound result because it demonstrates that distribution (the split of value among classes) is analytically separable from circulation (the technical movement of commodities and money). The apparent paradox of capitalist accumulation — that the capitalist class always withdraws more money than it casts in — is shown to be a false paradox generated by confusing the starting-point of circulation with the source of money. Once we recognize that the capitalist class casts money into circulation both as capital and as revenue, the paradox disappears: the money that realizes surplus-value is the money the capitalist class spends on its own consumption.

Principle 2: The strict distinction between advancing money as capital and spending money as revenue. This distinction is the analytical pivot of the chapter. It separates two identical movements of money (payment) whose economic significance is completely different: in the first case the money is advanced in the expectation of return as capital plus surplus-value; in the second case the money is spent in the expectation of return as the money form of already-produced surplus-value. This distinction will be fundamental to Marx's later theory of crisis, credit, and the cycle, because the rhythm and volume of revenue-spending versus capital-advance are different, and can fall out of synchronization.

Principle 3: The complementarity of gold production and commodity production within a closed monetary system. The system works because, at the level of the whole capitalist class, the mass of commodity value cast into circulation (which exceeds the money advanced) is balanced by the mass of money cast into circulation by gold producers (which exceeds the commodity value they withdraw). This is a structural insight of great elegance: the social money stock is reproduced by a continuous two-way flow, one side contributing commodities, the other contributing money. It is the monetary expression of the fact that a portion of social labour must always be devoted to producing the medium of circulation itself — a faux frais that Marx explicitly acknowledges.

Principle 4: The material and institutional forms of latent money capital. Latent money capital is not primarily physical coin; it consists of bank deposits, government paper, and shares — i.e. legal claims on future production. This dissolves the crude image of hoarding as a stock of gold in a vault. In reality, "storage" of money capital and "expenditure" of money capital are two sides of the same coin: the money that A deposits is lent to B and spent by C. This unity-in-difference of hoarding and investment is the conceptual bridge from Volume II to Volume III's theory of the credit system, interest-bearing capital, and fictitious capital.

Principle 5: The impossibility of universal hoarding within the capitalist class. Because the working class's aggregate purchasing power is limited to variable capital, and because the capitalist class's own purchases (constant capital + surplus-value revenue) must be internally balanced, there can be no general storage of money by all capitalists simultaneously. What appears as hoarding at one point is always investment or consumption at another point. This principle undermines any theory that treats hoarding as a systemic tendency.

Principle 6: The real wealth/productive-power distinction. Drawn from William Thompson, this principle asserts that the "accumulated wealth" of society is overwhelmingly nominal — legal claims on future productive powers, not physical assets. The productive powers of society, by contrast, are incalculably larger. This has dramatic implications for political economy: the true measure of social wealth is not the stock of accumulated capital but the annual productive power of labour, and the distribution of the annual product (not the ownership of the accumulated stock) should be the primary object of political economy.

Principle 7: The material-historical precondition of the precious-metals supply. Capitalist production on a large scale presupposes a supply of monetary metal sufficient for circulation and for hoard formation. The sixteenth-century bullion influx was therefore a decisive historical moment in the development of capitalism. This embeds monetary history within the historical materialism of the transition to capitalism.

Principle 8: The critique of the wage-cost-push theory of prices. A general wage rise need not — indeed cannot — raise the general price level, under the assumptions of unchanged productivity and unchanged value of money. It merely redistributes value between wages and profit, and between branches according to their organic composition. The three "facts" that are invoked as pretexts for the wage-push theory are each shown to be misreadings of superficially correlated phenomena.

Broader Theoretical Implications

For the theory of value. Chapter 17 extends the labour theory of value into the monetary sphere. The total value of commodities is determined by labour-time; the quantity of circulating money is determined by the value of commodities and the velocity of circulation. Surplus-value does not alter the quantity of money needed to circulate the commodities; it only determines how the value of the product is distributed. The chapter thus demonstrates that Marx's value theory is not a "monetary" theory in the naive quantity-theory sense but a theory of the social-relations that underlie monetary phenomena.

For the theory of accumulation. Accumulation is not simply the conversion of surplus-value into productive capital. It is a staged, multi-form process: surplus-value is realized in money → some is consumed as revenue → some is held as latent money capital → latent money capital is placed on the money market → it is lent out to other capitalists → it is finally converted into productive capital. The "identity" of the capitalist who produces and the capitalist who accumulates is broken: a capitalist whose own surplus-value is not yet sufficient to expand may expand using other capitalists' surplus-value via the credit system. This is the theoretical basis for understanding the socialization of capital — the emergence of a class-level mechanism of accumulation in which individual capitalists are mere nodes in a collective process.

For the theory of credit. The chapter's treatment of latent money capital and credit is foundational. Marx explicitly states that the credit system develops simultaneously with capitalist production and that it is not merely an accessory but a material precondition of production on the present scale. The "pointless question" of whether capitalism would be possible without credit is dismissed on the ground that the required metallic circulation would exceed the limits of precious-metal production. But Marx also warns against mystical fetishism of credit's "productive power." Credit merely makes money capital fluid; it does not create value.

For the reproduction schemes. The chapter's observation that the working class can buy back only a value equal to variable capital is one of the crucial presuppositions of the two-department reproduction schemes that follow (Chapters 18–20). In those schemes, Marx will show how the capitalist class itself must absorb the surplus product through productive and luxury consumption — and how the constant capital of each department must be exchanged for the means of consumption of workers and capitalists in the other department. Chapter 17 supplies the monetary foundation: the money for these inter-departmental exchanges is circulated by the capitalist class's own advance of revenue and capital.

For the critique of political economy. The chapter mounts a sustained critique of bourgeois political economy on three fronts: (a) the Tooke debate about the hoard of money needed for realization; (b) the wage-cost-push apologetic; (c) the physiocratic concentration on the visible stock of wealth rather than the annual flow of production. Marx draws on pre-Marxian socialist critique (Thompson) to strengthen his anti-apologetic argument.

Philosophical and Practical Implications

The temporal constitution of capital. The chapter is fundamentally about time — turnover time, realization time, hoarding time, the time required for large-scale expansion. Capital is not a thing but a social relation that exists only in its own movement through time. Value must be realized in money before it can become revenue or capital; realization takes time; the time of realization is itself determined by the turnover period. Hence a slower turnover is not just less efficient but structurally different: it requires greater advances, greater reserves, deeper credit, and a greater capacity to live off accumulated funds.

The materiality of money. Against both naive metallicism and pure nominalism, the chapter insists that money is both material (gold is a commodity, produced by labour) and social (it functions as money only because it is the universal equivalent). The gold producers' labour is identical in kind to other labour; what makes their product money is its role in the system, not its physical character. And yet the system cannot dispense with this material base: the "limited scale of precious-metal production" sets a real bound on metallic circulation, which is precisely why credit is materially necessary. This is a sophisticated dialectic of the material and the social.

The illusory "wealth" of the rich. The Thompson quotations raise a theme that seems out of place in a technical chapter but is entirely consistent with Marx's project: the vast majority of what is called wealth is nothing but legal title to future production. To imagine that the rich are rich because they own "things" is an illusion. They are rich because they own claims on other people's future labour. This is the moral-philosophical heart of the chapter. It also grounds an important political implication: any serious politics of distribution must target the legal relations by which claims on future production are monopolized, not the physical stock of goods.

The class-content of "advance." The chapter repeatedly returns to the fact that the capitalist appears to "advance" what the workers have actually produced. In gold production this is directly visible: workers are paid with gold they themselves have just mined. In general, the "advance" of wages is a form that conceals the reality that the workers' own product sustains them. This is a restatement, in the monetary register, of the fundamental thesis of Volume I: the wage-form conceals the appropriation of unpaid labour.

Historicity of the monetary framework. By treating the sufficient supply of precious metals as a historical precondition, and by dating the decisive moment to the sixteenth century, Marx embeds his analysis in an empirical-historical narrative. This is not a theory of an abstract capitalism but of a historically situated capitalism whose material preconditions were themselves historically contingent.

Practical and Political Implications

For trade-union strategy and the wage-struggle. The chapter arms labour with an argument against the wage-cost-push apologetic: a general rise in wages need not raise the general price level. It also warns that local wage increases can, in specific conditions, generate local price effects — a nuance that matters to any serious strategy of wage bargaining. The distinction between general and partial wage rises, between variable-capital-dominant and constant-capital-dominant branches, is directly relevant to how wage increases are absorbed.

For understanding the money market and financial crisis. The chapter's treatment of latent money capital, bank deposits, and reserve funds provides the analytical skeleton for understanding how the financial system functions — and how it can malfunction. The "double function" of reserve funds (available for the owner, circulating for society) is a source of systemic fragility: the same money is claimed by two sets of actors at the same time.

For the critique of contemporary "financialization" discourse. Marx's observation that latent money capital consists of claims, not things, and that what appears as hoarding is really expenditure, anticipates modern debates about the financial system as a system of claims on future production. Government paper is not capital; shares are titles to real capital. The distinction matters because it resists the reduction of all financial assets to a homogeneous "wealth."

For the theory of the state. Thompson's remark that a full third of annual output is appropriated under the name of "public burdens" and consumed unproductively directs attention to the fiscal dimension of the class struggle. Marx cites it without comment, but its inclusion within a chapter on monetary circulation suggests that the state's absorption of value must be analytically integrated into the circulation of surplus-value.

Concluding Reflection

Chapter 17 is, in the final analysis, a philosophical meditation on the nature of capitalist wealth. It tells us that wealth in capitalism is not a stock of things but a claim on a flow; that the flow is produced by living labour; that the "advances" of the capitalist are only formal appearances behind which the workers' product circulates; that the money form is both necessary (material foundations in precious-metal production) and illusory (claims on future production often mistaken for present wealth); and that the system as a whole is articulated by a delicate, unstable, but structurally necessary circulation of money that no individual capitalist commands but that all collectively produce. The chapter thus closes Volume II's analysis of the process of circulation of capital with a demonstration that the social and monetary character of capital is irreducible to its individual form. The secret of surplus-value is produced in production; but the secret of its circulation — and hence of the possibility of accumulation — lies in a monetary architecture that no single capitalist can control. That architecture will be the subject of Volume III.

The dictum that captures the chapter best is Marx's own, quoted from Volume I and repeated here: "Circulation sweats money from every pore." What is hidden within this poetic formulation — and what Chapter 17 has established with relentless analytical precision — is that the money that sweats from every pore is the collective product of the working class, that it circulates between capital and labour in a rhythm set by the turnover period, and that its social destination is the expansion of capital. No mystery, then, surrounds the money that realizes surplus-value: it is simply the money of the capitalist class itself, advanced as revenue, returning as the money form of its surplus, and — through the credit system and the gold mines — continuously reproduced so that the process may begin again.
