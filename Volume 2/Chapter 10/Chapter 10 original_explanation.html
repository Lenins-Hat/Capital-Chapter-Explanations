Volume 2 • Chapter 10: Theories of Fixed and Circulating Capital. The Physiocrats and Adam Smith

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

This chapter is one of the strategic hinges of Marx's Capital, Volume 2, and it must be read as such before a single sentence of its individual arguments is assessed. Its stated surface topic — the "theories of fixed and circulating capital" — is in fact a critical-historical excavation: Marx is digging into the buried foundations of political economy in order to demonstrate that a single, deceptively technical terminological confusion (the equation of circulating capital with capital of circulation) has, across a century of theory, systematically concealed the economic essence of capitalist production itself — namely, the distinction between constant capital and variable capital, and with it the origin of surplus-value.

The chapter's core thesis can be stated in three interlocking propositions. First, the Physiocratic distinction between avances primitives (original, or "primitive" advances) and avances annuelles (annual advances), as formulated by Quesnay, was substantively correct in its logic even though it was confined to agricultural capital: Quesnay understood that the distinction is internal to productive capital, and that it is grounded in the different ways in which value circulates and is reproduced — one portion replaced completely each year, the other bit by bit over a longer (decennial) period. Second, Adam Smith's "only step forward" was to generalize these categories from farmer's capital to all productive capital, thereby converting them into the modern distinction between fixed and circulating (fluid) capital and replacing the agricultural twelve-month rhythm with the general notion of variable turnover periods. Third — and this is the polemical heart of the chapter — Smith's development then collapses: he commits a foundational confusion between (a) fixed vs. fluid capital, a distinction valid only within productive capital, derived from how value is transferred and replaced, and (b) productive capital vs. capital of circulation (commodity capital and money capital), a distinction between forms that the same capital value successively assumes in its circuit. This confusion is compounded by a second error — substituting the workers' means of subsistence for labour-power in the list of circulating capital — which makes it structurally impossible for Smith and his successors to perceive that the portion of capital laid out on labour-power is variable capital. The result is that "the whole secret of capitalist production is still further obscured."

The central problem the chapter addresses is thus both analytical and historical. Analytically, it asks: what is the correct basis for dividing productive capital into fixed and circulating components? Marx's answer, developed by critical contrast, is that the distinction cannot be grounded in the material properties of things (a machine "does not change masters"; raw materials do), nor in the apparent way profit arises for the individual capitalist (profit "by keeping" vs. profit "by parting with"), but only in the mode of circulation of value through the labour-and-valorization process: whether the value laid out on an element of productive capital is transferred wholly and at once into the product (and thus must be wholly replaced out of the product's value each turnover) or gradually, in fragments, over a series of turnovers (and thus replaced bit by bit). Historically, the chapter asks: how did this correct insight get lost, and by what mechanism was it replaced by a definition that obscures the variable/constant distinction? The answer traces a direct line from Smith's blunder to the doctrines of his successors — including the "labour fund" doctrine that fixes the workers' share of the social product as a given magnitude.

The chapter's intellectual objective is, in effect, negative-critical and positive-defensive at once. Negatively, it dismantles the Smithian apparatus category by category: the merchant's-capital example, the "yield a revenue by remaining in possession" formula, the machine-builder paradox, the seed-as-fixed-capital absurdity, the four-part list of circulating capital, and the tripartite division of social wealth into consumption fund/fixed/circulating. Positively, it vindicates Quesnay's method of deriving categories from the exigencies of the actual reproduction process, and it prepares the ground for the properly Marxian category of variable capital — the portion of capital that is not merely "fluid" but self-valorizing.

How does the chapter fit into the broader theoretical framework of Capital? Volume 2 as a whole analyzes capital in the sphere of circulation — the metamorphoses, circuits, and turnovers of capital — as distinguished from Volume 1's analysis of the production of capital. This chapter is a critical appendix or interlude within that analysis: after Marx has (in preceding chapters) worked out the circuit of capital M–C…P…C'–M', the turnover, and the fixed/circulating distinction in its proper sense, he here turns to intellectual history to show that classical political economy — even at its best, in Smith — failed precisely because it did not hold firm to the distinction between capital in its productive form and capital in its circulation forms. The chapter therefore functions both as a history of theory and as a diagnostic demonstration: it shows, by concrete exegesis of Smith's text, that the categories of political economy are not neutral technical tools but carry within them, so to speak, their doctrinal commitments — that the displacement of labour-power by "means of subsistence" was not an incidental slip but the conceptual price of Smith's confusion, and that this price was the loss of the theory of surplus-value. Read in this way, the chapter is not a digression but a necessary clearing of the theoretical ground — "clearing the decks," as it were — before the mature analysis of reproduction can proceed.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 Quesnay's Foundational Distinction: Avances Primitives and Avances Annuelles

Marx opens the chapter by affirming that in the work of François Quesnay — the founder of the Physiocratic school — the distinction between fixed and circulating capital already appears, in embryo, as the distinction between avances primitives ("original" or "primitive advances") and avances annuelles ("annual advances"). These two French terms are not incidental vocabulary: they name the two parts of the farmer's capital stock. The avances primitives comprise the more durable, longer-lived investment — the initial fund required to "commence cultivation," the implements, draught animals, land improvements, and the like. The avances annuelles comprise the recurring, yearly outlays — seed, fodder, wages, incidental materials — that must be made afresh each production cycle.

Critically, Marx insists that Quesnay is correct in treating this as a distinction within productive capital — indeed, "capital incorporated into the immediate production process." This point is decisive and it is worth spelling out why. Quesnay's advances are not money and they are not commodities sitting on the market. As Marx puts it, "as advances for production, i.e. as productive capital, the two contrast both with money and with commodities on the market." The distinction thus arises only after money advanced has been transformed into the elements of productive capital — that is, once the capitalist has bought means of production and labour-power and put them to work. The distinction belongs to the inner anatomy of production, not to the monetary or market surface.

Marx credits Quesnay with reducing this distinction to a precise, value-theoretic criterion: the different ways in which the two elements enter the value of the finished product, and correspondingly the different ways in which "their value is circulated together with the value of the product, and the different ways in which they are replaced or reproduced, the value of one being completely replaced each year, that of the other bit by bit over a longer period." Two things follow. First, there emerges the notion of an annual turnover time for one part of capital, and a more-than-annual (decennial, i.e. ten-year) turnover time for the other. Second, because Quesnay treats agricultural capital as the only "really productive" capital (the famous Physiocratic doctrine that only agriculture yields a produit net, a net surplus), these distinctions "in fact only arise for the farmer's capital." Marx notes with a historian's accuracy that "in the course of development, the Physiocrats incidentally transferred these distinctions to other kinds of capital as well, to industrial capital in general" — but the transference was incidental, not systematic.

The footnote to this opening section is itself a miniature history of theory. Marx cites Quesnay's Analyse du tableau économique (in the Daire edition of the Physiocrates, Part I, Paris, 1846), where Quesnay writes: "The annual advances consist of the expenditures annually made for the work of cultivation; these advances must be distinguished from the original advances which form the fund for the commencement of cultivation" (p. 59). He then tracks the conceptual drift of the categories through three later figures. Dupont de Nemours, in the Maximes du Docteur Quesnay, already describes these advances "much more directly as capital: 'capital or avances'." Le Trosne, in De l'intérêt social (Daire, II, pp. 928–9), generalizes the durability point into a statement about the nation as a whole: "As a consequence of the longer or shorter lifespan of the instruments of labour, a nation possesses a considerable stock of riches independent of its yearly reproduction; this represents a capital accumulated over a long period and originally paid for with products, and it is continually maintained and increased." And Turgot, in the Réflexions sur la formation et la distribution des richesses (1766), "already uses the term 'capital' for the avances more regularly, and more closely identifies the advances of the manufacturiers with those of the farmers." The trajectory traced here matters because it establishes the standpoint from which Marx will judge Smith: the Physiocrats, starting from the concrete exigencies of reproduction, arrived at the distinction from the inside; everything Smith does afterward must be measured against that standard.

2.2 Smith's One Genuine Advance: Generalization of the Categories

Marx is scrupulously fair at this juncture. "The only step forward taken by Adam Smith," he writes, "was to generalize these categories." Where Quesnay restricted the distinction to the farmer's capital, Smith "no longer relate[s] them to just one special form of capital... but to every form of productive capital." What follows automatically is a generalization at the level of time. Quesnay's agricultural scheme, based on the natural rhythm of the harvest, placed the two parts of capital in a fixed relationship: an annual turnover for one, a more-than-annual turnover for the other. Once the distinction is generalized, this becomes a general distinction between turnovers of varying times, so that — and this formula is the pivot of Smith's advance — "a turnover of fixed capital always comprises more than one turnover of circulating capital, whatever the length of turnover of this circulating capital may be — a year, greater than a year, or less than a year."

This last clause is not rhetorical padding; it is analytically consequential. Under Quesnay, the avances annuelles necessarily had a one-year cycle. Under Smith's generalization, the circulating capital can have any turnover period whatsoever — shorter than a year, equal to a year, or longer — and the fixed/circulating distinction becomes the relational fact that one component of capital recovers itself across multiple turnovers of another. "In Smith, therefore," Marx concludes, "avances annuelles are transformed into circulating capital, avances primitives into fixed capital." But he immediately slams the door on any generosity: "the progress he made was confined to this generalization of categories. In the development of his presentation, he falls far behind Quesnay."

2.3 "Two Different Ways in Which a Capital May Be Employed" — The Opening Ambiguity

Marx locates the beginning of Smith's decline in the very method of his opening. "The crudely empirical way in which Smith opens his investigation immediately introduces an ambiguity." Smith writes: "There are two different ways in which a capital may be employed so as to yield a revenue or profit to its employer" (Wealth of Nations, Book Two, Chapter I, p. 185 in the Aberdeen 1848 edition; the Pelican 1974 edition, p. 374).

Marx's objection is that this formulation misplaces the question. "The ways in which value may be employed to function as capital, to yield a surplus-value to its owner, are as varied and manifold as the spheres of investment of capital" — agriculture, manufacture, trade, and the many branches thereof. But so formulated, the question "goes still further": it includes "the problem of how value, even if it is not invested as productive capital, can function as capital for its owner, e.g. as interest-bearing capital, merchant's capital, etc." Here "we are already a world away from the real object of the analysis, i.e. the question how the division of productive capital into its various elements affects the turnover, irrespective of its different spheres of investment." The ambiguity, in short, is that Smith's opening frame conflates the question of investment sphere with the question of the internal composition of productive capital — and the latter, the only question that matters for the fixed/circulating distinction, is precisely the one Smith does not isolate.

2.4 The Merchant's-Capital Example: Smith Abandons the Physiocratic Ground

Smith's next move makes the drift concrete. He says capital "may be employed in raising, manufacturing, or purchasing goods, and selling them again with a profit." Marx reads this as a pure statement of spheres of investment — agriculture, manufacture, trade — with the crucial consequence that "as in trade, capital is not incorporated into the immediate production process, and thus does not function as productive capital." At a stroke, "he thus already abandons the basis on which the Physiocrats depicted the distinctions within productive capital and their influence on the turnover." Worse, "he immediately takes merchant's capital as an example in a question where what is at issue is exclusively the differences within productive capital in the process of forming products and value, differences which in turn produce differences in its turnover and reproduction."

Smith proceeds: "The capital employed in this manner yields no revenue or profit to its employer, while it either remains in his possession, or continues in the same shape." Marx pounces. "'The capital employed in this manner'! But Smith speaks of capital that is invested in agriculture and industry, and later tells us that the capital thus invested can be divided into fixed and circulating capital! The employment of capital in this manner can thus make the capital neither fixed nor circulating."

Marx then grants Smith a charitable reconstruction in order to show it also fails. Perhaps Smith means that capital employed to produce commodities and sell them at a profit must, after transformation into commodities, be sold; by the sale it first passes from seller to buyer and secondly is converted from commodity form into money form, hence is "useless to the possessor while it either remains in his possession, or continues in the same shape." But if that is the meaning, "what emerges then is this: the same capital value that functioned previously in the form of productive capital... now functions as commodity capital and money capital, in the forms pertaining to the circulation process, and thus is no longer either fixed or fluid capital." And, crucially, "this holds just as much for the elements of value that are added by way of raw and ancillary materials, thus by fluid capital, as for those added by the use of means of labour, i.e. by fixed capital. Thus we do not get a step nearer to the distinction between fixed and fluid capital."

2.5 Smith's Definition of Circulating Capital = Marx's "Capital of Circulation"

Smith continues: "The goods of the merchant yield him no revenue or profit till he sells them for money, and the money yields him as little till it is again exchanged for goods. His capital is continually going from him in one shape, and returning to him in another, and it is only by means of such circulation, or successive exchanges, that it can yield him any profit. Such capitals, therefore, may very properly be called circulating capitals."

Here Marx's diagnosis becomes terminological with surgical precision. "What Adam Smith here calls circulating capital is what I intend to call capital of circulation, capital in the form pertaining to the circulation process, pertaining to the change of form mediated by exchange (material change and change of hands), i.e. commodity capital and money capital, in contrast to the form pertaining to the production process, that of productive capital." The distinction between Marx's two terms is exactly the distinction Smith collapses:

  • Capital of circulation (Marx's term) = the same capital value appearing successively as commodity capital (C) and money capital (M) — forms belonging to the circulation sphere. These are "not particular ways in which the industrial capitalist divides his capital, but rather different forms that the same capital value, once advanced, successively assumes and discards throughout its curriculum vitae."
  • Circulating/fluid capital (Marx's term) = a component of productive capital, defined by the speed and mode of its value transfer into the product.

Smith "lumps these together with the distinctions of form that arise within the circulation of the capital value, in its circuit through its successive forms, while the capital value exists in the form of productive capital, and this is a great step backward in relation to the Physiocrats." The real distinctions, Marx reiterates, "arise in fact from the various ways in which the different elements of productive capital participate in the process of value-formation and transfer their value to the product." Let this sentence be underscored, because it is the positive criterion Marx will repeatedly apply to expose Smith's errors.

Marx then states the equalization that Smith's confusion obscures: "The capital value advanced in fixed capital is circulated via the product just as much as that advanced in fluid capital, and it is transformed into money capital through the circulation of the commodity capital every bit as much as the other." The only difference — the distinction itself — "simply arises from the fact that its value circulates bit by bit, and must thus also be replaced bit by bit, in shorter or longer periods, and so be reproduced in this way in its natural form."

2.6 The Absurdity of Beginning with Merchant's Capital

Marx sharpens the point by noting that Smith's own example is self-indicting. Smith takes "a kind of capital that does not belong to the production process at all, but exclusively inhabits the circulation sphere and consists solely of capital of circulation — merchant's capital." And Smith says openly: "The capital of a merchant... is altogether a circulating capital." "The distinction between circulating and fixed capital, however, is supposedly, as we are later told, one arising from basic distinctions within productive capital itself." So Smith's example cannot illustrate the distinction he is supposedly defining. "The two are completely jumbled up." Marx adds the substantive objection: "There is no way of seeing how a profit is supposed to arise through the change of form between money and commodity, through a mere transformation of value from one of these forms to the other. Explanation of this is even made absolutely impossible in so far as Smith begins with merchant's capital, which moves solely within the circulation sphere."

2.7 Smith on Fixed Capital — and the Machine-Builder Paradox

Turning to fixed capital, Smith writes: capital "may be employed in the improvement of land, in the purchase of useful machines and instruments of trade, or in such-like things as yield a revenue or profit without changing masters, or circulating any further. Such capitals, therefore, may very properly be called fixed capitals. Different occupations require very different proportions between the fixed and circulating capitals employed in them... Some part of the capital of every master artificer or manufacturer must be fixed in the instruments of his trade. This part, however, is very small in some, and very great in others... The far greater part of the capital of all such master artificers" (tailors, shoemakers, weavers), "however, is circulated, either in the wages of their workmen, or in the price of their materials, and repaid with a profit by the price of the work."

Marx sets aside the "childish definition of the source of profit" — profit "repaid by the price of the work" — to strike at the logical * contradiction. His example is the machine-builder. "For a machine-builder, for example, the machine is the product that circulates as his commodity capital, i.e. in Smith's words, 'is parted with, changes masters, circulates further'. The machine would thus not be fixed but circulating capital, even according to his own definition." This is the machine-builder paradox, and it is a reductio ad absurdum*: by Smith's own criterion (does the thing change masters?), the identical machine is "circulating" for its producer and "fixed" for its user. Marx then generalizes: "According to the position they assume in the life process of capital, therefore, the same things can function for Adam Smith as fixed capital (as means of labour, elements of productive capital), and as 'circulating' capital, commodity capital (as the product that is ejected from the sphere of production into that of circulation)."

2.8 Smith's Second Self-Contradiction: Independent Capitals vs. Portions of One Capital

Marx now catches Smith contradicting the very premise he started from a few lines earlier. The opening had framed fixed and circulating capital as "two different ways in which a capital may be employed" — as "different modes of employment of distinct and independent capitals," so that a capital might be invested either in industry or in agriculture. But Smith then says: "Different occupations require very different proportions between the fixed and circulating capitals employed in them." Marx notes the reversal: "Fixed and circulating capital are now no longer distinct and independent capital investments, but rather different portions of the same productive capital, which form different shares of the total value in different spheres of investment. They are thus distinctions that arise from the division of productive capital itself, as it lies in the facts, and they therefore apply only in relation to this." And Smith again contradicts himself by counterposing commercial capital, as "altogether a circulating capital," to fixed capital — even though "what it is in fact is a capital functioning within the circulation sphere; as such it contrasts with productive capital in general... and for this very reason it can never be counterposed to the fixed component of productive capital as a fluid (circulating) component of productive capital." Three incompatible framings in a few pages: independent capitals (opening), portions of one capital (proportions passage), and merchant's capital as circulating capital (examples). "The two are completely jumbled up."

2.9 The Materialist Fallacy: Why "Fixed" Is Not Fixity in the Means of Labour

In Smith's examples, "fixed capital" is defined as "instruments of trade," and "circulating capital" as the share laid out on wages and raw materials "repaid with a profit by the price of the work." Marx observes that the starting-point here is "simply the various components of the labour process, labour-power (labour) and raw materials on the one hand, instruments of labour on the other." These are components of capital only because "a sum of value that is to function as capital is laid out on them" — they are "the material elements, modes of existence, of productive capital." So why is one part called fixed? Because "some part of the capital must be fixed in the instruments of trade." But — and here is the materialist fallacy — "the other part, however, is also fixed, in wages and raw materials." Smith's criterion ("yield a revenue or profit without changing masters, or circulating any further") is a criterion drawn from material fixity in the labour process, not from value-circulation.

Marx then delivers the decisive corrective: "Thus capital is not fixed capital simply because it is fixed in the means of labour, but rather because a part of the value laid out on means of labour remains fixed in these, while another part circulates as a value component of the product." This sentence is the positive definition of fixed capital that the chapter has been building toward. The criterion is value-theoretic, not physical: what makes capital fixed is the partial, gradual transfer of its value into the product and the corresponding gradual, bit-by-bit replacement of that value.

2.10 The Copper-Mine Test Case

Marx introduces a Gedankenexperiment — the copper mine — to demonstrate that Smith's definition, taken strictly, yields absurd results. In mining, "there is no raw material involved, since the object of labour, e.g. copper, is a natural product that has first to be appropriated by labour." The as-yet-unappropriated copper does not form an element of the productive capital; no part of the value is laid out on it (it is nature, not a product). The other elements — labour-power, ancillaries like coal and water — "do not enter materially into the product. The coal is entirely consumed, and only its value enters the product, just as a part of the value of the machine, etc. enters the product." The worker "still exists just as independently vis-à-vis the product as does the machine. It is only the value that he produces through his labour that is now a component of the value of the copper."

Now the reductio: "In this example, therefore, not a single component of the productive capital changes hands ('masters'): none of these components is circulated further, because none of them materially enters the product. Where then is the circulating capital in this case? According to Smith's own definition, the whole of the capital employed in a copper mine consists solely of fixed capital." The consequence is fatal to Smith, because it destroys the universality of his dichotomy: if some capitals are entirely fixed, the distinction ceases to be a universal division of capital as such — the biological analogy Marx mocks later ("a mammal is by natural necessity either male or female") is already here exposed as false.

2.11 The Cotton-Yarn Test Case: Fluid Capital Is "Fixed" in Production

The counter-example is the spinning of cotton into yarn, which does use raw material that becomes the substance of the product. Here, "when the product, yarn for instance, changes hands, so does the raw material, the cotton, of which it consists, passing from the production process into that of circulation." But — and this is Marx's key point — "as long as cotton functions as an element of productive capital, its owner does not sell it but works on it, makes yarn out of it. He does not let it go." To use Smith's own trivial phrase, he does not make a profit "by parting with it, by its changing masters, or by circulating it. He no more has his materials circulate than his machines. They are fixed in the production process, just as much as are the spinning machines and factory buildings." Fluid capital, then, is just as fixed in the production process as fixed capital. "As elements of productive capital, all its components are constantly fixed in the production process, since this cannot proceed without them. And all elements of productive capital, fixed as well as fluid, are, as productive capital, equally distinct from circulation capital, i.e. from commodity capital and money capital."

Marx then gives the real distinction with precision: "The distinction is simply that the cotton, coal, etc. needed for a week's production of yarn, for example, is completely consumed, and must therefore be replaced by new cotton, coal, etc.; thus these elements of productive capital, although they remain identical in kind, always consist of new items, whereas the same individual spinning machine and the same individual factory building continue to serve for a whole series of weeks of production, without being replaced by new items."

2.12 Labour-Power: The Same Logic, and the Crucial Differentia

Marx extends the analysis to labour-power. "A part of the productive capital must constantly be fixed in it, and it is generally the very same labour-powers, like the same machines, that are used by the same capitalist for a long period." Thus labour-power, too, is "fixed" in the labour process in the trivial sense Smith uses. But the real distinction between labour-power and machine is not that the machine is "bought once and for all" — "which is in fact not the case when it is paid for by instalments, for example" — "while the worker is not," but rather "that the labour that the worker expends enters entirely into the value of the product, while the value of the machine enters only bit by bit." This is the discriminating criterion: labour-power is fluid; machinery is fixed — on the value-transfer criterion, not on the material/ownership criterion.

2.13 Smith's Conflation of Formal and Bodily Metamorphoses

Marx now pinpoints the deeper conceptual conflation. Smith says of circulating capital in contrast to fixed: "The capital employed in this manner yields no revenue or profit to its employer, while it either remains in his possession, or continues in the same shape." Marx's charge: "He places the merely formal commodity metamorphosis which the product, the commodity capital, undergoes in the circulation sphere and which mediates the commodities' change of hands, on the same level with the bodily metamorphosis which the various elements of the productive capital undergo during the production process." The two kinds of change are categorically different:

  1. Formal metamorphosis (circulation): the change of form C–M–C undergone by commodities as they are bought and sold — the change of hands and of value-form.
  2. Bodily metamorphosis (production): the material transformation of the elements of production into the intended product within the labour process.

"The metamorphoses that productive capital undergoes within its productive process, on the other hand, are metamorphoses pertaining to the labour process, which are necessary in order to transform the elements of production into the intended product." Smith, however, "confines himself to saying that one part of the means of production (the means of labour proper) serve in the labour process... not by changing their natural form, but simply by being gradually worn out; whereas another part, the materials, are changed, and fulfil their function as means of production precisely through their alteration." But Marx insists: "This differing behaviour of the elements of productive capital in the labour process, however, forms only the starting-point of the distinction between fixed and non-fixed capital, and not the distinction itself, as is already shown by the fact that it obtains equally for all modes of production, non-capitalist as well as capitalist." That is, the mere fact that tools wear out gradually while materials are consumed is a fact of every labour process, including a patriarchal family's — it is not yet the capital-theoretic distinction. What completes the distinction is: "Corresponding to this different material role is the way in which value is surrendered to the product, to which further corresponds the way in which value is replaced by the sale of the product; and it is only this that constitutes the distinction in question."

2.14 The False Ground of Profit: "By Staying With Him" vs. "By Going From Him"

Smith's formula at p. 380 — "If it [the stock] is employed in procuring further profit, it must procure this profit either by staying with him [the employer], or by going from him. In the one case it is a fixed, in the other it is a circulating capital" — draws from Marx a double indictment. First, it is "the crudely empirical conception of profit, taken from the manner in which it appears to the ordinary capitalist, something that stands in complete contradiction to Smith's own better and esoteric insight." Marx's editorial footnote explains his usage: "Marx frequently counterposes the falsely superficial or 'exoteric' elements in Adam Smith's writings with the deeper 'esoteric' insights that occasionally emerge." Second, the formula is substantively false: "In the price of the product, the price of both materials and labour-power is replaced, but so, too, is the portion of value transferred from the instruments of labour to the product by wear and tear. Profit can in no case flow from this replacement." Whether value is replaced completely or bit by bit "can alter only the manner and time of the replacement; in no case however can it transform what is common to both — the replacement of value — into a creation of surplus-value." The everyday notion "that, because surplus-value is only realized by the sale of the product, by its circulation, it therefore arises simply from sale, from circulation" lies at the bottom of the error. In truth, "saying that profit arises in 'different ways' is here only an incorrect way of saying that the various elements of productive capital serve or function differently in the labour process as productive elements." The distinction, so framed, is "not derived from the labour and valorization process itself... but is rather one that simply obtains subjectively for the individual capitalist."

2.15 Back to Quesnay: The Reproduction-Process Criterion

The contrast with Quesnay is now drawn as a methodological verdict: "Quesnay, on the other hand, derived the distinctions from the actual reproduction process and its exigencies. In order for this process to be continuous, the value of the annual advances has to be completely replaced each year out of the value of the annual product, whereas the value of the original investment capital need only be replaced bit by bit, so that it is only completely replaced over a series of e.g. ten years, and only in this way is it entirely reproduced (replaced by new items of the same kind). Thus Adam Smith falls a long way behind Quesnay."

What Smith retains, after all his errors, is only the material fact "that it [fixed capital] consists of means of labour that do not change their shape in the production process, and continue to serve in production until they are worn out, as opposed to the products which they help to form." But this is not the distinction. "He forgets that all elements of productive capital are always distinct from the product... in their natural form (as means of labour, materials and labour-power), and that the distinction between the part consisting of materials and labour-power and the part consisting of means of labour simply lies, in the case of labour-power, in that it is always bought anew (not bought for its duration as with means of labour), and, in the case of the materials, in that it is not the very same ones, but ever new items of the same kind, that function in the labour process." Smith thus generates "the illusion... that the value of the fixed capital does not also circulate, although Adam Smith has of course earlier explained that the wear and tear of the fixed capital forms part of the price of products."

2.16 The Fatal Concealment: Burying the Variable/Constant Distinction

At this point Marx makes the strongest claim of the chapter. When Smith distinguishes circulating from fixed capital, "what he emphasizes is not that this circulating capital is simply that component of the productive capital that must be completely replaced out of the value of the product, and must therefore go through all its metamorphoses together with the latter, whereas this is not the case with fixed capital." Instead, "circulating capital is rather lumped together with the shapes that the capital assumes on its transition from the sphere of production to that of circulation, as commodity capital and money capital. These two forms, however, commodity capital and money capital, are bearers of both the fixed and the fluid components of the value of productive capital. Both are capital of circulation, in contrast to productive capital, but not circulating (fluid) capital in contrast to fixed."

The consequence is the doctrinal catastrophe. "The wholly erroneous explanation that fixed capital makes a profit by remaining in the production process, while circulating capital makes a profit by leaving this and circulating, permits the similarity of form that variable capital and the fluid component of constant capital have in the turnover to conceal the basic difference that they have in the valorization process and the formation of surplus-value, and in this way the whole secret of capitalist production is still further obscured." Because labour-power (variable capital) and raw materials (a fluid part of constant capital) share the same turnover form — both are entirely consumed and entirely replaced each cycle — Smith's framework treats them as categorically one: both are "circulating capital." The inclusive characterization of both forms as circulating capital abolishes this fundamental distinction — and "this was carried still further by later economists, who took the contrast between fixed and circulating capital as the basic and sole distinction, instead of distinguishing between variable and constant capital."

2.17 "No Fixed Capital Can Yield Any Revenue but by Means of a Circulating Capital"

Smith nonetheless provides, in passing, the correct connecting proposition: "No fixed capital can yield any revenue but by means of a circulating capital. The most useful machines and instruments of trade will produce nothing without the circulating capital which affords the materials they are employed upon, and the maintenance of the workmen who employ them." Marx interprets: "Here we see what the earlier expressions 'yield a revenue', 'make a profit', etc. really mean, i.e. that both parts of capital serve in the formation of products."

2.18 The Farmer's Examples: Draught Cattle, Fodder, Fattening Cattle, Seed

Smith now offers a sequence of agricultural examples, which Marx works through case by case.

Draught cattle and implements (fixed). "That part of the capital of the farmer which is employed in the implements of agriculture is a fixed, that which is employed in the wages and maintenance of his labouring servants is a circulating capital." Marx grants this is "correctly related simply to the difference in circulation, to the turnover of different components of the productive capital."

Cattle as value (the correct kernel). Smith: "He makes a profit of the one by keeping it in his own possession, and of the other by parting with it. The price or value of his labouring cattle is a fixed capital in the same manner as that of the instruments of husbandry." Marx flags this as "the further correct assertion that it is value to which the distinction refers, and not the material element." This is Smith almost reaching the correct, value-theoretic criterion.

Fodder (circulating) and the draught-cattle distinction. Smith: "Their maintenance [that of the labouring cattle] is a circulating capital, in the same manner as that of the labouring servants. The farmer makes his profit by keeping the labouring cattle, and by parting with their maintenance." Marx glosses: "The farmer keeps the cattle's fodder, he doesn't sell it. He needs it as cattle-fodder while he uses the cattle themselves as instruments of labour. The distinction is simply that the cattle-fodder that enters the maintenance of the draught cattle is completely consumed and must be constantly replaced by new cattle-fodder from the agricultural product or its sale, while the cattle themselves are replaced only to the extent that each animal in succession becomes incapable of further work." Note the pattern: fodder = fluid, because wholly consumed and wholly replaced; cattle = fixed, because replaced only piecemeal.

Fattening cattle (circulating). Smith: "Both the price and the maintenance of the cattle which are brought in and fattened, not for labour, but for sale, are a circulating capital. The farmer makes his profit by parting with them." Marx objects on two grounds. First, the triviality: "Every commodity producer, and thus the Capitalist producer as well, sells his product... but this does not make the product either a fixed or a fluid component of his productive capital. It now exists rather in a form in which it has been ejected from the production process and must function as commodity capital." Second — and this is the substantive point — the real reason fattening cattle are fluid is not that they are sold, but that "fattening cattle function in the production process as raw material, not as an instrument like draught cattle. They therefore enter the product as substance, and their entire value enters the product, just as that of the ancillary materials — their fodder." The fact that the sold product (fattened cattle) shares its natural form with the raw material (unfattened cattle) "is a mere accident." And Marx drives home the lesson: "At the same time, Smith should have been able to see from this example that it is not the material shape of the element of production that makes the value contained in it fixed or fluid, but rather its function within the production process."

Seed (fixed, in Smith's absurd account). Smith: "The whole value of the seed, too, is properly a fixed capital. Though it goes backwards and forwards between the ground and the granary, it never changes masters, and therefore does not properly circulate. The farmer makes his profit not by its sale, but by its increase." Marx's rejoinder is withering: "Here the utter shallowness of Smith's distinction comes into the open. In his conception, the seed is fixed capital because there is no 'change of masters', i.e. the seed is directly replaced out of the annual product, subtracted from it. It would be circulating capital, however, if the entire product were sold and new seed-corn were bought with one part of the product's value. In the one case there is a 'change of masters', in the other case not. Here Smith confuses fluid capital with commodity capital."

Marx then corrects the matter in two moves. "Whether the seed is directly subtracted from the product, or whether the whole product is sold and a part of its value is replaced by the acquisition of new seed, what occurs in both cases is no more than a replacement, and no profit is made by this replacement. In the one case the seed passes into circulation as a commodity along with the rest of the product, while in the other case it figures only in the book-keeping as a component of the value of the capital advanced. In both cases, however, it remains a fluid component of the productive capital. It is completely consumed in preparing the product, and it must be completely replaced out of this if reproduction is to be made possible." Thus Smith's book-keeping (whether seed is "subtracted" or "bought back") is irrelevant to the capital-theoretic question of whether the seed is fixed or fluid.

2.19 The Volume 1 Citation: Means of Labour Persist in Form

Marx supports this with a citation from Capital Volume 1, Chapter 8 (p. 311): "Hence raw material and auxiliary substances lose the independent form with which they entered into the labour process. It is otherwise with the actual instruments of labour. Tools, machines, factory buildings and containers are only of use in the labour process as long as they keep their original shape, and are ready each morning to enter into it in the same form. And just as during their lifetime, that is to say during the labour process, they retain their shape independently of the product, so too after their death."

Marx then characterizes Smith's double falsification of this genuine labour-process distinction: he "(1) introduces what is here the quite inapposite characteristic that some means of production bring their owner profit by maintaining their shape, others by losing it; (2) lumps the alterations suffered by one part of the elements of production in the labour process together with the change of form pertaining to the exchange of products, to commodity circulation (buying and selling), which at the same time includes the change of ownership of the commodities in circulation."

2.20 Turnover, Direct Replacement, and the Seed's Book-Keeping

Marx pauses over the concept of turnover: "Turnover implies that re-production is mediated by circulation, i.e. by the sale of the product, by its transformation into money and transformation back from money into its own elements of production." But there is an important special case: "in so far as a part of his product again directly serves the same capitalist producer as means of production, the producer appears as selling this to himself; this is how the matter figures in his book-keeping. This part of reproduction is then not mediated by circulation, but directly." And this direct-replacement case concerns fluid capital, not fixed: "The part of the product that serves again in this way as means of production replaces fluid capital, not fixed, in so far as (1) its value goes completely into the product and (2) it is itself replaced completely in kind by a new item from the new product." So the "seed is fixed because it never changes masters" argument is exploded once we see that direct replacement is a feature of fluid capital's turnover.

2.21 Capital as Function, Not Thing: The House Example and the Malleability of Roles

Smith then lists the things that constitute fixed and circulating capital, "as if this characteristic belonged to these things materially, by nature, and did not rather derive from their specific function within the capitalist production process." Yet — Marx notes the inconsistency — Smith himself says in the same chapter that a house reserved for direct consumption "may yield a revenue to its proprietor, and thereby serve in the function of a capital to him, it cannot yield any to the public, nor serve in the function of a capital to it, and the revenue of the whole body of the people can never be in the smallest degree increased by it." Marx comments: "Here Adam Smith clearly asserts that the property of being capital cannot be attributed to things as such and under all circumstances, but is rather a function with which they are or are not endowed according to the given conditions. But what is true of capital in general is also true of its subdivisions."

Thus the same things may be fixed or fluid depending on function. "Cattle used as draught-cattle (means of labour), for example, form a material mode of existence of fixed capital, while as fattening cattle (raw material) they are a component part of the farmer's circulating capital." And further, "the same thing can function at one time as a component of productive capital, and at another time form part of the direct consumption fund. A house, for example, when it functions as a place of work, is a fixed component of productive capital; when it functions as a dwelling, it is in no way a form of capital in this capacity. The same means of labour can in many cases function at one time as means of production, at another time as means of consumption."

Marx draws two consequences. First, the error bequeathed by Smith is "that of taking fixed and circulating capital as characteristics attributable to things." The analysis of the labour process (Volume 1, Chapter 7) has shown "how the determinations of means of labour, material of labour and product change according to the various roles that one and the same thing assumes in the process." The characteristics of fixed and non-fixed capital are built on these roles but are not reducible to them — they are built on "the particular roles that these elements play in the labour process and hence in the process of value formation." Second, in Smith's enumeration, "the distinction between fixed and fluid components, which is only valid, and only has any meaning, in relation to productive capital... [is] lumped together... with the distinction between productive capital and the forms pertaining to capital in its circulation process: commodity capital and money capital."

2.22 Smith's Four-Part List of Circulating Capital: A Catalogue of Confusions

Smith says (p. 378): "The circulating capital consists... of the provisions, materials, and finished work of all kinds that are in the hands of their respective dealers, and of the money that is necessary for circulating and distributing them..." Marx reads this as confirming that Smith again equates circulating capital with commodity capital and money capital — "two forms of capital that do not belong to the production process at all, which are not circulating (fluid) capital in opposition to fixed, but rather circulation capital in opposition to productive capital." Only "alongside these" do the genuinely productive components (raw material, semi-manufactured goods) figure.

Smith's fuller statement at pp. 377–8 lists four parts of "circulating capital": 1. Money — "of which the characteristic is, that it affords a revenue only by circulating or changing masters." 2. Stock and provisions in the hands of "the butcher, the grazier, the farmer," etc., "from the sale of which they expect to derive a profit." 3. Materials — "whether altogether rude, or more or less manufactured, of clothes, furniture, and building, which are not yet made up... but which remain in the hands of the growers, the manufacturers, the mercers and drapers, the timber merchants, the carpenters and joiners, the brick-makers etc." 4. Finished work — "the work which is made up and completed, but which is still in the hands of the merchant or manufacturer."

Marx's commentary is systematic. On money (part 1): "money is never a form of productive capital, capital functioning in the production process. It is never more than one of the forms which capital assumes within its process of circulation." On parts 2 and 4 (provisions, finished work): These "simply contain products that have been ejected from the production process as such and have to be sold; in short, products that now function as commodities and hence as commodity capital — i.e. possess a form and assume a position in the process in which they do not constitute an element of productive capital, whatever may be their eventual destination, i.e. whether their purpose (use-value) finally fits them for individual or for productive consumption." On part 3 (materials): This is the most revealing, because it appears in a double aspect — first as finished commodities in the general social product alongside provisions, and second "as incorporated into productive capital... in the hands of the producer." The confusion shows itself in the way raw materials are conceived "as functioning both in the hands of the producer... and in the hands of merchants ('mercers, drapers, timber merchants'), where they are mere commodity capital, not components of productive capital."

Smith also demonstrates the confusion in his treatment of the merchant: "If the producer has sold his product to the merchant, this no longer constitutes capital of his in any form. From the social point of view, however, it is still just as much commodity capital, even if in other hands than those of its producer. But precisely because it is commodity capital, it is neither fixed nor fluid capital."

Marx then states the general truth that Smith loses sight of: "In every production not directed towards satisfying the producer's own immediate needs, the product must circulate as a commodity, i.e. be sold, not so that a profit may be made on it, but simply so that the producer may live. In the case of capitalist production, the sale of the commodity also realizes the surplus-value contained in it. The product passes out of the production process as a commodity, and is therefore no longer either a fixed or a fluid element of this process."

2.23 Smith Refutes Himself: The Same Things Change Category Upon Sale

Smith's finished products, "whatever may be their material shape or use-value... are all commodity capital." "Because they exist in this form, they cannot constitute a component of their owner's productive capital; but this in no way prevents them, once they are sold, from becoming components of productive capital, whether fluid or fixed, in the hands of their buyer. It is evident here that the same things that enter the market at one time as commodity capital in opposition to productive capital may function as either fluid or fixed components of productive capital, or as neither, once they are withdrawn from the market."

Marx illustrates with the cotton spinner. The spinner's product, yarn, "is the commodity form of his capital, commodity capital for him. It cannot function again as a component of his productive capital, either as material of labour or as means of labour. The weaver who buys it, however, incorporates it into his productive capital, as a fluid part of this. For the spinner, on the other hand, the yarn is the bearer of the value of a part of both his fluid and his fixed capital (we ignore surplus-value)." Likewise a machine: for the machine-builder it is commodity capital, "and as long as it persists in this form, it is neither fluid nor fixed capital. When sold to a manufacturer who puts it to use, it becomes a fixed component of a productive capital." Even the case where a product re-enters its own production — "as when coal re-enters the production of coal" — does not change the category: "the part of the coal product destined for sale still represents neither fluid nor fixed capital, but rather commodity capital."

And in the extreme case: a means of subsistence is "completely incapable of forming any element of productive capital." "It is none the less commodity capital for its producer, the bearer of value of both the fixed and the fluid capital; and in the proportion that the capital bestowed on its production must be completely or partially replaced, its value has been transferred wholly or partly to it."

Marx then diagnoses the specific confusion in Smith's "third case" (materials): they appear "in the first place not as a component already incorporated into productive capital, but in fact only as a special kind of those use-values, the mass of commodities, which the social product consists of in general, alongside the other material components, means of subsistence, etc. listed in the second and fourth cases. Secondly, however, they are also presented as incorporated into productive capital, and hence as elements of the latter in the hands of the producer." Smith's list thus "completely forgets the distinction between fixed and fluid capital, which is applicable only to productive capital. Instead he counterposes commodity capital and money capital... to productive capital, although even this he does unconsciously."

2.24 The Forgotten Labour-Power: Why Smith Cannot See Variable Capital

Marx: "A final striking thing is that Adam Smith forgets labour-power in his list of the components of circulating capital." He gives two reasons.

Reason one: labour-power on the market is not capital. "We have already seen that, leaving aside money capital, circulating capital is [for Smith] only another name for commodity capital." But "in so far as labour-power circulates on the market, it is not capital, and so not a form of commodity capital. It is not capital at all; the worker is not a capitalist, even though he brings a commodity to market, i.e. his own skin. It is only when labour-power has been sold and incorporated into the production process — i.e. after it has ceased to circulate as a commodity — that it becomes a component of productive capital: variable capital as the source of surplus-value, a fluid component of the productive capital in relation to the turnover of the capital value laid out on this." This is a compressed statement of Marx's entire theory: labour-power enters productive capital as variable capital — the source of surplus-value — while being, in its turnover, a fluid component.

Reason two: Smith substitutes means of subsistence for labour-power. "Because Smith confuses fluid capital with commodity capital, he cannot bring labour-power under his heading of circulating capital. Variable capital thus appears here in the form of the commodities that the worker buys with his wages, the means of subsistence. It is in this form that the capital value laid out on wages is supposed to form part of the circulating capital." Marx's rebuttal: "But what is incorporated into the production process is labour-power, the actual worker, and not the means of subsistence with which the worker maintains himself." He concedes that, "considered from the society's standpoint, the reproduction of the worker himself by his individual consumption forms part of the reproduction process of the social capital" (citing Volume 1, Chapter 23), "but this does not hold for the individual production process taken by itself, which is what we are considering here." He adds the pointed aside — with Smith's notion that "acquired and useful abilities" (p. 377) belong to fixed capital — that these "form on the contrary components of fluid capital once they are 'abilities' of the wage-labourer, who has sold his abilities together with his labour."

2.25 The Great Error: Dividing Social Wealth into Consumption Fund / Fixed / Circulating

Marx now elevates the critique to the level of social wealth. "It is a great error on Smith's part that he divides up the whole social wealth into (1) immediate consumption fund, (2) fixed capital and (3) circulating capital." Under this scheme, "wealth would be divided into a consumption fund that does not form a part of the functioning social capital, although parts of it may always function as capital, and capital. One part of the wealth accordingly functions as capital, the other part as non-capital or a consumption fund." And it appears "as an indispensable necessity for all capital to be either fixed or fluid, just as a mammal is by natural necessity either male or female." The biological analogy is deliberately derisory: it naturalizes a historically specific distinction and treats it as exhaustive. But "the opposition of fixed and fluid is only applicable to the elements of productive capital, and... alongside this there is still a very significant amount of capital — commodity capital and money capital — which exists in a form in which it cannot be either fixed or fluid."

Marx then offers a corrective on the circulation of the social product: "Since, with the exception of the part of the product that is directly used in its natural form as means of production by the individual capitalist producer himself, without sale or purchase, the entire mass of social production — on the capitalist basis — circulates on the market as commodity capital, it is clear that both fixed and fluid elements of productive capital, and, in addition, all elements of the consumption fund, are drawn from the commodity capital." This is "saying no more than that both means of production and means of consumption first appear, on the basis of capitalist production, as commodity capital... just as labour-power itself is found on the market as a commodity, even if not as commodity capital."

2.26 Smith's "Fixed Capital Derives from Circulating Capital" — and Its Self-Refutation

Smith's doctrine at p. 378 — that "three [provisions, materials, finished work]... are, either annually, or in a longer or shorter period, regularly withdrawn from it [circulating capital], and placed either in the fixed capital or in the stock reserved for immediate consumption. Every fixed capital is both originally derived from, and requires to be continually supported by a circulating capital" — draws Marx's full counter-argument.

Marx grants the truism: "All products come onto the market as commodities... whether the natural or use-form of these products means that they can or must function as means of production... or whether they can serve only as means of individual rather than productive consumption." Thus "all means of production and consumption, all elements of productive and individual consumption, must therefore be withdrawn again from the market as commodities, by purchase." And this "holds good equally for the fixed and for the fluid elements of productive capital, for means of labour as well as material of labour in all forms." Marx adds a caveat in parentheses — "(It is still overlooked here that there are elements of productive capital which are given by nature, and are not products.)" — which is a nod to his own mining example: land, ore deposits, and natural forces enter productive capital without having been commodities.

From the truism, nothing follows about fixed capital deriving from fluid capital. "The machine is bought on the market as much as the cotton is. But it in no way follows from this — it follows only from Smith's confusion of circulation capital with circulating or fluid, i.e. non-fixed capital — that every fixed capital originally derives from a fluid one."

Marx then turns Smith against himself. "According to him, machines, as commodities, belong to the fourth part of the circulating capital. That they derive from the circulating capital thus only means that they functioned as commodity capital before they functioned as machines, although materially they derive from themselves; just as cotton as a fluid element of the spinner's capital derives from cotton on the market." And if Smith means, more substantively, that machines are made from labour and raw material, "it is still the case, firstly, that means of labour, i.e. fixed capital, are necessary to make machines, and secondly, too, that fixed capital — machinery, etc. — is necessary in order to make raw materials, since productive capital always includes means of labour, but not always material of labour." Smith himself concedes as much: "Lands, mines, and fisheries, require all both a fixed and a circulating capital to cultivate them" — "he thus concedes that fixed capital is needed to produce raw material, as well as circulating capital." But then Smith slides into a "new muddle": "and their produce replaces with a profit, not only those capitals, but all the others in society." Marx's assessment: "This is totally confused. Their product supplies the raw material, ancillaries, etc. for all other branches of industry. But their value does not replace the value of all other social capitals; it replaces only its own capital value (plus surplus-value). Here again Smith is looking back to the Physiocrats."

2.27 Mobility, Immobility, and the Fixity of "Fixed Capital"

Marx now considers the important qualification that a product's physical mobility affects whether it can ever function as fixed capital, without thereby becoming the criterion of fixed capital.

From the social standpoint, "the part of commodity capital that consists of products that can only serve as means of labour, also functions sooner or later as means of labour — otherwise the products will have been produced to no avail, will be unsaleable." On the capitalist basis, "once they have ceased to be commodities, they must form actual elements of the fixed part of the social productive capital, which they already were prospectively." But "there is a distinction here which arises from the natural form of the product."

Consider a spinning machine. It "has no use-value if it is not used for spinning, i.e. does not function as an element of production, and thus, from the capitalist standpoint, as a fixed component of a productive capital. But the spinning machine is mobile. It can be exported from the country where it is produced and be sold, directly or indirectly, to a foreign country... In the country where it was produced it then functions only as commodity capital, but never, not even after its sale, as fixed capital."

Now consider immobile products — "products that have been localized by being incorporated into the earth, and hence can only be used locally, e.g. factory buildings, railways, bridges, tunnels, docks etc., soil improvements, and so on." They "cannot be exported body and soul. They are immobile. If they are not to be useless, they must function after their sale as fixed capital in the country in which they were produced." For the speculative builder, these things are "the form of his commodity capital, and so according to Smith the form of his circulating capital. But from the society's standpoint, they must ultimately function as fixed capital, if they are not to be useless, in the country in question."

Marx guards against a hasty inference: "It in no way follows from this that immobile objects as such are automatically fixed capital; they may be dwelling-houses, etc. that belong to the consumption fund and thus do not form part of the social capital at all, even though they form an element of the social wealth, of which capital is only one part. The producer of these things, to express ourselves in Smith's terms, makes a profit by their sale. So they are circulating capital! The person who puts them to use, their ultimate buyer, can use them only by employing them in the production process. So they are fixed capital!"

The railway illustrates the dissociation of legal form from physical function: "Property titles to a railway, for instance, can change hands daily, and their owners can even make a profit by selling them abroad. The property titles are thus exportable, but the railway itself is not. It is no less the case, however, that these things must either function as the fixed component of a productive capital in the actual country where they are located, or else lie idle." Similarly, "manufacturer A can make a profit by selling his factory to manufacturer B, but this does not prevent the factory from functioning now as before as fixed capital."

The general conclusion is stated with the precision of a logical theorem: "The locally fixed means of labour... must necessarily function prospectively as fixed capital in the country in question. But it in no way follows, conversely, that fixed capital necessarily consists of immovable objects." Marx offers two counter-examples: - Mobile yet fixed: "A ship and a locomotive operate only by moving, yet they function as fixed capital for their users, even if not for their producers." - Immobile within production yet fluid: "Things on the other hand that are most fully fixed in the production process, live and die in it, and never leave it after they have once entered it, can be fluid components of productive capital. For example, the coal that drives the machine in the production process, the gas consumed in lighting a factory building, etc. These are fluid not because they physically leave the production process along with the product, and circulate as commodities, but rather because their value enters completely into the value of the commodity that they help to produce, and must thus be entirely replaced from the sale of the commodity." This is the positive criterion once more: fluidity is a matter of the completeness of value-transfer, not physical movement.

2.28 The Physiocratic Origins of Smith's Wage Conception — and Why It Doomed the Theory

Marx now returns to the phrase in Smith's passage: "A circulating capital, which furnishes... the maintenance of the workmen who make them" (the machines). "With the Physiocrats, the portion of capital advanced in wages figured correctly under the heading 'avances annuelles', as contrasted with 'avances primitives'. On the other hand, what appears with them as a component of the productive capital applied by the farmer is not labour-power itself, but rather the means of subsistence given to the agricultural workers ('the maintenance of the workmen', as Smith puts it)." Marx explains this as directly related to the Physiocratic doctrine.

"The portion of value which labour adds to the product (like the portion of value added by raw materials, instruments of labour, etc. — in short by the material components of the constant capital) is equal only to the value of the means of subsistence paid to the workers and necessarily consumed by them to maintain their function as labour-powers." The Physiocrats could not, on their own premises, find surplus-value in labour as such, because "if it is labour that produces surplus-value (as well as reproducing its own price), then it produces this in industry just as much as in agriculture. But since in the Physiocratic system labour produces surplus-value only in one branch of production, agriculture, surplus-value was not seen as arising from labour, but rather from the special activity (collaboration) of nature in this branch. It was for this reason that they saw agricultural labour as productive labour, in distinction from other kinds of labour." The very doctrine of the Physiocrats thus "prohibited them from discovering the distinction between constant capital and variable capital."

2.29 The Two Roots of Smith's Error on Wages

Marx then gives a landmark two-fold diagnosis of why Smith defines the workers' means of subsistence as circulating capital in opposition to fixed.

(1) The confusion of circulation capital with fluid capital. "He therefore confuses commodity capital with the fluid component of productive capital, and it is then self-evident that, where the social product takes the form of a commodity, the workers' means of subsistence, just like those of the non-workers — not to mention the materials and means of labour themselves — have to be supplied out of commodity capital."

(2) The Physiocratic conception creeps in. "But the Physiocratic conception also creeps in with Smith, although it contradicts the esoteric — genuinely scientific — part of his own theoretical presentation."

Marx then lays out the reasoning that exposes the substitution as a theoretical substitution with a definite function. "All capital advanced is converted into productive capital, i.e. it assumes the shape of elements of production which are themselves the product of earlier labour. (Including labour-power.) Only in this form can it function in the production process." Now, "if we substitute the worker's means of subsistence for the actual labour-power into which the variable part of capital has been transformed, then it is clear that these means of subsistence as such are not different from the other elements of productive capital as far as the formation of value is concerned, not different for example from raw materials and from the means of subsistence of draught cattle" — and Marx notes this is why Smith, following the Physiocrats, "puts these all on the same level in one of the passages quoted above" (cross-referencing p. 279). "The means of subsistence cannot themselves valorize their value or add to it a surplus-value. Their value, like that of the other elements of productive capital, can reappear only in the value of the product. They cannot add more value to it than they themselves possess."

By this substitution, labour-power is reduced to one more material element of constant capital, and the specific role of the capital laid out on it — that it is variable, self-valorizing — disappears. "They are only distinguished from the fixed capital, which consists of means of labour, in the same way as are raw material, semi-finished goods, etc., namely in that they are completely consumed in the product that they help to form... and their value must thus be completely replaced, whereas replacement occurs only gradually, bit by bit, in the case of fixed capital." Hence: "The part of productive capital advanced in labour-power (or the means of subsistence of the worker) is thus distinguished here only materially, and not with regard to the labour and valorization process, from the remaining material elements of the productive capital. It is only distinguished in that it falls into the category of circulating capital, along with one part of the objective elements of product formation ('materials' is Smith's general term for them), in opposition to another part of the objective elements that falls into the category of fixed capital."

2.30 The Decisive Separation: Fluidity in Turnover vs. Variability in Valorization

Marx then states the pivotal distinction with full clarity. "Although the part of capital spent on wages belongs to the fluid part of productive capital, and has this fluidity in common with a portion of the objective elements of product formation, the raw materials, etc., as opposed to the fixed component of productive capital, this has absolutely nothing to do with the role that this variable part of capital plays in the valorization process as opposed to the constant part." To be "fluid" is a fact about turnover: "It is simply related to how this part of the capital value advanced has to be replaced, renewed, and thus reproduced out of the value of the product, by way of circulation. The purchase and re-purchase of labour-power pertains to the circulation process." But "it is only within the production process that the value laid out on labour-power is transformed (not for the worker, but for the capitalist) from a definite, constant quantity into a variable one, and the value advanced in capital value, in capital, is thereby transformed for the first time into self-valorizing value."

The distinction between fluidity and variability is thus the hinge: it is possible for a portion of capital to be fluid without being variable (raw materials), and it is the defining property of the portion laid out on labour-power to be both fluid in turnover and variable in valorization. Because Smith replaces labour-power with means of subsistence, however, "it is impossible for him to understand the distinction between variable and constant capital, and thus the capitalist production process in general. The characteristic of this part of capital as variable capital in opposition to the constant capital laid out on the objective elements of product formation is buried underneath the characteristic that the part of capital laid out on labour-power belongs to the fluid part of the productive capital with respect to the turnover." And "this burial is made complete in so far as in place of labour-power it is the worker's means of subsistence that are counted as an element of productive capital." Marx adds a qualification: "Whether the value of the labour-power is advanced in money or in means of subsistence is immaterial, even though the latter can of course only be the exception on the basis of capitalist production."

2.31 The Legacy: The Labour-Fund Doctrine

The concluding paragraphs trace the historical consequences. "Because Adam Smith fixed in this way upon the characteristic of circulating capital as the decisive one for capital value laid out on labour-power — the Physiocratic definition without the premises of the Physiocrats — he managed to make it impossible for his successors to perceive that the part of capital laid out on labour-power was variable capital. The profound and correct explanation that he himself offered elsewhere did not prevail, whereas this blunder did." Marx then states the terminal drift of the doctrine: "Indeed, later writers went even further, and not only made it the decisive characteristic of the part of capital laid out on labour-power to be circulating capital in opposition to fixed, but also made it the fundamental characteristic of circulating capital to be laid out on means of subsistence for the worker. This naturally linked up with the doctrine of the labour fund of necessary means of subsistence as a given magnitude, which on the one hand physically restricts the share of the workers in the social product, but on the other hand has to be spent to its full extent on the acquisition of labour-power." The footnote reference is to Volume 1, pp. 758–61, where Marx discusses this "labour fund" doctrine.

The final footnote, at the very end of the chapter, closes the case against Smith with a quotation in which the reduction of labour-power to a mere natural input reaches its clearest expression. Marx writes: "How much Adam Smith barred his own way to an understanding of the role of labour-power in the valorization process is shown by the following sentence, which puts the labour of the worker on the same level as that of draught cattle, in the Physiocratic manner: 'Not only his [the farmer's] labouring servants, but his labouring cattle, are productive labourers' (Book Two, Chapter V, p. 462)." The chapter thus ends not with a mild observation but with a deliberate juxtaposition of worker and beast — the logical terminus of a theory that has substituted means of subsistence for labour-power and must, to be consistent, treat both labour-power and cattle-fodder as "productive" in identical fashion.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter's fundamental systematic laws and essential takeaways can be enumerated as follows.

(1) The law of the value-theoretic criterion. The distinction between fixed and circulating (fluid) capital is a distinction in the mode of value circulation through the labour-and-valorization process, not in the natural properties of things, nor in their physical mobility, nor in their legal relation to their owner, nor in the subjective experience of the individual capitalist ("profit by keeping" vs. "profit by parting with"). Fixed capital is fixed because a part of the value laid out on the means of labour remains fixed in them and transfers only bit by bit to the product, and thus must be replaced bit by bit. Fluid capital is fluid because its value enters the product wholly and at once and must therefore be wholly replaced each turnover. Consequently, the same thing can shift category as it shifts function: cattle as draught animals are fixed; the same cattle as fattening stock are fluid; a house functioning as a workplace is fixed capital; the same house as a dwelling is not capital at all.

(2) The law of the separateness of circulation forms from productive forms. The forms commodity capital (C) and money capital (M) belong to the sphere of circulation and are assumed successively by every capital value in its curriculum vitae; they are bearers of both fixed and fluid value components and cannot themselves be classed as either fixed or fluid. To confuse them with fixed/fluid capital is the foundational error of Smith's theory — the error of "circulating capital" equivocation.

(3) The law of the priority of the valorization distinction. Because labour-power and raw materials share the turnover characteristic of being wholly consumed and wholly replaced (both "fluid"), Smith's generic category "circulating capital" collapses them. But this destroys the ability to see that the portion of capital laid out on labour-power alone is self-valorizing — variable capital — while the portion laid out on raw materials is constant. The turnover similarity is superficial; the valorization difference is fundamental. Smith's category works against — not merely fails to reach — the correct analysis.

(4) The law of historical determination of categories by doctrine. Marx's final thesis is that economic categories are not innocent technical tools. The Physiocrats could not discover variable capital because their doctrine tied surplus-value to the productivity of nature in agriculture (the produit net); Smith could not discover it because he inherited their substitution of "means of subsistence" for "labour-power" and compounded it with the confusion of circulation forms with productive forms. The "labour fund" doctrine of Smith's successors is thus not a deviation from Smith but a logical development of his error.

The broader theoretical implications for Marx's system are substantial. First, the chapter demonstrates that Volume 2's analysis of turnover presupposes the Volume 1 analysis of valorization — that the fluids and fixities of turnover can only be properly understood once one has separated constant from variable capital. The chapter is thus the methodological bridge between the two volumes: it shows that the "turnover" categories, taken by themselves (as Smith took them), become ideological instruments that obscure the origin of surplus-value. Second, the chapter clarifies Marx's own vocabulary: "capital of circulation" is carefully distinguished from "circulating capital"; the difference matters because it preserves the distinction between a form of capital in a particular phase of its circuit and a component of productive capital. Third, the chapter prepares the ground for the later, completed analysis of the reproduction schemes, in which fixed and circulating capital must be carefully tracked in value terms, not in natural or book-keeping terms.

The practical or ideological implications are also clear. The "labour fund" doctrine that emerges from Smith's error is, as Marx specifies, not merely scholarly misclassification but a material doctrine: it treats the workers' means of subsistence as a fixed, pre-given quantity of capital, and hence, in effect, restricts the workers' wage to whatever that fund contains — a conclusion with direct implications for class struggle. The chapter thus documents, at the highest level of abstraction, the way that a technical error in classification is not neutral: it licenses a political conclusion that labour's share is naturally bounded. When Marx writes at the opening of the chapter that Smith's manner of treating capital "was carried still further by later economists, who took the contrast between fixed and circulating capital as the basic and sole distinction, instead of distinguishing between variable and constant capital," he is not merely correcting a taxonomy — he is exposing the doctrinal cost of an entire century of "scientific" economics.

Finally, the chapter is a philosophical lesson in the nature of abstraction. Marx does not simply reject Smith; he carefully separates Smith's "esoteric" insights from his "exoteric" formulations, preserving what is correct (the value-theoretic character of the fixed/fluid distinction, the correct observation that fixed capital can function only alongside circulating capital, the correct insight that land is needed to produce raw material) while criticizing the framing that reverses the truth. The chapter thus performs a paradigm of immanent critique: the errors are diagnosed out of Smith's own text, by using Smith's correct moments against his incorrect ones, and by showing that his categories, followed rigorously, generate absurdities (a machine is circulating for its maker and fixed for its user; seed is fixed and cattle are circulating on grounds that contradict the reasoned function of each). The example of the copper mine and the spinning factory, taken as extreme test cases, demonstrate both that Smith's classification yields the absurd result that an entire capital can be all fixed, and that fluid capital is just as "fixed in the process" as fixed capital — so that Smith's material criterion cannot generate the distinction. In this way the chapter demonstrates not just that Smith was wrong on a point, but that the whole habit of classifying capital by the natural properties of things is wrong — a lesson with implications far beyond the question of fixed and circulating capital, extending to every domain in which economic relations are naturalized. The final image — the worker on the same level as the draught ox, both classified as "productive labourers" — crystallizes the stakes: an error in the theory of capital becomes, at last, a falsehood about human beings.
