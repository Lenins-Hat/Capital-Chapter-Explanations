Volume 2 • Chapter 6: The Costs of Circulation

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem

Chapter 6 of Capital Volume 2 ("The Costs of Circulation") confronts a deceptively simple question that, once unpacked, opens onto the deepest structural fault lines of the capitalist mode of production: what does it cost capital to circulate, and who pays for that cost? Marx's central thesis is that the movements of capital in circulation—the buying and selling, the book-keeping, the money, the storage, the transport—do not create value. They consume value. But they consume it in fundamentally different ways, with fundamentally different consequences for the price of commodities and for the distribution of surplus-value. The chapter's intellectual objective is to sort these costs into logically distinct categories and to demonstrate that the distinction between "pure circulation costs" (which produce no value and enter no commodity's price) and "costs of storage and transport" (which, while unproductive from a social standpoint, nonetheless enter into the value of commodities and thereby take on a value-forming appearance) is not a matter of semantics but a matter of real, measurable economic consequence.

The Analytic Spine

The chapter proceeds through three sections that form a single argumentative arc:

  1. Pure circulation costs (buying and selling time, book-keeping, money) — costs that arise purely from the form of commodity production. They add nothing to the commodity's use-value, nothing to its value; they are faux frais of capitalist production, a deduction from the surplus product of society.

  2. Costs of storage — a heterogeneous category. To the extent storage arises from the form, it shares the character of pure circulation costs; to the extent it is a productive process continued in the sphere of circulation, it adds value and becomes a source of enrichment for the individual capitalist, even as it remains a pure deduction from the standpoint of social capital.

  3. Transport costs — perhaps the most conceptually startling section. Transport is a production process that happens to be located within the circulation sphere. It therefore genuinely adds value to commodities. This section serves to prove that spatial location within the circuit has no bearing on the productive or unproductive character of a labour process; only the theoretical relation to value-formation matters.

Intellectual Context Within Capital

This chapter is one of the most crucial hinges in Marx's entire critique. Having established in Chapter 4 (Volume 2) and Chapter 5 (the "Time of Circulation" section) that capital exists as a unity spanning the spheres of production and circulation, and having rigorously distinguished production time from circulation time, Marx here closes the circle by interrogating what happens in circulation time. His answer is that much of what occurs there is unproductive in the strict sense—it does not produce use-values, does not transfer value, does not produce surplus-value.

This distinction is foundational to Volume 3, where it explains the emergence of merchant's capital and the general rate of profit. The category faux frais — overhead—will be used to demonstrate that merchants extract a share of surplus-value not because their labour is productive but because their function is necessary to the realization of value already produced. The chapter is also a response, implicit and explicit, to the classical political economists—Adam Smith (whom Marx accuses of a "childish misunderstanding" about the stock), Quesnay (whose Physiocratic doctrine of "costs of trade" as the source of loss Marx cites approvingly), Ricardo (on transport and value), Say (whom Marx dismisses as a vulgarizer), and figures like Lalor and Sismondi (whom Marx acknowledges but finds one-sided on stock formation).

The Broader Theoretical Framework

The stakes could not be higher. If circulation costs did create value, then the entire labo(u)r theory of value would collapse: the amount of socially necessary labour time embodied in a commodity would depend not only on its production but on the costs of getting it to market—including the costs of the market itself. Marx insists this is not the case. Value is produced in production. Circulation merely realizes and redistributes it. But the circulation process itself is not free—it imposes real costs on society, and these costs are deducted from the surplus product before it can be distributed as profit, interest, and rent. The chapter thus demonstrates that the sphere of circulation is parasitical on the sphere of production, even as it is indispensable to it—a conclusion with profound consequences for political economy, for the theory of crises, and for the critique of apologetic economics.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Framing Problem: The Metamorphosis of Capital and the Question of Cost

The chapter opens from an observation established earlier in the volume: capital's circuit—the movement M-C...P...C'-M'—involves repeated changes of form. The commodities produced by industrial capital must be sold (C'-M') and the proceeds used to buy the elements of production again (M-C). Every one of these changes of form is, from the standpoint of the capitalist, a business transaction: an act of buying or selling, performed on the market, requiring time, effort, and often specialized personnel. The question Marx poses is what these transactions cost and how their cost is conceptualized within the value theory.

This framing is critical because it establishes that the circulation costs Marx investigates are not incidental frictions but structural features of a commodity-producing economy. Under capitalist production, virtually everything is produced as a commodity; hence virtually everything must pass through the metamorphosis C-M and M-C. The costs that arise from this passage are therefore inherent to the system, not contingent upon inefficiency, dishonesty, or bad management. They are, in Marx's phrase, faux frais — overhead costs — of commodity production in general.

But the investigation soon reveals that this general category faux frais conceals internal divisions of great theoretical importance. Some overhead costs are pure deductions. Others, while still "unproductive" in the strict sense, have the peculiar property of adding value to commodities. The chapter's structure is an exploration of this internal division, and its intellectual climax is the demonstration that the location of a labour process—inside or outside "circulation"—has no bearing on its productive or unproductive character.

I(a) Buying and Selling Time: The Unproductive Labour of Metamorphosis

The temporal and subjective character of circulation.

Marx begins with the simplest and most general of circulation costs: the time spent in buying and selling. Every change in the form of capital, considered from the standpoint of the capitalist, is a business transaction. The time required to complete it exists "subjectively, from the standpoint of the capitalist, as selling time and buying time"—that is, as time during which he functions on the market, prowling around for buyers and sellers. This time is a necessary part of his reproduction time, just as the circulation time of capital is a necessary part of its total turnover time. It is part of what Marx calls business hours, in contrast to the hours during which the capitalist functions as the organizer of production.

This distinction between business hours and production hours—and, correspondingly, between the traveling salesman and the factory manager—is not merely descriptive. It encodes a theoretical claim: the time the capitalist spends in circulation is time during which his capital is not producing. The capital is tied up, its valorization is suspended, and the labour expended is, considered strictly, unproductive. The chapter's first section sets out to demonstrate this rigorously.

The value-conservation argument.

Marx immediately establishes the fundamental proposition that will govern the whole chapter: if commodities are sold at their values, the act of exchange changes only the form of value, not its magnitude. C-M and M-C are "a change of state." The amounts of value in the hands of buyer and seller remain unchanged; only the form in which the value exists has altered (from commodity to money, or vice versa). Even if the commodities are not sold at their values—if the exchange is unequal—the sum of converted values remains unaffected: "what is a plus for one side is a minus for the other." This is the zero-sum property of the exchange relation, a proposition with deep roots in the classical labour theory of value.

From this Marx draws a strong conclusion: the labour expended in bringing about this change of form—the labour of negotiation, of inspection, of bargaining, of the "struggle" in which each side seeks to gain the upper hand—does not create value. It is labour that accomplishes a metamorphosis but adds nothing to the product. The reference to the Greek proverb—"when Greek meets Greek then comes the tug of war"—is more than a colorful aside; it captures the agonistic character of market exchange, a struggle whose outcome is distributional (one side gains what the other loses) but never a net creation of value.

The combustion analogy.

Marx elaborates this thesis through an analogy that has become justly famous: the labour of circulation is like the work of combustion in lighting a fire. To use coal as fuel, you must combine it with oxygen and effect a transformation of its physical state—from solid carbon to gaseous carbon dioxide. This transformation costs a certain expenditure of energy, and that energy is not transformed into heat; it detracts from the heat. The "work of combustion" is a necessary moment of the combustion process, but it is not itself productive of the useful effect (heat) that the process aims at. In the same way, the labour of buying and selling is a necessary moment of the total process of capitalist reproduction, but it does not itself produce value. It consumes a portion of the available labor-time and energy that could otherwise have gone into production.

This analogy has profound implications. It implies that the greater the circulation time relative to production time, the more "energy" is diverted from production into the mere preparation of production's products for sale. The efficiency of capital is thereby reduced. It also implies that the capitalist's effort to "get the better" of his counterparty—however successful—cannot increase the total value available for distribution, only redistribute it.

Historical digression: the peasant's feast-day deferral.

Marx pauses to note that when the commodity owners are not capitalists but independent direct producers—peasants or artisans—the situation is qualitatively different in appearance, though identical in substance. Their buying and selling time is a deduction from their labour time, and they therefore seek to postpone it to feast days. This historical observation serves to establish the generality of the proposition: the unproductiveness of circulation labor is not a peculiar feature of capitalism; it is a feature of any economy in which producers must exchange. Capitalism merely intensifies and concentrates it.

The "transubstantiation" fallacy.

Marx now addresses an illusion that arises from the mere organization of circulation. Could the unproductive character of circulation labour be overcome by transferring it to specialists? If the industrial capitalist hires third parties to do his buying and selling—"instead of themselves performing the 'work of combustion', make this into the exclusive business of third parties paid by them"—does this change the character of the labour? No. "These third parties will certainly not put their labour-power at the disposal of the capitalists for the sake of their blue eyes." They must be paid, and their payment is a deduction from the surplus. Marx compares them to the rent collector of a landlord or the bank porter: their labour adds nothing to the magnitude of the rent or the gold they carry. The "miracle of transubstantiation"—the conversion of unproductive labour into value-creating labour—cannot be accomplished by any transposition or reorganization. The character of a labour process is determined by its content and its relation to value, not by the identity of the person performing it or the location of the firm within the division of labour.

This argument is crucial for Volume 3, where Marx will deal with merchant's capital. It establishes in advance that the merchant's profit cannot be explained by claiming that the merchant's labour is productive. Whatever the merchant's profit is, it must be a share of surplus-value produced elsewhere.

The merchant as a "machine" reducing useless energy.

Marx now introduces a nuance that could otherwise undermine the argument. One merchant—considered merely as the agent of formal transformation, as pure buyer and seller—may, by his operations, shorten the buying and selling time for many producers. He effectively concentrates the unproductive function into fewer hands and realizes economies of scale. Marx acknowledges this: the merchant "should then be considered as a machine that reduces the expenditure of useless energy, or helps to set free production time." This is important because it shows that Marx's argument is not a crude denunciation of merchants as parasites. The merchant does perform a socially useful function: he reduces the total amount of social labor-power and time that must be devoted to the unproductive function of circulation. By concentrating this function, he frees labor-time for productive use.

But—and here is the crux—this is precisely the character of a machine. A machine is not productive because it creates value; it is productive because it reduces the labor time required to produce a given product, thereby lowering the value of the product. A machine that reduces the labor time required for circulation similarly reduces the cost of circulation. But the machine itself is a deduction from the product; it is part of the constant capital that must be maintained. Similarly, the merchant is a deduction from the social product, even if—or precisely because—he reduces the magnitude of that deduction.

Marx then assumes, for simplicity, that the merchant (or circulation agent) is a wage-labourer. This assumption allows him to explore the peculiar position of circulation workers within the theory of surplus-value. The circulation worker expends his labor-power and labor-time in C-M and M-C. He lives off his wage, like a spinner or a pill-maker. He performs a necessary function, because the reproduction process itself includes unproductive functions. "But the content of his labour creates neither value nor products. He is himself part of the faux frais of production." His usefulness lies not in transforming an unproductive function into a productive one—which would be a miracle—but rather in the fact that "a smaller part of society's labour-power and labour time is now tied up in these unproductive functions."

The surplus labour of the circulation worker.

Marx now introduces a subtle and important point: even if the circulation worker is a wage-labourer, even one of the better paid, he still performs surplus labour. He may receive the value product of eight hours' labour and function for ten; the two hours of surplus labour that he performs "no more produce value than do his eight hours of necessary labour." But—and Marx draws three conclusions here—this surplus labour has peculiar properties.

First: from the social point of view, the person's labor-power is used up for ten hours in this circulation function. It is not available for anything else, including productive labour. Second: society does not count these two hours of surplus labour, although they are spent by the individual who performs them. Society does not appropriate by this means any additional product or value. But the costs of circulation that the worker represents are reduced by a fifth—from ten hours to eight. Society pays no equivalent for a fifth of this circulation time whose agent he is. Third: if it is the capitalist who employs these agents, then the circulation costs of his capital, which form a deduction from his receipts, are reduced by the non-payment of the two hours. For him, this is a positive profit, because the negative restriction on the valorization of his capital is reduced—that is, the deduction from his surplus is smaller than it would otherwise be.

This analysis is remarkable. It shows that even in the sphere of pure circulation—where no value is created—capital extracts surplus labour from its wage-labourers, and this extraction benefits the individual capitalist by reducing his costs. But it does not thereby create any additional social value; the surplus labour of circulation workers is, from the social point of view, simply unpaid time whose product does not exist. The capitalist class as a whole gains nothing in value terms from this arrangement; it merely distributes the necessary costs of circulation differently among its members, and it may thereby make the system of circulation cheaper in terms of labor-power consumed.

In contrast, as long as small independent commodity producers spend part of their own time in buying and selling, this simply presents itself as time spent in the intervals between their productive function, or as a loss in their production time. They bear the cost directly, in the form of reduced output.

The "machine that buys and sells" — a formal conclusion.

Marx draws a striking image to summarize the argument. If the scale of the capitalist's business forces or enables him to hire his own circulation agents as wage-labourers, "this does not affect the substance of the phenomenon." Labour-power and labour-time must be spent to a certain degree in the circulation process, in so far as this is a mere change of form. But this now appears as an additional outlay of capital. A part of the variable capital must be deployed in acquiring these labour-powers that function only in circulation. This capital advance creates neither products nor value. It proportionately reduces the scale on which the capital advanced functions productively. "It is the same as if a part of the product was transformed into a machine that bought and sold the remaining part of the product. This machine means a deduction from the product." It is not involved in the production process, although it can reduce the labour-power spent on circulation. It simply forms a part of the circulation costs.

The image of a "machine that bought and sold the product" is doubly significant. First, it emphasizes the non-productive character of circulation labor: the machine is part of constant capital, a deduction from the product, and the same is true of circulation labor-power. Second, it foreshadows later analyses (for instance, in Grundrisse and in the "Fragment on Machines") in which Marx explores the transformation of living labor into fixed capital and the consequences for the production of surplus-value. The circulation worker is, in a sense, a living component of the constant capital that capital must maintain in order to realize value—a human "machine" whose maintenance is a cost, not a source of value.

The section concludes with the statement of the fundamental law: "In all circumstances, the time taken here is a cost of circulation, which does not add anything to the values converted. It is a necessary cost for transferring these from the commodity form into the money form."

I(b) Book-keeping: Ideality, Symbolic Reflection, and Deduction

The function of book-keeping.

Marx turns from the physical activity of buying and selling to the ideational apparatus that accompanies it: book-keeping. Book-keeping requires pens, ink, paper, desks, and other office equipment—objectified labor—as well as living labor-power. Thus labor-time is spent in this function both as labor-power and as means of labor. The same general state of affairs obtains as with buying and selling time: the labor is unproductive, and the expenses are deductions.

But book-keeping has a distinctive philosophical dimension that Marx is eager to explore. Capital, considered as a unity within its circuits, as value in process, exists in the sphere of production or circulation only ideally—as money of account, "at first in the head of the commodity producer, capitalist or otherwise." Money of account—the unit in which prices are reckoned and accounts kept—is a purely ideal or symbolic existence of value. It is not gold or silver coin; it is the form in which value is represented for the purpose of calculation. By way of book-keeping, which also includes the determination or reckoning of commodity prices (price calculation), the movement of capital is registered and controlled. The movement of production, and particularly of valorization—in which commodities figure only as bearers of value, as the names of things whose ideal value-existence is set down in money of account—"thus receives a symbolic reflection in the imagination."

This is a dense passage. It says that capital, as a process, cannot be directly perceived in its totality. It must be represented—symbolically, through accounts, ledgers, balance sheets. The book-keeper performs this representation. The book-keeper is thus the consciousness of capital, its reflective moment, whose function is to maintain the ideal unity of a movement that is scattered across time and space (raw materials in the warehouse, goods in transit, money in the bank, contracts on the market).

The historical digression: monasteries and Indian communities.

Marx inserts a footnote (from Manuscript II) noting that in the Middle Ages agricultural book-keeping was found only in the monasteries, and that a book-keeper for agriculture already figured in the primitive Indian communities. In the latter, book-keeping gained an independent position as the exclusive function of a communal official. This division of labor saves time, energy, and expense, but production and the book-keeping of production "remain as separate as the cargo of a ship and the bill of lading." In the person of the book-keeper, a portion of the communal labor-power is withdrawn from production, and the costs of its function are replaced, not by the book-keeper's own labor, but by a deduction from the common product. "Just as with the book-keeper of the Indian community, so the same applies, mutatis mutandis, to the capitalist's book-keeper."

The historical digression serves two purposes. First, it shows that book-keeping is not a historically specific feature of capitalism but a feature of any large-scale, organized production. Second—and more importantly—it establishes that the unproductive character of book-keeping is a general feature of social accounting, not a peculiarity of capitalist accounting. The book-keeper's labor is necessary, socially useful, and unproductive in the value-theoretic sense. This is not a criticism of book-keeping; it is an analytical classification.

The scale argument.

Marx emphasizes that the nature of the function is "in no way changed by the scale that it assumes" when concentrated in the hands of a capitalist producer. Whether the function is performed by many small producers or by one large capitalist enterprise, whether it is torn loose from the productive functions to which it is an adjunct and becomes the independent function of special agents—none of this changes its character. "The division of labour, with one function becoming independent in this way, does not make this into a product- or value-forming function if it is not so in itself, and thus was already so before it became independent."

This is a general methodological principle in Marx's value theory: the formal reorganization of labor—its division, its specialization, its concentration—does not alter the fundamental question of whether it produces value. Value is determined by the quantity of socially necessary labor time embodied in a product, not by the organizational form in which the labor is performed.

The distinction between book-keeping and buying/selling time.

Marx now introduces a nuanced distinction within the category of pure circulation costs. The costs arising from book-keeping or unproductive expenditure of labor-time differ in one respect from those of mere buying and selling time.

The latter—buying and selling time—arise simply from the particular social form of the production process, from the fact that it is a process of production of commodities. If production were not commodity production—if products were not destined for exchange—there would be no buying and selling.

Book-keeping, however, "as the supervision and the ideal recapitulation of the process, becomes ever more necessary the more the process takes place on a social scale and loses its purely individual character; it is thus more necessary in capitalist production than in the fragmented production of handicraftsmen and peasants, more necessary in communal production than in capitalist." This is a remarkable claim. It suggests that book-keeping is not merely a consequence of the commodity form; it is a consequence of any large-scale, socialized production. Even in a future communist society—Marx implies—book-keeping would be more necessary than in fragmented handicraft production, though its costs would be reduced with the concentration of production and in proportion to its increasing transformation into social book-keeping.

Here Marx departs from a crude "circulation-costs-are-purely-capitalist" thesis. Book-keeping has a dual character: it arises partly from the necessity of supervising and recording any complex social process, and partly from the specific need to calculate prices and record transactions under commodity production. The former necessity would persist under socialism; the latter would not. Marx does not develop this thought, but its presence in the text is significant. It shows that his analysis of circulation costs is not a blanket condemnation of all non-production labor but a differentiated critique that distinguishes between costs specific to the commodity form and costs arising from the complexity of social production as such.

Concentration and reduction of costs.

Marx concludes the section by noting that the costs of book-keeping are reduced with the concentration of production and in proportion to its increasing transformation into social book-keeping. The development of large-scale industry, of standardized accounting practices, of banking, and of credit reduces the relative cost of book-keeping even as its absolute scale grows. This is an anticipation of a theme that will be developed more fully in the sections on stock formation and transport: the productivity of labor in the sphere of circulation can increase, thereby reducing the relative burden of circulation costs on social capital.

Marx gives a concrete example in the following section (though it is presented as a bridge): the simple case of the receipt and dispensing of money, once this has become independent as an exclusive function of banks or of cashiers in individual businesses, and is concentrated on a large scale. This illustrates how circulation costs, which in the case of the individual commodity producer are "only evanescent and scarcely noticeable moments that run alongside his production or are dovetailed in with it," may strike the eye as "massive circulation costs" when they become independent and concentrated. But—and this is the point—"these circulation costs do not change their character with their altered form." The scale at which a function is performed does not alter its theoretical classification.

I(c) Money: Gold and Silver as a Social Cost of Commodity Production

The specific character of the money commodity.

Marx now turns to the costs of money itself. Whether a product is produced as a commodity or not, it is always a material form of wealth, a use-value destined for individual or productive consumption. As a commodity, its value exists ideally in its price, which does not affect its actual use form. But certain commodities—gold and silver—function as money and, as such, "dwell exclusively in the circulation process." They remain in the circulation sphere as hoard, reserve, means of payment, even if only latently. This is "purely a product of the particular social form of the production process, as a process of commodity production."

The argument here is subtle. Gold and silver are commodities, produced by labor, with value. But their function as money is not a natural property of the metal; it is a social property conferred by the commodity form of production. Gold would be a useful metal even if it were not money (for jewelry, for industrial uses). But when gold functions as money, it functions as "a machine for circulation." It enters neither individual nor productive consumption. It is a part of social wealth that is confined to an unproductive form—a form in which it serves merely to facilitate the circulation of other commodities.

The growth and replacement costs of money.

Since, on the basis of capitalist production, the commodity is the general form of the product, the great mass of products are produced as commodities and must assume the money form. Since the mass of commodities—the part of social wealth functioning as commodities—is constantly growing, the quantity of gold and silver that functions as means of circulation, means of payment, reserve, etc. also increases. A growing portion of social wealth is tied up in the money form.

Furthermore, the wear and tear of money requires its steady replacement, or the transformation of more social labor—in the product form—into more gold and silver. These replacement costs are significant in nations where there is developed capitalism, because the part of wealth confined to the form of money is considerable. Marx cites The Economist (8 May 1847): "The money circulating in a country is a certain portion of the capital of the country, absolutely withdrawn from productive purposes, in order to facilitate or increase the productiveness of the remainder. A certain amount of wealth is, therefore, as necessary in order to adopt gold as a circulating medium, as it is to make a machine, in order to facilitate any other production."

The citation is revealing. The bourgeois economist of The Economist recognizes—as Marx does—that money is a deduction from productive capital, a necessary cost of a certain form of economic organization. But where The Economist sees this as a natural technical necessity, Marx sees it as a historically specific consequence of commodity production. Gold and silver "constitute for society costs of circulation that arise simply from the social form of production. They are faux frais of commodity production in general, which grow with the development of this production, and with capitalist production in particular. This is a part of the social wealth which has to be sacrificed to the circulation process."

Transition to the next section.

This completes Marx's analysis of pure circulation costs. The section has established that buying and selling time, book-keeping, and money are all costs that arise from the form of commodity production (and, in the case of book-keeping, also from the social scale of production). They add nothing to the value of commodities. They constitute deductions from the social product. They are faux frais.

But Marx now announces that the next category of circulation costs—those dealt with under "Costs of Storage"—is "different in nature." They can arise from production processes that are simply continued in the circulation sphere, and whose productive character is thus merely hidden by the circulation form. They may also be nothing but costs from the social point of view—unproductive expenditure of labor—but precisely because of this they still have a value-forming effect for the individual capitalist and form an addition to the selling price of his commodities. This is a crucial pivot. Marx is about to demonstrate that the appearance of value-formation can attach to costs that are, from a social standpoint, pure deductions—and that this appearance is not illusory from the standpoint of the individual capitalist, because competition distributes these costs across the price of commodities.

II. Costs of Storage: The Pivotal Distinction

Before examining the individual forms of storage costs, Marx lays out the central theoretical claim of the section. The circulation costs that proceed from the mere change in form of value "do not enter into the value of commodities." The portions of capital spent on them constitute mere deductions from the capital productively spent. But the costs of storage are different. They can arise from production processes continued in the circulation sphere (whose productive character is hidden by the circulation form), or they can be unproductive expenditures from the social point of view that nonetheless have a value-forming effect for the individual capitalist because they add to the selling price of his commodities.

Marx explains why this appearance of value-formation arises. It "follows from the simple fact that these costs differ between different individual capitals within the same production sphere. The act of adding them to the price of the commodity means that they become distributed in proportion to the degree to which they occur for the individual capitalist." This is a competitive mechanism: if all capitalists must incur certain costs, and if these costs vary across firms, then competition will tend to equalize the rate of profit by allowing the firm with higher costs to charge a higher price (or the firm with lower costs to earn a higher profit). The costs are thus "socially validated" through the price mechanism, even though they are not productive of value.

Marx then adds a crucial corollary: "all labour that adds value can also add surplus-value and will always add surplus-value on the basis of capitalism, since the value that it forms is dependent on its own extent, and the surplus-value that it forms is dependent on the extent to which the capitalist pays for it." This is the general law of surplus-value production. If a cost involves the employment of labor that adds value (as storage labor may, if it is a continuation of production), then it will also add surplus-value, because the capitalist will pay the laborer less than the value the laborer creates. Thus "while costs that make commodities dearer without increasing their use-value are faux frais of production from the social point of view, for the individual capitalist they can constitute sources of enrichment."

The corollary to this is equally important: "in so far as what they add to the price of the commodity merely distributes these circulation costs equally, they do not thereby cease to be unproductive in character." Marx gives the example of insurance companies: they "divide the losses of individual capitalists among the capitalist class. But this does not prevent the losses thus adjusted from being losses as before, from the standpoint of the total social capital."

This is a critical analytical distinction that Marx will use throughout the chapter. The social point of view and the individual capitalist's point of view diverge. Storage costs may enrich individual capitalists (by allowing them to charge higher prices) while remaining pure deductions from social wealth. The price mechanism does not create value; it only redistributes it.

II(a) Stock Formation in General

The dual existence of commodity capital.

Marx begins the analysis of stock formation with an observation about the circuit of capital. During its existence as commodity capital—its stay on the market, in the interval between the production process from which it emerges and the consumption process which it enters—the product forms a commodity stock. Commodity capital figures twice in each circuit: once as the commodity product of the actual capital in process whose circuit is under consideration, and once as the commodity product of another capital that must be present on the market in order to be sold and transformed into productive capital. In other words, for any given capital to sell its product and buy means of production, there must be a stock of commodities already on the market—produced by other capitals—waiting to be bought.

Marx acknowledges that it is possible for the latter commodity capital to be produced only to order, in which case there is an interruption until it has been produced. But the flow of the production and reproduction process requires that a mass of commodities (means of production) is constantly present on the market—that is, forms a stock. Productive capital similarly includes the purchase of labor-power, and the money form is here only the value form of the means of subsistence that the worker must find for the greater part on the market.

The involuntary character of the commodity stock and its costs.

Marx takes up the standpoint of a particular capital value that has been transformed into commodity product and must now be sold. For this capital, the state in which it forms a stock is "an inexpedient and involuntary stay on the market." The more quickly it is sold, the more fluid the reproduction process. The delay in the formal transformation (C-M) hinders the material change (M-C, the purchase of new means of production) that must occur in the circuit of capital, and thus its further functioning as productive capital. On the other hand, the constant presence of commodities on the market—the commodity stock—appears for M-C as the condition for the flow of the reproduction process and for the investment of new or additional capital.

There is thus a tension: the stock is a burden for the individual capital that holds it (it delays the return to production) but a precondition for the reproduction of capital as a whole. This tension mirrors the more general tension in Marx's analysis between the individual capitalist's perspective and the social perspective.

The material costs of maintaining a stock.

The persistence of commodity capital as a commodity stock requires buildings, stores, containers, warehouses—an outlay of constant capital. It equally requires payment for the labor-power employed in placing the commodities in their containers. Furthermore, commodities decay and are subject to the damaging influence of the elements. Additional capital must thus be expended to protect them from this, partly in objective form as means of labor, and partly in labor-power.

Marx now draws the distinction that defines the section: these circulation costs "are distinguished from those mentioned under heading 1 in as much as they do enter into the value of commodities to a certain extent, and thus make the commodities dearer." Under all circumstances, the capital and labor-power which serve to maintain and store the commodity stock are withdrawn from the direct production process. On the other hand, the capital employed here, including labor-power, must be replaced out of the social product. Hence this outlay has the same effect as a reduction in the productivity of labor, so that a greater quantity of capital and labor is required to obtain a specific useful effect. "These are simply expenses."

The dual character of storage costs.

Marx now articulates the distinction with precision. In so far as the costs of circulation made necessary by the formation of the commodity stock arise solely from the time taken to transform existing values from the commodity form into the money form—that is, only from the specific social form of the production process (only from the fact that the product is produced as a commodity and must therefore pass through a transformation into money)—they share exactly the same character as the circulation costs enumerated under heading 1. They are pure deductions.

On the other hand, however, the value of the commodities is conserved, or increased, only because the use-value—the product itself—is transferred under certain objective conditions that cost an outlay of capital, and subjected to operations in which additional labor works on the use-values. The calculation of the commodity values (book-keeping) and the buying and selling, on the contrary, do not operate on the use-value in which the commodity value exists; they are only concerned with its form.

Thus, "although in the case assumed here these expenses of stock formation (which is here involuntary) arise purely from a delay in the change of form and from the necessity for this change, they are nevertheless distinguished from the expenses under heading 1 in that their actual object is not the formal transformation of value, but the conservation of the value which exists in the commodity as a product, a use-value." The use-value is not increased or raised; on the contrary, it declines. But its decline is restricted, and it itself is conserved. The value that is advanced and exists in the commodity is also not increased here. But new labor, both objectified and living, is added to it.

This is the crux of the distinction. Storage labor is unproductive in the sense that it does not increase the use-value of the product—the product is decaying, and storage only slows the decay. But it is labor that operates on the product as a use-value, and it does add value (in the sense of transferring the value of the labor-power and means of production consumed) to the conserved product. The value added is, in a sense, a necessary addition to preserve the value already present. Storage labor is thus the labor of conservation, not of creation—but it nonetheless enters the value of the stored commodity.

Historical and comparative dimensions: Smith, Lalor, Sismondi.

Marx now poses a series of broader questions. How far do these expenses proceed from the particular character of commodity production in general? How far from commodity production in its universal, absolute form, i.e. capitalist commodity production? How far are they common to all social production and simply assume a particular shape, a specific form of appearance, within capitalist production?

To answer these questions, he engages with the history of economic thought. Adam Smith put forward the "incredible opinion" that the formation of a stock is a phenomenon peculiar to capitalist production. Later economists, e.g. Lalor, stressed that with the development of capitalist production, stock formation declines. Sismondi even regarded this as one of the negative features of capitalist production.

Marx's response is that all these positions are one-sided. In point of fact, stock exists in three forms: in the form of productive capital, in the form of the individual consumption fund, and in the form of the commodity stock or commodity capital. Stock declines relatively in one form when it increases in the other, although its absolute size may grow simultaneously in all three forms.

Marx then develops the argument in detail. Where production is oriented directly toward the satisfaction of the producers' own requirements, and only a small portion of goods are produced for exchange or sale—where the social product does not assume the commodity form, or does so only to a small extent—the commodity stock forms only a small and evanescent part of wealth. Here, however, the consumption fund, i.e. the fund of means of subsistence, is relatively large. Marx gives the example of the peasant economy of antiquity: an overwhelming part of the product was transformed directly, without forming a commodity stock, into a stock of means of production or means of subsistence, precisely because it remained in the hands of its possessor. Because it did not assume the form of a commodity stock, Adam Smith held that no stock existed in societies based on this mode of production. "Adam Smith thus confused the form of stock with the stock itself, and believed that society previously lived from hand to mouth, abandoning itself to the hazards of the next day. This is a childish misunderstanding."

The Indian famine evidence.

In a long footnote (from Manuscript II), Marx provides powerful empirical evidence for his claim that the transition from production for the producers' own needs to commodity production gives rise to "the most violent crises in the producers' economy." In India, "the disposition to hoard largely the grain for which little could be got in years of abundance" has been observed "right up to this day" (quoting Return. Bengal and Orissa Famine, House of Commons, 1867). The sudden increase in demand for cotton, jute, etc. as a result of the American Civil War led to a great limitation of rice cultivation in India, a rise in the price of rice, and the sale of old stocks of rice by the producers. On top of this, there was the unparalleled export of rice to Australia, Madagascar, etc. in 1864–6. Hence the acute character of the famine of 1866, which carried off a million people in Orissa alone. The report stresses "the drain of old stock" as one of the causes of the famine.

This footnote is important for several reasons. First, it provides concrete historical evidence for a theoretical claim about stock formation. Second, it illustrates the catastrophic consequences of the subordination of subsistence production to commodity production. Third, it shows that the transition from non-commodity to commodity production is not a smooth evolutionary process but a violent dislocation that can destroy the livelihoods of millions. The footnote is, in effect, a materialist history of the Indian famine, grounded in the categories of Marx's theory of circulation.

The growth of productive stock.

Marx now turns to the second form of stock: stock in the form of productive capital. This exists as means of production that are already engaged in the production process, or at least in the hands of the producer, i.e. latently already in the production process. As the productivity of labor develops, and thus with the development of the capitalist mode of production—which develops the social productivity of labor more than all previous modes of production—the mass of means of production that are incorporated once and for all in the process in the form of means of labor, and function repeatedly in it over a longer or shorter period (buildings, machines, etc.) constantly grows. This growth is both premise and effect of the development of the social productive power of labor.

The material forms of existence of the constant capital do not consist only of such means of labor, but also of material for labor at the most varied stages of elaboration, as well as ancillary materials. As the scale of production grows, and the productive power of labor grows through cooperation, division of labor, machinery, etc., so does the mass of raw material, ancillaries, etc. that go into the daily reproduction process. These elements must be ready to hand at the place of production. The extent of this stock in the form of productive capital thus grows absolutely.

For the process to keep flowing—quite apart from whether this stock can be renewed daily or only at definite intervals—there must always be a greater store of raw material, etc. at the place of production than is used up daily or weekly. The continuity of the process requires that the existence of its preconditions should depend neither on the possible interruption of daily purchases, nor on whether the commodity product is sold daily or weekly, and can therefore only irregularly be transformed back into its elements of production.

Four conditions determining the relative size of the productive stock.

Marx now identifies four conditions that determine the relative size of the productive stock, all of which "essentially all derive from the greater speed, regularity and certainty with which the necessary mass of raw material can be constantly supplied in such a way that no interruption arises."

First: The general development of capitalist production. The less these conditions are fulfilled, and the less therefore the certainty, regularity, and speed of the supply, the greater must be the latent part of the productive capital—the stock of raw materials, etc. in the hands of the producer and still waiting to be worked up. These conditions stand in inverse proportion to the level of development of capitalist production, and thus of the productive power of social labor. And so too, therefore, does the stock in this form.

But Marx immediately qualifies: "what appears here as a decline in the stock (e.g. with Lalor) is in part only a decline of stock in the form of commodity capital or of commodity stock proper; i.e. a mere change of form of the same stock." For example, if a great mass of coal is produced every day in the country in question, i.e. if the scale and intensity of coal production is large, then the mill-owner does not need a great store of coal in order to secure the continuity of his production. The constant and certain renewal of the coal supply makes this superfluous.

Second: The development of the means of transport and communication. The speed with which the product of one process can be transferred to another process as means of production depends on this. The cheapness of transport plays a great role. The constantly repeated transportation of coal, for example, from the mine to the spinning mill will be dearer than the storage of a larger amount of coal for a longer period, if transport is relatively cheap.

Third: The development of the credit system. The less dependent the mill-owner is for the renewal of his stocks of cotton, coal, etc. on the direct sale of his yarn—and the more developed the credit system, the smaller this direct dependence—the smaller the relative size of these stocks need be, in order to secure a continuous production of yarn independent of the accidents of its sale.

Fourth: The production period of raw materials. Many raw materials, semi-finished goods, etc. require lengthy periods of time for their production, and this holds in particular for all raw materials provided by agriculture. If there is to be no interruption of the production process, then a definite stock of these must be present for the whole period of time in which new products cannot replace old.

Marx then draws an important conclusion from the combination of these conditions: "If this stock in the hands of the industrial capitalist declines, this only means that it increases in the form of a commodity stock in the hands of the merchant." The development of the means of transport, for example, permits cotton lying in the import docks to be quickly delivered from Liverpool to Manchester, so that the manufacturer can renew his stocks of cotton in relatively small portions according to his needs. But then the same cotton exists in even greater amounts as a commodity stock in the hands of the Liverpool merchants. "There is thus simply a change in the form of the stock, which Lalor and others have overlooked." If we consider the social capital, there is the same quantity of products as before in the form of stock. The apparent decline in stock is an illusion generated by a change in its social form—from productive stock in the hands of the industrialist to commodity stock in the hands of the merchant.

For an individual country, the scale on which the quantity needed for the year must be held ready declines with the development of the means of transport. If there are many steamships and sailing ships plying between America and Britain, then the opportunities for Britain to renew its cotton stock are increased, and thus the average volume of the cotton stock that Britain must keep in store declines. The development of the world market and the consequent multiplication of sources of supply for the same article has the same effect. The article is supplied bit by bit from different countries and at different points in time.

II(b) The Commodity Stock Proper

The growth of the commodity stock with capitalist production.

Having established that the apparent decline in productive stock is largely a change of form, Marx now turns to the commodity stock proper. On the basis of capitalist production, the commodity becomes the general form of the product, and the more so, the more this production develops in scale and depth. Thus a far greater part of the product exists as a commodity, even at the same scale of production, in comparison either with earlier modes of production, or with the capitalist mode of production itself at a less developed stage.

Every commodity—and thus every commodity capital—in so far as it does not directly pass from the sphere of its production into productive or individual consumption, and finds itself on the market during the interval, forms an element of the commodity stock. In and for itself—assuming the scale of production is constant—the commodity stock therefore grows with capitalist production. This is only a change of form for the stock: it increases in commodity form because it decreases in the form of direct production or consumption stock. There is simply a changed social form of the stock. If at the same time there is an increase not only in the relative size of the commodity stock, in relation to the total social product, but also in its absolute size, this is because the volume of the total product increases with capitalist production.

The determination of production by capital, not by demand.

Marx now introduces a crucial dynamic factor. As capitalist production develops, the scale of production is determined to an ever lesser degree by the immediate demand for the product, and to an ever greater degree by the scale of the capital which the individual capitalist has at his disposal, by his capital's drive for valorization and the need of his production process for continuity and extension. The mass of products from every particular branch of production that are on the market as commodities, or seek an outlet, necessarily grows together with this. The mass of capital tied up for a shorter or longer time in the form of commodity capital grows, and hence the commodity stock grows as well.

This is a crucial point for the theory of crises. Under capitalist production, production is not directly determined by need or demand; it is determined by the drive for valorization and by the scale of capital already accumulated. The result is that the commodity stock grows unavoidably with capitalist development—not because of inefficiency or speculation, but because of the structural logic of capital accumulation. The market must always be oversupplied relative to immediate demand, because production runs ahead of demand in the pursuit of profit.

The proletarianization of the population and the consumption fund.

Marx adds a further dimension: ultimately, most members of society are transformed into wage-labourers, people who live from hand to mouth, who receive their wages by the week and spend them by the day, and must thus find their means of subsistence available as a stock. "However rapidly the particular elements of this stock may flow, a part of them must always stand still in order for the stock to remain in motion." The transformation of the population into wage-labourers—who have no means of subsistence of their own and must buy everything they consume—magnifies the commodity stock, because the entire consumption fund of the working class must now exist as a commodity stock on the market.

Storage costs and their social character.

Whatever the social form of the stock of products, its storage involves costs: buildings, containers, etc. which form receptacles for the product; similarly means of production and labor, more or less according to the nature of the product, which must be spent to ward off damaging influences. The more these stocks are socially concentrated, the smaller, relatively speaking, are the costs. These outlays always form part of social labor, whether in objectified or living form—thus in the capitalist form they are outlays of capital—which do not go towards the formation of the product itself, and are thus deductions from it. They are necessary expenditures of social wealth, for they are the costs of conserving the social product, whether its existence as an element of the commodity stock arises merely from the social form of production, i.e. from the commodity form and its necessary transformations, or whether we consider the commodity stock simply as a special form of the stock of products common to all societies, even if not in the form of a commodity stock, this particular form of stock pertaining to the circulation process.

The question of value: do storage costs enter into commodity value?

Marx now poses the decisive question: "The question now arises as to what extent these expenses enter into the value of commodities."

He answers by considering the case of a capitalist who has transformed his advanced capital into products ready for sale, and these remain in store unsold. It is not only the valorization process of his capital that is held up during this time. The expenditures that the conservation of this stock requires in buildings, additional labor, etc. form a positive loss. The eventual purchaser would laugh at the capitalist if he said: "I could not sell my commodity for six months, and it not only cost me so and so much in idle capital to maintain it for these six months, but also caused expenses x." "So much the worse for you," the buyer will say, "for next to you there is another seller whose commodity was finished only yesterday. Your commodity is evidently a white elephant, and probably more or less damaged by the ravages of time. You must therefore sell cheaper than your rival."

This vivid passage establishes a crucial point: the expenses of stock formation, in so far as the formation of a stock is a hold-up in circulation, add no value to the commodity. The buyer does not pay for the circulation time of the commodity. Even if the capitalist deliberately keeps his commodity off the market, in times of a real or anticipated revolution in values, it depends on whether this revolution actually comes about, on the correctness or incorrectness of his speculation, whether he realizes his additional expenses. The revolution in values is not the result of his expenses.

On the other hand, there can be no stock without a delay in the circulation sphere, without the capital persisting for a longer or shorter period in its commodity form; thus there can be no stock without a hold-up in circulation, just as no money can circulate without the formation of a money reserve. "That is to say, without the commodity stock, no commodity circulation." If the capitalist does not encounter this necessity in C'-M', then he encounters it in M-C; not for his own commodity capital, but for the commodity capital of other capitalists, who produce means of production for him and means of subsistence for his workers.

Voluntary vs. involuntary stock formation.

Marx now makes a distinction between voluntary and involuntary stock formation. Whether the formation of a stock is voluntary or involuntary makes no essential difference to the matter, but "it is useful to know, as a contribution towards solving this question, what it is that distinguishes voluntary from involuntary stock formation."

The involuntary formation of a stock arises from, or is identical with, a hold-up in circulation that is independent of the knowledge of the commodity producer and goes against his intentions. What characterizes voluntary stock formation? Here the seller still attempts to get rid of his commodities as fast as possible. He still offers his product for sale as a commodity. If he were to withdraw it from sale, it would form only a potential (δυνάμει) element of the commodity stock, and not an actual (ἐνεργείᾳ) one. The commodity as such is still for him simply the bearer of its exchange-value, and as such it can only have its effect by and through shedding its commodity form and assuming the money form.

The Aristotelian terminology—potential/actual—is significant. It suggests that the commodity stock has a modal dimension: a commodity that is offered for sale but not yet sold is potentially part of the stock, but actually only becomes so when it lingers unsold. The distinction between the two modal states is not a distinction in the physical thing but in its social relation to the process of circulation.

The necessary volume of the commodity stock.

Marx now examines the determinants of the necessary volume of the commodity stock. The commodity stock must have a certain volume in order to satisfy the scale of demand over a given period. The continual extension of the circle of buyers is taken into account in this connection. In order to last for one day, for example, one part of the commodities on the market must persist in the commodity form, while the other part flows and is transformed into money. Of course the part that stands still in this way steadily declines, as the scale of the stock itself declines, until it is finally all sold. This stagnation of commodities is thus taken into account here as a necessary condition for their sale. Moreover, it must be greater in scale than the average sale or the average demand, otherwise excesses above this average could not be satisfied. On the other hand, the stock must be constantly renewed, because it is constantly disappearing.

In the last instance, this renewal can derive only from production, from a supply of the commodity. It is immaterial whether this comes from abroad or not. The renewal depends on the periods that the commodities need for their reproduction. The stock of commodities must be adequate for this length of time. The fact that this stock does not remain in the hands of the original producers, but runs through various reservoirs, from the large-scale merchant to the retail trader, changes only the appearance, and not the thing itself. From the social point of view, a part of the capital still exists in the form of commodity stock, as long as the commodity has not entered into productive or individual consumption.

The producer himself attempts to have an inventory adequate for his average demand, in order not to be directly dependent on production, and to secure himself a constant circle of customers. The production periods give rise to dates of purchase, and the commodity forms a stock for a longer or shorter period of time before it can be replaced by new items of the same kind. It is only by way of this stock formation that the permanence and continuity of the circulation process is ensured, and hence that of the reproduction process which includes the circulation process.

The producer-merchant distinction.

Marx now returns to a point made earlier. C'-M' can be completed for the producer of C even though C is still on the market. If the producer himself intended to keep his own commodity in store until it was sold to the final consumer, he would have to set in motion a double capital, once as producer of the commodity, the other time as merchant. As far as the commodity itself is concerned—whether it is considered as an individual commodity or as a component part of the social capital—it makes no difference to the situation whether the expenses of stock formation fall onto its producers or onto a series of merchants from A to Z.

This is an important point for the theory of merchant's capital. The function of storage can be performed by the industrial capitalist or by a separate merchant. The social effect is the same—a portion of social capital is tied up in the form of commodity stock, and a portion of social labor is expended in maintaining it. But the distribution of the burden differs. If the industrial capitalist performs the function, he bears the cost directly. If a merchant performs it, the industrial capitalist may appear to have "sold" his commodity and returned to production more quickly, while the merchant bears the cost of storage and the risk of unsold goods. This appearance will be crucial in Volume 3's analysis of merchant's capital.

Storage costs as transposed costs of maintaining the social production and consumption funds.

Marx now returns to the social point of view. In as much as the commodity stock is nothing more than the commodity form of the stock that would still exist on the given scale of social production either as productive stock (latent production fund) or as a consumption fund (reserve of means of consumption), if it did not exist as a commodity stock, the expenses required to maintain the stock—"the expenses of stock formation"—are merely the transposed expenses of maintaining the social production fund and the social consumption fund. The increase in the value of the commodity to which they give rise simply distributes these expenses proportionately between the various commodities, as they are different for different sorts of commodity. The expenses of stock formation continue to be deductions from the social wealth, even though they are a condition of its existence.

This is a crucial reformulation. The commodity stock is not a new substance; it is the form assumed by the social product under capitalism. If the same products existed under a non-capitalist social form—say, as direct production stocks and consumption reserves in the hands of producers—they would still require storage costs. The specifically capitalist form of these costs is their appearance as commodity stock and their connection with the circulation process. But the substance of the costs—the labor of conservation—is common to all social production. The costs of storing the social product are therefore not uniquely capitalist; they are a feature of any complex society that produces a surplus and must maintain stocks against future need. What is uniquely capitalist is the form these costs assume and the way they are distributed through commodity prices.

Normal vs. abnormal stock formation and the mystification of crises.

Marx now introduces a crucial distinction. "It is only in so far as the commodity stock is a condition of commodity circulation and itself a form that has necessarily arisen in commodity circulation, in so far therefore as this apparent stagnation is a form of the flow itself, that it is normal." But once the commodities lingering in their circulation stores fail to make room for the incoming wave of production, and the stores are overfilled, the commodity stock expands as a result of the stagnation of circulation, just as hoards grow if the money circulation stagnates. It is quite immaterial here whether this stagnation takes place in the storeroom of the industrial capitalist or the warehouse of the merchant. The commodity stock is then not a condition of uninterrupted sale, but a consequence of the unsaleability of the commodities.

The expenses remain the same, but as they arise purely from the form—from the necessity of transforming the commodities into money, and the difficulty of this metamorphosis—they do not enter into the value of the commodities, but form deductions, a loss of value in the realization of value.

This is a crucial distinction with profound implications for the theory of crises. Normal stock formation is a condition of circulation; abnormal stock formation is a symptom of crisis. The two are indistinguishable in their form—both are "stagnations of circulation." Because the normal and the abnormal forms of the stock are not distinguished in their form, "the phenomena can be confused, and may deceive the agents of production themselves all the more, in that it is possible for the producer to feel that the circulation process of his capital is occurring, that it is in flux, even though the circulation of his commodities, which have passed into the hands of the merchants, is stagnating."

This is a remarkable passage. It describes the ideological mystification that arises from the credit system. If the extent of production increases, then, other circumstances remaining the same, so does the volume of the commodity stock. It is then renewed and absorbed just as quickly, but on a greater scale. The rise in the volume of the commodity stock as a result of a stagnation in circulation can thus be mistaken for a symptom of an expansion in the reproduction process, particularly if the real movement is mystified by the development of the credit system.

In other words: capitalists see their commodity stocks growing, and because credit allows them to continue producing even when goods are not being sold, they interpret the growth of stocks as evidence of economic growth rather than as a warning sign of overproduction. The credit system, which in one respect facilitates the circulation of capital, in another respect conceals the realities of the circulation process, allowing crises to develop unnoticed until they burst. This is a direct prefiguration of Marx's theory of crises and his critique of the credit system in Volume 3.

The three components of stock formation costs.

Marx concludes the section with a simple enumeration: "The expenses of stock formation consist of (1) a quantitative reduction in the mass of the product (e.g. with stocks of flour); (2) a deterioration in quality; (3) the objectified and living labour required to conserve the stock." This three-part enumeration summarizes the material reality of storage: it always involves some physical loss, some qualitative degradation, and the expenditure of labor and means of production.

III. Transport Costs

The general law restated.

Marx opens the final section by restating the general law: "all circulation costs that arise simply from a change in form of the commodity cannot add any value to it. They are simply costs involved in realizing the value or transferring it from one form into another. The capital expended in these costs (including the labour it commands) belongs to the faux frais of capitalist production. The replacement of these costs must come from the surplus product, and from the standpoint of the capitalist class as a whole it forms a deduction of surplus-value or surplus product, in just the same way as the time that a worker needs to buy his means of subsistence is lost time for him."

But transport costs, Marx says, "play too important a role not to be briefly considered here." And the consideration reveals that transport is fundamentally different from the other circulation costs. It is not a change of form; it is a change of location. And a change of location, unlike a change of form, requires labor that transforms the physical world and therefore adds value.

The metabolism of social labor and the distinction between circulation and transportation.

Marx introduces a crucial concept: within the circuit of capital and the commodity metamorphoses that form a section of it, the metabolism of social labor takes place. By "metabolism" (Stoffwechsel), Marx means—as the editorial footnote explains, referencing Volume 1, Chapter 7, p. 283—the exchange of matter between man and nature effected by labor. This metabolism may require a motion of the products in space, their real movement from one location to another.

But—and this is the crucial analytical point—circulation of commodities can take place without their physical movement, just as the transport of products can take place without commodity circulation, even without direct exchange of products. Marx gives two examples.

First: "A house that is sold by A to B circulates as a commodity, but it does not get up and walk." The circulation of property title is distinct from physical movement.

Second: "Movable commodity values, such as cotton or pig-iron, can remain in the same warehouse while they undergo dozens of circulation processes, and are bought and resold by speculators. What actually moves here is the property title to the thing and not the thing itself." Marx cites Storch, who calls this circulation factice (artificial circulation).

Conversely: "In the realm of the Incas, on the other hand, the transport industry played a major role, although the social product neither circulated as a commodity nor was distributed by means of exchange." Transport existed as a material process without commodity circulation.

The conclusion is that "if the transport industry therefore appears as a cause of circulation costs on the basis of capitalist production, this particular form of appearance in no way alters the substance of the matter." Transport is a production process, not a circulation process—even though, under capitalism, it appears as a cost of circulation because it occurs in the sphere between production and consumption.

Transport as a production process that adds value.

Marx now argues directly that transport adds value. "The quantity of products is not increased by their transport." This is true—the number of tons of coal remains the same whether the coal is at the pithead or at the factory. "The change in their natural properties that may be effected by transport is also, certain exceptions apart, not an intended useful effect, but rather an unavoidable evil." (For example, wine that is transported may age and improve, but this is an exception, not the rule; transport generally degrades the product, by breaking, spoiling, or shaking it.)

But: "the use-value of things is realized only in their consumption, and their consumption may make a change of location necessary, and thus also the additional production process of the transport industry. The productive capital invested in this industry thus adds value to the products transported, partly through the value carried over from the means of transport, partly through the value added by the work of transport." This latter addition of value can be divided, as with all capitalist production, into replacement of wages and surplus-value.

This is the fundamental argument: transport is a production process because it creates a use-value—the availability of the product at the place where it is to be consumed. A ton of coal at the pithead and a ton of coal at the factory are materially identical, but their use-values are different, because the factory cannot consume the coal at the pithead. The labor of transport therefore produces a real use-value, and hence real value, in the Marxian sense. It is a productive labor process that happens to occur within the sphere of circulation.

The universality of transport within production.

Marx emphasizes that transport is not confined to the sphere of circulation. "Within every production process, the change of location of the object of labour and the means of labour and labour-power needed for this plays a major role; for instance, cotton that is moved from the carding shop into the spinning shed, coal lifted from the pit to the surface." The transfer of the finished product as a finished commodity from one separate place of production to another a certain distance away shows the same phenomenon, only on a larger scale. The transport of products from one place of production to another is followed by that of the finished products from the sphere of production to the sphere of consumption. "The product is ready for consumption only when it has completed this movement."

This is crucial: the readiness for consumption is itself a use-value created by transport. A product that has not reached the consumer is not fully a product; it is a product in potentia. Transport realizes the product's potential use-value.

The inverse relation between productivity and value in transport.

Marx now states the general law of commodity production and applies it to transport: "it is a general law of commodity production that the productivity of labour and the value it creates stand in inverse proportion. This holds for the transport industry as much as any other. The smaller the quantity of labour, dead and living, that is required to transport a commodity for a given distance, the greater the productive power of the labour, and vice versa."

Marx cites Ricardo's critique of Say. Say had argued that commerce is a blessing because it increases the transport costs of products, thereby raising their value: "Commerce enables us to obtain a commodity in the place where it is to be found, and to convey it to another where it is to be consumed; it therefore gives us the power of increasing the value of the commodity, by the whole difference between its price in the first of these places, and its price in the second." Ricardo objected: "True, but how is this additional value given to it? By adding to the cost of production, first, the expenses of conveyance; secondly, the profit on the advances of capital made by the merchant. The commodity is only more valuable, because more labour is expended on its production and conveyance, before it is purchased by the consumer. This must not be mentioned as one of the advantages of commerce."

Marx endorses Ricardo's critique: transport adds value because it adds labor, not because it moves the commodity from a place of low price to a place of high price. The increase in value is a cost, not a benefit. Say's mistake—which Marx attributes to his "vulgar-economic doctrine of the 'factors of production'"—is to confuse the creation of value with the realization of a price differential. The editorial footnote (from the Marx-Engels editors) elaborates on Say's "law" of markets (supply creates its own demand), noting that it reigned supreme in bourgeois economics from 1817 to the Keynesian revolution, and that even Keynes's theory is itself simply one of under-consumption, teaching that the problem of over-production can be solved by an increase in the supply of money. This editorial commentary, while not part of Marx's text, highlights the continuity between Marx's critique of Say and later critiques of Keynesian economics.

The determinants of transport value.

Marx now states the quantitative relations governing transport costs with precision:

  1. "The absolute magnitude of value added by the transport of commodities stands in inverse proportion to the productive power of the transport industry and in direct proportion to the distance to be covered, other circumstances remaining the same."

This is a clear formula: V = L / P × D, where V is the value added, L is the quantity of labor, P is the productive power of the transport industry, and D is the distance. The value added increases with distance and decreases with productivity.

  1. "The relative part of value that transport costs add to the price of the commodity, under otherwise equal circumstances, stands in direct proportion to their size and weight."

This is why bulky, heavy, low-value commodities (like coal, grain, ore) are expensive to transport relative to their value, while small, light, high-value commodities (like jewelry, electronics) are cheap to transport relative to their value.

  1. "The modifying circumstances are numerous. Transport requires, for example, greater or lesser measures of precaution, hence more or less expenditure of labour and means of labour, according to the relative fragility, perishability and explosiveness of the article."

This sets up Marx's satirical passage on the railway classification of goods. "The railway magnates have shown greater genius in inventing fantastic species than have botanists or zoologists. The classification of goods on the British railways, for example, fills volumes, and rests for its general principle on the tendency to transform the variegated natural properties of goods into an equal number of transportation ailments and pretexts for obligatory impositions."

Marx quotes a manufacturer's testimony: "Glass, which was formerly worth £11 per crate, is now worth only £2 since the improvements which have taken place in manufactures, and since the abolition of the duty; but the rate for carriage is the same as it was formerly, and higher than it was previously, when carried by canal. Formerly, manufacturers inform me that they had glass and glass wares for the plumbers' trade carried at about 10s. per ton, within 50 miles of Birmingham. At the present time, the rate to cover risk of breakage, which we can very rarely get allowed, is three times that amount... The companies always resist any claim that is made for breakages."

The railway magnates' exploitation of the value relation.

Marx now draws a satirical conclusion from the quantitative relations he has established. "Moreover, the fact that the relative share that transport costs add to the value of an article stands in inverse proportion to its value is made by the railway magnates into a special reason for taxing an article in direct proportion to its value. The complaints of the industrialists and merchants on this score are repeated on every page of the evidence in the above-quoted report."

This is a brilliant observation. The railway companies recognize that the relative burden of transport costs falls more heavily on low-value goods. But instead of lowering rates for low-value goods, they raise them, on the theory that the transport cost is a smaller fraction of the value of high-value goods and therefore those goods can bear a higher rate. In other words, they use the analytical relation Marx has established—that transport costs are relatively more burdensome for low-value goods—as a pretext for price discrimination. The "classification of goods" is not a neutral technical necessity but an instrument of monopoly exploitation.

This passage is significant because it shows Marx applying the abstract theory of value and transport costs to the concrete analysis of a specific industry (railways) and a specific form of exploitation (freight rate discrimination). It demonstrates that the theory is not merely academic but provides tools for understanding the concrete behavior of capitalist firms.

The contradictory effects of capitalist development on transport costs.

Marx concludes the section with a passage that captures the dialectical character of capitalist development. "The capitalist mode of production reduces the transport costs for the individual commodity by developing the means of transport and communication, as well as by concentrating transport—i.e. by increasing its scale. It increases the part of social labour, both living and objectified, that is spent on commodity transport, firstly by transforming the great majority of all products into commodities, and then by replacing local by distant markets."

This is a remarkable summary. Capitalist development has two opposing effects on transport costs:

  1. It reduces the cost per commodity by improving and concentrating transport.
  2. It increases the total social labor spent on transport by transforming more products into commodities and by extending markets from local to global.

The net effect is that a larger and larger portion of social labor is devoted to transport, even as the cost per unit of product declines. This is a general pattern in the development of capitalism: the "social overhead" of circulation grows even as its efficiency increases. Capitalist development does not eliminate the faux frais of circulation; it expands them, both absolutely and relatively, even as it reduces them per unit of product.

The final analytical statement.

The section—and with it the chapter—ends with a concise characterization of the transport industry's peculiar status: "The 'circulating' of commodities, i.e. their actual course in space, can be resolved into the transport of commodities. The transport industry forms on the one hand an independent branch of production, and hence a particular sphere for the investment of productive capital. On the other hand it is distinguished by its appearance as the continuation of a production process within the circulation process and for the circulation process."

This concluding passage captures the double character of transport. It is (a) an independent branch of production, a sphere in which capital is invested productively and in which value and surplus-value are produced according to the general laws of capitalist production. But it is (b) distinguished by its appearance—it appears to be a continuation of a production process within the circulation process. This appearance is not illusory (transport genuinely occurs within the sphere of circulation, and it genuinely adds value); it is, rather, a manifestation of the unity of production and circulation in the total circuit of capital. Transport is where the material metabolism of society—the physical movement of products—becomes visible as a part of the circulation process, and hence where the theoretical distinction between production and circulation reveals itself to be a distinction within the unity of the total reproduction process, not a rigid separation.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles

Chapter 6 establishes several fundamental principles of Marx's theory of circulation, each with far-reaching consequences for the rest of the Critique of Political Economy:

First: Circulation labor is unproductive in the strict sense—but necessary. The labor of buying, selling, book-keeping, and money-handling does not create value, does not add to the use-value of the product, and is not a moment of the production process. It is a faux frais of commodity production—a deduction from the social surplus. But this does not mean it is unnecessary or dispensable. Under commodity production, value must be realized, and realization requires circulation, and circulation requires labor. The unproductive character of this labor is a cost of the system, not a criticism of the individuals who perform it.

Second: The character of a labor process is determined by its content, not by its location or organizational form. Whether labor is performed by the producer himself or by a hired specialist, whether it is performed within a large firm or by a small independent operator, whether it is concentrated or dispersed—none of this determines whether it produces value. The content of the labor—whether it transforms a use-value in a way that creates a new use-value, or merely facilitates the exchange of existing use-values—is what matters. This principle is crucial for the theory of merchant's capital in Volume 3, where Marx will argue that the merchant's labor is unproductive despite the merchant's centrality to the functioning of capitalist production.

Third: The social and individual points of view diverge systematically. Costs that are deductions from social wealth may be sources of enrichment for individual capitalists (via the price mechanism), and costs that enrich individual capitalists may be pure deductions from social wealth. The price mechanism does not create value; it only redistributes it. This principle is essential for understanding how costs that are "unproductive" from a social standpoint—storage, insurance, warehousing—can appear as legitimate business expenses that enter into the price of commodities.

Fourth: The development of capitalism transforms the form of circulation costs even as it maintains their substance. The apparent decline in productive stock (Lalor), the reduction in book-keeping costs through concentration, the transformation of the commodity stock from a productive reserve to a speculative holding—all of these are changes in the form of circulation costs, not in their fundamental nature. The substance—the expenditure of social labor on non-productive functions—persists.

Fifth: Transport is a production process occurring within the circulation sphere. This is perhaps the most important theoretical innovation of the chapter. Transport adds value because it creates a use-value (the availability of the product at the place of consumption), and it is therefore a productive labor process. But it occurs within the sphere of circulation, and it appears as a cost of circulation. The transport industry thus reveals the unity of production and circulation in the total circuit of capital—a unity that Marx's analysis has been building toward throughout Volume 2.

Broader Practical and Theoretical Implications

For the theory of crises. Marx's analysis of the distinction between normal and abnormal stock formation is directly relevant to his theory of crises. Normal stock formation is a condition of smooth circulation; abnormal stock formation is a symptom of overproduction. Because the two are indistinguishable in their form, and because the credit system allows producers to continue producing even when goods are not being sold, the growth of the commodity stock can be mistaken for evidence of economic growth. This is a mechanism of ideological mystification—the credit system conceals the buildup of unsold goods until the crisis breaks. The chapter thus provides the theoretical foundation for the analysis of commercial crises that Marx will develop in Volume 3 and in Theories of Surplus-Value.

For the theory of merchant's capital. The chapter establishes the framework for the analysis of merchant's capital in Volume 3. If buying and selling labor is unproductive, and if the merchant is merely a specialist in this unproductive labor, then the merchant's profit cannot be derived from the productivity of the merchant's labor. It must be derived from the share of surplus-value that the merchant extracts by virtue of his position in the circuit of capital. The merchant's capital is a deduction from the total surplus-value, not an addition to it. This analysis is anticipated here in the discussion of the circulation worker as "a machine that bought and sold the product"—a machine that is part of the constant capital of the capitalist class, a deduction from the product.

For the critique of apologetic economics. Marx's engagement with Quesnay, Smith, Lalor, Sismondi, Say, and the railway magnates is not merely historical. It is a methodological demonstration of how abstract economic categories (value, use-value, circulation, production) can be applied to concrete phenomena (famines, freight rates, warehouse costs, credit bubbles) to reveal the underlying material relations. The chapter thus serves as a model of Marxist method: rigorous theoretical analysis combined with concrete historical and empirical investigation.

For the theory of socialist economy. Marx's distinction between costs arising solely from the commodity form (buying, selling, money, speculative stock formation) and costs arising from the material requirements of social production (storage, transport, book-keeping as supervision) has profound implications for the theory of socialist economy. Under socialism, commodity production would be abolished (or at least radically transformed), and with it the faux frais of buying and selling, the costs of money, and the speculative commodity stock. But the material costs of storage, transport, and social book-keeping would persist, because they are costs of conserving and moving the social product, not costs of its commodity form. Marx gestures toward this in his remarks on book-keeping under communal production, where he notes that the costs of book-keeping are "more necessary in communal production than in capitalist" (though they are "reduced with the concentration of production"). The implication is that socialism would reduce but not eliminate the administrative and logistical costs of social production; it would eliminate the faux frais of commodity circulation while retaining the necessary costs of material reproduction.

For the philosophy of value. Finally, the chapter raises a philosophical question that runs throughout Marx's work: what is the relation between value and form? Marx's argument is that value is a material relation—it is the expenditure of socially necessary labor time—but that it appears only in forms (money, price, commodity stock) that are historically specific and socially constructed. The task of the critique of political economy is to see through these forms to the material relations they express. The chapter on circulation costs is a masterclass in this kind of analysis: it takes the most mundane phenomena of capitalist business life—buying, selling, book-keeping, storing, transporting—and reveals them as expressions of the fundamental contradiction between value and its forms of appearance. The result is not a rejection of these phenomena but a scientific explanation of them, grounded in the labor theory of value and in the historical analysis of the capitalist mode of production.

The chapter thus achieves what Marx set out to do: to show that the costs of circulation are neither random nor natural, neither inexplicable nor irrevocable. They are the necessary costs of a specific social form of production—a form that produces value in production and realizes it in circulation, and that must therefore bear the costs of this realization. To understand these costs is to understand the limit of capitalist rationality: capital can reduce the costs of circulation per unit of product, but it cannot eliminate them, because they are inherent in the commodity form itself. The only way to transcend them is to transcend the commodity form—that is, to transcend capitalism. This is the deepest implication of the chapter, and it is one that Marx leaves his readers to draw for themselves.
