Volume 2 • Chapter 18: Introduction (Part III: The Reproduction and Circulation of Aggregate Social Capital)

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 18 opens Part Three of Volume 2 of Capital and functions as a deliberate theoretical threshold. Its central problem is deceptively simple yet foundational to everything that follows: how does the analysis of individual capital — the object of Parts One and Two — relate to the analysis of the total social capital? Every earlier volume and every earlier part of Volume 2 treated a capital in isolation, "an autonomous part of the social capital." Now Marx announces that the object of inquiry shifts upward in scale: we must consider "the circulation process of the individual capitals as components of the total social capital, i.e. the circulation process of this total social capital."

The intellectual objective is therefore architectonic. Marx is not introducing a new empirical fact so much as a new level of abstraction. Individual capitals are not merely summed; they are interlinked, they "presuppose one another and condition one another," and it is precisely this interlinking that "constitutes the movement of the total social capital." This is the pivot on which the entire later theory of social reproduction and, by extension, the reproduction schemas of the chapters that follow, will turn.

The chapter's core thesis is twofold. First, the overall reproduction process of social capital is broader than the circuits of individual capitals. It necessarily incorporates not only the specific circuit of capital (production and circulation of value and surplus-value) but also commodity circulation in general — the sphere of purchases and sales by which workers spend wages and capitalists spend surplus-value. This "individual consumption" is not part of the circulation of capital narrowly defined, yet it is indispensable to the totality. Second, the role of money capital must be clarified precisely because it appears to impose an external limit on production. Marx demonstrates that although every capital must begin in the money form — money is the "prime mover" and "permanent driving force" — the scale of production is emphatically not determined by the volume of money capital. The productive powers latent in a given capital value are "elastic and variable," their limits not given by the value-forms that advance them. The chapter thus dissolves a powerful illusion: that money capital constitutes the boundary of capitalist production. It does not; only the material character of the labour process (notably the length of the working period) and the social form of production jointly determine how much money capital is required.

The chapter's broader framework is unmistakable. It bridges the microscopic analysis of turnover developed in Parts One and Two with the macroscopic analysis of social reproduction. It also plants the seed for the later, explicitly socialist/post-capitalist speculation: under collective production, "money capital is completely dispensed with," and society allocates labour-power and means of production directly, distributing consumption tokens that "are not money; they do not circulate." In sum, the chapter reframes the unit of analysis, clarifies the function of money in the aggregate circuit, and establishes that the limits of capitalist production are material and social — never monetary in the last instance.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Object of the Inquiry: Capital's Two Moments

Marx begins by restating the elementary grammar of his system. The immediate production process of capital is its "process of labour and valorization," whose result is the commodity product and whose "determining motive" is the production of surplus-value. This is the foundation laid down in Volume 1. But immediately the scope widens: the process of capital's reproduction "includes, on top of this immediate production process, the specific process of circulation with its two phases." The two phases are the classic conversion of commodity capital into money capital (C'–M') and money capital back into productive elements (M–C), the sale and the re-purchase that restore the capital to its productive form. Reproduction is thus the "overall circuit forming the turnover of capital," "a periodic process that is constantly repeated afresh at definite intervals."

Here the concept of the turnover — introduced and dissected in Part Two — is quietly reinstated as the minimal unit of analysis. Crucially, Marx emphasizes a point of structural symmetry: whether we examine the circuit in the form M…M' (money capital as the starting and ending point) or P…P (productive capital as the starting and ending point), the immediate production process P "never forms more than one term in this circuit." In the M…M' form, production mediates circulation; in the P…P form, circulation mediates production. Neither form grants production an absolute primacy in the phenomenal sequence; the two are dialectically interwoven. Yet a hierarchy is preserved: "the constant repetition of the process of production is the condition for the transformations that the capital undergoes over and again in the circulation sphere." Production, while not the only term, remains the enabling condition of circulation's persistent reproduction.

The Individual Capital as a Fraction of the Social Capital

The chapter now executes its pivotal move. Each individual capital "forms only a fraction of the total social capital, a fraction that has acquired independence and been endowed with individual life." The analogy is deliberately drawn with the class relation itself: "each individual capitalist is no more than an element of the capitalist class." Just as a single commodity's metamorphosis is one term in the "series of metamorphoses of the commodity world as a whole," so a single capital's turnover is "a single term in the circuit of the social capital." This is not a mere summation. The movement of the social capital is "the totality of movements of these autonomous fractions." The whole is composed of parts that retain apparent autonomy — individual capitals competing, making independent decisions — yet are bound together by mutual presupposition.

This is the theoretical crux of the chapter, and it will later generate the famous reproduction schemas (Departments I and II). Marx is preparing the reader to think in terms of aggregate flows: the sum of individual turnovers forms the total social turnover, but with emergent properties not visible at the level of the isolated capital.

The Dual Consumption of the Overall Process

Marx insists that the overall process, "taken in its entirety," involves two distinct kinds of consumption. On the one hand there is productive consumption — the immediate production process together with the changes of form that mediate it (which, "considered in their material aspect," are exchanges). On the other hand there is individual consumption with its own mediating exchanges. This twofold character generates a twofold role for the human agents. In productive consumption, the worker "enters the scene as the seller of his commodity, labour-power, and the capitalist as its buyer." This is the conversion of variable capital into labour-power and the "consequent incorporation of labour-power into the capitalist production process."

But there is a second circuit. The sale of commodities "involves their purchase by the working class, i.e. the workers' individual consumption." Here the roles reverse: "the working class appears as a buyer of commodities, and the capitalists as sellers of commodities to the workers." This doubled appearance — worker as seller then buyer, capitalist as buyer then seller — is the material form in which wage-labour reproduces itself as a class. The importance of the point is that the workers' consumption is not an incidental by-product; it is a necessary condition for capital's continuance, since the reconstitution of labour-power depends on it. Yet Marx is careful to distinguish this from capital circulation proper.

The Circulation of Surplus-Value and the Two Components of Commodity Circulation

A further layer is added: the circulation of commodity capital "involves the circulation of surplus-value," and hence the purchases and sales by which capitalists mediate "their individual consumption, the consumption of surplus-value." The fine distinction to retain is that the revenue expenditures of both classes — wages and surplus-value spent as income — do not form part of the circulation of capital, even though the spending of wages "at least, depends on this circulation." This is a crucial analytical boundary: capital flow and revenue flow are conceptually separable even while empirically entangled.

Marx then states the result with almost axiomatic brevity. The circuits of individual capitals, "when considered as combined into the social capital," encompass not only the circulation of capital but commodity circulation in general, which has "in its fundamentals" only two components: (1) "the specific circuit of capital," and (2) "the circuit of those commodities that go into individual consumption, i.e. the commodities on which the workers spend their wages and the capitalists their surplus-value." This binary division is the seed of the two-department model. The circuit of capital itself "comprises the circulation of surplus-value, in as much as this forms part of the commodity capital," and "similarly includes the transformation of variable capital into labour-power, the payment of wages." But the expenditure of surplus-value and wages on commodities lies outside the circulation of capital. The unity of the whole is therefore a unity of distinct circuits, not a homogeneous mass.

The Retrospective: Volume 1 and Parts One and Two as Analysis of Individual Capital

Marx then walks the reader through the methodological history of the preceding analyses, clarifying what was assumed and what was deferred. In Volume 1, "the capitalist production process was analysed both as an isolated event and as a process of reproduction." Two abstractions were made: it was assumed "that the capitalist sells the product at its value" and that "he finds in the circulation sphere the material means of production that he needs to begin the process anew." In other words, the circulation sphere was treated as a frictionless presupposition. The only circulation-sphere act requiring examination in Volume 1 was "the purchase and sale of labour-power as the basic condition of capitalist production."

In Part One of Volume 2, Marx considered "the various forms that capital assumes in its circuit," adding circulation time to the working time of Volume 1. In Part Two, the circuit was treated as periodic, i.e. as turnover. Here two advances were made. First, it was shown "how the various components of capital (fixed and circulating) complete the circuit of their forms at different intervals and in different ways," along with the causes of differing working periods and circulation periods. Second, the influence of periodicity and of the varying ratio of components "on the scale of the production process itself, and on the annual rate of surplus-value" was indicated. The conceptual contrast between Parts One and Two is stated sharply: Part One dealt with "the successive forms that capital constantly assumes and discards"; Part Two dealt with how, "within this flux and succession of forms, a capital of given size is simultaneously divided" into productive, money, and commodity capital, so that "different parts of the total capital value exist and function in these different states alongside each other at any one time." Money capital, in particular, "presented specific features that were not indicated in Volume 1," and "certain laws were discovered" governing how major components must "constantly be advanced and renewed in the form of money capital" to keep production running.

Yet the limitation of both parts is now named: "What we were dealing with in both Parts One and Two, however, was always no more than an individual capital."

The Transitional Thesis: Interlinked Circuits

Having established the limitation, Marx states the new object with precision. The circuits of individual capitals "are interlinked, they presuppose one another and condition one another, and it is precisely by being interlinked in this way that they constitute the movement of the total social capital." The analogy with simple commodity circulation is again invoked: just as the metamorphosis of a single commodity is one term in the metamorphoses of the commodity world, "now the metamorphosis of the individual capital appears as one term in the series of metamorphoses of the social capital."

A subtle but decisive qualification follows. Simple commodity circulation "in no way necessarily involved the circulation of capital — since it can proceed quite well on the basis of non-capitalist production." By contrast, the circuit of the total social capital "also involves a commodity circulation that does not fall within the circuit of any individual capital, i.e. the circulation of those commodities that do not form capital." This asymmetry is crucial: capitalism both presupposes pre-existing commodity circulation and incorporates a distinct stratum of commodity circulation (revenue purchases) that no single capital's circuit contains. The chapter thus ends its first section with the formal statement of the new object: "the circulation process of the individual capitals as components of the total social capital," which "taken in its entirety" is "a form of the reproduction process."

The Role of Money Capital: Two Aspects

Marx opens section 2 with a methodological aside of some candour: the subject "really belongs in a later section of this Part. However, we intend to investigate it here." This footnote-like admission signals the essayistic origin of the text (it is drawn from Manuscript 11) and marks the section as preparatory. The task is to explain money capital considered as a component of the total social capital.

Two aspects of money capital emerge from the earlier analysis of turnover. First, money capital "provides the form in which each individual capital steps onto the scene and commences its process as capital." It is thus "prime mover, giving the first impulse to the whole process." Second, according to the varying lengths of the turnover period and the ratio of its components, the portion of capital value that "must be advanced and renewed at any one time in the money form stands in a different ratio to the productive capital that it sets in motion." The critical consequence is a restriction: "the portion of the capital value in process that can function at any one time as productive capital is always restricted by the part of the capital value advanced that must always exist alongside the productive capital in the money form." Here is the germ of a claim about money capital as a bound on production — but it is immediately qualified. Marx specifies that what is involved is "simply the normal turnover, an abstract average," and that "we therefore disregard any additional money capital required to cope with delays in circulation." The analysis proceeds on the assumption of smooth, average turnover.

The First Aspect: Money as Prime Mover

On the first point, Marx develops a chain of presuppositions. "Commodity production presupposes commodity circulation, and commodity circulation presupposes the representation of commodities in money, monetary circulation." The "duplication of commodities into commodities and money" is "a law of the emergence of the product as a commodity." This is a philosophical restatement: the existence of money is not an accident of history but a necessary form of the commodity as a social relation. In a footnote, Marx refers the reader back to Volume 1, Chapter 2 ("Exchange").

Capitalist commodity production, "whether we consider it socially or individually," likewise presupposes money capital — "both as the prime mover for each business when it first begins, and as a permanent driving force." Circulating capital, especially, "presupposes the constantly repeated appearance, at short intervals, of the motor of money capital." The point is that money is not only the initial endowment but a recurring imperative: every round of turnover must begin again in the money form. "The entire capital value advanced, i.e. all components of the capital that consist of commodities — labour-power, means of labour and materials of production — must always first be bought with money and later on purchased again." And "what holds here for the individual capital, holds also for the social capital, which operates only in the form of many individual capitals."

But then the dialectical reversal. "As we already showed in Volume 1, it in no way follows from this that the field of operation of capital, the scale of production, even on the capitalist basis, has its absolute limits determined by the volume of money capital in operation." The apparent restriction is not a real limit. This is the chapter's most important negative thesis.

The Elasticity of Productive Powers

Marx now marshals a systematic demonstration that the productive powers embodied in a given money capital exceed the bounds of that capital's own value. Four main cases are given.

First, labour-power. "Labour-power with a certain rate of payment may be more or less severely exploited, both extensively and intensively." If the money capital increases with severer exploitation, i.e. "if wages rise, they still do not rise in relation to the degree of exploitation, i.e. not at all proportionately." The quantity of labour extracted can rise without a proportional rise in money advanced. This is the theory of surplus-value operating at the margin of the money-form.

Second, natural materials. Soil, sea, mineral ores, forests "may be more or less severely exploited, in extent and intensity, by greater exertion of the same amount of labour-power, without an increase in the money capital advanced." These natural materials "do not form an element of the capital's value" — a crucial reminder that nature's productive contribution is unpaid. "The real elements of productive capital can be increased without the need for additional money capital." Where additional ancillaries are needed, "the money capital in which the capital value is advanced is not increased in relation to the increased effectiveness of the productive capital, i.e. again not at all proportionately."

Third, fixed capital. "The same means of labour, i.e. the same fixed capital, may be used more efficiently without an additional outlay of money for fixed capital," either by prolonging daily use or by raising intensity. The result is "only a more rapid turnover of the fixed capital," which means "the elements of its reproduction are supplied more quickly."

Fourth, natural forces. "Natural forces that cost nothing may also be incorporated more or less effectively as agents in the production process," their effectiveness depending "on methods and scientific advances that cost the capitalist nothing."

Fifth, the social combination of labour and accumulated skill. This last case leads naturally into a polemic.

The Carey Polemic and the Capital in the Soil

Marx interrupts the exposition to attack Henry Charles Carey, identified in the footnote as "an American 'vulgar economist' and champion of the 'harmony of interests' between opposing classes." Carey reckons that the landowner never receives his due rent because he is not paid for all the capital and labour put into the soil from time immemorial to give it its present productive capacity. Marx adds sardonically: "(There is no mention, of course, of the productive capacity that is taken out of the soil.)" The parallel extension follows: "the individual worker should be paid according to the work that it cost the entire human race to build him up from a savage into a modern engineer."

Marx's rebuttal is economic: "What actually happens is quite the reverse. If all the unpaid labour put into the soil to the profit of the landowners and capitalists is counted up, then this capital contained in the soil has been repaid over and over again at an extortionate rate of interest, so that landed property has long since been redeemed by society, and redeemed time and again at that." The argument is that rent represents a perpetual re-extraction of value already repaid; the landowner is not an unrecognized creditor but a recurring beneficiary of unpaid labour.

The insert is not merely polemical. It reinforces the chapter's central claim about the disproportion between value advanced and wealth produced. "The increase in the productive forces of labour, in so far as this does not rest on any additional outlay of capital value, increases in the first instance only the quantity of products, and not their value, except to the extent that it enables more constant capital to be reproduced, and its value thus maintained, with the same amount of labour." Crucially, however, such an increase "also forms extra material for capital, and thus provides the basis for an increased capital accumulation." This is the passage connecting the argument about elasticity to the theory of accumulation: free productive gains enlarge the material basis on which capital grows, without any proportional monetary advance.

Centralization and the Money-Form

Marx recalls a further argument from Volume 1: the organization of social labour on a large scale requires "the advance of money capital in great quantities by individual capitalists," and this is achieved "partly as a result of the centralization of capitals in a few hands, without any absolute growth in the volume of capital values in operation, and nor therefore in the volume of money capital through which they are advanced." "The size of individual capitals can grow by way of centralization in a few hands, without any growth in their social sum. There is then simply a redistribution of individual capitals." Centralization — a category from the theory of accumulation — reappears here as a mechanism that decouples scale of operation from volume of money capital.

Finally, from the immediately preceding Part, Marx recalls that "a reduction in the turnover period enables either the same productive capital to be set in motion with less money capital, or more productive capital to be set in motion with the same money capital."

The General Conclusion on Money Capital and the Scale of Production

Marx draws the section together. "All this clearly has nothing to do with the specific question of money capital as such." What it indicates is that the capital advanced — "a given sum of value which, in its free form, its value form, consists of a certain sum of money — contains, once it has been transformed into productive capital, productive powers whose limits are not given by the bounds of its own value, but, within a given field of action, can operate differently, both in extent and intensity." Two distinct quantities must therefore be distinguished. Once prices of the elements of production (means of production and labour-power) are given, "the size of the money capital required to buy a certain quantity of these elements of production, present as commodities, is also determined. In other words, the capital value that has to be advanced is determined." However, "the scale on which this capital operates to form values and products is elastic and variable." The chapter thereby balances a strict determination (money capital required) with a flexible operation (product and value creation), and so defuses any attempt to derive a theory of capitalist limits from the quantity of money.

The Second Aspect: Money Creation as a Deduction from Social Production

Marx turns to a second, more aggregate consideration. "It is self-evident that the portion of social labour and means of production that has to be spent each year on the production or acquisition of money, in order to replace worn-down coins, involves a proportionate reduction in the scale of social production." Here is a genuine, if modest, cost: the wear and replacement of coinage diverts labour and means of production from other uses. But Marx immediately relativizes it: "as for the money value already functioning partly as means of circulation and partly as a hoard, this has already been obtained, and exists alongside the labour-power, the produced means of production, and the natural sources of wealth. It cannot be considered as a restriction on these." The existing stock of money is a datum, part of the world's wealth, not a limit upon it. "If it is transformed into elements of production, or exchanged with other nations, the scale of production may be expanded. But this presupposes that the money continues to play, as before, its role of world money." A footnote refers back to Volume 1, Chapter 3, section 3c, on world money. The category of world money reappears to underline that even at the level of the world economy, money functions as a universal equivalent medium and does not constitute an absolute ceiling on material wealth.

The Length of the Turnover Period and the Money Form

Marx then returns to the dependence of required money capital on the turnover period. "According to the length of the turnover period, a greater or lesser quantity of money capital is needed to set the productive capital in motion." And again from the preceding analysis: "the division of the turnover period into working time and circulation time gives rise to an increase in the capital that is latent or suspended in the money form."

Crucially, "to the extent that the turnover period is governed by the length of the working period, it is determined by the material character of the production process, conditions remaining otherwise the same, and not by the specific social character of this production process." This is a pivotal distinction. The working period — the time required by the material nature of the labour process to produce a finished product (e.g. the maturing of crops, the building of a ship) — is a natural-technical determinant, not a social one. On the basis of capitalist production, "extended operations of long duration require greater advances of money capital for a longer time. Production in these branches is therefore dependent on the extent of the money capital which the individual capitalist has at his disposal." Here, at last, money capital appears as a real barrier — but a private, not a social, one.

Credit, Joint-Stock Companies, and Money-Market Disturbances

How is the private barrier overcome? "This limit is overcome by the credit system and the forms of association related to it, e.g. joint-stock companies." The credit system and the joint-stock form are thus theorized as institutional responses to the mismatch between the material duration of certain labour processes and the monetary capacity of individual capitalists. But the solution breeds new vulnerabilities: "Disturbances in the money market therefore bring such businesses to a halt, while those same businesses, for their part, induce disturbances in the money market." Here Marx articulates a dialectic of dependency and causation: long-duration production is sensitive to credit conditions and simultaneously a source of financial instability. This prefigures later analyses of financial crisis and the specific vulnerability of long-gestation industries.

Social Production and the Elimination of Money Capital

Marx then projects the analysis beyond capitalism. "On the basis of social production, it would be necessary to determine to what extent it was possible to pursue these operations, which withdraw labour-power and means of production for a relatively long period without providing any product or useful effect during this time, without damaging those branches of production that not only withdraw labour-power and means of production continuously or several times in the course of a year, but also supply means of subsistence and means of production."

The problem is thus framed as a material one. "With social production just as with capitalist production, workers in branches of industry with short working periods will withdraw products only for a short time without giving other products back in return, while branches of industry with long working periods will continue to withdraw products for a long time before they give anything back." And this occurs "from the material conditions of the labour process in question, and not from its social form." The material structure of long- versus short-gestation production persists irrespective of the social form.

But the form of mediation changes decisively. "With collective production, money capital is completely dispensed with. The society distributes labour-power and means of production between the various branches of industry. There is no reason why the producers should not receive paper tokens permitting them to withdraw an amount corresponding to their labour time from the social consumption stocks. But these tokens are not money; they do not circulate." This is one of the most theoretically loaded passages in the chapter. Under collective production, allocation is conscious and direct; consumption claims are recorded in non-circulating tokens (a proto-anticipation of labour certificates in later socialist theory). The distinction between a circulating money and a non-circulating accounting token is insisted upon: the latter lacks the self-expanding, universal equivalent character of money and therefore does not constitute money capital.

The Two Factors Conditioning the Need for Money Capital

The chapter closes by summarising the conditions under which the need for money capital arises from the working period. "We see that in so far as the need for money capital arises from the length of the working period, this is conditioned by two factors."

First, "that money is the general form in which each individual capital must enter the scene (leaving aside credit), in order to be transformed into productive capital; this arises from the nature of capitalist production, and of commodity production in general." This is a form-determinate necessity: capital must begin in money because the elements of production are commodities.

Second, "the size of the money advance needed arises from the circumstance that labour-power and means of production are withdrawn from society for a long period without the return of a product that can be transformed back into money." This is a material necessity: long-gestation production ties up resources for an extended period before any return.

The final sentences distinguish the fate of each factor. "The first factor, that the capital has to be advanced in the form of money, is not abolished by the form this money takes, whether it is metallic money, credit money, tokens of value, etc." In other words, credit and its innovations change the vehicle but not the necessity of a monetary advance. "The second factor is not affected in any way either by the monetary medium or by the form of production in which labour, means of subsistence and means of production are withdrawn, without an equivalent being cast back into circulation." The material constraint, the temporal withdrawal-and-return pattern of long-duration production, is invariant across forms — capitalist or collective, metallic or credit. The chapter thus ends with two cleanly separated necessities: one historically specific (the money-form of the advance) and one materially universal (the withdrawal-return profile of long working periods). This separation is the analytical gift the chapter hands to the theory of social reproduction that follows.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter yields a set of fundamental principles that govern everything thereafter. Chief among them is the shift in unit of analysis: from the individual capital to the total social capital as the interlinked totality of autonomously-managed fractions. This is not a matter of simple aggregation; the whole has properties the parts lack. Specifically, the total circulation encompasses not only the circuit of capital but also the revenue circulation of wages and surplus-value — a sphere lying outside any single capital's circuit yet necessary to the whole. This conceptual bifurcation, between capital circulation and general commodity circulation, is the indispensable precondition for the reproduction schemas of the following chapters.

A second principle is the non-monetary character of capitalism's fundamental limits. Money is the form of the advance, not the measure of its productive potential. The demonstration that labour-power, natural materials, fixed capital, natural forces, and social cooperation can all be exploited more intensively without proportional monetary increases establishes that the limits of production lie in the material conditions of the labour process and in the social relations of production — not in the quantity of money. "The scale on which this capital operates to form values and products is elastic and variable." This dissolves the monetarist illusion that the volume of money determines the volume of production; money is the necessary vehicle, never the boundary.

A third principle is the material/formal distinction that runs through the chapter's conclusion. The length of the working period is materially determined — it arises from "the material character of the production process," not its "specific social character." This material determinant persists under capitalist and collective production alike. But the form of mediation differs: under capitalism, long working periods require large private advances of money capital, generating dependence on the credit system and joint-stock companies and, transitively, on the fragility of the money market. Under collective production, society consciously allocates labour-power and means of production and distributes non-circulating consumption tokens, in which "money capital is completely dispensed with."

The broader implications are several. First, Marx diagnoses the credit system as a dialectical response to a material mismatch: long-duration industries need more money than individual capitalists can supply, so credit and joint-stock association arise — yet this very solution creates systemic vulnerability, since "disturbances in the money market bring such businesses to a halt, while those same businesses… induce disturbances in the money market." Here is an early, trenchant theory of financial fragility rooted in the temporal structure of production.

Second, the Carey polemic and the discussion of unpaid natural and social productivity serve to underscore the disproportion between value advanced and wealth produced — the theme of unpaid labour (both in the worker and in the soil) that grounds the theory of rent, surplus-value, and accumulation. The "extra material for capital" created by free productivity gains is the material basis for expanded accumulation without proportional monetary investment.

Third, the passage on collective production sketches the outlines of an economy without money capital. Society allocates resources directly; producers receive token claims measurable in labour time; but these tokens "are not money; they do not circulate." This signals that Marx's critique of political economy is oriented toward a mode of production in which the form-determinate necessity (the money-advance) is eliminated, while the material constraint (long gestation periods) remains a planning problem to be solved by conscious social allocation. The chapter thus delivers both a rigorous closure of the individual-capital analysis and a rigorous opening onto the aggregate, necessarily pointing beyond the horizon of the capitalist mode of production.
