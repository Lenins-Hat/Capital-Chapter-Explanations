Volume 2 • Chapter 3: The Circuit of Commodity Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem

Chapter 3 of Capital, Volume 2, completes Marx's systematic construction of the three circuits of industrial capital. Having already analyzed in Chapter 1 the circuit of money capital (M...M') and in Chapter 2 the circuit of productive capital (P...P), Marx now turns to the third and most complex figure: the circuit of commodity capital, whose general formula is C'–M'–C...P...C'. The chapter's central intellectual problem is this: the third form is not a mere permutation of the first two. It possesses properties the other two structurally cannot possess, and these properties force the analysis of capital beyond the horizon of the individual industrial capital toward the total social capital.

The Core Thesis

Marx's thesis unfolds in three interlocking claims:

First, in the circuit of commodity capital the valorized capital value—C' = capital value + surplus-value, i.e. C + c—appears from the very start as the point of departure. Unlike forms I and II, where the circuit opens with capital value still to be valorized (M and P respectively), form III opens with capital that is already pregnant with surplus-value. This is the decisive formal difference. In forms I and II, the closing points M' and P' must be "stripped" back to M and P when the circuit renews; in form III, the starting-point remains C' even under simple reproduction. The surplus-value is present at the threshold.

Second, because surplus-value is embedded in the opening extreme, the circuit of commodity capital includes from the start both the movement of the capital value and the movement of the surplus-value. This means that consumption—both productive consumption and individual consumption (the worker's and, as a social act, the capitalist's)—enters the circuit as an explicit presupposition. The division of the total product into a reproduction fund and an individual consumption fund is inscribed in the form of the circuit itself. No other circuit makes this visible.

Third, and most consequentially, form III presupposes the existence of other industrial capitals. For C' to open a circuit, its conversion into money must be matched by another capital's purchase; and C (L + mp) must already exist on the market as the commodity product of other capitals. Therefore, when the figure C'...C' is conceived "in its particularity," it can no longer be treated as the isolated motion of a single capital. It "demands to be considered... as the form of motion of the sum of individual capitals, i.e. of the total social capital of the capitalist class." The circuit of commodity capital is thus the gateway to the reproduction schemas and to the Tableau économique of Quesnay.

Fit Within the Broader Framework

This chapter is the hinge of Volume 2. The three circuits are not alternatives—they are moments of a single, integrated, self-reproducing totality. Marx treats them as the differentiated forms through which one and the same industrial capital moves. Forms I and II were analyzed in relative abstraction; form III exposes the interdependence that forms I and II presupposed but concealed. The chapter therefore transitions from the analysis of the individual capital (Chapters 1–3) to the analysis of the total social capital and its reproduction on a simple and expanded scale (later chapters, and the reproduction schemas). Its closing invocation of Quesnay's Tableau économique—the first attempt to depict the total annual product as a circuit—signals precisely this transition. The chapter is, in short, where Marx's theory of capital becomes macroeconomics.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The General Formula and the Double Role of C'

Marx opens with the general formula: C'–M'–C...P...C'. The first move is to establish that C' here is not simply the result of the two earlier circuits but also their premise. This bidirectional relation is crucial. What is M–C for one capital is simultaneously C'–M' for another, "at least in so far as a part of the means of production are themselves the commodity product of other individual capitals in their circuits." Coal, machinery, and so on, are the commodity capital of the mine-owner, the capitalist engineer, etc. Thus the very existence of a given capital's means of production requires that other capitals have already completed their own C'–M' phase. Marx notes that even in Chapter 1, when M...M' is first repeated, both the circuit P...P and the circuit C'...C' are presupposed. The circuits are mutually embedded: no single capital can be understood in isolation.

For expanded reproduction, the concluding C' is larger than the starting C', designated C". This notation matters: at every scale of reproduction the circuit opens with a composite—capital value plus surplus-value—not with a pure magnitude.

The Three Structural Differences Between Form III and Forms I and II

Marx isolates three formal distinctions.

First difference: the placement of circulation. In form III, "the circuit commences with the entire circulation, in its two opposing phases." By contrast, in form I (M...M') the circulation was interrupted by the production process—money → commodities → production → commodities → more money, where production sits between the two halves. In form II (P...P) the entire circulation and its two complementary phases "simply appeared as a mediation for the reproduction process," i.e., as the mediating movement between P and P. Form III is different again: its circulation opens the circuit rather than interrupting or mediating it. The formal pattern of circulation also differs: with M...M', the circulation takes the form M–C–M (money → commodity → more money); with P...P and with C'...C', it takes the form C–M–C (commodity → money → commodity). The direction of the money-commodity metamorphosis's purpose—money-seeking-more-money versus commodity-seeking-again-commodity—shapes each form's appearance.

Second difference: what happens to the starting-point when the circuit renews. When circuits I and II repeat, "the form in which they were produced vanishes." M' = M + m and P' = P + p both begin the new process again as M and P. Why? Because in form I, M' functions as money capital M, the advance in monetary form of capital value yet to be valorized. Whether the advance is £422 or £500 makes no difference—it appears "simply as capital value." M' is no longer capital pregnant with surplus-value, no longer a capital-relation; it becomes valorized only in the course of the process. Similarly, P' must always function as P—capital value that should produce surplus-value—and repeat the circuit. The surplus-value achieved is cast off at the renewal point; the circuit restarts from a purified magnitude.

In form III, however, the starting-point must be designated C' even when the circuit is renewed on the same scale. The reason: "The circuit of commodity capital... does not just open with capital value, but with expanded capital value in the commodity form, and thus it includes from the start not only the circuit of the capital value present in the commodity form, but also that of the surplus-value." If simple reproduction occurs, the circuit closes with a C' of equal magnitude to its starting-point. If a part of surplus-value is capitalized, the closing is C"—a bigger C"—but the next circuit still opens with C", which is simply a larger accumulated capital value carrying relatively more newly produced surplus-value. "In all cases, C' always opens the circuit as a commodity capital equal to capital value plus surplus-value." This is the formal signature of form III: the surplus-value never "vanishes" from the point of departure.

Third difference (developed later in the chapter): the nature of the concluding transformation. We will return to this under the heading of the closing extreme.

The Intercapital Dimension: C' as the Form of Another Capital

Marx now develops the social dimension of C'. "In the circuit of an individual industrial capital, C' as C appears not as the form of this capital, but as the form of another industrial capital, in so far as the means of production are the product of this other capital." Concretely: the act M–C (i.e. M–mp) of the first capital is for the second capital C'–M'. The purchase of means of production by capital A is the sale of commodity capital by capital B.

This leads to a subtle analysis of the two elements of the production process, L (labour-power) and mp (means of production), within the act M–C. In the circulation act M–C^L_mp, "L and mp behave identically in so far as they are commodities in the hands of their sellers"—the workers who sell labour-power, and the possessors of means of production who sell the latter. For the buyer, whose money here functions as money capital, both figure merely as commodities, as long as he has not yet bought them, thus as long as they confront his money-form capital as the commodities of others. But they differ in one respect: mp is C' in the hands of its seller and can thus be capital if mp is the commodity form of that seller's capital, whereas L is always just a commodity for the worker, becoming capital only in the hands of the buyer, as a component part of P. The worker's labour-power is never capital in the worker's hands; it is made into capital only at the moment of purchase, and then only as an element of the buyer's productive capital. This asymmetry is a compact statement of the capital-relation.

Why C' Can Never Open the Circuit as Mere C

Marx insists: "C' can therefore never open a circuit as mere C, as merely the commodity form of the capital value." As commodity capital, C' has a dual aspect:

  • From the point of view of use-value: it is the product of the function of P—here, yarn—whose elements L and mp, emerging from circulation as commodities, have only functioned to fashion this product.
  • From the point of view of value: it is the capital value P plus the surplus-value m produced in the function of P.

The two aspects cannot be collapsed. It is only in the circuit of C' itself that C = P = the capital value can and must separate itself from the portion of C' in which surplus-value exists—from the surplus product in which the surplus-value is hidden. Whether the two are actually separable (as with yarn, where the physical product can be divided) or not (as with a machine, a single indivisible unit), they become separable in principle as soon as C' has been transformed into M'. Money is homogeneous and infinitely divisible; the value composition becomes explicit in the money-form.

The Divisibility Analysis: 10,000 lb. of Yarn

This is the chapter's most technically detailed passage. Marx works through an extended numerical example to demonstrate that the conceptual division of C' into c + v + s can be materially performed. The total commodity product is 10,000 lb. of yarn with a value of £500. The capital value is £422, the surplus-value £78.

  • 8,440 lb. = £422 = the capital value, separable from the surplus-value.
  • 1,560 lb. = £78 = the surplus-value (surplus product).

If the capitalist first sells 8,440 lb. for £422, this 8,440 lb. represents C, the capital value in commodity form; the surplus product (1,560 lb., £78) only circulates later. The capitalist can complete C–M–C^L_mp (reconstituting his production elements) before the circulation of the surplus product c–m–c.

Alternatively, he can sell 7,440 lb. at £372 and 1,000 lb. at £50. With the first part (7,440 lb. = £372) he replaces constant capital c; with the second (1,000 lb. = £50) he replaces variable capital v (labour-power). Note the arithmetic: 7,440 + 1,000 + 1,560 = 10,000; £372 + £50 + £78 = £500.

Marx then pushes the decomposition further, showing that each portion can itself be broken down in the same proportions. The 7,440 lb. = £372, representing constant capital, decomposes into:

  • 5,535.360 lb. = £276.768 — replaces the constant part (value of means of production used up in the 7,440 lb.)
  • 744 lb. = £37.200 — replaces the variable capital
  • 1,160.640 lb. = £58.032 — the surplus-value in the form of surplus product

Having sold 7,440 lb., the capitalist can replace the capital value contained in it (from the sale of 6,279.360 lb. at £313.968—note 5,535.360 + 744 = 6,279.360, and £276.768 + £37.200 = £313.968) and spend the value of the surplus product (1,160.640 lb. = £58.032) as revenue.

The 1,000 lb. = £50 (variable capital) decomposes into:

  • 744 lb. = £37.200 — constant capital value in the 1,000 lb.
  • 100 lb. = £5 — variable capital value
  • 156 lb. = £7.800 — surplus product

So 844 lb. (744 + 100) = £42.200 replaces the capital value contained in the 1,000 lb.; 156 lb. = £7.800 is consumable surplus.

The remaining 1,560 lb. = £78 decomposes into:

  • 1,160.640 lb. = £58.032 — replaces the value of means of production contained in the 1,560 lb.
  • 156 lb. = £7.800 — replaces variable capital value
  • Together: 1,316.640 lb. = £65.832 — replacement of total capital value
  • 243.360 lb. = £12.168 — surplus product to be spent as revenue

(The arithmetic checks: 1,160.640 + 156 + 243.360 = 1,560; £58.032 + £7.800 + £12.168 = £78.)

The crowning move is the per-pound decomposition. Since each element c, v, s in the yarn is divisible into the same component parts, so is each individual pound of yarn worth 1 shilling = 12d:

  • c = 0.744 lb. = 8.928 d.
  • v = 0.100 lb. = 1.200 d.
  • s = 0.156 lb. = 1.872 d.
  • c + v + s = 1 lb. yarn = 12 d.

The conceptual proportions of the total are physically present in every single pound. Marx then tabulates the three partial sales and sums them, showing that the aggregate is identical to a single sale of the whole 10,000 lb.: constant capital = 7,440 lb. = £372; variable capital = 1,000 lb. = £50; surplus-value = 1,560 lb. = £78; total 10,000 lb. = £500. The conceptual decomposition reproduces the empirical totality exactly.

The Sale "As Such" Versus the Circuit Function

Marx then draws a sharp limit on what this example proves. "Taken by itself, C'–M' is nothing more than a sale of 10,000 lb. of yarn." To the buyer, yarn is just a commodity; what interests him is the price of 1 shilling per lb. If the buyer enquires into value composition, it is only "with the crafty intention of showing that it could be sold below 1 shilling per lb. and the seller would still be doing good business." The quantity the buyer purchases depends on his own needs, and if he owns a weaving-mill, on the composition of his own capital—not on that of the spinner. The ratio in which C' serves partly to replace capital and partly as surplus product "exists only in the circuit of the capital whose commodity form is represented by the 10,000 lb. of yarn. It has nothing to do with the sale as such."

This is an important methodological clarification. The decomposition c + v + s describes a functional relation internal to the circuit, not a description of the market act. Marx adds the standing assumption that "C' is sold at its value"—so only the transformation of form is at issue. He acknowledges that for C' as a functional form, "it is of course decisive... whether and to what extent price and value diverge"—but "here, where we are merely considering distinctions of form, this is of no concern." The abstraction is deliberate and bounded.

Retrospective Characterization of Forms I and II

Before completing form III, Marx recapitulates the other two circuits to sharpen the contrast.

Form I, M...M'. Production appears in the middle, between the two complementary and opposed phases of circulation; it is over before the concluding C'–M' begins. Money is advanced as capital, transformed into elements of production, then into the commodity product, then back into money. "This is a finished and complete cycle of business, the result being money which can be used by anyone for anything." Hence the recommencement of the cycle is indicated only as a possibility. M...M' may be the final circuit—concluding the functioning of a capital that is then withdrawn from business—or the first circuit of a newly entering capital. The general movement is M...M', from money to more money. Note the ideological consequence: because production appears merely as an interruption between two money-phases, form I is the natural home of Mercantilist thinking, which fixes on money as the goal.

Form II, P...C'–M–C...P(P'). Here the entire circulation process follows the first P and precedes the second, in the opposite order to form I. The first P is productive capital; its function is the production process, as precondition for the subsequent circulation. The concluding P, however, is not the production process: it is only the renewed existence of the industrial capital in its form of productive capital—the result of the transformation of capital value into L + mp, the objective and subjective factors whose union constitutes productive capital. Whether P or P', the capital is present at the conclusion in a form in which it must again function as productive capital. The general form P...P' is the form of reproduction and does not announce that valorization is the purpose of the process.

This has major ideological consequences. Because P...P' presents reproduction as the purpose, "classical economics found it all the more easy to ignore the specifically capitalist form of the production process, and to present production as such as the purpose of the process"—to produce as much and as cheaply as possible and exchange the product for as many other products as possible, partly to repeat production (M–C), partly for consumption (m–c). Since M and m appear only as evanescent means of circulation, "the peculiarities of both money and money capital could be overlooked, the whole process then appearing simple and natural, i. e. possessing the naturalness of superficial rationality." In the case of commodity capital, similarly, profit "was occasionally forgotten," and capital figured simply as a commodity—whenever the production circuit as a whole was discussed; only when the value components were discussed did it figure as commodity capital. "Accumulation, of course, appeared in the same light as production." Marx is showing that each form has a corresponding ideological trap: form I breeds Mercantilism, form II breeds the "naturalness" of productionist classicism that erases the capitalist specificity of the process.

Completion of Form III: The Determining Role of C'

Form III, C'–M'–C...P...C', opens with the two phases of circulation in the same order as form II (C–M–C); then P follows with its function (the production process), as in form I; the circuit closes with C', the result of that process. Just as form II closed with P (the merely renewed existence of productive capital), so form III closes with C' (the renewed existence of commodity capital); just as in form II the capital in its concluding form P had to begin again as a production process, so here it must reopen with the reappearance of industrial capital in the form of commodity capital, with the circulation phase C'–M'. "Both forms of the circuit are incomplete, because they do not conclude with M', with the valorized capital value transformed back into money." Both must be continued further and hence include reproduction. The overall circuit in form III is C'...C'.

What differentiates the third form: "it is only in this circuit that the valorized capital value, and not the original capital value that has still to be valorized, appears as the starting-point of its own valorization." C', as capital-relation, is the point of departure and thus has a determining effect on the whole circuit, in so far as the circuit includes, even in its first phase, both the circuit of the capital value and that of the surplus-value. And surplus-value must on average, even if not in every individual circuit, be partly spent as revenue (passing through the circulation c–m–c) and partly function as an element of capital accumulation.

Consumption as Presupposition

A major consequence follows: "In the form C'...C', the consumption of the entire commodity product is presupposed as the condition for the normal course of the circuit of capital itself." The individual consumption of the worker and the individual consumption of the non-accumulated part of the surplus product together comprise total individual consumption. Thus consumption in its entirety—individual and productive—enters the circuit of C' as a precondition.

Marx distinguishes carefully:

  • Productive consumption — which "in the nature of the case includes the individual consumption of the worker, for labour-power is the permanent product, within certain limits, of the worker's individual consumption" — is carried on by every individual capital.
  • Individual consumption — other than what is necessary for the existence of the individual capitalist — "is presupposed only as a social act, in no way as the act of the individual capitalist."

In forms I and II, the overall movement presents itself as a movement of the advanced capital value. In form III, the valveorized capital, in the shape of the total commodity product, forms the starting-point and possesses the form of capital in movement—commodity capital. "It is only after its transformation into money that this movement splits up into movement of capital and movement of revenue." The division of the total social product—and the particular division of the product of every individual commodity capital—into an individual consumption fund and a reproduction fund is included in this form of the circuit of capital.

Expansion and Contraction Across the Three Forms

Marx now compares how each form accommodates changes in scale.

  • M...M' allows possible expansion according to the scale on which m enters the new circuit.
  • P...P: P can begin the new circuit with the same value, perhaps even a lesser value, and yet still represent expanded reproduction—if, for example, the commodity elements are cheapened by increased productivity of labour. Conversely, a productive capital that has grown in value may represent materially more restricted reproduction, if the elements of production have become dearer. "The same applies for C'...C'."

This is a critical point: the magnitude of value of the opening extreme does not transparently index the material scale of reproduction. Value and material scale can move in opposite directions because productivity and prices shift. The circuit's formal magnitude is not a reliable measure of its real content.

C' as Premise of Production—the Social Dimension

Marx deepens the "premise" argument. "In C'...C', capital in the commodity form is the premise of production; it reappears as a premise within this circuit in the second C." If this C is not yet produced or reproduced, the circuit is inhibited; it must be reproduced, "for the most part as the C' of another industrial capital." In this circuit, C' exists as point of departure, point of transit, and conclusion; "in other words it is always there. It is a permanent condition for the reproduction process."

The Special Character of the Closing Transformation in Form III

Marx now specifies a further distinguishing characteristic, addressing all three circuits' commonality: each opens and closes in the same form, returning to its initial form and recommencing. The initial forms M, P, and C' are the forms in which the capital value is advanced (in form III together with the surplus-value adhering to it)—the original forms for the circuit; the concluding forms M', P, and C' are in each case the transformed form of a preceding functional form which is not the original.

  • In form I, M' is the transformed form of C', the transformation effected by a simple process of commodity circulation (a formal change of position between commodity and money).
  • In form II, the closing P is the transformed form of M (again a formal change of position).
  • In form III, C' is the transformed form of P, the productive capital.

But in form III the transformation differs in two respects: (1) it affects not just the functional form of the capital but also the magnitude of its value; and (2) the transformation is not the result of a merely formal change of position belonging to the circulation process, but rather the real transformation which the use form and the value of the commodity components of the productive capital have undergone in the production process. In forms I and II the closing extremes are the direct results of the circulation process; in form III the closing C' is the result of the production process. This is why only in form III does the closing extreme bear a new magnitude (surplus-value) born of production rather than circulation.

The Presupposition of Outside Capitals—Form III's Unique Demand

Marx examines what each circuit presupposes outside itself.

  • Form I: M', as closing point, is the transformed form of C' (C'–M'); it presupposes M in the hands of the buyer, existing outside the circuit M...M', brought in by the sale of C' and made into the closing form.
  • Form II: the closing P presupposes L and mp (C) as existing outside it, incorporated as the closing form by M–C.
  • "But apart from the final extreme, the circuit of the individual money capital does not presuppose the existence of money capital as such, and the circuit of the individual productive capital does not presuppose the existence of productive capital in the circuit itself. In form I, M may be the only money capital, and in form II P may be the only productive capital, that appears on the historical scene."

Form III is different: C is twice presupposed outside the circuit. First, in the circuit itself C'–M'–C...P...C'—where M–C, i.e. the purchase of L and mp, presupposes C as a commodity in the hands of its seller; this C is itself commodity capital in so far as it is the product of a capitalist production process ("and even when this is not the case, it appears as commodity capital in the hands of the merchant"). Second, in the second c of c–m–c, which must similarly be present as a commodity in order to be bought. In either case, whether commodity capital or not, L and mp are commodities as much as C' is, and act towards one another as commodities. Thus, in so far as C' = C (L + mp), commodities are its own elements of formation and must be replaced by equivalent commodities in circulation—just as must the second c in c–m–c.

All Commodities Tend to Become Commodity Capital

Marx adds a systematic generalization: "on the basis of the capitalist mode of production, as the prevailing mode, all commodities must be commodity capital in the hands of their sellers." They continue to be so in the hands of the merchant, or they become so if they were not previously. Alternatively, they can be commodities such as imported articles, which replace original commodity capital and hence simply give it another form of existence. This is the historical-universalizing claim: under capitalism, the commodity form and the capital form tend to coincide for the seller of any commodity.

The Regrouping of L and mp into Productive Capital

Marx notes that L and mp, the elements of P, "do not possess the same shape, as forms of existence of P, as they did on the various commodity markets from which they were brought together. They are now united, and in their combination they can function as productive capital." The scattered commodities of the market are gathered and fused into a new functional unity—the very act of production.

Why C Is Premise of C Only in Form III

Marx offers the decisive formal explanation: "If it is only in this form III, within the circuit itself, that C appears as a premise of C, this is because the starting-point is capital in the commodity form." The circuit opens by the conversion of C' (in so far as it functions as capital value, whether or not increased by surplus-value) into the commodities forming its elements of production. This conversion comprises the entire circulation process C–M–C (= L + mp) and is its result. So C stands at both extremes—though the second extreme, receiving its form C from outside (from the commodity market) by way of M–C, "is not the last extreme of the circuit, but only the latter of the first two stages that comprise its circulation process." Its result is P; then P's function begins. It is only as the result of this, i.e. not as the result of the circulation process, that C' appears as the close of the circuit, in the same form as the original extreme C'. In M...M' and P...P, by contrast, the closing extremes M' and P are the direct results of the circulation process. Hence in form III the closing C' presupposes the existence of other commodities in the hands of others; the circuit's opening draws them in.

From Individual to Total Social Capital

This brings the chapter's pivotal theoretical leap. "Precisely because the circuit C'...C' presupposes in its description the existence of another industrial capital in the form C (= L + mp) (and mp comprises other capitals of various kinds, e.g. machines, coal, oil, etc.), it itself demands to be considered not only as the general form of the circuit... but at the same time as the form of motion of the sum of individual capitals, i.e. of the total social capital of the capitalist class." The movement of any individual industrial capital appears simply as a partial one, "intertwined with the others and conditioned by them."

Marx gives a concrete instruction: if we consider the total annual commodity product of a country and analyze the movement in which one part replaces the productive capital of all individual businesses while another part goes into the individual consumption of the different classes, then we are considering C'...C' as a form of motion of both the social capital and the surplus-value or surplus product produced by it.

He then states the crucial sum-relation and its limitation: "The fact that the social capital is equal to the sum of the individual capitals (including joint-stock capital and also state capital, in so far as governments employ productive wage-labour in mines, railways, etc., and function as industrial capitalists), and that the total movement of the social capital is equal to the algebraic sum of the movements of the individual capitals, in no way prevents this motion, as the motion of an isolated individual capital, from displaying phenomena different from those displayed by the same motion, when it is viewed as a part of the total motion of the social capital." In the latter aspect, "problems can be resolved whose solution must be presupposed in considering the circuit of a single individual capital, instead of resulting from the study of this." This is the fallacy-of-composition insight: aggregate phenomena are not merely sums of individual phenomena; the social level reveals regularities that the individual level must assume but cannot explain.

The Unique Claim of C'...C' on Total Movement

"C'...C' is the only circuit in which the capital value originally advanced forms only a part of the extreme that opens the movement," and in which the movement thereby "proclaims itself from the start as a total movement of industrial capital." This total movement is both of the part of the product that replaces the productive capital and of the part that forms surplus product—partly spent as revenue, partly serving as an element of accumulation. In so far as surplus-value is spent as revenue, individual consumption is involved. And this is also involved in so far as the starting-point C, the commodity, exists as some particular useful article: "every capitalistically produced article is commodity capital, irrespective of whether its use form destines it for productive or individual consumption, or for both."

Marx's comparative summary of the three circuits' announcements is definitive:

  • M...M' indicates only the value aspect—the valorization of the advanced capital value as the purpose of the whole process.
  • P...P (P') points to the production process of capital as a reproduction process, with productive capital remaining the same or growing (accumulation).
  • C'...C', already in its initial extreme, proclaims itself as a form of capitalist commodity production and comprises both productive and individual consumption from the start; productive consumption and the valorization included in it "appear simply as a branch of its movement."

Finally, because C' can exist in a use form incapable of entering any further production process (consumption goods, for instance), it is apparent from the start that the various value components of C', expressed in portions of the product, must assume different positions depending on whether C'...C' is taken as the form of motion of the total social capital or as the independent movement of an individual industrial capital. "In all these peculiarities, this circuit points beyond its own existence as the isolated circuit of a merely individual capital."

The Methodological Reckoning: Why Forms I and II Were the Basis

Marx states the payoff: in the figure C'...C', the movement of the commodity capital appears both as premise of the independent circuit of the individual capital and as conditioned by it in turn. Hence if this figure is conceived in its particularity, "it is no longer sufficient to rest content with the fact that the metamorphoses C'–M' and M–C are on the one hand functionally determined sections of the metamorphosis of the capital, and on the other hand links in the general circulation of commodities." It becomes necessary to show how the metamorphoses of an individual capital are intertwined with those of other individual capitals and with the part of the total product destined for individual consumption. This is why Marx's analysis of the circuit of the individual industrial capital was "primarily based on the first two forms." Forms I and II provided the simpler, methodologically manageable entry; form III is the portal to the more complex social totality.

Agriculture, the Physiocrats, and the Historical Illustration

Marx illustrates with agriculture, where accounts run from one harvest to the next. There, C'...C' does appear as the form of a single individual capital. Figure II proceeds from the sowing; figure III from the harvest—or, in Physiocratic language, from avances and reprises (advances and returns). A footnote clarifies that the French Physiocratic writers of the 1750s and 60s—especially Quesnay and Turgot—were the first economists to begin to analyze production rather than merely circulation; they believed, however, that only agricultural labour was truly productive (a doctrine Marx examines in Theories of Surplus-Value, Part I, Chapter 11).

The agricultural example yields a further point: "In figure III the movement of the capital value appears from the start simply as a part of the movement of the general mass of products, while in figures I and II the movement of C' simply forms a moment in the movement of a single capital."

Form III's Illusion and Its Corrective

Marx issues a warning: "In figure III the commodities on the market form the permanent premise of the process of production and reproduction. Hence if attention is fixed exclusively on this figure, all the elements of the production process seem to proceed from commodity circulation and to exist only as commodities. This one-sided conception overlooks the elements of the production process that are independent of the commodity elements." The very completeness of form III's social picture can breed an illusion—that everything is circulation—erasing the natural, technical, and material conditions of production that are not themselves commodities.

Expanded Reproduction and the Material Conditions

Because in C'...C' the total product (the total value) is the point of departure, it becomes evident that, "leaving aside foreign trade, reproduction on an expanded scale, with productivity otherwise remaining the same, can take place only if the material elements of the additional productive capital are already contained in the part of the surplus product to be capitalized." That is, in so far as one year's production serves as precondition for the next, or in so far as production can occur together with simple reproduction within a year, surplus product is immediately produced in the form that enables it to function as additional capital. This is a fundamental condition of accumulation: the physical, material means for expanded reproduction must be produced as surplus product before they can be reinvested.

Marx adds a crucial qualification regarding productivity: "Increased productivity can increase only the material substance of capital, and cannot raise its value; but it still forms additional material for valorization." Productivity raises the quantity of use-values produced (the material substance) without directly raising value—yet this additional material is precisely what makes expanded valorization possible. Value and material wealth diverge.

The Quesnay Conclusion

The chapter closes: "C'...C' is the basis of Quesnay's Tableau économique, and it shows great discernment on his part that he selected this form in opposition to M...M' (the form fixed on and isolated by the Mercantile System), and not P...P." Quesnay's choice of the harvest-based circuit, as opposed to the money-circuit of Mercantilism, was conceptually correct—the circuit of commodity capital is the appropriate form in which to depict the total annual product's movement and reproduction. Marx thereby situates his own achievement in relation to the first genuine attempt at a macroeconomic reproduction schema.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Fundamental Principles Revealed

First, the circuit-form is ideologically consequential. Each of the three circuits foregrounds a different moment and thereby occludes others. Form I (M...M') invites Mercantilism—money as the terminus. Form II (P...P') invites the classicist ideology of production "as such," its "naturalness of superficial rationality," erasing the specifically capitalist character of production. Form III (C'...C') announces the total movement, but, viewed one-sidedly, invites the illusion that everything originates in circulation. Marx's formal analysis is thus simultaneously an ideology-critique: the very shapes in which capital moves generate characteristic misrecognitions.

Second, the surplus-value is present at the threshold of form III. Because C' opens as capital value + surplus-value, the circuit of commodity capital structurally includes consumption and the capital/revenue split. The distinction between the reproduction fund and the individual consumption fund is inscribed in the form itself. This is what makes form III the appropriate entry to the analysis of total social capital and the reproduction schemas.

Third, interdependence is constitutive, not incidental. Form III presupposes other industrial capitals in the form C = L + mp. This is not an external complication but a defining feature: the circuit of any one capital literally embeds the circuits of others. The mutual presupposition of capitals (what is M–C for one is C'–M' for another) means the "individual" capital is an abstraction; the concrete reality is the total social capital, whose movement is the algebraic sum of individual movements while nevertheless exhibiting emergent properties the individual circuit cannot display.

Fourth, productivity expands material substance but not value. Productivity cannot raise value yet supplies the additional material for valorization. Value and material wealth are conceptually distinct, and expanded reproduction at constant productivity requires the material elements of additional productive capital to be already contained in the surplus product to be capitalized.

Broader Implications

For economic methodology: The chapter enacts a movement from the abstract (individual capital) to the concrete (total social capital) by showing that the third form is structurally more complex and demands the social level. The fallacy-of-composition insight—that aggregate phenomena differ from isolated ones and that social analysis resolves problems the individual level must presuppose—is a foundational contribution to macroeconomics as a distinct level of analysis.

For the theory of reproduction: The chapter is the threshold to Quesnay's Tableau économique, which Marx vindicates as the first genuine depiction of total social reproduction. By showing that C'...C'—the harvest-based, commodity-capital circuit—is the correct basis for the Tableau, Marx places his own reproduction schemas in a line of descent from the Physiocrats while correcting their fundamental error (that only agricultural labour is productive).

For the critique of political economy: The chapter demonstrates that the systematic analysis of forms is not formalist pedantry but the key that unlocks both the ideological illusions and the social realities of capital. Each circuit-form corresponds to a theoretical tradition (Mercantilism, classicism, and the circulation-illusion), and the three-circuit analysis is at once a taxonomy of these traditions and a refutation of their one-sidedness.

For practice: The agricultural analogy—reckoning from harvest to harvest—shows that form III is not merely analytic but experienced as the standpoint of any enterprise that begins with a finished total product and must divide it into replacement and surplus, capital and revenue, reproduction and consumption. In this sense, the circuit of commodity capital is both the most concrete form of capital's motion and the most immediately visible to the participants themselves—which is precisely why its surface can mislead.

The essential lesson is this: capital is a totality in motion. Its three circuits are not three separate things but three aspects of one self-reproducing process, and only the circuit of commodity capital—because it opens with the whole valorized product and presupposes the whole world of other capitals—makes the total social character of that process visible. The chapter thus completes the individual-capital analysis and simultaneously opens the door to the study of the total social capital, its reproduction, and its crises.
