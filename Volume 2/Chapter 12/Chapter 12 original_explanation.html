Volume 2 • Chapter 12: The Working Period

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 12, "The Working Period," is the opening chapter of Part Two of Capital, Volume II, the section devoted to the turnover of capital. It arrives immediately after Marx has established, in Part One, the conceptual machinery of the circuit of capital and the division between fixed and circulating capital. The chapter's job is to introduce a new determinant of capital's motion that cannot be reduced to any of the categories already established: the sheer physical duration of the act of production, independent of the length of the working day, independent of the ratio of fixed to circulating capital, and independent of the organic composition of capital.

The core thesis is deceptively simple and enormously consequential: the length of time a labour process must be continuously sustained before it yields a finished, saleable commodity is itself a variable that regulates the turnover of capital, and it forces the capitalist to advance additional capital in proportion to that length. Marx contrasts cotton spinning — in which a finished commodity is turned out every day and every week — with locomotive manufacture — in which one and the same act of production must be sustained for perhaps three months before a single finished product exists. The two lines of business may share an identical ten-hour working day, an identical division between constant and variable capital, and identical proportions of fixed and circulating capital — and yet the locomotive manufacturer will require twelve times the circulating capital of the spinner, because the spinner recovers his outlay weekly while the machine-builder must add fresh doses of capital week after week for three months before any of it returns.

To name and theorize this, Marx introduces a concept that is his own: the working period [Arbeitsperiode], as distinct from the working day. The working day is the length of time the worker must daily expend his labour-power. The working period is the number of interrelated working days required in a given branch to complete a finished product. One hundred working days of ten hours each are, for the workers, one hundred discrete labour processes; for the product, they are a single continuous act of production of a thousand working hours. This duality — discrete for labour-power, continuous for the product — is the hinge on which the entire chapter turns.

The chapter's intellectual objective is therefore fourfold. First, to establish the working period as an independent variable in the theory of turnover. Second, to derive from it a quantitative law: the additional circulating capital required grows with the length of the working period. Third, to show that this has historical consequences — long working periods are only compatible with fully capitalist production once capital is sufficiently concentrated and credit sufficiently developed, because only then can the capitalist afford to risk other people's capital over a long period. And fourth, to demonstrate that the natural limits of the working period (planting, breeding, timber-growing) impose constraints that no technique can abolish, and that the coercion of ready cash therefore produces systematic distortions in agriculture — such as the premature slaughter of cattle.

The chapter thus situates itself at the intersection of pure economic theory, the history of capitalism, and political economy in its most classical, Ricardian sense — while also deploying literary, colonial, and agricultural sources to show that the abstraction of turnover has concrete, sometimes brutal, human results.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The founding contrast: discrete versus continuous production

Marx opens with a deliberately minimal comparison. Take two branches — cotton spinning and locomotive manufacture — and assume that each has the same working day of ten hours. In spinning, "a definite quantity of the finished product, cotton yarn, is turned out every day and every week." In locomotive manufacture, "the labour process must be repeated for perhaps three months in order to produce a finished product, one locomotive."

The distinction Marx draws is not that one process is "slow" and the other "fast," but something structurally different. In spinning, the product is discrete in nature: the labour process begins afresh each day or each week, and each day or week yields a self-contained, finished commodity. In locomotive building, the labour process is continuous: it "stretches over a large number of daily labour processes, which supply a finished product only after a protracted interval, through the connectedness and continuity of their operations."

Even though the daily labour process is identical in both cases, there is a stark divergence in the duration of the act of production — the duration of the repeated labour processes needed to turn out the product in its finished form, to send it to market as a commodity, and thus to transform it from productive capital into commodity capital. This is the terminology that anchors the chapter: productive capital is capital locked in the production sphere, commodity capital is capital in the form of a finished, saleable product. The length of time separating the one from the other is what is at stake.

The first theoretical move Marx makes is defensive: he insists this distinction "has nothing to do with" the fixed/circulating capital distinction. It would obtain even if exactly the same proportions of fixed and circulating capital were applied in both lines of business. The fixity or fluidity of capital components is a different axis altogether. This is a crucial methodological warning: Marx is separating out one variable and holding others constant, so that when he later re-introduces the fixed/circulating distinction, its effects can be measured against the working-period effect. The chapter is, in this sense, a controlled thought experiment built up piece by piece.

Infinite variety: the working period as an empirical spectrum

Marx immediately widens the frame. The differences in duration of the act of production are not a simple binary of "short" and "long," but a spectrum of "infinite variety," and they occur not only between branches but within the same branch according to the scale of the object produced. He offers a rapid-fire catalogue:

  • A dwelling-house is built faster than a large factory; hence requires fewer continuous labour processes.
  • A locomotive takes three months; a battleship takes a year, "if not several."
  • The production of grain demands almost a year; horned cattle, several years; timber, anywhere from twelve to a hundred years.
  • A road can be built in a few months; a railway requires years.
  • An ordinary carpet takes perhaps a week; a Gobelin tapestry takes years.

The catalogue is not ornamental. Each example layers a different dimension of the problem: scale (house vs. factory), physical mass (locomotive vs. battleship), biological reproduction (cattle, grain), arboreal growth (timber), infrastructural capital (road, railway), and artisanal skill-intensity (carpet, Gobelin). By the end of the list, the reader is meant to grasp that the working period cannot be treated as an exception or a curiosity of a few trades — it is a universal feature of production, and its variation is continuous and open-ended.

The controlled experiment: posing equal capitals against unequal turnover

The analytical heart of the chapter is a rigorously specified hypothetical. Marx asks us to assume that a spinning mill and a locomotive works apply equal capitals, with:

  • the same division between constant and variable capital,
  • the same division between fixed and fluid (circulating) components,
  • the same length of the working day,
  • the same division between necessary and surplus labour.

He then brackets out the circulation process entirely (to isolate the production-time effect), assuming that "both yarn and locomotive are produced to order and paid for on delivery of the finished product." This is a vital methodological cleanliness: since circulating capital normally flows back only through sale, and sale times can differ, Marx removes sale-time differences so that what remains is purely the effect of production time.

Under these assumptions, the outcomes diverge dramatically:

  • The spinner: at the end of the week, upon delivery of the yarn, he "receives back his outlay of circulating capital as well as the wear and tear of the fixed capital contained in the value of the yarn." He can now repeat the same circuit with the same capital. His turnover is complete. Note that the parenthetical "(we ignore the surplus-value)" simply abstracts from profit to focus on the reproduction of the capital advanced.
  • The locomotive manufacturer: he must "lay out fresh capital on wages and raw material week after week for three months, and only after three months, when the locomotive is delivered, does the circulating capital laid out bit by bit during this time for one and the same act of production, to produce one and the same commodity, exist again in a form in which it can begin its circuit once more." Wear and tear on the machinery, similarly, is replaced only at the end of the three months.

The numerical consequence is stated bluntly: "One business has an outlay for one week, the other the same weekly outlay multiplied by twelve. All other circumstances being assumed equal, the one must have twelve times as much circulating capital at its disposal as the other." The fact that the weekly capitals are equal is irrelevant; the critical variable is the total capital advanced before any reflux occurs. In arithmetic terms, if the weekly outlay is c and the working period is p weeks, the circulating capital that must be kept committed is c × p. For the spinner, p = 1; for the machine-builder, p = 12. The difference of 12× is entirely a function of p, not of c.

Marx sharpens the point: "Whatever may be the size of the capital advanced, in the one case it is advanced only for one week, in the other for twelve, before it can be used for a new operation, before the same operation can be repeated with it, or one of a different kind begun." The variable is the time of advance — the length of time for which a given capital value is locked up before it can serve a new labour or valorization process.

The conceptual innovation: the working period defined

Having shown that the working-period difference has massive consequences, Marx pauses to name the phenomenon. He poses a fine-grained example: the building of a locomotive takes 100 working days. For the workers engaged, those 100 days are "a discontinuous (discrete) quantity"; they consist of "100 successive and separate ten-hour labour processes." But in relation to the product, "the 100 working days form a continuous quantity, a working day of 1,000 working hours, a single related act of production."

This is the moment of definition. "A working day of this kind, which is formed by the succession of more or less numerous interrelated working days, I call a working period." Marx then contrasts:

  • The working day = "the length of time for which the worker must daily expend his labour-power, must work."
  • The working period = "the number of inter-related working days that are required, in a particular line of business, to complete a finished product."

The duality is essential. In the working period, "the product of each working day is here only a partial product, which is taken a step further day by day and receives its finished shape, is a finished use-value, only at the close of a longer or shorter period of working time." Every day's product is incomplete, is not yet a commodity, and therefore cannot yet function as commodity capital.

The term partial product is doing significant theoretical work. In spinning, the daily output is a commodity. In locomotive building, the daily output is only a moment in a larger unity. This means the form of the product — discrete commodity vs. node in a continuous production — governs the possible rhythms of valorization.

Crisis, interruption, and the irreversibility of the interconnected act

Marx now derives an important consequence: interruptions and disturbances of the social production process, such as crises, "have a very different effect on those products of labour that are discrete in nature, and those whose production requires a longer connected period."

For discrete products like yarn or coal, an interruption means one day's production is simply not followed by a fresh production the next day. The damage is limited to the day's output being withheld.

For ships, buildings, railways, "it is not only work that is interrupted, but an inter-connected act of production. If the job is not carried any further, then the means of production and the labour already consumed in its production have been spent to no avail. Even if it is taken up again, deterioration will always have taken place in the meantime." The capital already sunk is not merely idle but partially destroyed — the unfinished ship rusts, the unfinished building weathers, and the labour already objectified in it is, in part, wasted. This is a qualitative asymmetry with profound implications: the longer the working period, the more the total capital advanced is exposed to the risk of irreversible decay during a stoppage.

Fixed capital in the long working period: layered depreciation and the twenty-year engine

Marx now reintroduces the fixed/circulating distinction — but carefully, to show that it behaves asymmetrically under the pressure of a long working period.

The portion of value that fixed capital surrenders every day "builds up in layers throughout the whole duration of the working period." The fixed capital itself, however, is "advanced to the production process for a longer period of time; it does not need to be renewed until an interval of perhaps several years has elapsed."

The illustrative case is the steam engine. Whether the engine gives up value bit by bit each day to yarn (a discrete product) or in larger, delayed amounts over three months to a locomotive (a continuous product), this "in no way alters the outlay of capital needed to acquire the steam engine." The engine is renewed, in both cases, only after perhaps twenty years. The reflux of its value — weekly in one case, quarterly in the other — is fast enough in both cases relative to the engine's own lifespan (twenty years). Therefore, "as long as the individual period in which its value flows back bit by bit with the sale of the product is always shorter than its own period of existence, the same steam engine continues to function in the production process for several working periods."

The principle buried here is a ratio of horizons: as long as the turnover period of the fixed asset's value is shorter than the physical lifetime of the asset, the fixed asset is not a limiting factor. A three-month delay in the reflux of a steam engine's depreciation is trivial against a twenty-year lifespan. This is why Marx can say that the lengthened working period does not require renewed outlay of fixed capital, only delayed reflux.

Circulating capital: the adding of new doses and the impossibility of self-renewal

"It is otherwise with the circulating components of the capital advanced." Here the logic is fundamentally different, and the chapter's gravity shifts.

The labour-power bought for a given week is used up during that week, has objectified itself in the product, and must be paid for at the end of the week. This outlay is repeated weekly throughout the three months. Crucially: "the expenditure of this portion of capital in the one week does not enable the capitalist to cover the acquisition of labour in the next week." The capital advanced does not reproduce itself from within the unfinished product.

Hence: "New, additional capital must be spent each week in payment for labour-power, and if we set aside all credit relations the capitalist must be able to lay out wages for the whole period of three months, even though he pays them only in weekly doses." This is the decisive point. If credit relations are set aside, the capitalist must have the entire wage bill for the whole working period at his disposal — even though he disburses it in weekly tranches. The same holds for raw materials and ancillaries. Layer after layer of labour and material is deposited in the unfinished product, but "all this is transferred to an unfinished product that does not yet have the shape of a finished commodity, and is thus not capable of circulation."

The formulaic version is important. The circulating capital required is not simply the weekly outlay but the accumulated outlay for the entire working period: C_circ = c × p, where c is the weekly circulating outlay and p the number of weeks in the working period. And unlike the fixed capital case, this is a binding constraint, because the circulating capital cannot spontaneously renew itself from an incomplete product.

Turnover time as production time plus circulation time: two structural consequences

Marx now formally places the working period within the theory of turnover. "The turnover time of capital, however, is the sum of its production time and its circulation time. A lengthening of the production time thus reduces the speed of turnover as much as a lengthening of the circulation time." In symbols: T = P + C, where T is turnover time, P is production time (dominated here by the working period), and C is circulation time. Increase P and T rises; increase C and T rises; the effects on turnover speed are symmetrical.

But Marx notes two specific features of the present case:

First, the "lengthened stay in the production sphere." Capital advanced in the first week — on labour, raw materials, and the value given up by the fixed capital — "remains confined to the production sphere for the entire term of three months," and it is incorporated only into a product in formation, hence cannot pass into circulation as a commodity. The capital is stuck, not because it cannot circulate, but because its material form is not yet a commodity.

Second, since the working period "lasts three months, and in actual fact simply forms a single interrelated labour process, every week a new dose of circulating capital must be added to the preceding one." From this Marx derives the key dynamic law: "The quantity of additional capital successively advanced thus grows with the length of the working period." As p grows, C_circ = c × p grows linearly.

He then restates the consequences under the controlled-experiment assumptions. In the first week, the outlay is the same for spinner and machine-builder. But the spinner can sell his yarn and rebuy labour-power and materials with the proceeds, continuing on the same scale. The machine-builder must wait three months before his first week's circulating capital can be transformed back into money for a fresh operation. Therefore:

  • First difference: a difference in the reflux of the same quantity of capital laid out.
  • Second difference: over three months, the same amount of productive capital is applied, but the amount of capital laid out differs enormously. The spinner renews the same capital quickly and repeats the operation; the machine-builder's capital renews slowly, so "new amounts of capital must therefore be steadily added to the old until its renewal period arrives."

Marx here anticipates a point he says will be treated "in the following chapter": the time of advance can grow without the amount of capital advanced growing in proportion to this length of time. That is, there are cases (as will be shown later — for example, when the working period is interrupted by natural processes rather than extended by brute addition of labour) where capital must be tied up longer but does not have to be quantitatively increased proportionally. He warns us not to conflate the two dimensions: length of the time of advance and magnitude of the capital tied up are related but not identical. Even so, the general case here is stark: "The capital has to be advanced for longer, and a larger amount of capital is tied up in the form of productive capital."

Historical sociology of the working period: from municipal roads to speculative London

The chapter now pivots to historical and institutional analysis — a move characteristic of Marx's method, in which an abstract economic distinction is shown to have specific historical bearings.

At less developed stages of capitalist production, "enterprises that require a long working period, and thus a large capital outlay for a longer time, particularly if they can be conducted only on a large scale, are often not pursued capitalistically at all." He gives concrete examples:

  • Roads and canals were built at the cost of the municipality or state — "in earlier periods mostly by forced labour, in so far as labour-power is concerned."
  • Products requiring long working periods are manufactured only to a minor extent with the capitalist's own financial means.
  • In house construction, the private individual for whom the house is being built "pays advances to the builder in successive portions," paying for the house bit by bit in proportion to the progress of production.

The logic is transparent: when a working period is long, the capital required is large; when no institutional mechanism (state, credit, joint-stock company) exists to pool or advance that capital, the transaction migrates out of the sphere of capitalist production and into the sphere of custom, state action, forced labour, or piecemeal client-financing. In effect, before credit, the customer becomes the financier.

In developed capitalism, however, "massive capitals are concentrated in the hands of individuals" and "the associated capitalist (joint-stock companies) steps onto the scene alongside the individual capitalist" — and "credit, too, is developed." Under these conditions, "it is only in exceptional cases that a capitalist builder still builds houses to order for individual clients." Instead, he "makes a business out of building rows of houses and whole districts of towns for the market, just as individual capitalists make a business out of building railways as contractors."

The transition is thus triply institutional: concentration of capital, development of the credit system, and the arrival of the joint-stock form. All three mature alongside — and are partly necessitated by — the long working period.

The builder's testimony: the Bank Acts Committee of 1857

Marx supplies empirical evidence on the transformation of London housebuilding via a builder's testimony to the Bank Acts Committee of 1857. The source reference (Report from the Select Committee on Bank Acts, part 1, 1857, Evidence, nos. 5413–5418, 5435–5436) signals a documentary, official-inquiry provenance — the material is not Marx's own invention but a witness statement before a British parliamentary committee.

The witness's account, as Marx abbreviates it, runs as follows. In his youth, houses were generally built to order, and the price was paid to the contractor in instalments as stages of construction were completed. There was little speculative building; contractors resorted to it mainly just to keep their workers regularly occupied and hold the labour force together. Over the last forty years — roughly the 1817–1857 span — "all that has changed." There is now little building to order. A person wanting a new house "looks for one that has already been built on speculation, or is already in the process of being built." The contractor "no longer works directly for a client, but rather for the market; just like any other industrialist, he has to have finished goods for sale."

The scale-shift is quantified. Where previously a contractor might build three or four houses at a time on speculation, he "now has to buy an extensive piece of land (in the Continental sense, he leases it, usually for ninety-nine years), erect on it up to 100 or 200 houses, and thus involve himself in an undertaking that exceeds his own means some twenty to fifty times over." Funding is obtained by mortgage, and the mortgage money "is put at the contractor's disposal bit by bit as the building of the houses progresses." Marx draws the crisis dynamic sharply: "If a crisis breaks out, bringing the payment of these instalments to a halt, then the whole undertaking generally collapses; in the best case, the houses remain uncompleted until better times, while in the worst they are auctioned off at half price."

Two further features merit highlighting:

  1. The structural dependence on speculative building: "It is impossible nowadays for any contractor to get along without speculative building, and on a large scale at that." The long working period has ceased to be an inconvenience and has become an obligate condition of the trade.
  2. The locational shift of profit: "The profit on the actual construction is extremely slight; the main source of profit comes from raising the ground rent, and from the clever selection and exploitation of the building land." This is a striking anticipation of later analyses of urban rent — the builder's profit migrates from the production process to the land on which it occurs. "Almost the whole of Belgravia, Tyburnia and the countless thousands of villas around London have been built in this way, by speculative anticipation of the demand for houses."

The evidence is thus not just a case study of the working period; it is a case study of how a long working period reshapes an entire sector's industrial organization, financing, and profit source.

Credit, concentration, and the capitalist threshold of the long working period

Marx states the conclusion about the institutional preconditions directly. "Large-scale jobs needing particularly long working periods are fully suitable for capitalist production only when the concentration of capital is already well advanced, and when the development of the credit system offers the capitalist the convenient expedient of advancing and thus risking other people's capital instead of his own."

This formulation is important. Credit does not merely smooth out the timing mismatch produced by a long working period; it transfers the risk. The individual capitalist no longer has to hazard his own fortune; he hazards his creditors' funds. The chapter, in other words, links the theory of turnover to the theory of financial fragility — a linkage that will return again in Volume III.

But Marx immediately adds a purity caveat, preserving the analytical value of his earlier abstraction: "It is self-evident, however, that whether the capital advanced for production belongs to the person who uses it or not has no effect on the speed and time of turnover." Ownership of the capital does not modify the physical structure of the working period. Credit alters who bears the burden and whether the undertaking can be mounted at all — but not the duration or the arithmetic.

Shortening the working period: machinery, cooperation, and its dialectical cost

Marx now turns to the means of shortening the working period. He lists:

  • Cooperation
  • Division of labour
  • Application of machinery — all increase the product of the individual working day
  • These also shorten the working period for interconnected acts of production.

Concrete instances:

  • Machinery shortens the building time of houses, bridges, etc.
  • Reaping and threshing machines shorten the working period required to transform ripened corn into a finished commodity.
  • Improved ship-building techniques, producing greater speed, shorten the turnover time of the capital invested in shipping.

But here Marx introduces a dialectical twist that shows how subtle his analysis is. "These improvements, which shorten the working period and hence the time for which circulating capital has to be advanced, are generally bound up with an increased outlay of fixed capital." That is, the shortening of the working period is achieved by substituting fixed capital for advanced time — a trade-off, not a free lunch.

There is, however, another route: "The working period, however, can be shortened in some branches simply by an extension of cooperation; the completion of a railway is hastened by setting afoot great armies of workers and tackling the job from many different points in space." Here, no machine is required — merely the assembling of more workers and more material. But that too is expensive: "Here the turnover time is shortened by the growth of the capital advanced. More means of production and more labour-power have to be united under the capitalist's command."

The dialectical statement follows: "If the shortening of the working period is thus generally bound up with an increase in the capital advanced for this shorter time, so that the amount of capital advanced increases to the degree that the time of advance is shortened, we should remember that, apart from the total volume of social capital available, it comes down to a question of the extent to which the means of production and subsistence, i.e. disposal over them, are fragmented, or united in the hands of individual capitalists, i.e. the extent reached by the concentration of capital."

This is the crucial social-relational turn. The availability of any shortening technique is not a pure engineering fact; it depends on whether capital is fragmented or united. A given technique may be technically feasible but socially impossible if the capital cannot be gathered. And once again, credit enters: "In so far as credit mediates, accelerates and intensifies the concentration of capital in a single hand, it contributes to shortening the working period, and with this also the turnover time." Credit is thus not only a response to a long working period; it is also a means of reducing it, by enabling concentration that can be deployed to accelerate the job.

The natural limit: agriculture, cattle, and the coercion of ready cash

Now a pivotal restriction is introduced. "In branches of production where the working period, whether it is continuous or interrupted, is prescribed by specific natural conditions, no shortening can take place by the means described above."

He supports this with a quotation from W. Walter Good, Political, Agricultural, and Commercial Fallacies (London, 1866, p. 325): "In regard to quicker returns, this term cannot be made to apply to corn crops, as one return only can be made per annum. In respect to stock, we will simply ask, how is the return of two- and three-year-old sheep, and four- and five-year-old oxen to be quickened?"

The point is: agriculture and animal husbandry are biologically rhythm-bound. No finance, no cooperation, no machinery can make a sheep into a four-year-old in two years. The working period is a natural constant of these branches, not a social variable.

From this constraints follows a systematic distortion. The need for "ready cash as soon as possible (e.g. to pay fixed obligations such as taxes, ground-rent, etc.)" is resolved by "selling and slaughtering cattle before they have reached the normal economic age, to the great detriment of agriculture; this also leads, moreover, to a rise in meat prices." Financial pressure (fixed obligations) forces premature liquidations. The economic optimum (wait for full maturity) clashes with the cash-flow necessity (sell now).

The falling rate of calf-slaughter: Good's agricultural polemic

Marx extends the evidence with a second quotation from Good (pp. 11–12). The passage describes how English cattle-breeders in the Midland and eastern counties had been "crippled through the uncertainty and lowness in the prices of corn." They shift to dairy:

  • Butter is taken to market weekly to help pay current expenses.
  • Cheese is drawn upon from a factor, who "takes the cheese when fit to move, and, of course, nearly at his own price."
  • Because of these pressures, "the calves which used to come south from the dairying counties for rearing, are now largely sacrificed, at times at a week and ten days old, in the shambles of Birmingham, Manchester, Liverpool, and other large neighbouring towns."

The counterfactual follows: "If, however, malt had been free from duty, not only would farmers have made more profit and therefore been able to keep their stock till it got older and heavier, but it would have been substituted for milk and rearing by men who did not keep cows, and thus the present alarming scarcity of young cattle which has befallen the nation would have been largely averted." The reformism is transparent — a tax policy (malt duty) is invoked as a lever that would ease cash-flow pressure and permit cattle to be matured.

The core of the problem is given voice by the farmers themselves: "We know very well it would pay to rear on milk, but it would first require us to put our hands in our purse, which we cannot do, and then we should have to wait a long time for a return, instead of getting it at once by dairying." Here the long working period appears as a liquidity barrier: the farmers know the long-run operation is more profitable, but they lack the working capital to fund the interval — and cannot borrow it.

Marx then universalizes: "If the lengthening of turnover can have consequences like this even among the smaller English farmers, it is easy to understand what disturbances it must provoke among the small peasants of the Continent." The turnover problem, harsh in Britain, is catastrophic where credit is even less developed.

Fixed versus circulating capital revisited: asymmetry of delayed reflux

Marx uses this empirical excursion to reinforce a theoretical distinction he had begun earlier. "According to the duration of the working period, and thus also the period till a commodity ready for circulation is completed, the portion of value that the fixed capital surrenders layer by layer to the product mounts up, and the reflux of this portion of value is delayed."

But — crucially — "this delay, however, does not necessitate a renewed outlay of fixed capital. The machine continues to operate in the production process whether replacement for its wear and tear flows back quicker or more slowly in the money form."

With circulating capital, however: "Here not only must capital be tied up for a longer time, in proportion to the duration of the labour process, but new capital must continually be advanced for wages, raw and ancillary materials. The delayed reflux thus has a different effect in the two cases."

The asymmetrical logic, restated:

  • Fixed capital: delayed reflux means the capitalist has to wait longer to recover value already invested, but the asset keeps working regardless. There is no additional outlay.
  • Circulating capital: delayed reflux changes nothing about the existing advanced capital, which is stuck in an unfinished product — "if it is tied up in the form of unsold, or unfinished and not yet saleable products, and there is no additional capital to renew it in kind," the production process simply cannot continue.

This asymmetry generates the immediately practical conclusion: the long working period is above all a burden on circulating capital, and the capacity to bear it depends on having hoarded liquidity or secured credit. The fixed capital is patient; the circulating capital is imperious.

India, Manu, and the social conservatism of the ox

Marx extends the analysis with an extraordinary colonial quotation from the Return, East India. Madras and Orissa Famine, no. 4, p. 44: "While the peasant farmer starves, his cattle thrive. Repeated showers had fallen in the country, and the forage was abundant. The Hindoo peasant will perish by hunger beside a fat bullock. The prescriptions of superstition, which appear cruel to the individual, are conservative for the community; and the preservation of the labouring cattle secures the power of cultivation, and the sources of future life and wealth. It may sound harsh and sad to say so, but in India it is more easy to replace a man than an ox."

The point being illustrated is that, when the working period of the means of production is long — as it is when livestock must be preserved across lean seasons in order to secure future harvests — societies may develop cultural rules that enforce what cash-flow logic cannot. The "superstition" against killing the ox is not really superstition but an institutional solution to a working-period problem. If the ox is eaten, the plough cannot be pulled next year; the working period of agricultural production would be catastrophically broken. The taboo preserves the capital.

Marx then cites the Manava Dharma Sastra (the classical Hindu code ascribed to Manu as the progenitor of mankind), from the English translation by Graves Chamney Haughton (third edition, Madras 1863). The quoted passage (Chapter X, 62) reads: "Desertion of life, without reward, for the sake of preserving a priest or a cow ... may cause the beatitude of those base-born tribes." The religious sanction — permitting the sacrifice of life to preserve a cow — is presented as an ancient cultural codification of the same economic necessity: the animal's long working period makes it more valuable, in a certain schema, than a human life that can be replaced.

It is worth being precise about how Marx is using this material. He is not endorsing the proposition that a man is worth less than an ox; he is observing that, given the length of the working period required to raise a replacement animal, pre-industrial society — regardless of its moral ideals — operates under a harsh economic logic in which the animal is irreplaceable within the relevant time horizon, while the human labourer is more readily replaceable. The two quotes are offered as documentation of that structure, not as a moral stance.

Bakewell and the breeding of time

Marx returns to a possible mitigation. It is "impossible, of course, to deliver a five-year-old animal before the end of five years." But "what is possible within certain limits is to prepare animals for their fate more quickly by new modes of treatment." Enter Bakewell.

Previously, British sheep — like French sheep as late as 1855 — were not ready for slaughter before the fourth or fifth year. In Bakewell's system, one-year-old sheep can already be fattened, and in any case are fully grown before the second year has elapsed. Bakewell, farmer of Dishley Grange in North Leicestershire, achieved this by careful selective breeding, which "reduced the bone structure of his sheep to the minimum necessary for their existence." The result: the New Leicesters.

Marx then quotes Lavergne, The Rural Economy of England (1855, p. 20): "The breeder can now send three to market in the same space of time that it formerly took him to prepare one; and if they are not taller, they are broader, rounder, and have a greater development in those parts which give most flesh. Of bone, they have absolutely no greater amount than is necessary to support them, and almost all their weight is pure meat."

The illustration is instructive because it shows that even biologically natural working periods can be compressed — not by violating biology, but by altering the organism so that the natural maturation horizon itself is shortened. Bakewell did not make a five-year-old sheep in one year; he bred a sheep whose economic maturity is reached in one to two years. The working period is thus, in principle, technically malleable even in agriculture — but only within natural limits, and at the cost of an enormous long-run investment in the reproductive stock.

The incommensurable improvements: relative lengthening despite absolute shortening

The chapter closes with a final, subtle caution. "The methods that shorten the working period differ greatly in the extent to which they can be applied in different branches of industry, and they do not cancel out the differences in length of the different working periods."

To make this vivid: "To stick to our example, the application of new machine-tools may, in absolute terms, shorten the working period necessary for the production of a locomotive. But if improved processes in spinning increase the finished product turned out daily or weekly here to an even greater extent, then the length of the working period in machine-building will still have increased relatively, compared with that in spinning."

This is a comparative-dynamics point of considerable sophistication. Absolute shortening in both branches does not eliminate the relative difference — and it is the relative difference that governs the comparative circulation requirements of the two branches. Improvements in spinning, by increasing the spinner's turnover speed even more, widen the relative gap, so that from the standpoint of capital allocation the machine-building sector becomes, comparatively, even more capital-intensive in its turnover structure than before. The absolute easing of one side is not the disappearance of the gap; often it is its amplification.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Chapter 12 delivers a fully articulated theory that is at once technically precise, historically grounded, and politically pointed. Its central lesson is that the physical duration of production is an independent economic force in its own right. It cannot be dissolved into the theory of fixed and circulating capital, into the length of the working day, or into organic composition. It has its own quantitative law — the additional circulating capital required equals the weekly outlay multiplied by the number of weeks of the working period — and its own qualitative consequences: the imperative of additional liquidity, the vulnerability of the unfinished product to interruption, and the systematic exposure of the capitalist to time itself.

From this, several broader implications follow.

First, time is a factor of production. Marx's analysis shows that in the long-working-period industries, time itself is not free. The necessity of waiting — of "advancing" capital for weeks, months, or years before any reflux occurs — imposes a cost that must be borne by someone, and the form of that bearing — self-financing, joint-stock financing, credit, state provisioning, client advances — becomes a historical marker of the stage of capitalist development. Where credit is underdeveloped, the customer pays in instalments (as in the older house-building model) or the state builds by forced labour (roads, canals). Where credit is developed, the customer disappears and the speculative builder appears.

Second, the theory of the working period is a theory of financial institutions. Credit is not merely a neutral channel; it is the mechanism by which the long working period becomes capitalistically feasible. But it is also the mechanism by which other people's capital is put at risk. The 1857 crisis in the building trade — where the halting of mortgage instalments collapses an undertaking that has exceeded the contractor's own means twenty to fifty times over — is a miniature of the financial fragility that a long working period generates. Credit simultaneously solves and amplifies the turnover problem.

Third, the analysis of agriculture shows that natural constraints produce specific kinds of systemic distortion. The premature slaughter of calves and the alarm at the scarcity of young cattle demonstrate that when the working period is fixed by biological time, cash-flow pressure produces outcomes that are rationally irrational: individually intelligible, collectively destructive. The farmers in Good's testimony know that rearing pays better in the long run; they cannot afford to wait. The contradiction between the economically optimal and the financially feasible is one of the enduring features of capitalist agriculture, and Marx places it squarely within the theory of turnover.

Fourth, the cultural material (India, Manu, Bakewell) shows that social institutions — religion, taboo, breeding practice — grow up around the working period as ways of managing its consequences. The prohibition on killing the ox, drafted into priestly law, is a functional response to the length of time required to replace the animal. Bakewell's selective breeding is a technical response to the same underlying problem. Both are recognitions, in different registers, that the working period cannot be abolished — only navigated.

Fifth, the method of the chapter is itself a lesson. Marx moves from pure abstraction (equal capitals, equal composition, working period as the sole variable), through quantitative derivation (12× the circulating capital, the growing additional doses), through historical and institutional analysis (joint-stock companies, credit, speculative building), to documentary evidence (Builder's testimony, Good's agricultural polemic, the Madras famine return, Manu), and back to a refined theoretical conclusion (the relative lengthening of the working period despite absolute shortening). This layered movement — abstraction → arithmetic → history → evidence → refined abstraction — is a model of how a category of capital theory can be shown to be not merely an analytic convenience but a lived structure of economic society.

Finally, Marx's chapter points forward. It announces that the "time of advance can grow ... without the amount of capital advanced growing in proportion to this length of time" — a distinction that will be developed in the following chapter on production time, where the working period will be separated from the broader category of time during which capital is present in the production process (including natural interruptions such as ripening, drying, chemical reaction). This chapter, then, is the first step in a longer argument: that capital's velocity — its turnover — is not simply an epiphenomenon of capital's magnitude but a co-determinant of the social relations it sustains. The chapter's lesson, ultimately, is that capital cannot escape time — and that the effort to discipline time, through machinery, cooperation, credit, and speculation, is one of the deepest and most distinctive historical features of the capitalist mode of production.
