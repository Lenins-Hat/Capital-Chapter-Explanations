Volume 2 • Chapter 15: Effect of Circulation Time on the Magnitude of an Advanced Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Core Thesis

Chapter 15 of Capital Volume II marks the point at which Marx pivots from describing the mere anatomy of turnover (the taxonomy of working period, circulation period, and turnover period established in Chapters 13 and 14) to demonstrating the causal power of circulation time over the quantity and disposition of capital that a capitalist must commit. The chapter's central thesis can be stated with precision: circulation time is not a passive interlude between working periods but a material force that dictates how much total capital must be advanced, how that capital is divided into functional portions, and how much of the social circulating capital necessarily exists in the idle, latent form of money capital or surplus productive stock.

Marx's controlling problem is disarmingly simple and devastatingly consequential. If a capitalist advances a fluid capital to produce commodities over a working period of, say, nine weeks, that capital cannot simply be reused at the end of the nine weeks: it must first complete a circulation period — pass through commodity capital, then money capital, and only then be reconvertible into productive capital. If the circulation period is three weeks, then at the close of week nine the entire £900 that had been functioning as productive capital steps out of the production process and stands idle, as commodity capital, for three weeks. During those three weeks the workshop stands empty, the labour power that had been purchased is not engaged by this capital, and production halts — unless a second, additional capital is thrown into the breach. This creates what Marx treats as an iron structural law of industrial capital:

Law of continuity. If production is to proceed uninterruptedly and on a constant scale, the total capital advanced must always be divided into at least two parts: one part that is functioning as productive capital (in the working period) and another part that is functioning as commodity capital or money capital (in the circulation period). The ratio between these two parts is fixed by the ratio between the circulation period and the turnover period.

This is the chapter's foundational law, and it ramifies into every subsequent calculation. From it follow all the subsidiary conclusions: that a portion of capital must always exist in the money form; that periodically a chunk of fluid capital is "set free"; that the reduction or prolongation of circulation time acts directly on the money market independently of the quantity of money in circulation or the prices of commodities; and that price changes in the elements of production or in the product act as an independent mechanism for precipitating or absorbing money capital.

The Central Problem: Two Possible Responses to the Circulation Gap

Marx poses the problem through a series of worked examples, and the problem has — as he demonstrates — only two possible solutions when production is to be continuous on the same scale:

  1. Reduce the scale of production so that the same total capital (£900) can be spread across the whole twelve-week turnover period (£75 per week instead of £100 per week). This presupposes a lower normal minimum of investment than most industries tolerate, and hence is not generally available as a solution.

  2. Advance an additional capital equal to the fraction of the total capital represented by the ratio of circulation time to turnover time (£300 on the £900 of the example), so that while the original capital is circulating, a second capital keeps the labour process going.

The chapter's intellectual objective is to trace, with algebraic and arithmetic precision, the consequences of each of these solutions and their combinations across all possible ratios of working period to circulation period — equal, longer, shorter, simple multiple, non-multiple — and to determine in each case (a) how much capital must be advanced, (b) what portion is periodically "set free," and (c) how these mechanics connect to the money market and the credit system.

How the Chapter Fits into the Broader Framework

Chapter 15 belongs to Part II of Volume II ("The Turnover of Capital"), which is the analytical heart of Marx's entire treatment of the circulation process of capital. Prior to this chapter, Marx had already established:

  • Chapter 13: The distinction between working period (time during which labour is actually applied to the object of labour) and production time (which can exceed working time, e.g. in agriculture or in wine-aging). The turnover period is the sum of production time and circulation time.
  • Chapter 14: Turnover time as the sum of production time and circulation time, with turnover measured as a repeated cycle.
  • The concept of fixed vs. fluid capital (developed earlier in Volume II and Volume I), which determines which parts of the capital are subject to this turnover logic. Crucially, Chapter 15 concerns fluid (circulating) capital only, because fixed capital does not need to be re-advanced within each turnover cycle in the same way (the means of labour persist across multiple turnover periods).

The chapter thus supplies the micro-foundations for Part III of Volume II on the reproduction of social capital, and it directly sets up Chapter 16, in which the same analytical apparatus is applied to the specific case of variable capital and the annual rate of surplus value. The concept of "capital set free" introduced here is the conceptual hinge on which much of Marx's later discussion of credit, crises, and the industrial cycle (particularly in Volume III) depends. The famous parenthetical note by Engels at the end of Section 4 signals that Marx himself was uncertain about the weight that should be given to "setting-free" — and Engels' correction sharpens rather than undermines the central thesis, arguing that the release of money capital happens even more generally than Marx claimed.

In sum: Chapter 15 is the technical crucible in which Marx forges the concept of the turnover mechanism as a constraint on capital advance, dismantling the vulgar notion that capital works continuously and loses nothing in circulation. Its thesis is simultaneously mathematical, structural, and dialectical — and it is the necessary precondition for understanding how credit, speculation, and crisis grow out of the ordinary motions of industrial capital.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Framework: The First Example and the Two Possibilities

Marx opens the chapter with an explicit statement of scope: this chapter and the next deal with "the influence of circulation time on the valorization of capital." Crucially, the subject matter is the magnitude of the capital advanced, not simply the amount produced or the rate of value creation.

He sets up the first example with methodological care. Take a commodity capital that is the product of a working period of nine weeks. Two elements are abstracted away at the outset: (1) the portion of the product's value added by the average wear and tear of fixed capital, and (2) the surplus-value added during production. What remains is the value of the fluid capital advanced for production, i.e., the value of wages plus raw and ancillary materials consumed. This abstraction is important: it isolates the turnover of fluid capital from the analytically separable phenomenon of fixed capital depreciation and from the surplus. The point of the abstraction is not to deny surplus-value but to render the capital recovery problem visible in its purest form. We might note in passing that this is one of the many places in Volume II where Marx temporarily sets aside the surplus only to reintroduce it later; the structure of his exposition requires this temporal separation of analytical layers.

Setting the value of this commodity capital at £900, this implies a weekly outlay of £100 (900 ÷ 9 = 100). The periodic production time coincides here with the working period — nine weeks. Marx is careful to note that this coincidence is not essential to the argument: it is immaterial whether the working period issues in a continuous stream of product or in a discrete product, "as long as the quantum of the discrete product that is put on the market at one stroke simply takes nine weeks' labour." This is an important defence against a possible objection — that the analysis applies only to industries with long, discrete outputs (shipbuilding, construction, etc.) — and rules out that objection by stipulating that the labour-time content is the relevant magnitude.

The circulation time is assumed to be three weeks. Therefore the total turnover period is twelve weeks (9 + 3).

What happens at the close of week nine? The productive capital of £900 has been transformed into commodity capital, and it now must spend three weeks in the circulation period. The next cycle of production can begin only at the start of week thirteen (the number thirteen being counted from the start of week one, so that the interval weeks 10, 11, 12 is the circulation gap). During those three weeks, "production is at a standstill," and Marx draws out the annual consequence: in every three months production halts for three weeks, i.e., 4 × 3 = 12 weeks across a year, "or a quarter of the annual turnover period."

Marx wants to be rigorous: he treats the three-week circulation time as general — it does not matter whether the three weeks represent the average time to sell, the distance to market, or the terms of payment. Here is an early instance of the analytical neutrality he defends throughout: circulation time may be affected by purely commercial, purely spatial, or purely credit-related factors, but as circulation time it has identical effects on the advance of capital.

The Two Possible Solutions to the Circulation Gap

If production is to be continuous, "pursued on the same scale week in, week out," there are only two possibilities:

Possibility 1: Reduce the scale. Spread £900 across the twelve-week turnover period. The result is £75 per week (900 ÷ 12 = 75). But this "presupposes different dimensions for the fixed capital, and thus a reduced investment in general." Marx turns this into a sociological observation rather than a mathematical one: whether such a reduction is possible depends on whether the industry tolerates such a low level of investment. There is a normal minimum of capital investment below which a business "will cease to be competitive," and this minimum "grows steadily with the development of capitalist production." Above this minimum and up to a continuously expanding normal maximum, there exists a "middle range that permits varying degrees of capital investment" within which reductions of scale are feasible. This is a crucial move: Marx is distinguishing between the logical possibility of scaling down and the historical-institutional conditions under which it is actually permitted. He also notes practical mechanisms for varying the scale in real time: reduction of working hours (e.g., half-days), extension or intensification of hours in prosperity, employment of more workers with reserve fixed capital (reserve locomotives on railways, etc.). These are bracketed away as "abnormal fluctuations," with the analysis retaining the assumption of normal conditions.

Possibility 2: Advance additional capital. If the nature of the investment "excludes a reduction in the scale of production and hence also in the fluid capital to be advanced each week," then continuity requires an additional fluid capital of £300 in the example. The logic: across the twelve-week turnover, £1,200 is successively advanced (12 × £100), of which £300 "makes a quarter, as does three weeks out of twelve." Note the ratio Marx identifies: circulation time / turnover period = 3/12 = 1/4 = additional capital / total capital = £300 / £1,200. This is the algebraic core of the entire chapter, and it will be confirmed and refined through the later sections.

The additional capital functions during the three weeks the original capital spends as commodity capital — a period during which, for the production process, the original capital "is the same… as if it did not exist at all." Two things are explicitly abstracted away here: all credit relations (so we assume the capitalist operates only with his own capital), and any accidental interruptions from the market. The abstraction from credit is important because it means that throughout this chapter the money capital advanced is not available from banks or bills of exchange; the consequences for the money market are deferred to Section 4 and 5, where Marx explicitly reintroduces them.

The First Example Rendered in Detail: The Interweaving of Capitals

Marx then lays out the first example (9-week working period, 3-week circulation, 12-week turnover) in a sequential narrative to make the interweaving of the two capitals visible. This is where the chapter's essential difficulty emerges — the arithmetic is not hard, but the interleaving of two capitals with offset turnover periods makes the verbal exposition dense.

First turnover period (weeks 1–12). The first nine-week working period uses capital I of £900. During the last three weeks of this turnover (weeks 10–12), the additional capital II of £300 opens the second working period. Note that capital II's own "working period" begins at the close of the first working period of capital I, not at the start of the year — so the two capitals have different turnover years.

Second turnover period (weeks 13–24). At the start of week thirteen, capital I of £900 returns. But the second working period (which began at week 10) has already completed one third of its course by this point; only six of its nine weeks remain. Therefore only £600 of capital I can re-enter the production process. £300 of the original £900 is "set free, to play the same role that the capital of £300 played in the first working period." At the close of week 15 (that is, six weeks after week 10), the second working period concludes. The £900 laid out on it (i.e., the total involved in the second working period, part capital I and part capital II) flows back three weeks later, at the end of week 21 (nine weeks after the start of week 13). During weeks 19–21 (the circulation of capital I), the £300 set free enters the scene, beginning the third working period in week 19 of the year.

Third turnover period (weeks 25–36). The end of week 21 brought a new reflux of £900. But the third working period (beginning week 19) already had six weeks elapsed by the start of week 25. Only three more weeks of that working period remain, so only £300 of the returning £900 enters production. The fourth working period comprises the remaining nine weeks of this turnover period, and thus the fourth turnover period and the fifth working period begin together with the thirty-seventh week of the year.

This narrative is doing three things simultaneously. First, it demonstrates the interweaving of the two capitals (they cannot be treated as two mutually exclusive capitals beyond the first turnover). Second, it demonstrates the systematic setting-free of a portion of the returning capital. Third, it immediately begins to erase the artificial distinction between "capital I" and "additional capital II": from the second turnover onward, the functioning capital is composed partly of I and partly of II, so the identity of "II" as a distinct capital dissolves — a point Marx returns to when comparing the three examples.

The Second Example (Equal Halves) and the Striking Contrast It Provides

Marx now takes a second example "to simplify the example for purposes of calculation": working period = 5 weeks; circulation time = 5 weeks; turnover period = 10 weeks; 50 weeks/year; £100 weekly outlay. Therefore, the working period requires fluid capital of £500 (capital I), and the circulation time requires additional capital of a further £500 (capital II). The table gives:

Working Period Weeks Commodities in £ Returning
1 1–5 500 end of week 10
2 6–10 500 end of week 15
3 11–15 500 end of week 20
4 16–20 500 end of week 25
5 21–25 500 end of week 30
... etc.

What is analytically rewarded by this example? Marx makes an elegant comparison:

If circulation time were zero — if turnover period = working period — the number of turnovers would equal the number of working periods in the year, i.e., 50 ÷ 5 = 10, and the value of the capital turned over would be 500 × 10 = £5,000.

With a five-week circulation time, a value of £5,000 in commodities is still produced each year — total product is unchanged. But one tenth of this (£500) is always in the shape of commodity capital, and returns only after a five-week delay. At the end of the year, the product of the tenth working period (weeks 46–50) has only completed half its turnover time, since its circulation time falls into the first five weeks of the ensuing year. Total value produced is identical, but the disposition of that value — how much of it is tied up in unrecovered form at any given moment — has wholly changed.

Three General Observations from the Comparison of Examples

Comparing the three examples (first: 9/3; second: 5/5; third: 6/3, examined below), Marx draws three general observations.

Observation 1: Only in the second example do the two capitals "relieve one another" without intersecting. In the second example (5/5), capital I of £500 and capital II of £500 "successively replace one another… the two portions of capital perform their movements separately." This is because working and circulation periods form two equal halves of the turnover period. This is "the highly exceptional case." In every other case — however unequal the two sections of the turnover may be — the movements of the two capitals intersect from the second turnover period onward. The capital active during the circulation of the commodity capital "is no longer identical with the capital II originally advanced for this purpose, but it is equal to it in value and forms the same aliquot part of the total capital advanced." This is a subtle but crucial point: the identity of the set-free capital is not preserved across turnovers — only its magnitude and its functional role.

Observation 2: The idle time of capital is proportionate to the ratio, not the absolute amount. The capital that has functioned during the working period lies idle during the circulation time. In the second example, it functions for five weeks and is idle for five. Over a year, capital I is idle for half the year. However, the additional capital required to maintain continuity "is not determined by the total sum of circulation time within the year, but simply by the ratio of circulation time to turnover period." Hence, only £500 of additional capital is needed, not £2,500. The intuition is that the additional capital enters the turnover just as much as the originally advanced capital, and is replaced after a number of turnovers "just as the former was." It participates in the recycling.

Observation 3: Longer production time changes nothing regarding additional capital for the labour process. If production time is longer than working time (as in agriculture or wine-aging), the turnover period is extended, but this extension "does not require any additional capital for the labour process." The additional capital's job is solely to fill up the gaps in the labour process that are due to circulation time, and hence it protects production "only from disturbances that arise as a result of this circulation time." Disturbances arising from the specific conditions of production are "taken care of in another way, which is not under consideration here." Marx adds a caveat: in businesses where work is done spasmodically, to order, or seasonally, the need for additional capital is proportionately reduced, and there is a limit for the reflux; if the interval between production periods is longer than the circulation time, then the capital can lie idle unless it is used elsewhere.

The Composition of the Advance: Fixed vs. Fluid, Money vs. Stock (Fourth Observation)

Marx's fourth observation is dense with technical detail and prefigures the money-market discussion. A capital advanced for one working period — e.g., the £600 in the third example — is laid out partly on raw and ancillary materials (constant circulating capital in the form of productive stock) and partly on wages (variable circulating capital). Two important qualifications:

  • Constant circulating capital need not exist for the same length of time in the form of productive stock. Raw material may not be stored for the whole working period; coal may be procured every two weeks. To the extent that it is not yet in the form of productive stock, this part must yet remain available in the money form, to be transformed on demand. But this does not alter the value of the constant circulating capital advanced for six weeks.

  • Wages, apart from the money kept for unforeseen expenses ("the specific reserve fund to cope with disturbances"), are paid at shorter intervals, "mostly weekly." Hence, except where the capitalist forces the worker to make particularly long advances of his labour, "the capital needed for wages must be present in the money form." When capital returns, therefore, "one part must be kept in the form of money for payment of labour, while another part can be transformed into productive stock."

This distinction — money form vs. productive stock form within the returning capital — is the seed for the entire "capital set free" analysis. The additional capital is divided up like the original capital, but with the crucial distinguishing feature that it must be advanced in sufficient quantity for the whole duration of the first working period of capital I (in which it is not involved) in order to be available for its own working period. During this time it "can be at least partially transformed already into constant circulating capital" — i.e., the additional capital may already begin to function as stored raw material, or it may persist as additional money capital. The extent to which it assumes one form or the other depends on the particular production conditions, local circumstances, and fluctuations in raw-material prices.

What is Marx's social observation here? If we consider the total social capital, "a more or less significant part of this additional capital exists for a prolonged time in the state of money capital." And as for the portion to be advanced for wages, it can only be transformed gradually into labour power, so it must remain in the money form for the whole duration of the working period, until it is slowly consumed in the labour process. Hence:

The intervention of the additional capital required for the conversion of capital I's circulation time into production time "not only increases the size of the capital advanced and the length of time for which the total capital has to be advanced, but it also specifically increases that part of the capital advanced that exists as a money reserve."

This same phenomenon — an increase in the money-capital portion — occurs whether the needed division between working-period capital and circulation-period capital is achieved by increasing the advance or by reducing the scale of production. In the latter case, in relation to the smaller scale, the money-capital portion grows still further in proportion. This is a foundational insight: the more "efficient" (from the standpoint of functional capital) the organisation of production, the larger the money reserve that must lie idle relative to the functioning capital.

Reversal of Perspective: The "Empty Heads of Capitalists" and the Role of Money Capital

Marx now stages a powerful perspectival reversal. Consider the second example (5/5). From the standpoint of the £500 that functions directly and uninterruptedly in production, the circulation time "appears to have disappeared completely." The turnover period coincides with the working period; the circulation time is as if zero. Ten turnovers, £5,000 product, £500 functioning capital.

But if the same £500 were the only capital — if it were regularly inhibited by a five-week circulation time and could only be ready for production again after the full ten-week turnover — then across the year there would be only five ten-week turnovers, containing five five-week production periods (25 weeks total, product = £2,500) and five five-week circulation periods (25 weeks). In that case, "for half of each turnover period this capital of £500 has not functioned as productive capital at all."

With the replacement capital of £500 entering during the circulation periods, the turnover is raised from £2,500 to £5,000 — but now the total capital advanced is £1,000 instead of £500. £5,000 ÷ £1,000 = 5. So "instead of ten turnovers we have five." But here comes the famous critique:

"Because it is then said that the capital of £1,000 has turned over five times in the year, the memory of the circulation time vanishes from the empty heads of the capitalists, and the confused idea is formed that this capital has functioned constantly in the production process throughout the five successive turnovers."

Marx clarifies: when we say the capital of £1,000 has turned over five times, we include the circulation time as well as the production time. To have £1,000 continuously active would require a capital of £2,000. The "economists," Marx argues, "constantly overlook this basic aspect, i.e., the fact that only a part of the industrial capital can be actually engaged in the production process, if production is to proceed without interruption. In other words, one part can function as productive capital only on condition that another part is withdrawn from production proper in the form of commodity or money capital." Because this is overlooked, "so also is the importance and role of money capital in general."

The paragraph is one of the chapter's rhetorical peaks. It grounds a critique of classical political economy — the tendency to treat capital as if it worked continuously — in a rigorous structural argument about the division of capital. This is a crucial dialectical move: what appears (from the standpoint of the individual capital) to be a mere technical wrinkle (money capital "waiting") is revealed as a structural necessity of the system, on the same level as the working period itself.

Section 1: Working Period = Circulation Period

Marx now announces the structure of the remaining investigation: "the difference in the turnover that arises according to whether the two sections of the turnover period — working period and circulation period — are equal, or whether the working period is longer or shorter than the circulation period; further, how this affects the tying-up of capital in the form of money capital."

Throughout, the working assumption is: £100 advanced each week; 9-week turnover period; £900 total advanced per turnover.

Case: Working period = circulation period. Marx calls this "in reality… only a chance exception," but it is analytically the necessary starting-point, "since it is here that conditions are present in their simplest and most palpable form."

With a nine-week turnover period, the working and circulation periods are each four and a half weeks. Capital I and capital II (each £450) "relieve one another in their movements without crossing each other's path." With the exception of the first period, each of the two capitals is advanced only for its own turnover period. Looking at the year (51 weeks), the table (Table I, given in the original text but not reproduced here in detail) shows:

  • Capital I concludes six full working periods (6 × 450 = £2,700)
  • Capital II concludes five full working periods (5 × 450 = £2,250), plus a further £150 in the final one-and-a-half weeks (mid-week 50 to end of week 51)
  • Total product = £5,100 in 51 weeks

Now Marx distinguishes two senses of turnover. From the standpoint of direct surplus-value production (which occurs only during working periods), the total capital of £900 has turned over 5⅔ times (5⅔ × 900 = £5,100). But from the standpoint of real turnover, capital I has turned over 5⅔ times (5⅔ × 450 = £2,550), and capital II has turned over 5⅙ times (5⅙ × 450 = £2,325); the real amount turned over = £4,875. The difference arises because capital II began its turnover cycle four and a half weeks later than capital I, and at year-end only a portion of its cycle is complete.

The convolution of "turnover expressed in units of capital returned" is unpacked at length:

"We say that £450 completes its turnover in nine weeks, and therefore £300 does so in six weeks."

But Marx warns that this expression "leaves aside the organic relations" between the two components of turnover time. Its correct sense is simply that £450 advanced has made five turnovers and only completed two thirds of its sixth. This is nonetheless a legitimate formulation, because it expresses in quantitative terms the pressing reality that the capital must always be split — one part in production, one part in circulation — in the ratio of the phases. And this is the same law — "the same law as that which determines the mass of productive capital functioning at one time by the ratio of circulation time to turnover time."

Marx then extends the analysis from an individual business to the social capital: capital I and capital II, if they were independent, would still be two parts of the same branch of social capital. The same calculation — sum of capital turned over ÷ sum of capital advanced — holds for the branch and, by extension, for the social capital as a whole. Moreover, just as capitals I and II have different turnover years (II begins 4.5 weeks later than I), so too do the various private capitals in the same branch begin business at different points and complete their annual turnover at different times of the year. The same averaging procedure reduces them to a uniform turnover year. This move is significant: it shows that the microeconomic problem of synchronisation extends into a macroeconomic problem of coordinating many capitals, and that individual capital turnover years are constitutively desynchronised.

Section 2: Working Period Longer Than Circulation Period

We now assume the working period exceeds the circulation period. Marx states at once the crucial consequence: "the working and turnover periods of capitals I and II cut across one another, instead of following on from each other. We also find capital set free, which was not the position in the case considered previously."

Two invariants remain, however:

  1. The number of working periods of the total capital advanced equals the value of the annual product of the two parts, divided by the total capital advanced.
  2. The number of turnovers of the total capital equals the sum of the two amounts turned over, divided by the sum of the two capitals advanced.

Principal example: 6-week working period; 3-week circulation; 9-week turnover; £100/week; Capital I = £600; Capital II = £300.

The table (Table II) attempts to present the two capitals "in complete independence" — cutting through "their actual intersections and entanglements" — and this yields the misleading aggregate calculation:

  • Capital I: 5⅔ × 600 = £3,400
  • Capital II: 5 × 300 = £1,500
  • Total: 5⅔ × 900 = £4,900

This is "not correct," Marx immediately notes. The reason lies in the fact that capital II (only £300) cannot cover a full working period (which requires £600). This is the key feature distinguishing this case from the previous one. Capital II "does not have working and circulation periods separate from those of capital I." It can only partially cover a working period, and therefore the two capitals interpenetrate within single working periods.

Marx walks through the correct dynamics:

Turnover period I (weeks 1–9). - First working period: weeks 1–6. Capital I of £600 functions. - First circulation period: weeks 7–9. At end of week 9, £600 returns.

Turnover period II (weeks 7–15). - Second working period: weeks 7–12. - First half (weeks 7–9): Capital II of £300 functions. At end of week 9, £600 returns in money (capital I). - Second half (weeks 10–12): £300 of capital I functions. The other £300 of capital I remains free. - Second circulation period: weeks 13–15. At end of week 15, £600 returns in money (formed half from capital I, half from capital II).

Turnover period III (weeks 13–21). - Third working period: weeks 13–18. - First half (weeks 13–15): The £300 set free begins to function. At end of week 15, £600 returns. - Second half (weeks 16–18): Of the £600 returned, £300 functions, the other £300 again remains free. - Third circulation period: weeks 19–21. At its close, £600 returns; capital I and II have now "merged indistinguishably together."

By end of week 51 (the assumed year), we have eight full turnover periods (I: 1–9; II: 7–15; III: 13–21; IV: 19–27; V: 25–33; VI: 31–39; VII: 37–45; VIII: 43–51). But since weeks 49–51 fall in the eighth circulation period, the £300 set free must enter production during this time. Therefore:

  • £600 has completed its circuit eight times = £4,800 turnovered
  • The final three weeks (49–51) product counts only for one third of its turnover (since it has completed only 3 of 9 weeks): £100
  • Total capital turned over = £4,900
  • Total capital advanced = £900
  • Number of turnovers = 4,900/900 = 5 4/9 times, i.e., slightly less than in Case I.

Testing the Generality: Additional Cases Where Working Period Exceeds Circulation Time

Marx asks whether the "setting-free" phenomenon depends on the working period being a simple multiple of the circulation time (as in the 6/3 case where working = 2 × circulation). He tests three cases.

Second case: working period 5 weeks; circulation 4 weeks; £100/week. - Capital I = £500. - Turnover period I: weeks 1–9. Working period weeks 1–5. Circulation weeks 6–9. At end of week 9, £500 returns. - Turnover period II: weeks 6–14. Working period weeks 6–10. First section weeks 6–9: capital II of £400 functions. At end of week 9, capital I returns. Second section (week 10): £100 of the returned £500 functions. The remaining £400 stays free for the following working period. - Second circulation period: weeks 11–14. At end of week 14, £500 returns. - The pattern repeats: at every working period's start there is £400 available, which suffices for the first four weeks. At the end of the fourth week, £500 returns, and only £100 is needed for the final week — £400 is set free.

Third case: working period 7 weeks; circulation 2 weeks; £100/week. - Capital I = £700; capital II = £200. - Turnover period I: weeks 1–9. Working period weeks 1–7; circulation weeks 8–9. At end of week 9, £700 returns. - Turnover period II: weeks 8–16. Working period weeks 8–14. Weeks 8 and 9 met by capital II. At end of week 9, £700 returns; £500 used up by end of working period (weeks 10–14). Remaining £200 is set free. - Second circulation period: weeks 15–16. At end of week 16, £700 returns. - Again: £200 set free at the close of every working period.

General conclusion: whenever working period > circulation period, "there is always set free at the close of each working period, under all circumstances, a money capital of the same magnitude as the capital II." In the three examples (6/3, 5/4, 7/2), capital II was £300, £400, £200 — and the capital set free was correspondingly £300, £400, £200.

The structural reason: since circulation time < working period, the returning capital I arrives before the end of the working period, and can only be partially absorbed by the remaining time. The portion it cannot absorb — equal to the capital originally required to bridge the circulation gap — reappears as free capital.

Section 3: Working Period Shorter Than Circulation Period

Now the reverse: the working period is shorter than the circulation period.

Principal example: 3-week working period; 6-week circulation; 9-week turnover; £100/week. Capital I = £300; capital II = £600. But capital II can be subdivided into two capitals of £300, each catering to one working period. The result is three capitals of £300 each: one always occupied in production, two circulating (Table III). This is "the exact counterpart of case I, with the simple distinction that three capitals now relieve one another instead of two." No capital is set free at the close of a working period — the even and complete replacement of the three capitals excludes this. Capital I is completely laid out by end of week 3, completely returns at end of week 9, and begins to function again at the start of week 10; similarly for II and III. The aggregated turnover:

  • Capital I: £300 × 5⅔ = £1,700
  • Capital II: £300 × 5⅓ = £1,600
  • Capital III: £300 × 5 = £1,500
  • Total: £900 × 5⅓ = £4,800

Non-multiple case: working period 4 weeks; circulation 5 weeks. Capital I = £400; capital II = £400; capital III = £100. Here the capitals are "intertwined" — capital III, which is sufficient only for one week, doesn't have an independent working period; it coincides with the first working week of capital I. Therefore a capital of £100, equal to capital III, is set free at the close of the working periods of both capitals I and II. The logic:

  • Capital III serves the first week of the second (and all subsequent) working periods of capital I.
  • At the close of this first week, the entire capital I of £400 returns. But the working period of capital I now has only three weeks left, requiring only £300. Therefore £100 is set free — and this £100 suffices for the first week of the directly following working period of capital II. When that week closes, capital II of £400 returns, but the working period in progress can absorb only £300, so again £100 is set free. And so on.

General rule stated: "Capital is thus set free at the close of the working period whenever the circulation time is not a simple multiple of the working period; and this capital that is set free is moreover equal to the portion of capital which has to fill in for the excess of the circulation period over a working period or over a number of working periods."

Note the symmetry with Section 2: whether the working period is greater than the circulation period or vice versa, the key criterion for setting free is the absence of a simple integer ratio between them.

Fixed Capital Exclusion: A Clarifying Parenthesis

Before results, Marx inserts a crucial methodological note: throughout this Part, "we are considering only the turnovers of circulating capital, and not those of fixed capital." The justification is not merely technical convenience. Fixed capital is defined as capital whose use-time exceeds the turnover period of fluid capital; it functions across n turnover periods of fluid capital without being re-advanced. Whatever the length of the overall interval formed by those n turnover periods, the fixed capital remains in its old use-form and is not re-advanced within the interval. The only difference is that, depending on the working period and circulation time, it surrenders a greater or smaller part of its value to the product, and that surrendered value returns in money form faster or slower. But this does not change the analysis of the fluid capital. Marx reinforces that it is precisely the nature of fluid capital that requires the new advance at the start of each new working period: fluid capital applied in one working period "cannot be applied in a new working period before it has completed its turnover, i.e., has been transformed into commodity capital, from the latter into money capital, and then back again into productive capital." Hence the need for additional capital, and hence the division of the fluid capital advanced.

This methodological note is important because it connects the chapter to the law of value developed earlier: the abstract labour embodied in fixed capital is transferred slowly through depreciation, whereas fluid capital must be replaced in value each turnover — an asymmetrical requirement with deep consequences for accumulation and crisis theory.

Section 4: Results (A–D)

Marx now consolidates the analysis into four results.

Result A. Portions of capital relieve each other like independent private capitals in two cases: (1) if the working period equals the circulation period, and the turnover period is thereby divided into two equal sections; (2) if the circulation period is longer than the working period but a simple multiple of it — i.e., 1 circulation period = n working periods, n a whole number. In both cases no part of the successively advanced capital is set free.

Result B. In all other cases — (1) circulation period greater than working period but not a simple multiple; or (2) working period greater than circulation period — a part of the overall fluid capital is always periodically set free at the close of each working period. The magnitude of this capital: equal to the portion advanced for the circulation period (if working > circulation), or equal to the portion which has to stand in for the excess of circulation over a whole number of working periods (if circulation > working).

Result C. For the total social capital (considered in its fluid part), the setting-free of capital is "the rule," while the simple mutual replacement of portions of capital functioning successively in production "must form the exception." This is because the equality of working period and circulation period, or of circulation period with a whole number of working periods, "has nothing at all to do with the nature of the case, and can therefore occur, by and large, only exceptionally." A very significant portion of the social circulating capital, which is turned over several times a year, will periodically exist in the course of the annual turnover cycle in the form of capital set free.

Marx then notes the growth tendency of this set-free capital: assuming all other circumstances stay the same, the magnitude of set-free capital grows with the extent of the labour process or the scale of production, and thus with the development of capitalist production in general. In case B(2), simply because the total capital advanced grows; in case B(1), both because of the growth of the total capital and because the length of circulation time grows with the development of capitalist production (while the circulation period is not a simple multiple of the working period). Example: if the weekly outlay doubles from £100 to £300, then the working-period capital becomes £1,800 and the circulation-period capital £900, and £900 is periodically set free instead of £300 — a proportional expansion of the idle mass.

Result D. The total capital of £900 must be divided into two portions: £600 for the working period and £300 for the circulation period. The portion actually laid out in the labour process is thus reduced by one third, from £900 to £600 — and the scale of production correspondingly by a third. The £300, on the other hand, functions only to make the working period continuous, so that £100 can be laid out in the labour process each week.

Now Marx introduces a crucial qualitative argument — one that raises the analysis above the purely quantitative. Taken abstractly, it is all the same whether £600 operates for 6 × 8 = 48 weeks (product = £4,800), or whether the entire £900 is laid out for six weeks and then lies idle for the three-week circulation period (running 32 weeks, producing 5⅓ × 900 = £4,800 total, and lying idle 16 weeks). But this abstract equivalence does not hold under real conditions: the "greater waste of fixed capital during the idle period of sixteen weeks, and the increased cost of labour, which has to be paid for the whole year even if only a part of this is worked" — combined with the fact that "a regular interruption of this kind in the production process would be incompatible with the running of modern large-scale industry" — renders the cheaper abstract solution unworkable in practice.

This leads to one of the chapter's most quoted lines: "Continuity is itself a productive force of labour." This formulation deserves attention. It is not merely descriptive: continuity, being the precondition of large-scale cooperation, becomes a source of productive power analogous to cooperation, division of labour, and machinery. And continuity is precisely what requires the additional capital advance and the money-reserve. The material-empirical point that "labour has to be paid for the whole year even if only a part is worked" ties the analysis back to the wage contract and to the immiseration/exploitation themes of Volume I.

The Composition of Capital Set Free: Money Capital as the Minimum Form

Marx then analyses what form the set-free capital takes. In the 6/3 example with £100/week: - At the end of week 9, £600 returns; only £300 needed for the remainder of the working period. - At the close of the second working period, £300 is set free.

Composition of the returned £600: suppose one third for wages (£200) and two thirds for raw and ancillary materials (£400). Of the £300 set free: the £400 portion returns as either money or productive stock — the capitalist may react to market conditions, deciding whether to transform the £200 surplus productive stock back into stock or to hold it as money capital. But the £200 to be laid out on wages must be kept in the money form. As Marx puts it: "The capitalist cannot dispose of labour-power, once he has bought it, as he can the raw material in his storeroom. He has to incorporate it into the production process and pay for it at the end of the week." So of the £300 set free, at least the £100 wage-equivalent (one third of the £300, matching the variable-capital share) must exist as money capital. Hence:

The capital set free in the form of money capital must therefore at least be equal to the variable portion of the capital, that laid out on wages; at the maximum, it can include the whole of the capital set free. In reality it constantly fluctuates between the minimum and the maximum.

Marx adds a crucial forward-reaching observation: this money capital set free by the turnover mechanism itself — together with the money capital set free by the successive reflux of fixed capital and that needed for variable capital in every labour process — "must play a significant role, as soon as the credit system has developed, and must also form one of the foundations for this." This is the pivot toward Volume III's treatment of credit.

The Effect of a Contraction of Circulation Time on the Money Market

Marx now hypothetically narrows the circulation time from three weeks to two (due to improved business conditions, shortened payment terms, etc.). Two consequences:

  1. The £600 laid out during the working period returns one week earlier than needed — set free for one week.
  2. £300 (part of the £600) is again set free mid-working-period — but for four weeks instead of three.

So £600 is on the money market for one week, and £300 for four weeks instead of three. Since this pattern affects many capitalists in different branches at different times, a greater quantity of disposable money capital is brought onto the market. If persistent, either (a) production expands where possible, (b) capitalists using borrowed capital exert less demand on the money market (relieving it), or (c) the sums superfluous for turnover are definitively cast onto the money market.

Concretely: contraction of the turnover period from nine to eight weeks means one ninth of the total capital advanced becomes superfluous: the six-week working period can be kept going with £800 instead of £900. A portion of commodity capital, £100, persists as money capital and no longer functions as capital advanced for the production process. The value of capital advanced declines from £900 to £800; the remaining £100 is "precipitated out in the form of money capital" and enters the money market as additional capital functioning there.

Marx draws the profound conclusion:

A surfeit of money capital can arise — not just in the ordinary sense that supply exceeds demand (a relative surplus, e.g., in a depression). Rather, a definite part of the capital advanced becomes superfluous for the overall process of social reproduction (which includes circulation) and is precipitated out in the form of money capital — a surplus arising with the scale of production and prices remaining the same, simply by a contraction in the turnover period. The mass of money in circulation, whether larger or smaller, does not have the slightest influence on this.

This is a decisive clarification. Money capital abundance can arise without any expansion of the money supply, simply through the mechanical effect of turnover acceleration. It sharpens Marx's critique of quantity-theory reasoning, which conflates money-capital supply-demand dynamics with the quantity of circulating media.

The Effect of a Prolongation of Circulation Time

The reverse: circulation time extended from three to five weeks. The reflux of advanced capital arrives two weeks too late. The last part of the working period cannot be completed through the turnover mechanism of the originally advanced capital alone. If the situation persists, either: - production contracts (reduction in scale), or - the total capital advanced must be increased by 2/9 (= £200) to compensate for the circulation extension.

This additional capital can only come from the money market. If the prolongation affects major lines of business, it may exert pressure on the money market — unless counteracted from another direction. Again: this pressure has nothing to do with changes in commodity prices or the quantity of the means of circulation.

Engels' Editorial Note

At the close of Section 4, Friedrich Engels appends a lengthy parenthetical note acknowledging that the editing of the chapter "involved no small difficulties." Engels observes that Karl Marx, despite his "firm grasp of algebra," was "never at ease in reckoning with figures, i.e., in commercial calculations" (though there is a thick sheaf of notebooks in which Marx worked through commercial calculation). The turnover calculations became confused and contain "many errors and contradictions." Engels retains only "the simplest and the arithmetically correct calculations," largely because of the "uncertain results of this tiresome calculation business" that "led Marx to ascribe an undeserved significance to what in my opinion is in fact a matter of little importance" — namely, the "setting free" of money capital.

Engels reformulates the point: whatever the ratio between working period and circulation time, once the first turnover has occurred, at regular intervals equal to the length of the working period, there returns the capital needed for one such working period — a sum equal to capital I. Examples: - 5-week working, 4-week circulation, capital I £500 → £500 returns at end of weeks 9, 14, 19, 24, etc. - 6-week working, 3-week circulation, capital I £600 → £600 returns at end of weeks 9, 15, 21, 27, 33, etc. - 4-week working, 5-week circulation, capital I £400 → £400 returns at end of weeks 9, 13, 17, 21, 25, etc.

Whether this returned capital is superfluous for the current working period (and thus "set free") makes no difference to production continuity: for production to proceed on the same scale, money must be present and flow back, whether or not it is "set free." If production is interrupted, the setting-free comes to an end.

Engels' summary judgement:

"There is in any case a release of money, i.e., a formation of latent, only potential capital, in the money form; but this happens in all circumstances, and not only under those particular conditions specified in the text; it happens, moreover, to a greater extent than that assumed in the text."

Engels closes: in relation to circulating capital I, the industrial capitalist finds himself, at the end of each turnover, "in precisely the same situation as when he set up his business; he has this capital completely in his hands once more, and at one go, while he can only gradually transform it again into productive capital."

His summing-up defends Marx's core argument even as it demotes a peripheral one: the main point of the text remains the proof that "a considerable part of industrial capital is always present in the money form, while a still more considerable part must assume this form from time to time" — and this proof "is reinforced, if anything, by these additional remarks of mine."

This note is a fascinating instance of editorial and analytical collaboration. Engels is right that Marx's word-choice ("setting free") overweights the phenomenon; but as Engels himself shows, the central thesis of the chapter — the structural necessity of money capital within the industrial circuit — is thereby strengthened rather than weakened.

Section 5: Effect of Changes in Price

Marx now switches the variable under consideration. Previously: prices and scale constant; circulation time varied. Now: turnover period and scale constant; prices of elements of production or of the product vary. He distinguishes three cases.

Case I: scale of production constant; prices of elements and products constant; change in circulation period (hence in turnover period).

The example: circulation time reduced from three to two weeks. Consequence: 1/9 less total capital needed; total drops from £900 to £800; £100 is precipitated as money capital. Business X continues to supply the same six-weekly product of £600 value, and the year's quantity and price of product are unchanged (total product £5,100 in 51 weeks). So there is no change in the terms on which the product is placed on the market; but £100 is precipitated — an idle money capital.

Marx then decomposes the £100 in detail. Suppose the fluid capital I = £600 is split: 4/5 (£480) on materials and 1/5 (£120) on wages. Capital II = £300 is similarly split: £240 on materials and £60 on wages. The wage portion must always be advanced in money form. So when £600 returns: £480 converts back to material elements of production; £120 remains in money form for six weeks of wages.

Now, if £100 is precipitated out of the £300 that is periodically set free for three weeks (of which £240 is productive stock, £60 is wages), the question is: where does the money for this £100 come from? Only one fifth (£20) is money capital periodically set free. The remaining £80 was previously replaced by additional productive stock of the same value. How is this additional stock transformed into money?

Answer: if the reduction in circulation time has already taken place, only £400 of the above £600 (instead of £480) is transformed back into production stock. The remaining £80 stays in money form and, together with the £20 for wages, makes the £100 of capital precipitated. Even though the £100 is derived from the sale of the £600 commodity capital and is now withdrawn from circulation, it is in exactly the form in which it was originally cast into circulation: it is now a new money capital seeking investment, a new element on the money market — while being in no sense a superfluous element of the existing social money stock, since it existed at the start of business and was cast into circulation by it. It also is not a newly accumulated hoard.

Marx returns to his earlier caution: this money capital "already existed periodically in the form of money capital set free, and in the form of superfluous productive capital, but these latent states were themselves conditions for the accomplishment of the process, as preconditions of its continuity." Now they are no longer needed as conditions and so form new money capital and a component of the money market.

He also adds an important clarification: even if the advanced capital were borrowed, the analysis would be unchanged. Reduction from £900 to £800 means only £800 borrowed capital is needed; the £100 repaid to the lender becomes additional money capital in the lender's hands. And if the capitalist obtains materials on credit, so that he advances only wages in money, then the amount of material elements he must obtain on credit is £80 less, which is so much additional commodity capital for the credit-giver, while the capitalist himself also precipitates out £20 in money.

The cotton example. The additional production stock is reduced by a third: from £240 to £160 (additional stock for two weeks instead of three). Purchases on the cotton market are repeated more frequently and in smaller quantities, but the total amount withdrawn from the market annually is the same. Marx illustrates with a vivid quantitative example:

Original (renovation every 3 months): - 1 Jan: 300 bales sold, 900 left - 1 Apr: 300 sold, 600 left - 1 Jul: 300 sold, 300 left - 1 Oct: 300 sold, 0 left

After reduction to 2-month intervals: - 1 Jan: 200 sold, 1,000 left - 1 Mar: 200, 800 left - 1 May: 200, 600 left - 1 Jul: 200, 400 left - 1 Sep: 200, 200 left - 1 Nov: 200, 0 left

The money invested in cotton completes its return one month later (November instead of October). Symmetrically, corresponding to the £80 reduction in the surplus production stock on the manufacturer's part, there is an increased commodity stock in the hands of the cotton broker. "The same cotton lies as much longer in the broker's warehouse as a commodity, as it exists for a shorter time as a production stock in the stores of the manufacturer."

This is a startlingly modern insight about the interpenetration of productive and commodity stocks across the sphere of circulation. A reduction in one capitalist's inventory can increase another's, simply because the timing of turnovers shifts.

Enhanced transport as a cause of the reduced circulation time. Marx considers the case where the reduction in circulation time depends not on X's market conditions but on the working period or circulation time of the capitals supplying him with elements of production. Example: coal and cotton took 3 weeks to travel from production to X's site. The minimum productive stock X had to hold pending new arrivals was 3 weeks — but during transit, goods cannot serve as means of production; they are, instead, the object of labour for the transport industry and commodity capital in circulation for the coal producer or cotton broker. Now improved transport reduces transit to 2 weeks: the production stock can be transformed from a 3-week supply to a 2-week supply. An additional capital of £80 is set free (plus £20 for wages), because the capital of £600 completes its turnover and returns one week sooner. If instead the working period of the capital supplying the raw material is reduced, the same result obtains through quicker replacement of raw material.

Conversely, if circulation time and turnover period are prolonged, additional capital must be advanced — from the capitalist's own reserve (if invested elsewhere, it must be "prised out," e.g., shares sold, deposits withdrawn — an indirect effect on the money market) or borrowed. Wages must always be advanced as money, so this exerts direct pressure on the money market. For raw materials, if obtained on credit, the capitalist exerts no direct influence; the creditor's placing of the bill may have indirect effects, and if the creditor uses the bill to settle his own debts, then the additional capital has neither a direct nor an indirect effect on the money market.

Case II: Change in price of the materials of production; all other circumstances unchanged.

Suppose of the £900 total, 4/5 (£720) is on materials and 1/5 (£180) on wages. If raw material prices fall by half: - Materials: £480 → £240 for six-week working period - Wages: £120 → unchanged at £120 - Capital I: £600 → £360 (240 + 120) - Capital II: additional capital → £180 (120 + 60)

The total £900 reduces to £540. Therefore £360 is precipitated.

Marx's interpretation: "This precipitated money capital, which is now unoccupied and is therefore seeking investment on the money market, is simply a fragment of the capital of £900 originally advanced as money capital which has now become superfluous owing to the fall in price of the elements of production into which it is periodically transformed." If the price fall was accidental (good harvest, over-supply), then the effect is transient. But if it was due to increased productivity in the branch supplying the raw material, then the unoccupied money capital becomes an absolute addition to the money market — an absolute addition to capital available in money form, because it has ceased to form an integral component of capital already invested. This points toward a classically Marxian insight: technological or productivity improvements in one branch release capital value in the form of money, which then seeks investment elsewhere.

Case III: Change in the market price of the product itself.

Two subcases.

  • Fall in product price: A part of the capital is lost and must be replaced by a new advance of money capital. This loss may be recouped by the buyer (if the price fall was accidental and prices subsequently recover) or indirectly (if the price change derives from a value change that redistributes through other branches of production). In either case, the capitalist exerts pressure on the money market to replace the lost capital, and his business friends may supply it as new additional capital — so there is only a transfer.

  • Rise in product price: A portion of capital not advanced is appropriated from the circulation sphere. This is not an organic part of the capital advanced in production; if production is not extended, it becomes precipitated money capital. Even assuming product prices were given before entry onto the market, the price rise might have been caused by a real value change with retroactive effect (e.g., raw materials subsequently rose in value). In that case capitalist X would profit both on his product (as commodity capital) and on his existing production stock — this profit supplies him with the additional capital he would otherwise need to carry on business after his elements of production have risen. Alternatively, if the price rise is only transitory, what one capitalist needs as extra capital is precipitated out elsewhere, "to the extent that his product forms an element of production for other branches of business. What the one lost, the other has gained."

The chapter closes on this note of systematic redistribution. Price changes act as a mechanism for transferring capital between branches, generating simultaneous pressures and releases in the money market, without any change in the quantity of money or in the productive capacity of the system. This is the final maturation of the chapter's thesis: the turnover mechanism, and the price movements that intersect with it, continuously generate and absorb money capital as a distinct functional form of capital — a form whose dynamics are not reducible to those of commodity production, but which are essential to it.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Fundamental Principles Revealed

Chapter 15 establishes several systemic laws that continue to shape Marx's analysis through Volume III and remain among his most fertile contributions to a political economy of circulation.

First principle — the law of continuity and its cost. The continuity of production is not a natural property of capital but a produced property, requiring a specific structural organisation of capital: the advance of additional fluid capital equal to the ratio of circulation time to turnover time. Continuity is "itself a productive force of labour," but it must be purchased at the price of an increased total capital advance and an enlarged money reserve. This dissolves the notion — which Marx attributes to the "empty heads of the capitalists" and the economists — that capital works continuously. It does not; only the division of capital allows the appearance of continuity.

Second principle — the ratio law of advanced capital. The additional capital required to bridge the circulation gap is determined, not by the absolute length of circulation time across the year, but by the ratio of circulation time to turnover period. In the equal-halves example, only £500 was needed, not £2,500 — because the additional capital participates in the turnover cycle just like the original. This ratio-law is the analytical core of the chapter and the reason its later effects (money-market surpluses and squeezes, redistribution across branches) can be quantified.

Third principle — the law of "capital set free." Whenever the ratio between working period and circulation period fails to be a simple integer ratio (equal or whole-multiple), a portion of the returned capital is systematically set free at the close of each working period. Marx regards this as the rule for the social capital as a whole; the simple mutual relief of two strictly autonomous capitals is the exception. Engels refines this: the phenomenon of latent money capital formation occurs in all cases, and to a greater extent than Marx describes; what Marx identified as a special phenomenon is a special case of a more universal one.

Fourth principle — the necessary money form. At least the variable portion of the set-free capital (the wage bill) must exist in the money form, and at most the whole set-free capital may do so. Hence, at any moment, a significant portion of industrial capital exists as money capital, not because of any choice by the capitalist but because of the mechanism of turnover itself. This is the structural foundation on which the credit system is built.

Fifth principle — the immanent generation of money-capital surpluses and deficits. Reductions in circulation time (through faster sales, better transport, shorter payment terms) precipitate money capital out of the turnover process, forming new money capital on the market with scale and prices held constant. Prolongations of circulation time create a demand for additional money capital from the market. Both are independent of quantity-of-money changes and of changes in commodity prices — though they interact with price changes in ways that produce further surpluses and deficits. Price changes in elements of production (Case II) and in products (Case III) generate further money-capital movements and can transfer capital across branches. Marx's conclusion: the money market is not a passive mirror of production; it is a separate arena whose dynamics are partly generated by the turnover mechanism itself.

Broader Practical, Theoretical, and Philosophical Implications

For the theory of the business cycle and crisis. The chapter supplies an essential mechanism for explaining how apparently sound capitalist enterprises can be caught short by timing alone. A prolongation of circulation time — whether from disrupted transport, credit contraction, or sudden market glut — imposes a demand for additional money capital that may not be obtainable at the moment required. Because turnover is desynchronised across branches (as Marx stresses when he notes that different capitals have different "turnover years"), and because different branches face different ratios of working period to circulation period, the aggregate money-market effects of these individual decisions can be substantial. The famous passage in which the "surfeit of money capital" arises with production and prices unchanged is a prefiguration of Volume III's treatment of the credit system and of crisis as the forcible restoration of the unity of the circuit of capital.

For the theory of credit. Marx explicitly identifies "the money capital that is set free simply by the mechanism of the turnover movement" — together with money capital set free by fixed capital reflux and needed for variable capital in every labour process — as forming one of the foundations of the credit system. This is a strong claim: credit is not simply a technological or institutional innovation, but a necessity forced by the internal timing requirements of industrial capital.

For the theory of price and value. Cases II and III show that price changes act as capital-transferring mechanisms. A productivity-induced fall in raw material prices precipitates a permanent addition to money capital, reflecting a real value change (a social reallocation of labour-time across branches). A transitory price movement produces a redistribution, one capitalist's loss becoming another's gain without any underlying productivity change. This is not a Hayekian view of prices as neutral signals; it is a view of prices as capital-reallocating forces operating through the money market.

For the critique of political economy. The chapter systematically dismantles the classical assumption that capital is functionally homogeneous and continuously active. Marx's target is not only the "empty heads of the capitalists" but the analysts (in his period, largely Ricardian and vulgar economists) who could not see why the whole quantity of advanced capital could not simply be working all at once. The argument that only a portion can function as productive capital at any moment, and that another portion must exist as commodity or money capital, is a fundamental insight into the heterogeneity of capital in time.

Philosophical-metaphysical resonance. At the deepest level, Chapter 15 establishes the productive role of time itself in the valorization process. Not just labour-time (as in Volume I's theory of value) but circulation-time enters the magnitude of the capital advance and the pace of accumulation. Time is not a mere backdrop for production; it is a force that decomposes and recomposes capital. The dialectic between value-in-process and value-in-rest, between active and latent capital, is worked out with arithmetical precision. The result is a picture of capitalist production as a restless self-interrupted process, held together only by the constant interposition of new advances and reserves. What appears as the "turnover" of a single capital is revealed to be the rotation of at least two capitals, each partly idle, each partly active — a rhythm the capitalist cannot escape, and which the credit system attempts to lubricate, with all the ambivalence that Marx will explore in Volume III.

Final Lesson

Chapter 15 teaches, in the most rigorous form Marx ever gave it, the truth that was to become a leitmotif of his mature work: the circuit of capital is a unity of production and circulation, and this unity imposes material, systematic, and immovable constraints upon the capitalist. The magnitude of capital advanced is not what the capitalist decides it should be; it is dictated by the ratio of the working period to the circulation period, and by the demand for continuity that capitalist large-scale industry cannot renounce. The money capital — that most liquid, most elusive of forms — is not marginal to production but woven into its very mechanisms. When the empty heads of capitalists and their spokesmen imagine that capital functions constantly and money is merely a convenience, they overlook the very mechanism that makes their profit possible. To reveal that mechanism, arithmetically and dialectically, is the enduring achievement of this chapter.
