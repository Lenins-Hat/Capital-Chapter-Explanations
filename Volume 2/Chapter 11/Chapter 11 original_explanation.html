Volume 2 • Chapter 11: Theories of Fixed and Circulating Capital. Ricardo

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem: A Category That Swallows Its Own Object

Chapter 11 of Marx's Theories of Surplus Value — the opening salvo of his extended engagement with David Ricardo — is not a chapter about capital in the ordinary sense. It is a chapter about the epistemological damage done by a single conceptual conflation: the identification of the antithesis fixed capital vs. circulating capital with the antithesis constant capital vs. variable capital. Marx opens with the flat declaration that "the basic confusion is however evident from the start," and the rest of the chapter is a forensic demonstration of how far the consequences of that confusion ramify — into the theory of value, the theory of surplus-value, the theory of wages, the theory of the rate of profit, and ultimately into the ideological self-concealment of the capitalist mode of production itself.

Ricardo's motive for raising the distinction is narrow and technical. As Marx states in the first sentence, Ricardo "introduces the distinction between fixed and circulating capital only in order to present the exceptions to the law of value, i.e. those cases in which the rate of wages affects prices." Marx defers his own full treatment of those exceptions to Capital Volume 3, Chapter 11 (a cross-reference the editors supply). But the narrowness of Ricardo's purpose is precisely what makes his confusion so instructive: a technical question about price-formation turns out to rest on a fundamental category error about what capital is and what makes it capital. Marx's thesis is therefore dialectical in structure: the very distinction that appears to explain the exception to the law of value, if it is confused with the valorization-distinction, destroys the law of value itself — and, with it, "the whole secret of surplus-value formation and of capitalist production."

The Intellectual Objective: Recovering the Esoteric Ricardo

The chapter has three interlocking objectives. First, exegetical dissection: to show exactly where and how Ricardo adopts Adam Smith's "esoteric/exoteric" split — Ricardo, Marx insists, is more surprising in his uncritical reception of Smith's confusion than the later apologists or Smith himself, because "Ricardo, in contrast to Smith, presented value and surplus-value consistently and clearly, and in point of fact upheld the esoteric Adam Smith against the exoteric." Second, constructive clarification: to state precisely what fixed/circulating capital does mean (a distinction derived from the circulation process, resting on the manner in which value is transferred to the product), and why it is a secondary and derivative determination that cannot carry the theoretical weight of the constant/variable distinction. Third, genealogical critique: to trace the century-long transmission of Smith's error through Barton, Ricardo, John Stuart Mill, Ramsay, and down to the "bank clerk" standpoint of MacLeod and Patterson, in whom the whole distinction collapses into the banking distinction between "money at call" and "money not at call."

Its Place in the Broader Framework

The chapter sits at the hinge of Marx's entire theoretical architecture. It presupposes Capital Volume 1 — the labour process and valorization process (Ch. 7), the organic composition of capital (Ch. 25 §2, cited at p. 772), and the fetishism of the commodity (Ch. 1 §4) — and it points forward to Capital Volume 3, where the equalization of the general rate of profit and the transformation of values into prices of production (Part Two) and the effect of wage fluctuations on prices of production (Ch. 11) are elaborated. Its methodological signature is the distinction between analyzing production "from the standpoint of the phenomena in their finished form" versus "penetrating through to the inner mechanism of the capitalist production process." Everything in the chapter is an application of that methodological pivot. The chapter is thus simultaneously a work of intellectual history, a work of category-critique, and a work of ideological unmasking — and it establishes the vocabulary for the whole subsequent Marxian critique of "circulating capital," including the critique of the wage-fund doctrine and of the notion that capital is simply "advanced value awaiting reappearance."

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Opening Gambit: Why Ricardo Raises the Distinction at All

Marx begins with a strategic restriction of scope that is also a diagnosis. Ricardo's reason for invoking fixed and circulating capital is not to characterize production but to handle a deviation from the norm. The law of value, in its Ricardian form, says that exchange-values are determined by quantities of embodied labour and are therefore, in the first instance, independent of the rate of wages: a general rise or fall of wages redistributes surplus-value between capitals but does not alter values. Ricardo notices, however, that when capitals of equal size are composed differently — some dominated by durable machinery, others by wages — a change in wages does alter relative prices. The fixed/circulating ratio is Ricardo's device for tracking these "exceptions." Marx quarantines this question at the outset: "We shall only come to speak of these in Volume 3" — that is, in the treatment of the general rate of profit and prices of production. The bracketing is not evasion but methodological: Marx wants the reader to see that the legitimate question (how do wage changes affect prices of production?) is being answered with an illegitimate apparatus (a confusion of two distinct distinctions).

2.2 The Smithian Quid Pro Quo: "Capital That Is to Support Labour"

The confusion, Marx says, is visible in the very juxtaposition of two formulations Ricardo places side by side. First, Ricardo speaks of "this difference in the degree of durability of fixed capital, and this variety in the proportions in which the two sorts of capital may be combined." Marx then asks the naive, devastating question — what are the two sorts of capital? — and reads the answer straight out of Ricardo's next sentence: "The proportions, too, in which the capital that is to support labour, and the capital that is invested in tools, machinery, and buildings, may be variously combined." The equation is thereby fixed in place:

  • Fixed capital = means of labour (tools, machinery, buildings);
  • Circulating capital = capital laid out on labour.

The phrase "the capital that is to support labour" is, Marx says, "itself an absurd expression taken over from Adam Smith." Its absurdity is threefold. (i) It suggests that the wage-bill supports labour, when in fact the worker's labour supports the capital. (ii) It treats the fund of wages as a thing with a natural function rather than as a social relation. (iii) It conflates the worker's means of subsistence with the capital that buys labour-power — a substitution Marx will dismantle at length later in the chapter.

Because the opposition is "not derived from the valorization process — constant and variable capital — but rather from the circulation process (the old Smithian confusion)," two misconceptions arise. Marx names the first immediately.

2.3 First Misconception: Durability Versus Organic Composition

The first misconception is that "the differences in the degree of durability of the fixed capital, and the variations in the composition of capital in terms of constant and variable, are taken as equivalent." Marx's refutation is a precise statement of what each distinction governs:

  • Variation in the constant/variable composition (the relation between capital laid out on means of production and capital laid out on labour-power) determines the variation in the production of surplus-value. It is a valorization-process determination.
  • Variation in the degree of durability of fixed capital is, "in so far as the valorization process is concerned, simply related to the manner in which a given value of means of production is transferred to the product." It is a value-transfer determination.
  • And "as far as the circulation process is concerned, it affects only the period of renewal of the capital laid out, in other words the time for which this is advanced." It is a turnover determination.

Three distinct determinations are here disentangled: surplus-value production (constant/variable), mode of value transfer (fixed/circulating, in part), and turnover/advancement time (circulation). Ricardo treats them as one. Marx then concedes the phenomenal plausibility of the error: "If, instead of penetrating through to the inner mechanism of the capitalist production process, you adopt the standpoint of the phenomena in their finished form, these distinctions do in fact coincide." When the total social surplus-value is distributed among branches as profit, differences in advancement time (long-lived fixed capital vs. quick-returning capital) and differences in organic composition both act as levers in the equalization of the general rate of profit and the transformation of values into prices of production. At the level of the "finished form" — the level Ricardo actually inhabits, the level of surface price phenomena — the two distinctions produce analogous effects. The error is therefore not a blunder of observation but a failure of abstraction: a surface equivalence is mistaken for an identity of inner causes.

2.4 Second Misconception: Two Cross-Cutting Classifications and the Vanishing Raw Material

The second misconception concerns the categorization of things. From the standpoint of the circulation process, capital splits into (a) means of labour = fixed capital, and (b) material of labour plus wages = fluid/circulating capital. From the standpoint of the labour and valorization process, capital splits into (a) means of production — both means and material of labour — = constant capital, and (b) labour-power = variable capital. The two partitions cut reality differently.

Marx demonstrates this with the organic composition analysis (cross-referencing Capital Vol. 1, Ch. 25, §2, p. 772). For the organic composition, "it is quite immaterial whether the same value of constant capital consists of more means of labour and less material of labour, or of more material of labour and less means of labour, whereas everything depends on the relation between the capital laid out on means of production and that laid out on labour-power." Conversely, for the circulation-process distinction, "it is just as immaterial in what proportion a given value of circulating capital is divided between material of labour and wages." The partitions are orthogonal:

  • From the valorization standpoint, material of labour is ranked with means of labour (both are constant capital) against labour-power.
  • From the circulation standpoint, labour-power is ranked with material of labour against means of labour.

So far, this is merely a difference of perspectives. But Marx now springs the structural trap. In Ricardo, the part of capital value laid out on raw materials and ancillaries appears on neither side of the fixed/circulating divide. It cannot be sorted into fixed capital, because "it completely coincides in its mode of circulation with the part of capital laid out on labour-power" (both transfer their entire value at once). And it cannot be sorted into circulating capital, because circulating capital has already been defined as capital laid out on labour — so placing raw material there "would be a self-refutation of the equation taken over from Adam Smith." Ricardo's instinct for logic is his undoing — or rather, the evidence of his honesty: "Ricardo has far too great an instinct for logic not to be sensitive to this, and he therefore just lets this part of the capital disappear." The category system literally erases one-third of the material components of capital.

2.5 The Interlude on the "Advance": Who Advances Whom?

Before returning to Ricardo, Marx inserts a compact polemic on the language of "advance." Political economy habitually says the capitalist advances wages — for a week, a month, or three months, depending on the pay interval. Marx inverts this: "In point of fact, the opposite happens. The worker advances the capitalist his labour for a week, a month or three months, according to the intervals at which he is paid." The conditional is decisive: if the capitalist bought labour in the strict sense — paid for it before it was performed — then "advance" would be the right word. But since payment occurs after the labour has lasted, the worker's advance of labour is "transformed into an advance that the capitalist makes to the worker in money. This in no way alters the fact that the capitalist gets the product back from circulation... only after a shorter or longer period of time." The ideological function of the term "advance" is to present the capitalist as the party who gives first and takes a risk — when in truth the capital laid out on wages is itself labour already performed and not yet paid for.

Marx then generalizes the point against a deeper prejudice: the notion that the duration for which a value is tied up confers an economic advantage or disadvantage in itself. "What the buyer of a commodity might want to do with it is completely immaterial to the seller. The capitalist does not get a machine any cheaper because he has to advance its entire value all at once, while the same value flows back to him from the circulation sphere only gradually and bit by bit; nor does he pay more for cotton because its value enters completely into the value of the product made from it, and is thus completely replaced at one stroke when this is sold on the market." The length of time a value is advanced cannot by itself alter value. What it can do — and here Marx foreshadows Volume 3 — is differentiate the annual rates of profit of capitals of equal value, which then enters the equalization of the general rate of profit. Time is real in the sphere of profit-rate formation; it is not a source of value. This distinction is exactly the one Ricardo loses.

2.6 "Let Us Then Return to Ricardo" (1): The Obliteration of Variable Capital

Marx now states, in the first of two numbered points, the characteristic feature of variable capital. A definite, given (and "in this sense constant") sum of value — assumed equal to the value of labour-power, though Marx is explicit that "it is immaterial here whether the wage is the same as, or more or less than, the value of the labour-power" — is exchanged for "a force that valorizes itself and creates value — labour-power, which not only reproduces the value paid to it by the capitalist, but also produces a surplus-value, a value that did not previously exist and is not bought for an equivalent." Note the two-part structure of the definition: reproduction of the value advanced plus production of an excess that was never purchased. This is the differential specifica of variable capital, and it is precisely what disappears when wages are viewed purely as circulating capital.

Why does it disappear? Because recategorizing wages as "circulating" places them "together under a single heading (that of circulating capital) with a component of the constant capital, that laid out on material of labour," and counterposes that bloc to means of labour. Under this heading, "surplus-value, i.e. the very circumstance which transforms the sum of value laid out into capital, is completely ignored." A second erasure follows: the distinction between the freshly produced value added by labour-power and the merely conserved value re-appearing from raw material is lost. Raw material's value is "not freshly produced, and not really reproduced, but is simply maintained and conserved in the value of the product, and hence merely reappears as a component of the product's value." The fixed/circulating distinction, on inspection, says only this: the value of means of labour goes into the commodity partially and gradually; the value of labour-power and of objects of labour goes in completely and at once.

Marx's verdict is categorical. In both cases "what is involved is a transfer of given, previously advanced values to the product, and their replacement when the product is sold. The sole distinction here is whether the transfer of value, and therefore the replacement of value, proceeds bit by bit and gradually, or all at once." Therefore: "The all-important distinction between variable and constant capital is thereby obliterated, and with it the whole secret of surplus-value formation and of capitalist production, namely the circumstances that transform certain values and the things in which they are represented into capital." The categories of capital are reduced to distinctions in "the mode of circulation" — and circulation, Marx reminds us, "has of course only to do with already existing, given values." Circulation can redistribute and return value; it cannot create it. A theory that classifies capital solely by its mode of circulation has, by construction, no place from which to explain surplus-value.

2.7 The Genealogy of the Error: Smith, Ricardo, and the Physiocratic Contrast

Marx asks why bourgeois political economy "held instinctively to Adam Smith's confusion" for "a whole century." The answer is structural: once wages and raw materials are merged into a single "circulating" fund counterposed to machinery, "the basis for understanding the real movement of capitalist production, and thus of capitalist exploitation, was thus submerged at one blow. All that was involved, on this view, was the reappearance of values advanced." The theory becomes a theory of circulation without production of surplus-value — a comfortable resting place for an apologetic science.

Ricardo's case is, Marx says, more surprising — more surprising than the later apologists, "among whom the confusion of concepts is rather something unsurprising," and more surprising even than Smith — because Ricardo did have a consistent theory of value and surplus-value and did uphold "the esoteric Adam Smith against the exoteric." Marx implies a two-level reading of Smith: the esoteric Smith (the analyst who resolves value into labour and treats profit as deduction from the product of labour) and the exoteric Smith (the systematizer who explains profit as a "deduction" alongside rent and wages in a trinity of independent revenues, and who classifies capital by durability). Ricardo inherits the esoteric doctrine as his substance and the exoteric taxonomy as his scaffolding, never noticing that the scaffolding cannot support the structure.

By contrast, the Physiocrats are free of the confusion. Their distinction between avances annuelles (annual advances) and avances primitives (original/primitive advances) refers "solely to the different reproduction periods of the different components of capital, agricultural capital in particular." Crucially, their theory of surplus-value is "a part of their theory which is independent of these distinctions, a part in fact that they held up as its culminating point." The Physiocrats locate surplus-value not in capital as such but "simply to one specific sphere of capitalist production, agriculture" — a narrow and ultimately untenable localization, but one which at least does not corrupt their reproduction-period taxonomy with a valorization claim. Marx's point: the Physiocrats had a coherent circulation-process distinction because they did not confuse it with a valorization-process one. This is the standard against which Ricardo is measured — and to which, as we shall see, Ricardo winds up regressing.

2.8 "Return to Ricardo" (2): The Physicalist Reduction of Variable Capital

The second numbered point begins with a restatement of the essential feature of variable capital: "the capitalist exchanges a definite, given (and in this sense constant) value for value-creating power; a magnitude of value for the production of value, for self-valorization." The form of payment — money or means of subsistence — is indifferent to this. It affects "only the mode of existence of the value advanced by him," which exists either as money with which the worker buys subsistence on the market, or as subsistence directly consumed. Marx notes that developed capitalist production "in fact assumes that the worker is paid in money, just as it assumes in general that the production process is mediated by the circulation process, i.e. a money economy." The sentence is important: the money-wage form is the historically adequate form of capitalist wage-payment, but it is not the source of surplus-value. Surplus-value arises "neither from the money form nor from the natural form of wages... It arises from the exchange of value for value-creating power, from the conversion of a constant quantity into a variable one."

Marx now turns to the means of labour, to show that their fixedness is likewise not a physical property. "The more or less fixed character of the means of labour is a function of their degree of durability, i.e. a physical property." Durability determines speed of wear, hence the duration for which a thing can function as fixed capital. "But it is in no way simply this physical property of durability which leads them to function as fixed capital." The metal-works example is the proof: "In metal works, the raw material is just as durable as the machines with which it is processed, and more durable in fact than many components of these machines — leather, wood, etc. But the metal serving as raw material does not form any the less a part of the circulating capital, while the functioning means of labour that may be constructed of the same metal form part of fixed capital." Identical substance, identical durability, opposite category. Therefore "it is not its material, physical nature... which makes the same metal in one case fixed capital and in the other case circulating capital. This distinction rather arises from the role that it plays in the production process, in one case as object of labour, in the other case as means of labour."

Marx then states the correct relationship between function and material, in two steps. (i) The function of a means of labour requires it to serve repeatedly across many labour processes; "its function thus prescribes a greater or lesser degree of durability for its material." (ii) But "the durability of the material from which it is made does not make it in and for itself fixed capital." The same material becomes circulating capital when used as raw material. And for economists who confuse commodity capital with productive capital, "the same material or the same machine is circulating capital as a product, and fixed capital as a means of labour" — a confusion Marx will list explicitly among the chapter's four concluding "results."

2.9 Durability as Material Basis, Not as Criterion

Having denied that durability constitutes fixedness, Marx immediately grants it a real, subordinate role — a classic instance of his dialectical precision. "Even though it is not the durable material of which the means of labour is made that makes it fixed capital, its role as means of labour does require it to consist of a more or less durable material. The durability of its material is thus a condition for its function as means of labour, hence also a material basis of the mode of circulation that makes it fixed capital. Other things being equal, the greater or lesser perishability of its material imprints it to a lower or higher degree with the stamp of fixedness, and is thus very fundamentally bound up with its quality as fixed capital." The logical structure here matters enormously: condition ≠ criterion. Durability is causally necessary and quantitatively relevant; it is not definitionally decisive. This is precisely the hairsbreadth on which Ricardo's definition falls, because Ricardo does make perishability the criterion ("According as capital is rapidly perishable... it is classed under the heads of circulating, or of fixed capital").

2.10 The Material Substitution: Labour-Power Replaced by Means of Subsistence

Marx now traces the consequences of the confusion at the level of material determination. If you consider the capital laid out on labour-power "exclusively from the standpoint of circulating capital," and if you merge the constant/variable distinction into the fixed/circulating distinction, then — because the material reality of the means of labour (durable metal, wood, etc.) is an essential basis for its fixedness — you will "naturally" seek an equally material basis for the circulating character of wages, and "define circulating capital in terms of the material reality of variable capital."

But what is the material reality of the capital laid out on wages? Not means of subsistence — that is the great substitution Marx now exposes. "The real material of the capital laid out on wages is labour itself, self-acting, value-creating labour-power, living labour, which the capitalist has exchanged for dead, objectified labour, and incorporated into his capital, this being the way that the value existing in his hands is first transformed into a self-valorizing value." This is a compressed version of the entire theory of capital: dead objectified labour is exchanged for living labour, and it is this exchange that first converts a sum of value into capital.

And crucially, the capitalist's relation to this labour-power remains that of owner of productive capital: "the capitalist does not sell this power of self-valorization. It forms throughout simply a component of his productive capital, just like his means of labour, and is never a component of his commodity capital." The follow-up is structurally neat: "Within the production process, the means of labour, as components of productive capital, are not distinguished from labour-power as fixed capital, any more than the material of labour and ancillaries coincide with it as circulating capital." Put differently: within production, the fixed/circulating distinction simply does not apply as an internal split between means of labour and labour-power. From the labour-process standpoint, both means of labour and material of labour are objective factors confronting labour-power as the personal factor. From the valorization-process standpoint, both are constant capital as against labour-power as variable capital. There is no third partition corresponding to "fixed vs. circulating."

Marx then offers, hypothetically ("Alternatively, if we are to speak of a material difference that affects the circulation process..."), what the genuine material difference is. It follows "from the nature of value, which is nothing other than objectified labour, and from the nature of self-acting labour-power, which is nothing other than self-objectifying labour, that labour-power constantly creates value and surplus-value as long as it continues to function; that what presents itself on its side as movement, as the creation of value, presents itself on the side of its product in a motionless form, as created value." This is a striking phenomenological restatement of the labour theory of value in terms of motion versus rest: living labour is becoming; the product is being; value-added is the crystallized trace of that transition. Once the labour-power has functioned, "the capital no longer consists of labour-power on the one hand and means of production on the other. The capital value that was laid out on labour-power is now value which has been added to the product (together with surplus-value)." To repeat, "the product must be sold, and with the money released by this, labour-power has constantly to be bought afresh and incorporated into the productive capital. This then is what gives the portion of capital laid out on labour-power the character of circulating capital in contrast to the capital that remains fixed in the means of labour."

Note the precision: labour-power's "circulating" character derives from the fact that it is consumed entirely in each production period and must be repurchased from the market — a genuine circulation-process fact. It is a derivative entailment of the labor-process structure, not an alternative description of variable capital.

2.11 The Great Substitution and Its Payoff: Ricardo's Perishability Criterion

The error occurs at the next step. "But if the secondary characteristic of circulating capital, which labour-power has in common with a part of the constant capital (raw materials and ancillaries), is made into the fundamental one" — i.e., the fact that its value is transferred in its entirety rather than bit by bit — "then the portion of capital laid out on wages must also consist materially not of self-acting labour-power, but of the material elements that the worker buys with his wages, i.e. of the part of the social commodity capital that enters the worker's consumption, the means of subsistence in other words." From this follows a complete reclassification: fixed capital becomes means of labour ("which perish more slowly"); variable capital becomes means of subsistence ("which have to be replaced more rapidly"). Capital laid out on labour-power is now described by what the worker consumes rather than what the capitalist buys.

Marx then exposes the incoherence of the criterion itself with Ricardo's own list:

"The food and clothing consumed by the labourer, the buildings in which he works, the implements with which his labour is assisted, are all of a perishable nature. There is however a vast difference in the time for which these different capitals will endure: a steam-engine will last longer than a ship, a ship than the clothing of the labourer, and the clothing of the labourer longer than the food which he consumes."

Marx's rebuttal: "Ricardo forgets here the house in which the worker lives, his furniture, his tools of consumption such as knives, forks, dishes, etc., all of which possess the same character of durability as do the means of labour. The same things and the same classes of things thus appear now as means of consumption, now as means of labour." The durability continuum is continuous, not bipartite. A worker's dwelling may outlast a ship; a merchant's cutlery is indistinguishable in durability from the worker's. Any criterion that sorts economic categories along a quantitative continuum of perishability will fail at the margins, and the margins are precisely where the categories must be defined. This is why Ricardo himself must concede:

"A division not essential, and in which the line of demarcation cannot be accurately drawn."

Marx's sardonic summary: "Thus we have happily ended up once again back with the Physiocrats, where the distinction between avances annuelles and avances primitives was a distinction in the times of consumption, and hence also in the varying reproduction times, of the capital applied." But the regression is a degeneration, not a return: "It is simply that what in their case expressed a phenomenon of importance for social production, and is depicted in [Quesnay's] Tableau économique in connection with the circulation process, here becomes a subjective distinction, and one that Ricardo himself says is superfluous." In the Tableau, the annual/primitive distinction organized the whole social reproduction process — it was a structural law of the economy. In Ricardo it becomes a matter of how long a thing happens to last, arbitrarily drawn and explicitly declared inessential.

Marx rounds this beat off with the general consequence: "As soon as the part of capital laid out on labour is distinguished from that laid out on means of labour only by its reproduction period and thus its term of circulation... then every pertinent difference between the capital laid out on labour-power and that laid out on means of production is obviously destroyed." The capital/labour relation is reformatted as a relation between two kinds of goods differentiated only by turnover speed. And this "completely contradicts Ricardo's doctrine of value, as well as his theory of profit, which is in point of fact a theory of surplus-value."

2.12 Ricardo's Two Errors Within His Own Restricted Investigation

Marx acknowledges that Ricardo's use of the distinction is limited: he "only ever considers the distinction between fixed and circulating capital in so far as different proportions of the two, in the case of capitals of equal size in different branches of industry, influence the law of value, and particularly the degree to which a rise or fall in wages affects prices." Yet "even within this restricted investigation, he commits very great errors... and in fact he starts his investigation on a completely false basis." Marx enumerates:

  1. Falsification of circulating capital itself. "In so far as the portion of capital value laid out on labour-power is subsumed under the heading of circulating capital, the characteristics of circulating capital are themselves falsely presented, and so in particular are the circumstances which subsume the portion of capital laid out on labour under this heading." That is, the error is not merely one of misplacement; it corrupts the definition of the category into which the misplacement occurs. Once wages are the paradigm of "circulating capital," economists redefine circulating capital as "capital laid out on wages," and then the durability criterion, the transfer criterion, and the wage-fund criterion are jammed together incoherently.

  2. Confusion of two qualities. "There is a confusion between the quality that makes the part of capital laid out on labour variable, and the quality that makes it circulating in contrast to fixed." Variability (value-creating power, self-valorization, production of surplus-value) is a valorization quality; circulatingness (full and immediate value transfer, rapid replacement) is a circulation quality. Ricardo treats them as one and the same property.

Marx then states the deeper stakes. "It is clear from the start that the definition of the capital laid out on labour-power as circulating or fluid is a secondary one, which glosses over its specific difference in the production process." Two reasons: - First, "in this definition the capitals laid out on labour and on raw materials, etc. are equivalent; and a classification that identifies one part of the constant capital with the variable capital does not come to grips with the specific difference of variable capital as opposed to constant." - Second, "although the portions of capital laid out on labour and on means of labour are counterposed to one another, this is in no way with respect to the fact that they are involved in the production of value in completely different ways, but simply with respect to the different periods of time during which the given value of both is transferred to the product."

The unifying statement is this: "What is at issue in all these cases is how a given value which is invested in the production process of a commodity, whether as wages, the price of raw materials or the price of means of labour, is transferred to the product, hence circulated by the product and brought back to its starting-point or replaced by its sale. The only distinction here consists in the how, in the particular way in which this value is transferred and thus circulates." Note the ellipsis contained in the emphasised word: the whole distinction concerns form of return, not source of creation.

2.13 The Money-Form Argument and the Wayland Quote: The Mystery of Reappearance

Marx then closes off a possible escape route. Someone might object that the confusion in Ricardo arises only because wages are paid in money, and money does not enter the labour process materially. Marx grants this: "in the case of wages paid in money, it is obvious that it is not the money itself that enters the production process, in the same way that it is not just the value but also the material of the means of production that enters this process." The objection proves nothing: the wage is a definite contractually fixed price regardless of its form of payment. "Whether the price of labour-power, which in any case is previously determined by contract, is paid in money or in means of subsistence, in no way changes its character of being a definite and given price."

The damage occurs when one translates the money-wage back into the goods it buys and then locates those goods within the circulation taxonomy: "if the means of subsistence that the worker buys with his wage are directly placed under one heading together with the raw materials, etc., as the material shape of circulating capital, and the means of labour counterposed to them, then this gives the matter a different appearance." Now the story becomes: "the value of one lot of things, the means of production, is transferred to the product in the labour process" while "the value of the other lot of things, the means of subsistence, reappears in the labour-power that consumes them, and is similarly transferred to the product by the labour-power's activity." Result: "What is involved in all these cases is similarly the mere reappearance in the product of the values advanced during production." Marx adds a parenthetical, historically loaded note: "(The Physiocrats took this seriously and denied that industrial labour created surplus-value.)" — a reminder that the reappearance-model is a substantive theoretical position with devastating consequences, not a harmless verbal simplification.

He then cites Wayland as the perfected form of this view:

"It matters not in what form capital reappears... The various kinds of food, clothing, and shelter, necessary for the existence and comfort of the human being, are also changed. They are consumed from time to time, and their value reappears..."

(Marx footnotes Wayland as an American economist-parson, "author of a popular manual" of 1843, whose combination of professions — economist and clergyman — parallels Malthus and Chalmers, an aside that is itself a jab.) Marx's conclusion: "The capital values advanced to production in the shape of means of production and means of subsistence here both equally reappear in the value of the product. The capitalist production process is thus successfully transformed into a complete mystery, and the origin of the surplus-value present in the product completely withdrawn from view." This is the theoretical payoff of the confusion: a system in which everything "reappears" cannot explain how anything increases.

2.14 Fetishism and the Functional Definition of Categories

Marx now names the underlying intellectual vice: "fetishism peculiar to bourgeois economics, which transforms the social, economic character that things are stamped with in the process of social production into a natural character arising from the material nature of these things." This is the direct application of the doctrine of commodity fetishism from Capital Vol. 1, Ch. 1, §4 (cross-referenced in Marx's own footnote) to the categories of capital. The formula "means of labour, for instance, are fixed capital" is "a scholastic definition which leads to contradictions and confusion." Its error is to treat capital-categories as inherent properties of things.

Marx then proposes the correct formulation, and it is one of the most theoretically consequential passages in the book. "Just as we have shown how, in the labour process (Volume 1, Chapter 7), it depends entirely on the role which the objective components play at the time in a particular labour process, on their function, whether they function as means of labour, material of labour or product, so, in precisely the same way, means of labour are fixed capital only where the production process is in fact a capitalist production process and the means of production are thus actually capital, i.e. possess the economic determination, the social character, of capital; secondly, they are fixed capital only if they transfer their value to the product in a particular way."

Two conditions are thereby laid down for "fixed capital": 1. The production process must be capitalist — the means of production must actually be capital, i.e., possess the social determination of capital (valorization of value through wage-labour). 2. They must transfer their value to the product in a particular way — bit by bit, gradually.

And the definition must be functional, not material: "If this is not the case, then they remain means of labour without being fixed capital. In the same way, ancillaries such as fertilizer, if they give up their value in the same particular way as do the greater part of means of labour, are fixed capital, although they are not means of labour." The fertilizer case is decisive against any material definition: fertilizer is not a means of labour at all (it is a material/ancillary), yet it is fixed capital because of the way it transfers value (over several production periods, as soil nutrients). "What is at issue here is not a set of definitions under which things are to be subsumed. It is rather definite functions that are expressed in specific categories." This is Marx's general epistemological principle for social science: categories express functions within relations, not classifications of things.

This principle immediately yields sharp critiques. "If it is the destiny of the means of subsistence in themselves, a property devolving on them under all circumstances, to be capital laid out on wages, then it also becomes the character of this 'circulating' capital 'to support labour' (Ricardo, p. 25)." And then the subversive reversal: "If the means of subsistence were not 'capital', then they would not support labour-power; though it is in fact precisely their character as capital that gives them the property of supporting capital by the labour of others." That is: the "support of labour" attributed to a natural class of goods is in reality a social relation of exploitation misrecognized as a natural function of goods.

2.15 The Wage-Fund Corollary

From the material substitution follows a further, politically explosive consequence. "If means of subsistence are inherently circulating capital — after this has been transformed into wages — then it further results that the size of the wage depends on the ratio between the number of workers and the given mass of circulating capital — a favourite proposition of the economists." This is the wage-fund doctrine in its Ricardian-Malthusian version: wages are determined by the ratio of the (physically given) circulating capital fund to the number of workers. Marx's refutation is compressed but complete: "whereas in point of fact the quantity of means of subsistence that the worker withdraws from the market, and the quantity which the capitalist has at his disposal for his own consumption, depend rather on the ratio between surplus-value and the price of labour." That is, what appears to be a natural limit (a fixed pile of foodstuffs divided among workers) is a social division between profit and wages. The wage-fund doctrine translates the exploitative distribution of the product back into the physical data of production and thereby naturalizes it. Marx's actual determination runs in the opposite direction: the wage determines the quantity of subsistence that flows to labour, not vice versa.

2.16 Barton, and the Turnover/Fixedness Confusion

Marx then makes a transitional observation: "Ricardo, like Barton, constantly confuses the ratio between variable and constant capital with the ratio between circulating and fixed capital. We shall see later on how this vitiates his investigation of the rate of profit." (Footnote: Barton's Observations on the Circumstances which Influence the Condition of the Labouring Classes of Society, London, 1817; the reference to Capital Vol. 3, Chapters 1–3 — "Cost-Price and Profit," "The Rate of Profit," and "The Relation of the Rate of Profit to the Rate of Surplus-Value.") This is the bridge from the conceptual analysis of the chapter to the mathematical analysis that follows it: because Ricardo substitutes circulating/fixed (a turnover distinction) for variable/constant (a valorization distinction), his treatment of the falling rate of profit will be corrupted.

Marx then identifies a second, independent error in Ricardo's apparatus: "Ricardo further equates the distinctions that arise in the turnover for reasons other than the distinction between fixed and circulating capital, with the latter distinction itself." He quotes Ricardo's seed-corn example:

"It is also to be observed that the circulating capital may circulate, or be returned to its employer, in very unequal times. The wheat bought by a farmer to sow is comparatively a fixed capital to the wheat purchased by a baker to make into loaves. One leaves it in the ground, and can obtain no return for a year; the other can get it ground into flour, sell it as bread to his customers, and have his capital free to renew the same, or commence any other employment in a week."

Marx's analysis: "It is characteristic here that wheat, although as seed-corn it serves not as means of subsistence but as raw material, is firstly circulating capital, because it is inherently means of subsistence, and secondly fixed capital, because its return stretches over a year." Ricardo's own scheme generates a contradictory double-valuation of the same object: seed-corn is circulating by nature but fixed by return-time. "But it is not just the slower or more rapid return that makes a means of production into fixed capital, but rather the specific manner in which it gives up value to the product." The formal point: seed-corn, like any raw material, transfers its entire value into the product at once (it is consumed in the act of sowing and re-emerges as the value of the crop) — its mode of transfer is circulating. What makes its return slow is the turnover period of the capital, which is a different determination. Ricardo fuses turnover time (a circulation velocity) with fixedness (a transfer mode).

2.17 The Fourfold Harvest: Consequences of Smith's Confusion

Marx closes the chapter with a numbered list of four historical results of the Smithian confusion. They are, in effect, a small genealogy of nineteenth-century economics.

(1) Fixed/fluid confused with productive/commodity capital. "Thus the same machine is circulating capital, for example, when it exists on the market as a commodity, and fixed capital when it is incorporated into the production process. It is impossible to see here why one particular kind of capital should be more fixed or more circulating than another." The error reduces a functional distinction to a contextual one, so that the same object is both categories at different moments — which empties the distinction of content.

(2) All circulating capital identified with capital laid out on wages. "This is the case with John Stuart Mill among others." Marx's footnote directs us to Mill's Essays on Some Unsettled Questions of Political Economy (London, 1844), criticized in detail in Theories of Surplus-Value, Part III, pp. 190 ff., and quotes his own Postface to the second German edition of Capital Volume 1 to characterize Mill's general position: "The Continental revolution of 1848 also had its reaction in England [i.e. on economic thought]. Men who still claimed some scientific standing and aspired to be something more than mere sophists and sycophants of the ruling classes tried to harmonize the political economy of capital with the claims, no longer to be ignored, of the proletariat. Hence a shallow syncretism, of which John Stuart Mill is the best representative." The Mill case therefore illustrates not only the internal error but its class-political function: to synthesize capital's theory with concessions to labour without disturbing the foundations.

(3) Complete reduction of constant/variable to circulating/fixed — Ramsay. "The distinction between variable and constant capital, which Barton, Ricardo and others already confused with that between circulating and fixed capital, is eventually reduced completely to the latter distinction, as with Ramsay for example, who takes not only means of labour, but all means of production, raw materials etc. as fixed capital, and only the capital laid out on wages as circulating capital. But because the reduction is accomplished in this way, the real distinction between constant and variable capital is not grasped." Ramsay's move is a simplification that looks more rigorous than Ricardo's — he eliminates the raw-material anomaly by folding raw materials into fixed capital — but it is a reductio: form of turnover is now the entire content of the capital distinction.

(4) The bank-clerk reduction. "The most recent English economists, and even more so the Scottish ones, who view everything from the unutterably narrow standpoint of a bank clerk — such as MacLeod, Patterson and others — transform the distinction between fixed and circulating capital into that between 'money at call' and 'money not at call' (that is to say, between deposit money that can be withdrawn without prior notification, and money whose withdrawal requires such notification)." (Footnotes: Henry Dunning MacLeod, The Elements of Political Economy, London, 1858; Robert Hogard Patterson, The Science of Finance, Edinburgh and London, 1868.) This is Marx's reductio ad absurdum of the whole trajectory: a distinction which once concerned the reproduction periods of agricultural capital in Quesnay's Tableau has dissolved into a distinction between two types of bank deposit, differentiated only by the legal conditions of withdrawal. The disappearance of production from the categories is complete: capital has become "money" and its distinction has become a term in a banking contract.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles Revealed

The chapter yields several interlocking theoretical laws.

First: the primacy of the valorization process over the circulation process in the definition of capital. Every specific analytical gain in the chapter depends on the priority of "constant/variable" (derived from the way capital produces value) over "fixed/circulating" (derived from the way capital's value returns). Where the priority is reversed, the surplus-value-producing relation disappears into an accounting of value transfer. Marx's formula — "the circumstance which transforms the sum of value laid out into capital" — locates capital's essence in the exchange of value for value-creating power, not in any physical or temporal property of the things involved.

Second: functional, not material, definition of categories. The metal-works and fertilizer examples establish that the fixed/circulating distinction tracks functions within a production relation, not substances. "What is at issue here is not a set of definitions under which things are to be subsumed. It is rather definite functions that are expressed in specific categories." This has urgent methodological consequences: any economics that classifies capital by the material character of the objects that embody it is committing, in Marx's terms, fetishism — mistaking a social relation for a natural property.

Third: the historical specificity of economic categories. Fixed capital, strictly speaking, exists only "where the production process is in fact a capitalist production process" — means of labour "remain means of labour without being fixed capital" where capital relations do not obtain. Categories are historically bounded. This corrects any temptation to treat fixed/circulating as transhistorical technical concepts applicable to any economy.

Fourth: the distinction between condition and criterion, and between continuum and category. Durability is a condition for fixedness, but not the criterion; perishability is a continuum, but fixed/circulating purports to be a bipartite classification. Ricardo's collapse follows necessarily from his attempt to draw a categorical line on a continuous physical scale. Categories must have an internal relational differential specifica, which continuous physical properties cannot supply.

Fifth: the ideological production of mystery. The whole apparatus of "reappearance" — Smith, Wayland, Malthus — "successfully transformed [the capitalist production process] into a complete mystery, and the origin of the surplus-value present in the product completely withdrawn from view." This is the crucial ideological lesson: the same set of categories can either illuminate or obscure, and the obscure version is typically preferred because it conceals exploitation, naturalizes wage-fund limits, and reduces capital to "money at call." The category system is not politically innocent.

Broader Practical and Theoretical Implications

For the theory of value and profit. The chapter explains the location of Ricardo's errors that later mature into the contradictions of the Ricardian school and its dissolution. Ricardo's doctrine that values and surplus-value are consistently labour-determined is correct; his taxonomy of capital is Smithian. The two cannot live together. Marx's subsequent Vol. 3 treatment of the rate of profit, and Ch. 11 of Vol. 3 on wage fluctuations and prices of production, is the completion of this chapter's demolition. Anyone reading Ricardo's "exceptions to the law of value" without this chapter's distinction will misplace the entire problem.

For the critique of the wage-fund doctrine. Marx's counter-formulation — the wage depends on "the ratio between surplus-value and the price of labour," and the worker's subsistence depends on the wage, not the other way around — is the destruction of a doctrine that persisted through Malthus, Mill, and Senior and that served to blame working-class poverty on workers' own procreation rather than on the distribution of surplus-value. Marx here shows that the wage-fund doctrine is not an empirical mistake but a necessary corollary of the materialization of variable capital into means of subsistence.

For the critique of physiocracy and of the "advance" ideology. The Physiocratic Tableau was, for all its agricultural limitations, a serious account of social reproduction. Ricardo's version, on his own admission, is "a division not essential." The comparison is instructive about how progress in one dimension (Ricardo's labour theory of value) can coexist with regress in another (his capital taxonomy). Similarly, Marx's inversion of "advance" — the worker advances labour, and only then is paid — uncovers the temporal lie at the heart of the ideological narrative of capital's benevolent initiative.

For the philosophy of social science. Marx's insistence that categories express functions within relations rather than properties of things is a general methodological proposition of wide import. It rules out, in advance, both naive materialist classifications (things classified by their physical properties) and purely formalist classifications (things classified by their accounting treatment). It insists on the primacy of the production relation.

For the critique of modern economics. The final irony — capital reduced to "money at call" and "money not at call" — is not merely a nineteenth-century curiosity. Marx's target is a mode of thought in which financial circulation becomes the master-category of capital. The chapter's analytical spine — never confuse the form of return of value with the production of surplus value — remains a usable criterion for evaluating any economics that treats capital in terms of liquidity, turnover, or asset duration.
