Volume 3 • Chapter 6: The Effect of Price Fluctuations

An Exhaustive Deconstruction and Analysis

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem: How Do Price Fluctuations Move the Rate of Profit?

Chapter 6 of Capital, Volume 3, occupies a deceptively narrow niche in Marx's larger architecture: it asks what happens to the rate of profit when the prices of the material inputs to production rise or fall, holding the exploitation of labour constant. The chapter's surface is empirical — a long cascade of Factory Inspector reports, cotton statistics, wool prices, and flax distempers — but its underlying engine is a single analytical claim: the rate of profit is a ratio (p′ = s/C), and therefore anything that changes the magnitude of the denominator C, even while surplus-value and the rate of surplus-value remain untouched, must move the rate of profit. Raw materials are a major component of constant capital c, and c is a major component of C. Hence raw-material prices become a direct, autonomous determinant of the profit rate, entirely separate from the class struggle over wages.

The intellectual objective is twofold. First, Marx wants to isolate this channel — to demonstrate analytically that price fluctuations of raw and ancillary materials affect the rate of profit in the opposite direction to the movement of those prices, other things equal. Second, he wants to dissolve a confusion he attributes to both practical men and great theorists: those who feel the influence of raw-material prices (like Torrens) cannot explain it theoretically, while those who hold firmly to general principles (like Ricardo) fail to recognise the influence of world trade on the profit rate. The chapter exists to give the correct theoretical account of a phenomenon that practical spinners, factory inspectors, and free-traders all testified to but nobody had properly conceptualised.

Why This Chapter Matters in Marx's Framework

In the preceding chapters of Volume 3, Marx has established the transformation of surplus-value into profit, the concepts of cost-price and profit, and the difference between the rate of surplus-value (s/v) and the rate of profit (s/C = s/(c+v)). Chapter 5 examined economy in the use of constant capital. Chapter 6 now extends that analysis to the revaluation and devaluation of capital caused by price changes in its material elements. The move is crucial: it shows that the rate of profit is not simply a function of exploitation (s/v), nor even of the organic composition of capital in some static sense, but is also exposed to the contingent, historically volatile prices of organic raw materials — agricultural products whose supply is subject to organic laws, harvests, seasons, and the uneven development of world trade. This has profound consequences: it introduces a source of systemic turbulence into capitalist reproduction, explains why crises can originate in the sphere of raw-material provision, and grounds Marx's claim that capitalist production is inherently hostile to rational agriculture.

The chapter also introduces two new analytical categories — the release and tying-up of capital and the revaluation and devaluation of capital — which recur throughout Volume 3 and which are essential for understanding how capitalist crises can appear as if the profit rate (or even the mass of profit) moved independently of surplus-value. These categories are Marx's tool for explaining why the surface phenomena of the credit system, world-market competition, and crises are so "crazy" (verrückt): the mass of profit and the rate of profit can genuinely fluctuate while the underlying extraction of surplus labour is unchanged, because capital values and releases shift beneath them.

Finally, the chapter serves an empirical-rhetorical purpose. By the end, Marx has assembled the most sustained documentary indictment in Volume 3: the cotton famine of 1861–65 treated as a laboratory of capitalist behaviour under raw-material scarcity. It is here that abstraction meets the wage lists of starving weavers, the "experiments in corpore vili," the Relief Committees acting as "guard-dogs," and the public works that resurrect the Ateliers nationaux of 1848 — this time for the profit of the bourgeoisie. The chapter is thus simultaneously a theorem and a prosecution.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Framing Assumption: Freezing the Rate of Surplus-Value

Marx opens with a methodological declaration that governs the entire chapter: "Here, as before, we assume there is no change in the rate of surplus-value. This is a necessary assumption, if we are to investigate the situation in its pure form." This is the classic ceteris paribus move, but with a specific purpose: to isolate the effect of variations in c (constant capital) on the profit rate, so that whatever movements in p′ are observed cannot be attributed to a changed intensity of exploitation. The assumption is not a claim that s′ never changes in reality — indeed, Marx immediately concedes that a capital could employ more or fewer workers as a result of price-induced contraction or expansion, which would change the mass of surplus-value even at constant s′. But he sets that "side-effect" aside as a complication to be handled later, and insists that where such complications occur simultaneously with machinery improvements or wage changes, "we simply have to combine (1) the effect that the variation in constant capital has on the profit rate, and (2) the effect that the variation in wages has on the profit rate. The result is then immediately given." This is additive decomposition — a signal that the chapter will proceed by analytic components whose separate effects can later be summed.

The critical consequence he draws immediately: variations from raw-material prices affect the rate of profit "even if they leave wages, and thus the rate and mass of surplus-value, completely undisturbed." Because p′ = s/C, they "alter the value of C and therefore the value of the fraction as a whole." This is the hinge on which everything else swings. It gives rise to a striking claim of indifference: it is "completely immaterial here — as distinct from what we found in considering surplus-value — in what spheres of production these variations take place." Unlike the value of labour-power (where what matters is whether the commodity is a means of subsistence for workers), raw-material price changes matter wherever they occur, even in luxury production — defined rigorously here as "all production that is not required by the reproduction of labour-power." This is a direct corollary of the denominator logic: C is C regardless of what the produced commodity is destined for.

Nuance and implicit counter-argument: This indifference claim is a marked contrast with the analysis of surplus-value, where the use of the product (wage-good vs. luxury) determines whether a change in its value raises or lowers surplus-value. Marx is signalling that the two channels — s/v and C — obey different logics. A reader accustomed to thinking that only wage-goods matter would be misled; the rate of profit is exposed to the whole price-structure of the material inputs of production, not merely to those that enter worker consumption.

2.2 The Scope of "Raw Material": Ancillary Materials and the Machinery Clarification

Marx immediately broadens the category. "Under raw material we also include the ancillary materials such as indigo, coal, gas, etc." This matters because some branches — mining, extractive industries, transport — use little raw material proper, yet are still exposed to price fluctuations through their ancillary inputs. And he adds a subtle clarification about machinery: machinery itself has a raw-material composition (iron, wood, leather), so its price is affected by fluctuations in the price of those materials. "In as much as its price is raised by fluctuations in the price of the raw material of which it consists, or of the ancillary material that it needs in the course of its operation, the rate of profit falls in proportion to this, and vice versa."

But then he deliberately narrows the field for the investigation that follows: he will confine himself "to fluctuations in price of that raw material which actually goes into the process of production of the commodity," setting aside the raw material of machines and their ancillary operating materials. A single point is reserved for attention: the natural riches — iron, coal, wood — that constitute "the main elements in the construction and use of machines, appear now as a natural fruit borne by capital and form an element in the determination of the rate of profit that is independent of the high or low level of wages." This is a proto-rental/natural-monopoly observation: the productivity of nature embedded in the means of production becomes a determinant of p′ that has nothing to do with labour costs. It connects forward to the theory of ground-rent, which Marx will treat later, and it quietly establishes that the profit rate has natural as well as social determinants.

2.3 The Inverse Law: Raw Material Prices Against the Rate of Profit

The analytical core arrives quickly. "Since the rate of profit is s/C or s/(c+v), it is clear that everything that gives rise to a change in the magnitude of c, and therefore of C, also brings about a change in the profit rate, even if s, v and their reciprocal relationship remain constant." Raw material is "a major component of constant capital." So when the price of raw material falls by a sum d, "c or c+v is changed to c − d or (c − d) + v, and the rate of profit rises." The explicit conclusion: "As long as other circumstances are equal, the rate of profit falls or rises in the opposite direction to the price of the raw material." This is the chapter's central theorem — an inverse relationship between raw-material price and the rate of profit, holding s, v, and their ratio fixed.

Let us write the mathematics out fully, since the OCR text is partly garbled here. Let:

  • s = surplus-value (assumed constant in this thought-experiment)
  • v = variable capital (assumed constant)
  • c = constant capital, of which raw material is a major part
  • C = c + v = total capital advanced
  • p′ = s/C = s/(c + v) = the rate of profit

Now suppose the price of raw material falls by d (where c > d > 0). Then c → c − d, so:

p′₁ = s / [(c − d) + v] > s / (c + v) = p′₀

The rate of profit rises. Conversely, if the price of raw material rises by d:

p′₂ = s / [(c + d) + v] < s / (c + v) = p′₀

The rate of profit falls. Hence d(p′)/d(c) < 0: p′ is a decreasing function of the price of constant capital, and since raw material is a major component of c, p′ moves inversely to raw-material prices. (The OCR's statement that "the rate of profit falls" when raw material price falls is a transcription error; the surrounding sentence and the entire logic of the chapter make the correct direction unambiguous.)

Marx draws three important implications from this theorem immediately:

  1. The importance of cheap raw materials for industrial countries, "even if variations in raw material prices were not accompanied by fluctuations in the product's orbit of sale, i.e. quite apart from the relationship between demand and supply." This detaches the argument from any supply-and-demand apparatus: the effect operates through the value-composition of capital, prior to any market-clearing adjustment.

  2. How foreign trade influences the rate of profit — "irrespective of any effect that it has on wages by cheapening the necessary means of subsistence." This is a decisive point. The classical argument for free trade (and Ricardo's) was that cheapening wage-goods cheapens labour-power, raises s/v, and raises profit. Marx adds a second, independent channel: foreign trade slightly affects "the prices of the raw or ancillary materials used in industry and agriculture," and through them it moves the rate of profit. The two channels are analytically distinct, and the second had been missed.

  3. A genealogy of theoretical error. "The fact that any understanding of the rate of profit and its specific difference from the rate of surplus-value has been so completely lacking" explains why (a) economists who emphasise the influence of raw material prices (Torrens) "give this a quite false theoretical explanation," while (b) economists who hold firmly to general principles (Ricardo) "fail to recognise the influence of such things as world trade on the profit rate." Marx here is doing what he does throughout Volume 3: correcting both an empirical school that intuits the phenomenon without theory and a theoretical school that loses the phenomenon in its principles. The footnote directs us to Torrens's An Essay on the Production of Wealth (1821), Ricardo's Principles, Chapter VI ("On Profits"), and Marx's own Theories of Surplus-Value, Part III, Chapter XX, I.b., pp. 71–9.

2.4 Policy Corollary: Corn Laws, Cotton Duties, and the Ten Hours Bill

The theorem yields an immediate political-economic corollary: "how important for industry is the abolition or reduction of import duties on raw materials." Marx notes that even the "more rational" protectionists accepted letting raw materials in freely, and that the English Free-Traders, alongside the repeal of the Corn Laws, made the abolition of the cotton duty "a main preoccupation." He then produces an empirical illustration of the ancillary-material case using a material that is also a foodstuff: flour used in the cotton industry for smoothing the warp.

R. H. Greg's 1837 calculation (The Factory Question and the Ten Hours Bill, p. 115) is reported: Britain's 100,000 power-looms and 250,000 hand-looms consumed some 41 million pounds of flour annually for warp-sizing, plus a further third of that amount for bleaching and other processes. Greg puts the total value of flour so consumed at £342,000 per year over the preceding ten years. Comparison with Continental flour prices showed that the higher flour price forced on factory-owners by the corn duties amounted to £170,000 per year alone; for 1837 Greg estimates it at "at least £200,000," and cites one single firm for which the excess price was £1,000 a year. The consequence, quoted from the Factory Inspectors' Reports (31 October 1848, p. 98): "great manufacturers, thoughtful, calculating men of business, have said that ten hours' labour would be quite sufficient, if the Corn Laws were repealed."

The point is that a foodstuff functioning as an ancillary material demonstrates the indifference-of-sphere argument in practice: flour mattered to cotton profit not (or not only) because it fed workers, but because it sized their warps. And the political sequel is grim and instructive: "The Corn Laws were repealed, and the duties on cotton and other raw materials abolished as well. But scarcely had this been achieved when the factory-owners' opposition to the Ten Hours Bill became more violent than ever." When the Ten Hours Bill nonetheless became law, "its first effect was an attempt at a general reduction of wages." This is Marx at his most dialectical: the removal of a raw-material tax relieves the profit rate through c; the capitalists then turn with renewed ferocity against the working-day limitation, because the other determinant of p′ — the rate of surplus-value — has now become the only one they can attack. The footnote cross-references Capital, Volume 1, pp. 395 ff., where Marx treats the post-Corn-Law wage offensives.

2.5 The Asymmetry Between Raw Material and Fixed Capital: A Crucial Structural Distinction

Marx now draws a distinction that structures much of the chapter's later empirical reasoning:

"The value of the raw and ancillary materials goes at a single stroke into the value of the product for which they are used, while the value of the elements of fixed capital goes in only to the extent of their depreciation, and thus only gradually."

Therefore the price of the product is affected to a much higher degree by the price of raw material than by that of fixed capital. But — and this is the sharp point — "the rate of profit is determined by the total value of the capital applied, irrespective of how much of this is consumed or not." So we get a double asymmetry:

  • On the product price side: raw materials transmit their value wholly and immediately; fixed capital only transfers a fragment (depreciation) per turnover.
  • On the profit rate side: the denominator C counts the entire value of capital applied, consumed or not.

This yields a further elaboration: since commodities are assumed (for now) to be sold at their values, "the expansion or contraction of the market depends on the price of the individual commodity and stands in an inverse relationship to the rise or fall in this price." Hence a rise in raw-material price does not cause the manufactured product's price to rise in the same proportion, nor does a fall cause an equal fall. Consequently "the rate of profit thus falls more sharply in the one case, and rises more sharply in the other, than would be the case if commodities were sold at their values." In other words, the market-price dynamics amplify the pure value-composition effect in a particular direction: the rate of profit is more volatile than the value analysis alone would suggest.

This is a remarkable anticipation of themes later developed under "counteracting influences" to the tendency of the profit rate to fall, and it foreshadows the role of demand elasticity in transmitting raw-material shocks.

2.6 The Growing Weight of Raw Material in the Value of the Product

Marx then develops a long-run compositional argument. As the productivity of labour develops, the size and value of the machines employed grows, "but not in the same proportion as this productivity itself, i.e. the proportion to which these machines supply an increased product." In any branch that uses raw materials — "wherever the object of labour is already the product of earlier labour" — increasing productivity expresses itself in a greater mass of raw material absorbed by a given amount of labour: "the increasing mass of raw material that is transformed into products ... in an hour, for example."

The consequence is a relative shift in the value-composition of the commodity:

  • The raw material component grows, because it enters "as a whole," and
  • The parts formed by machine depreciation and by newly added labour both "constantly decline" in each aliquot part of the product.

This relative growth of the raw-material component occurs provided that it is not cancelled out by a corresponding decline in the raw material's value arising from the increasing productivity of the labour applied in its own creation. This caveat is important: Marx is not predicting that raw material necessarily becomes an ever-larger share of value — productivity gains in raw-material production itself may cheapen it. But the use-value mass of raw material per unit of labour grows inexorably, and this is what exposes the system to organic supply constraints (developed later).

2.7 Circulating Replacement, Waste, and the Interruption of Reproduction

A further consequence follows from the fact that raw and ancillary materials "just like wages, form components of the circulating capital and must therefore be constantly replaced out of each sale of the product," whereas "as far as the machine is concerned it is only the depreciation that has to be replaced and at first only in the form of a reserve fund." Marx notes a subtlety: it is "in no way so essential that each individual sale should contribute its part to this reserve fund, as long as we assume that the year's sale as a whole provides its annual share."

From this, "we see here again how a rise in the price of raw material can cut back or inhibit the entire reproduction process, since the price obtained by the commodity's sale no longer suffices to replace all of its elements; or it makes it impossible to continue the process on a scale that corresponds with its technical basis, so that either only a section of the machinery is being used, or the whole machinery cannot work for the full customary time." This is the reproduction-interruption mechanism: raw materials must be replaced in kind and in full out of each sale, whereas fixed capital only requires an annual bookkeeping provision. A sharp raw-material price spike can therefore generate a physical shortfall of circulating capital — and thus short-time working, idle machinery, and crisis — even when the enterprise is profitable on paper.

The waste issue exemplifies this. "The costs resulting from waste ... vary in direct proportion to the fluctuations in the price of the raw material, rising when this rises and falling when it falls." From the Reports (30 April 1850, p. 17): when cotton advances, the spinner's cost rises "in a ratio beyond the advance actually paid, because the waste made in spinning coarse yarns is fully 15 per cent; and this rate, while it causes a loss of ¾d. per lb. on cotton at 3½d. per lb., brings up the loss to 1d. per lb. when cotton advances to 7d." During the American Civil War (Reports, October 1863, p. 106): with Surat cotton the waste is 25 per cent, so "the cost of the cotton to the spinner is enhanced one-fourth before he has manufactured it," and when cotton costs 2s. per lb., the waste loss equals 6d. per lb. Engels' editorial footnote 14 corrects the arithmetic — the loss should be 3d. rather than 6d., the percentage having been misapplied — but affirms that the proportion of waste was often significantly higher than before on American cotton shipped to Europe during the war's later years.

The waste analysis is doubly important. First, it shows that a rise in raw-material price is not a linear cost increase: waste-losses magnify the effective burden. Second, it demonstrates how the technical quality of the raw material — a use-value property — becomes an economic determinant of the profit rate, another instance of the use-value/value dialectic that runs through the chapter.

2.8 Definitions: Revaluation/Devaluation, Release/Tying-up

Marx transitions to Section 2 with an honest statement of scope: "The phenomena under investigation in this chapter assume for their full development the credit system and competition on the world market, the latter being the very basis and living atmosphere of the capitalist mode of production." Since those "concrete forms" can be "comprehensively depicted only after the general nature of capital is understood," they are deferred to "a possible continuation" (see p. 426). Nonetheless, the phenomena can "still be discussed here in broad lines," because they are inter-related with the rate and mass of profit and because they create the appearance that not only the rate but also the mass of profit can move independently of surplus-value.

Then the definitions:

  • Revaluation and devaluation are "self-explanatory": "the capital present increases or decreases in value as the result of certain general economic conditions (since what is involved here is not the particular fate of one single private capital), i.e. that the value of the capital advanced to production rises or falls independently of its valorization by the surplus labour it employs." The key phrase is general economic conditions — this is a systemic, not idiosyncratic, phenomenon.

  • Tying-up of capital: "out of the total value of the product, a certain additional proportion must be transformed back into the elements of constant or variable capital, if production is to continue on its old scale."

  • Release of capital: "a part of the product's total value which previously had to be transformed back into either constant or variable capital becomes superfluous for the continuation of production on the old scale and is now available for other purposes."

Crucially, Marx distinguishes the release/tying-up of capital from that of revenue. If annual surplus-value on a capital C = x, a cheapening of consumption goods may make x − a sufficient to procure the same satisfactions as before; then "a portion of the capitalist's revenue = a is thus set free" and can expand consumption or be accumulated. Conversely, if x + a is needed, expenditure must be restricted or previously accumulated income spent as revenue. This distinction is essential: revenue-release is about the capitalist's consumption; capital-release is about the funds available to continue and expand production. Confusing them would produce a category error, and Marx's insistence here shows how carefully he is building the analytical scaffolding for later chapters on credit and crises.

He then sets out the possible loci of revaluation/devaluation: it may affect constant or variable capital or both; and within constant capital, fixed or circulating (raw material) or both.

2.9 The "Unconditionally Correct" Law — and Its Limits for Already-Functioning Capital

Marx restates the theorem from Section 1: "with other things being equal, the rate of profit varies inversely as the value of the raw material." He adds the qualification: "This law is unconditionally correct for capital that is newly engaged in a certain business and where the investment of capital, the transformation of money into productive capital, takes place for the first time."

But for already functioning capital, the situation is more complex. This is because only a portion of capital is in the sphere of production; a large part is in the sphere of circulation, distributed as:

  1. Commodity capital on the market, awaiting transformation into money;
  2. Money capital, awaiting transformation back into the conditions of production;
  3. Capital within the sphere of production, partly as means of production (raw material, ancillary material, semi-finished articles, machinery, fixed capital), partly as products still in the course of completion.

The effect of a price change "depends here very largely on the respective proportions of these components." Simplifying by leaving fixed capital out of account, Marx considers the part of constant capital consisting of raw and ancillary materials plus commodities in preparation and finished form on the market.

The key mechanism is the retrospective revaluation of stocks. If cotton rises in price, "the price of cotton goods rises as well: both semi-finished goods such as yarn, and finished products such as cloth, etc." And "cotton that has not yet been worked up, but is still in the warehouse, rises just as much in value as cotton that is in the course of manufacture." Why? Because "as the retrospective expression of more labour-time, this cotton adds a higher value to the product which it goes into as a component than it possessed originally and the capitalist paid for it."

Consequently, if a raw-material price rise occurs when there are significant stocks of finished goods on the market, "the value of these commodities rises and there is a corresponding increase in the value of the capital involved. The same applies to stocks of raw material, etc. in the hands of the producers." This revaluation can compensate the individual capitalist or even a whole sphere "— even more than compensate, perhaps — for the fall in the rate of profit that follows from the raw material's rise in price."

But competition complicates the picture (noted "for the sake of completeness"): 1. Substantial stocks of raw material in the warehouse "counteract the price increase arising from the conditions of their production" (i.e., the price rise at source is dampened by existing inventory); 2. If semi-finished or finished goods on the market "press heavily on the supply," they may prevent the price of these goods from rising in proportion to the price of their raw material.

The reverse holds for a fall in raw-material prices: commodities on the market, goods in preparation, and raw-material stocks are all devalued, "and this counteracts the simultaneous rise in the rate of profit." The smaller the stocks at the end of the business year — "at the time when raw materials are supplied afresh on a massive scale (or, in the case of agricultural production, after the harvest)" — the more visible the effect of a raw-material price change.

Marx then makes an important methodological concession: "Our whole investigation has proceeded from the assumption that any rise or fall in prices is an expression of real fluctuations in value. But since we are dealing here with the effect that these price fluctuations have on the profit rate, it is actually a matter of indifference what their basis might be. The present argument is just as valid if prices rise or fall not as a result of fluctuations in value, but rather as a result of the intervention of the credit system, competition, etc." The theorem is robust to the underlying cause of the price change — a point of enormous importance for extending the analysis to the credit system later.

A crucial accounting paradox then appears: "Since the rate of profit is equal to the proportionate excess in the value of the product over the value of the total capital advanced, an increase in the rate of profit that arose from a devaluation of the capital advanced would involve a loss in capital value, while a decline in the profit rate that arose from a rise in value of the capital advanced could well involve a gain." This is a decisive insight: the rate of profit and the value of capital can move in opposite directions such that appearances deceive. A capitalist whose capital is devalued may enjoy a higher p′ while being poorer in value terms; one whose capital is revalued may suffer a lower p′ while becoming richer. This is the seed of the "illusory" appearance of profit movements independent of surplus-value.

2.10 Devaluation of Fixed Capital: Moral Depreciation and Second Owners

For machinery and fixed capital generally, Marx says, the revaluation "that takes place here and particularly affects buildings, land, etc. cannot be explained without the theory of ground-rent and thus does not belong here." However, he identifies two general points relevant to devaluation:

(1) Technological obsolescence: "The constant improvements which rob existing machinery, factories, etc. of a part of their use-value, and therefore also their exchange-value." This is particularly significant "at times when new machinery is first introduced, before it has reached a certain degree of maturity, and where it thus constantly becomes outmoded before it has had time to reproduce its value." This is "one of the reasons for the unlimited extension of working hours that is usual in periods of this kind, work based on alternating day and night shifts, so that the value of the machines is reproduced without too great costs having to be borne for wear and tear." If the short working life of the machines were not counter-balanced this way, "they would transfer too great a portion of their value to the product in the way of moral depreciation and would not even be able to compete with handicraft production." The footnote (15) cites Babbage for examples and notes the "customary expedient — reduction of wages — was applied here too," so that this constant devaluation has a completely different effect from the "harmony" that Mr Carey dreams of.

The concept of moral depreciation (moralischer Verschleiss) — the footnote explains the term, referring to Capital Volume 2, pp. 250, 264 — is here extended: it is the loss of value of fixed capital through obsolescence or through cheaper reproduction, and it forces capitalists into intensified exploitation of labour (longer hours, wage cuts) to compensate.

(2) Cheaper reproduction: Once machines have matured so that their basic construction remains unchanged, "a further devaluation takes place as a result of improvements in the methods of reproduction of this fixed capital." The value of machines falls "not because they are quickly supplanted ... by newer, more productive machines, but because they can now be reproduced more cheaply." Hence: "large enterprises often flourish only under their second owners, after the first have gone bankrupt. The second owner, by buying them cheaply, starts production with a smaller outlay of capital."

Marx adds that this is "particularly apparent in the case of agriculture how the same causes that raise or lower the price of the product also raise or lower the value of the capital, since this consists to a large extent of that product itself, e.g. corn or cattle. (Ricardo.)" The footnote cites Ricardo's Principles, Chapter II, "On Rent." This is the agricultural special case: because agricultural capital largely consists of the very products it produces (seed, livestock, fodder), a change in the product price is simultaneously a change in the value of the capital. This is a profound point for understanding why agriculture behaves differently in the fluctuations of the profit rate — and it connects to the chapter's later argument that capitalist agriculture is inherently unstable.

2.11 Variable Capital: The Release and Tying-up Mechanism with Numerical Examples

Marx now turns to variable capital. "In as much as the value of labour-power rises because the value of the means of subsistence required for its reproduction rises, or conversely falls because the value of these means of subsistence falls (and a revaluation or devaluation of the variable capital can mean nothing more than these two cases), and assuming that the working day remains constant, a revaluation of this kind means a fall in surplus-value and a devaluation means a rise."

But the key new phenomenon is release and tying-up. If wages fall owing to a fall in the value of labour-power (possibly associated with a rise in the actual price of labour), "a portion of the capital previously laid out on wages is set free. There is a release of variable capital."

For newly invested capital, the effect is simply an increased rate of surplus-value. "The same quantity of labour is set in motion with less money than before, and in this way the unpaid portion of labour is increased at the cost of the paid portion."

For already invested capital, however, there is a dual effect: not only does the rate of surplus-value increase, but "a portion of the capital previously laid out on wages is set free." It was "formerly tied up and formed a portion constantly deducted from the proceeds of production, a portion which was laid out on wages and had to function as variable capital if the business was to proceed on the old scale. This portion now becomes available and can be used for new capital investment, whether to extend the same business or to function in another sphere of production."

Numerical Example 1 — Wage Fall (Release of Variable Capital)

Originally £500 sets 500 workers in motion for a week; now only £400 is required. Mass of value produced = £1,000 in each case.

  • Before: s = £1,000 − £500 = £500; s′ = 500/500 = 100%.
  • After: s = £1,000 − £400 = £600; s′ = 600/400 = 150%.

So the rate of surplus-value rises from 100% to 150%, and £100 of variable capital is set free. For a newly-opened business in that sphere with v = £400 and corresponding c, the only effect is the higher s′. For an already functioning business, "not only has the mass of surplus-value risen from £500 to £600 and the rate of surplus-value from 100 to 150 per cent ... apart from this, £100 of variable capital has been set free, and this is now available to exploit more labour. Not only is the same amount of labour exploited more profitably, but the release of £100 enables the same variable capital of £500 to exploit more workers than before at the higher rate."

Numerical Example 2 — Wage Rise (Tying-up of Variable Capital)

Take the original division with 500 workers employed: 400v + 600s = 1,000; s′ = 150%. The worker receives a weekly wage of £1/1.25? Let us read carefully: "The worker thus receives a weekly wage of £1- = 16 shillings." With v = £400 and 500 workers, each wage = £400/500 = £0.8 = 16 shillings. Good.

Now if these 500 workers cost £500 per week (weekly wage rises to £1 each), and £400 can only set 400 workers in motion. If we keep 500 workers employed: 500v + 500s = 1,000, so s′ falls from 150% to 100% (a fall by one-third).

For newly invested capital: only effect is a lower s′ and, other things equal, a lower p′ (not proportionately).

With c = 2,000: - First case: 2,000c + 400v + 600s = 3,000; s′ = 150%; p′ = 600/2,400 = 25%. - Second case: 2,000c + 500v + 500s = 3,000; s′ = 100%; p′ = 500/2,500 = 20%.

For already operating capital, the effect is dual: - With £400 variable capital, only 400 workers are employed, producing (at s′ = 100%) £400 of surplus-value. - Since £2,000 constant capital requires 500 workers to be set in motion, 400 workers only set in motion £1,600 constant capital. - To continue on the former scale and prevent a fifth of the machinery from halting, v must be increased by £100. - This is possible "only because capital that was formerly available is now tied up, in that part of the accumulation fund designed to expand the business now serves simply to fill the gap, or, alternatively, a portion designed to be spent as revenue is added to the original capital." - With a £100 increase in the outlay of variable capital, £100 less surplus-value is produced. "More capital is needed to set the same number of workers in motion, and at the same time the surplus-value that each of these individual workers supplies is reduced."

The general law: "The advantages that arise from the release of variable capital, and the disadvantages that arise from its being tied up, both exist only for capital that is already in operation and thus reproduces itself in given conditions. For capital that is to be newly invested, the advantage or disadvantage is in each case confined to this: there will occur a rise or fall in the rate of surplus-value and a corresponding if not proportionate change in the rate of profit."

This distinction — between new and already functioning capital — is one of the most important methodological contributions of the chapter. It explains why the same underlying change (e.g., a wage-good cheapening) has genuinely different effects on aggregate profit depending on whether it is realised as a higher exploitation rate on new investments or as a release of funds for existing firms. It shows the profit rate is path-dependent in a way that pure ratio analysis (p′ = s/(c+v)) conceals.

2.12 Productivity-Driven Release and Tying-up (Beyond Revaluation)

Marx adds a second source of release/tying-up, independent of revaluation: "Variable capital can also be set free if the development of productivity leads to a reduction in the number of workers required to set the same amount of constant capital in motion, with the rate of wages remaining the same." Conversely, "additional variable capital may be tied up if more workers are required for the same amount of constant capital, owing to a decline in the productivity of labour."

However, "If a portion of the capital earlier applied as variable capital is now applied in the form of constant capital, however, i.e. if there is only a different distribution of the component elements of the same capital, then although this certainly has an influence on the rate of surplus-value and the rate of profit, it does not come under the heading of the tying-up and release of capital that we are considering here." This is a definitional boundary: release/tying-up concerns changes in the total capital required, not intra-capital redistribution.

For constant capital, Marx notes parallel mechanisms: - Constant capital can be tied up or released by revaluation of its material elements. - Otherwise, constant capital can be tied up (without variable capital being transformed into it) only if productivity increases, so that the same labour produces a larger product and sets more constant capital in motion. - The same can happen if productivity declines (e.g., in agriculture, where the same labour needs more seed, fertilizer, drainage to produce the same product). - Constant capital can be released without devaluation "if improvements, the harnessing of natural forces, etc. place a constant capital of lesser value in a position technically to perform the same service as one of higher value did earlier."

This taxonomy completes the machinery of release and tying-up. Marx then returns to the raw-material-specific danger: "We saw in Volume 2 how, after commodities are transformed into money, are sold, a definite portion of this money must be transformed back into the material elements of constant capital, and moreover in the proportions that are required by the specific technical character of the sphere of production in question." Ignoring wages, "the most important element in all branches of production is raw material, including the ancillary materials that are particularly important in branches of production which do not involve any raw material proper, as with mining and the extractive industries in general."

The contrast between raw material and wear-and-tear is again drawn: the depreciation portion "enters the account more in an ideal sense, as long as the machinery is still at all serviceable; it does not very much matter whether it is paid for and converted into money today or tomorrow, or at any particular point in the capital's turnover time." But with raw material, "if its price rises, it may be impossible to replace it completely after deducting wages from the value of the commodity. Violent fluctuations in price thus lead to interruptions, major upsets and even catastrophes in the reproduction process."

2.13 The Organic Asymmetry: Why Agricultural Raw Materials Are Chronically Volatile

Marx now provides the natural-material foundation for the whole chapter's catastrophism. Agricultural products, "whose raw materials derive from organic nature," are "most subject to these fluctuations in value, as a result of variations in the harvest, etc. (Quite apart from the impact of the credit system.)"

Two elements are identified:

(1) The use-value/value elasticity of organic products. "The same quantity of labour may here be expressed in very diverse amounts of use-values, depending on uncontrollable natural conditions, the seasons of the year, etc., and a particular quantity of these use-values will accordingly have very different prices." Marx gives a clean algebraic illustration: "If a value x is expressed in 100 lb. of a commodity a, the price of 1 lb. of a is x/100; if it is expressed in 1,000 lb. of a, the price of 1 lb. is x/1,000; and so on." Because labour can be embodied in wildly varying use-value quantities, the unit price of organic raw materials is intrinsically volatile.

(2) The asymmetric elasticity of supply. "In the nature of the case, plant and animal products, whose growth and production are subject to certain organic laws involving naturally determined periods of time, cannot suddenly be increased in the same degree as, say, machines and other fixed capital, coal, ore, etc., which, assuming the requisite natural conditions, can be significantly increased in a very short period in an industrially developed country." Therefore, "it is possible, therefore, and indeed unavoidable when capitalist production is fully developed, that the production and increase of the portion of constant capital that consists of fixed capital, machinery, etc. may run significantly ahead of the portion consisting of organic raw materials, so that the demand for these raw materials grows more rapidly than their supply, and their price therefore rises."

This is one of the most important structural arguments in all of Volume 3. Machines, coal, and ore can be expanded almost elastically in the short run; organic raw materials cannot, because they obey biological clocks (a year for many crops, longer for livestock). Since fixed capital tends to grow faster than organic raw material supply in a rapidly accumulating economy, there is a built-in tendential scarcity and price inflation of raw materials.

The consequences of the resulting price rise are threefold:

  1. Spatial extension of supply: "these raw materials are supplied from a greater distance, since the rise in their price can meet greater costs of transport";
  2. Temporal expansion of production: "their production is expanded, though by the nature of things the volume of products can only increase a year later";
  3. Substitution and economy: "all kinds of surrogates are now employed that were previously unused, and more economical use is made of waste products."

But here the cycle turns. "When the price rise begins to have a marked effect on the expansion of production and supply, the turning-point has generally been already reached, at which demand falls as a consequence of the continuing increase in the price of the raw material and of all commodities it enters into as an element, bringing about a reaction in its turn on the raw material's price." This is a hog-cycle / cobweb dynamic avant la lettre. The demand-side collapse and the supply-side expansion arrive simultaneously, and the price is violently reversed. "Apart from the convulsions that achieve this effect by devaluing capital in various ways, still other circumstances come into play."

The consequence is a "regularly" recurring cycle of raw-material price inflation and collapse:

  • The more capitalist production develops, "bringing with it greater means for a sudden and uninterrupted increase in the portion of the constant capital that consists of machinery, etc., and the more rapid the accumulation (particularly in times of prosperity), the greater is the relative overproduction of machinery and other fixed capital, the more frequent the relative overproduction of plant and animal raw materials, and the more marked the previously described rise in their price and the corresponding reaction. The more frequent, therefore, are those revulsions which have their basis in this violent price fluctuation, and are a major element in the reproduction process."

There is a beautiful irony here: the more developed capitalist production is, the more frequent and severe are these raw-material crises. Industrial progress intensifies rather than mitigates the turbulence, because it accelerates fixed-capital accumulation relative to the inelastic supply of organic raw materials.

2.14 The Collapse Phase, the Monopoly Re-established, and the Futility of Regulation

When high prices collapse — because the rise provoked demand decline, supply expansion, and distant-region supply — the result is analysed in several aspects:

  1. "The sudden collapse in the price of raw materials places shackles on their reproduction, and in this way the monopoly of the original supplying countries, which produce in favourable conditions, is re-established — perhaps with certain limitations, but re-established anyhow."
  2. "The impulse that was given may indeed cause the reproduction of the raw materials to proceed on an expanded scale, particularly in those countries that more or less possess a monopoly in this production."
  3. "But the basis on which production proceeds as a result of the expanded machinery, etc. and which must now prevail as the new normal basis, after a few fluctuations, has been very much expanded by the events of the previous turnover cycle."
  4. "Among some of the secondary sources of supply, however, the reproduction that has at first increased will have again experienced a significant restriction."

The evidence: "The export tables readily show how during the last thirty years (up to 1865) Indian cotton production has risen whenever there has been a shortfall in American production and then suddenly contracted more or less seriously."

In periods of high raw-material prices, "the industrial capitalists get together and form associations to regulate production" — e.g., Manchester in 1848 after cotton prices rose, and Irish flax production. But: "As soon as the immediate impulse has gone by and the general principle of competition ('buying in the cheapest market') reigns sovereign once more, instead of promoting productive capacity in suitable countries of origin, which these associations set out to do, irrespective of the immediate momentary price at which these countries can supply the product, it is left once more to 'prices' to regulate supply."

Marx's verdict is categorical:

"All ideas of a common, all-embracing and far-sighted control over the production of raw materials — a control that is in fact incompatible, by and large, with the laws of capitalist production, and hence remains forever a pious wish, or is at most confined to exceptional common steps in moments of great and pressing danger and perplexity — all such ideas give way to the belief that supply and demand will mutually regulate one another."

Engels' editorial footnote 16 (added after 1865) extends this: "Since the above was written (1865), competition on the world market has increased significantly owing to the rapid development of industry in all civilized countries ... The fact that the modern productive forces ... are daily and increasingly outgrowing the laws of capitalist commodity exchange within which they are supposed to move — this fact impresses itself more and more today even on the consciousness of the capitalists." Two symptoms: (1) "the new mania for general protective tariffs, differing from the old protectionism because they are precisely designed to protect exportable articles"; and (2) "the cartels (trusts) formed by manufacturers in whole branches of production for the regulation of production and therewith prices and profits too." Engels' verdict: "these experiments can be pursued only in a relatively favourable economic climate. The first storm is bound to bowl them over and show how, much as production does need regulating, it is certainly not the capitalist class that is called to this task. In the meantime, the only purpose these cartels serve to promote is the swallowing of the little fish by the big fish even more rapidly than before."

This footnote is a hinge between Marx's text and later developments (trusts, cartels, protective tariffs), and it transforms the chapter's structural pessimism into a historical prediction: capitalist attempts at regulation are both necessary and doomed.

Marx concludes the theoretical section with the observation that "the capitalists' superstition on this matter is so crude that even the factory inspectors pass astonished remarks on it time and again in their reports." The alternation of good and bad years brings cheaper raw materials round again. The extension of demand, and the stimulating effect on the profit rate, then set the stage for "the process depicted above, with the production of raw materials being gradually overtaken again by the production of machines, etc., is then repeated once more on a larger scale."

The point about Indian cotton ("American-quality cotton from India") is that a real improvement in quality would require "a regular and steady rise in European demand over a long period (quite apart from the economic conditions to which Indian production is subject in its own country)." Instead, "the production of raw materials is thus expanded only in sudden jerks, before being violently contracted once more." The cotton famine of 1861–5 is the exemplar: "a situation in which a raw material that is one of the most essential elements of reproduction was quite lacking for a time."

Finally: "Prices can also rise in a situation of full supply, if this is full only under difficult conditions. Alternatively there may be a genuine lack of raw material. In the cotton crisis, we had originally the latter case."

2.15 The Moral of the Tale: Capitalist Agriculture as Contradiction

Marx draws the theoretical section to a close with a sweeping claim: "The more we look back at the history of production in the most recent period, the more regularly we find, particularly in the key branches of industry, a constantly repeated alternation between relative price increase and a subsequent depreciation of raw materials supplied by organic nature that arises from this." The Factory Inspectorate reports illustrate the argument.

Then the moral, which is also a political conclusion: "The moral of the tale, which can also be extracted from other discussions of agriculture, is that the capitalist system runs counter to a rational agriculture, or that a rational agriculture is incompatible with the capitalist system (even if the latter promotes technical development in agriculture) and needs either small farmers working for themselves or the control of the associated producers."

This is one of the most compact and radical statements in Volume 3: the very structure of capitalist agriculture — with its inelastic organic production cycles, its speculative price dynamics, its subordination of land to profit — is incompatible with rational agriculture. The resolution would require either independent small farmers or "the control of the associated producers" — a direct bridge to the socialist programme.

2.16 The Empirical Illustration: Prehistory 1845–60

The remainder of the chapter is documentation — precisely the material Marx promised to include. He treats the cotton crisis of 1861–5 as "the biggest example of an interruption in the production process caused by a lack of raw material and an increase in its price." The prehistory serves to show that the underlying dynamic was already in play long before the American Civil War.

1845 — high tide. Leonard Horner reports "a more active state of trade ... particularly in cotton spinning" than in eight years. New mills, enlargements, new engines, new machinery.

1846 — complaints begin. "For a considerable time past I have heard from the occupiers of cotton-mills very general complaints of the depressed state of their trade ... several mills have begun to work short time, usually eight hours a day instead of twelve." The cause was a great advance in raw-material prices without a corresponding advance in manufactured articles — indeed, manufactured prices were lower than before the cotton rise. Because of the previous four years' mill expansion, there was simultaneously a greater demand for raw material and a greater supply of manufactured articles on the market, "causes that must concurrently have operated against profits." Bradford had 490 mills in 1846 against 318 in 1836; flax-spinning was particularly affected. "All have contributed more or less, during the last ten years, to the overstocking of the market."

1847 — monetary crisis. October: bank rate at 8%; railway bubble collapse; speculation in East Indian bills. Baker's analysis: increased demand for cotton, wool, and flax coincided with harvests below average. Cotton rose from 4d. to 6d. per lb., while twist was only a fraction above its former price. The woollen trade in Yorkshire increased 40% since 1836; worsted increased 74%; flax 25% in England, 22% in Scotland, nearly 90% in Ireland. Raw material rose £10 per ton while yarn fell 6d. a bundle.

1849–50 — recovery, then new strains. Flax cheap; wool cheap and well-bought; worsted flourishing. But: "Speculation, and uncertainty as to the probable supply of cotton wool, have ever had the effect of causing greater excitement, and more frequent alterations in the state of that branch of manufacture, than any other." In 1850 the increased worsted machinery threatened "a similar reaction"; in 1849 alone worsted looms increased their produce by 40%, spindles by 25–30%. By October 1850, "the high price of raw cotton continues ... to cause a considerable depression in this branch of manufacture."

1853 — extreme prosperity, then overproduction. Horner in April: "At no period during the last seventeen years ... have I known such general prosperity." By October: "Overproduction."

1854 — Crimean War. Diminished supplies of flax and hemp from Russia.

1859 — Scottish flax depression, jute substitution. Raw material scarce and high; one-half of Dundee machinery employed in jute spinning.

1860–61 — the crisis begins.

  • April 1860: "a great scarcity of raw material ... it is ... the want of it alone, which keeps us within bounds. In the cotton trade, the erection of new mills ... can scarcely, I think, have been at any time exceeded. Everywhere there are new movements in search of raw material."
  • October 1860: trade good, "but for the high price of raw material." Flax spinners look to India for supply.
  • April 1861: trade depressed; "Raw material is high. In almost every branch of textile manufacture it is above the price at which it can be manufactured for the masses of the consumers."
  • 1860 was "a year of overproduction in the cotton industry"; "It has taken between two and three years to absorb the overproduction of 1860 in the markets of the world."
  • October 1861: "Trade has been for some time in a very depressed state ... short time must have been kept during the ensuing winter in consequence of the great increase of production during the last three years, and the unsettled state of the Indian and Chinese markets." This is the crucial point: the depression was already developing before the cotton blockade took full effect, because of the previous overproduction and high raw-material prices.

The chapter here includes detailed illustration of the substitution of sizing for cotton during the crisis. A manufacturer writes: "when cotton is high in price, every spinner of ordinary yarns (say up to 40s.) (principally 12s. to 32s.) will raise his counts as much as he can, that is, will spin 16s. where he used to spin 12s ... and the manufacturer using these fine yarns will make his cloth the usual weight by the addition of so much more size." Ordinary export shirting weighing 8 lbs. was made of 5½ lbs. cotton and 2½ lbs. size; in other cloths as much as 50% size was added. The manufacturer "may and does truly boast that he is getting rich by selling cloth for less money per pound than he paid for the mere yarn of which they are composed."

The weavers attributed "increased sickness to the size which is used in dressing the warps of Surat cotton, and which is not made of the same material as formerly, viz., flour." The substitute — ground talc ("China clay") or gypsum ("French chalk") — had "the very important advantage of increasing greatly the weight of the cloth manufactured, making 15 lbs. of the raw material to weigh 20 lbs. when woven into cloth." But it reduced weavers' earnings: "This sizing, which gives weight to the yarn, renders it hard and brittle ... the hard state of the warp causes the threads of the heald to break frequently; and it is said to take a weaver five minutes to tie up the threads every time they break; and a weaver has to piece these ends at least ten times as often as formerly, thus reducing the productive powers of the loom in the working-hours."

This is a striking empirical illustration of Marx's method: a quality degradation of raw material and its substitutes becomes an economic mechanism for transferring value from workers to capitalists — not by changing wage rates, but by intensifying labour time and reducing effective output.

2.17 The Cotton Famine 1861–65: Human and Social Consequences

The narrative of 1861–65 is unrelenting. The strike by power-loom weavers in early 1861 (against a 5–7½% wage reduction, demanding instead a cut in working hours) collapsed after a month, and the workers "suffered both things": wage cuts plus short time.

1862, April: "The sufferings of the operatives since the date of my last report have greatly increased; but at no period of the history of manufactures, have sufferings so sudden and so severe been borne with so much silent resignation and so much patient self-respect." The statistics: in Manchester in May 1848, 15% out of work, 12% short time, 70% full work. On 28 May 1862, 15% out of work, 35% short time, 49% full work.

1862, October: Of 2,887 cotton factories in the UK in 1861, 2,109 in Lancashire and Cheshire. Of these, 392 (19%) under 10 horsepower; 345 (16%) between 10 and 20; 1,372 (65%) 20+. More than a third of the small manufacturers "were operatives themselves at no distant period; they are men without command of capital." The brunt of the burden fell on the remaining two-thirds. Statistics: 40,146 (11.3%) fully employed; 134,767 (38%) short time; 179,721 (50.7%) unemployed. Excluding Manchester and Bolton (which spun finer grades): 8.5% fully employed, 38% short time, 53.5% unemployed.

1863, April: At most one half of cotton operatives would be fully employed. The use of Surat cotton forced great reductions of machinery speed, which hit wages because spinners were paid per pound and weavers per piece. "From enquiries I have made ... there is a diminution averaging 20 per cent upon their former earnings, in some instances the diminution has been as much as 50 per cent, calculated upon the same rate of wages as prevailed in 1861."

1863, October: A more healthy state, machinery improved, material better understood. But appalling figures: two young women returned to sewing school unable to earn 1s. per week; self-acting minders earning 8s. 11d. after a fortnight's full work (from which rent was deducted, though the manufacturer returned half as a gift — "How generous!"), taking away 6s. 11d.; minders ranging 5s.–9s., weavers 2s.–6s. in the last months of 1862. Causes: shorter staple of Surat cotton, dirty condition, mixing with waste, threads more liable to break, weavers can only mind one or two looms, direct wage reductions of 5, 7½, and 10%, fines for flaws.

The chapter's moral commentary becomes biting: "Wages were wretched enough even with full-time working. The cotton workers willingly volunteered for all the public works they could be employed in, such as drainage, road-building, stone-breaking and street-paving, so as to get relief (which was in effect a form of relief to the factory-owners; see Volume I, pp. 720–21) from the local authorities. The entire bourgeoisie stood guard over the workers. If starvation wages were offered and a worker was unwilling to accept them, the Relief Committee struck him off the relief list. This was a real golden age for the factory-owning gentlemen, in as much as the workers either starved or had to work at the price most profitable for the bourgeoisie, while the Relief Committees acted as their guard-dogs."

The Public Works Act of 1863 — designed to enable operatives to earn "remunerative wages" as independent labourers — had three purposes: to allow local authorities to borrow from the Exchequer Loan Commissioners; to improve towns; and to provide work and wages. By the end of October 1863, loans of £883,700 had been granted. Work included canal-digging, road-building, street-paving, and reservoir construction. Mr Henderson, President of the Blackburn Relief Committee, praised "the cheerful alacrity with which the unemployed operatives ... have accepted of the work offered." Workers were entitled to 4–12 shillings per week, sometimes for a family of eight. Municipal authorities received "money for improving their smoky and neglected towns at exceptionally low rates of interest" and "paid the workers far below the regular wage-rates." Marx describes it as "this new edition of the Ateliers nationaux of 1848, but this time set up for the advantage of the bourgeoisie" (footnote: the original National Workshops of February 1848, ostensibly a working-class demand).

The famous passage on experiments "in corpore vili" (on a worthless body): "The operatives are subject to great fluctuation, from the constant experimentalizing of the manufacturers upon different kinds and proportions of cotton and waste in the same mill, the 'mixings' as it is called being frequently changed; and the earnings of the operatives rise and fall with the quality of the cotton mixings." Wages: A, weaver, family of six, 4 days: 6s. 8½d.; B, twister, 4½ days: 6s.; C, weaver, family of four, 5 days: 5s. 1d.; D, slubber, family of six, 4 days: 7s. 10d.; E, weaver, family of seven, 3 days: 5s. "Work would become a misfortune in many a family, as it not merely reduces the income, but brings it so low as to be utterly insufficient to provide more than a small portion of the absolute wants, were it not that supplemental relief is granted."

By 25 March 1863, nearly £3 million had been dispensed by Poor Law authorities, the Central Relief Committee, and the Mansion House Committee. Wage reductions: spinners suffered 15% indirect reduction in one district (South Sea Island → Egyptian cotton); 5% reduction plus 20–30% loss working Surat and waste in another. Weavers reduced from 4 looms to 2; in 1860 they averaged 5s. 7d. per loom; in 1863, only 3s. 4d. Fines rose from 3d.–6d. on American to 1s.–3s. 6d. Mule spinners' average fell from 18s.–25s. (1860) to 10s.–18s.

Rent of houses: Frequently deducted from wages even when short time; house values diminished; cottages that cost 3s. 6d. per week could be had for 2s. 4d., sometimes less.

Emigration: The factory-owners opposed it, "firstly because, looking forward to the recovery of the cotton trade from its present depression, they keep within their reach the means whereby their mills can be worked in the most advantageous manner," and secondly because many were landlords of the workers' houses and expected to recover back rent.

The final line is a devastating rhetorical close: Mr Bernal Osborne said in a speech to his parliamentary electors on 22 October 1864 that the workers of Lancashire had behaved like the ancient philosophers (Stoics). Marx appends: "Not like sheep?"

This closing question crystallises the chapter's double movement: the theoretical theorem (the rate of profit is exposed to raw-material prices) and the political indictment (the whole burden of this exposure falls on the workers, who are the residual absorbents of every shock). The chapter is thus not merely an exercise in the mechanics of p′ but a proof that the abstract category "rate of profit" has a body count behind it.

2.18 Cross-Cutting Threads: Method, Object, and the Chapter's Internal Logic

Three threads weave through every section:

First, the primacy of the value-composition of capital over the apparent "profitability" of a business. Marx repeatedly insists that the rate of profit is determined by the total capital applied, irrespective of whether that capital is consumed. This is why a single raw-material price change can move p′ even when nothing has changed in the exploitation of labour, the productivity of labour in the final production process, or the market for the commodity. The theorem detaches the rate of profit from the visible surface of buying and selling.

Second, the dialectic between use-value and value. Raw material's quality (Surat vs. American cotton, waste percentage, sizing substitutions) has direct economic consequences for the profit rate. Machinery's durability and obsolescence (moral depreciation) affects the capital value. The biological rhythm of organic raw materials conditions their price elasticity and hence the entire cyclical dynamic. Use-value properties, which a purely value-theoretic analysis might abstract from, repeatedly re-enter as determinants.

Third, the asymmetry between the appearance and reality of profit movements. Because capital revaluation/devaluation can move in opposite directions from the rate of profit, and because stocks and competition complicate the transmission of price shocks, the observed movements of the rate or mass of profit can seem to be "independent of movements of surplus-value." The categories of release and tying-up are precisely Marx's tools for demystifying these appearances. They explain why "it appears as if it is not only the rate of profit but also its mass ... can increase and decrease independently."

The chapter, in short, is a demonstration that the capitalist economy has systemic sources of turbulence that are neither class struggle nor technical change in the immediate sense, but the collision of an elastic machine-based production apparatus with the inelastic, biologically constrained production of organic raw materials. That collision is expressed as recurring price crises; those crises are absorbed by the working class through wage cuts, short time, unemployment, and fabricated relief. The reconciliation of profit rate with surplus-value requires the intermediate categories — release, tying-up, revaluation, devaluation — that Marx introduces here.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

3.1 Fundamental Principles

Principle 1: The rate of profit has a denominator, and the denominator has a life of its own. The chapter's most durable lesson is that p′ = s/(c+v) cannot be reduced to a story about exploitation. Whatever raises c — raw-material price spikes, ancillary-material inflation, cheaper-reproduction difficulties, natural resource scarcity — lowers p′ even when s′ is untouched. This explains why the rate of profit is more volatile than the rate of surplus-value, and why it can fall during booms as well as slumps (when raw materials are bidding up).

Principle 2: The rate of profit has material determinants. The theorem is not merely formal. It rests on the biological and geological inelasticity of organic raw materials, on the natural riches (iron, coal, wood) embedded in machinery, and on the different turnover rhythms of circulating and fixed capital. Nature enters the profit rate. This is why the profit rate cannot be understood as a purely social category — it is jointly determined by social relations of production and by the physical properties of the material substratum.

Principle 3: Property-income illusions and crises arise from capital revaluation. The categories of release/tying-up and revaluation/devaluation show that a capitalist can experience a rising profit rate while suffering a capital loss (if capital is devalued), and a falling profit rate while enjoying a capital gain (if capital is revalued). This is a fundamental epistemological warning: profit-rate statistics can misrepresent the underlying valorisation of capital. Any serious analysis of crises must move beneath the surface of p′ to the movements of capital value itself.

Principle 4: The same structural cause generates both expansion and collapse. The cycle Marx describes — fixed capital races ahead, organic supply lags, raw-material prices spike, demand collapses, supply arrives, prices crash, secondary suppliers retrench, monopoly is re-established, then the cycle repeats on a larger scale — is a self-reproducing instability. It is intrinsic, not accidental. Capitalist development intensifies it: "The more capitalist production is developed ... the greater is the relative overproduction of machinery and other fixed capital, the more frequent the relative overproduction of plant and animal raw materials, and the more marked the previously described rise in their price and the corresponding reaction."

Principle 5: Capitalist regulation is structurally impossible. Marx's claim that all-embracing control of raw-material production is a "pious wish" incompatible with capitalist laws anticipates the modern theory of market failure in commodity markets and, indeed, the persistence of commodity price volatility despite cartels, futures markets, and international commodity agreements. Engels' footnote on trusts and tariffs shows the prediction being borne out within his lifetime.

3.2 Broader Theoretical Implications

The chapter has at least five broad theoretical ramifications:

(a) It extends the theory of crisis beyond underconsumption and disproportionality to raw-material supply. Marx here identifies a material source of crisis that is orthogonal to demand failure and to inter-sectoral imbalance: the mismatch between an elastic machine sector and an inelastic organic sector. This is a distinct crisis-theoretic mechanism, sometimes called the "raw-material cycle," and it anticipates later theories of commodity-price crises (e.g., Prebisch-Singer-type dynamics and the "resource curse" literature in a different idiom).

(b) It establishes the ground for the later theory of rent. By noting that natural riches appear "as a natural fruit borne by capital," and that agricultural capital's value is caught up in the price of its own product (Ricardo), Marx is laying the analytical groundwork for the theory of ground-rent and for the analysis of landed property that occupies later chapters of Volume 3.

(c) It distinguishes capital release from revenue release. This distinction, though technical, is essential for understanding the credit system's role later in Volume 3. It explains how credit can appear to create capital ex nihilo (when it is really mobilising released capital), and how crises appear to be "crises of confidence" when they are fundamentally crises of capital revaluation.

(d) It sharpens the critique of political economy. Marx's target is double: the empirical school (Torrens) that sees the phenomenon but cannot explain it, and the theoretical school (Ricardo) that explains markets but misses the phenomenon. This methodological critique — that every genuine science of the profit rate must integrate both the value-composition of capital and the material conditions of its reproduction — is a template for Marx's critique of the classics throughout Volume 3.

(e) It grounds the critique of capitalist agriculture. The claim that "the capitalist system runs counter to a rational agriculture" and that a rational agriculture "needs either small farmers working for themselves or the control of the associated producers" is a direct bridge between economics and political ecology. It anticipates twentieth-century concerns about soil exhaustion, monoculture, and the long-term unsustainability of profit-driven land use.

3.3 Practical, Political, and Philosophical Implications

Practical: The chapter is a manual for understanding industrial cycles. It tells us to look at raw-material inventories, waste rates, harvest prospects, and the ratio of fixed to circulating capital when diagnosing industrial disturbances. It explains why free-trade in raw materials is so important to industrial nations (a lesson as true today as in 1846, for oil, semiconductors, lithium, and rare earths). It explains why the "just-in-time" inventory system, which minimizes stocks, magnifies the volatility of the profit rate in the face of supply shocks — because the chapter explicitly notes that "the smaller the amount of stock ... the more visible the effect of a change in raw material prices."

Political: The chapter's final sections are a sustained indictment. The cotton famine demonstrates that every structural shock to the raw-material sector is transmitted to the working class through wage cuts, short time, unemployment, fines, rent deductions, and the complicity of relief committees. The factory-owners' opposition to emigration and their control of housing reveal the coercive architecture that holds labour in place to bear the burden. The chapter thus shows the class incidence of a phenomenon — the raw-material-supply cycle — that appears in the economic literature as a purely technical problem. Anything that affects p′ affects workers first and hardest.

Philosophical: The chapter is a profound meditation on the relation between value and nature. The profit rate, Marx shows, is not a purely human artefact; it is conditioned by the biological clocks of plants and animals, by the geology of mineral deposits, and by the seasons. Capital tries to subdue nature, but nature returns as a constraint on the valorisation process. The chapter thereby refuses both the pure-social-constructivism of value and the pure-naturalism of resource economics: value is social, but it is materially embedded. The dialectic between the social and the natural runs through every page of the chapter, from the waste rates of Surat cotton to the yield of Indian agriculture to the moral depreciation of machines.

3.4 Final Reflection

Chapter 6 is, at first reading, one of the more technical and empirically cluttered portions of Capital. But beneath the barrage of Factory Inspector quotations lies one of Marx's most subversive arguments: the rate of profit is a social relation that is materially constrained by the rhythms of nature and politically distributed by the coercive power of the class that owns the means of production. The theorem (raw-material prices against profit) is elegant; the empirical illustration (the cotton famine) is devastating; the reconciliation (release/tying-up, revaluation/devaluation) is analytically powerful. Taken together, they show that the "laws of motion" of capitalist society are not a smooth mechanism but a turbulence that periodically devalues capital, throws workers into poverty, resurrects the coercive apparatus of relief and rent, and then restarts — on an expanded scale — the same cycle. The final ironic question — the workers of Lancashire behaved like Stoic philosophers, "not like sheep?" — is Marx's way of saying that the cycle is not natural, not inevitable, and not finished. A rational organisation of production, he has argued throughout, would adjust the production of organic raw materials to the rhythms of both nature and social need; only a system driven by profit periodically destroys what it has built, and then asks its victims to bear the loss.

That is the chapter's enduring lesson: a system that cannot control the supply of its own raw materials cannot rationally control its own future.
