Volume 3 • Chapter 23: Interest and Profit of Enterprise

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Core Problem and Intellectual Objective

Chapter 23 of Capital, Volume 3, occupies the pivotal position within Part V — "The Division of Profit into Interest and Profit of Enterprise." The preceding two chapters had already established, in a formal-analytical sense, that interest is nothing but a portion of the surplus-value (profit) that the functioning capitalist — whether industrialist or merchant — must surrender to the owner-lender of capital whenever the capital he uses is not his own but borrowed. With that foundation laid, Chapter 23 confronts its genuinely difficult question: not the magnitude of the division, but its form. How does a division of profit that is at bottom purely quantitative — a split of one sum into two shares — harden into a qualitative distinction between two apparently different kinds of revenue, each seemingly springing from a different source, governed by different laws, and attached to different "capacities" of capital?

This is the axis on which the entire chapter turns. Marx states the question with surgical precision: "How does this purely quantitative division of profit into net profit and interest turn into a qualitative distinction?" And, more sharply: how does it happen that even the capitalist who works exclusively with his own capital nonetheless comes to class a portion of his gross profit under the special heading of "interest," treating it as though he were paying it to himself — so that all capital, borrowed or not, comes to be distinguished as interest-bearing capital from itself in its function as capital bearing a net profit?

Marx's central thesis is that this "ossification" and "autonomization" of the two parts of gross profit is not a subjective illusion or mere bookkeeping convention. It rests on an objective fact: interest genuinely accrues to the mere owner of capital who stands outside the production process, while profit of enterprise [Unternehmergewinn] genuinely accrues to the merely functioning capitalist who is not the owner. Once this duality of real economic roles exists — once money capitalists face industrial capitalists as two distinct classes with distinct legal titles to the same capital and its yield — the quantitative split converts into a qualitative opposition, and this opposition then generalizes to the entire capitalist class and the total social capital. The whole profit on any capital, and hence the average profit itself, breaks down into two qualitatively different, mutually autonomous parts — interest and profit of enterprise — each obeying its own particular laws.

The Deeper Objective: Unmasking the "Wages of Superintendence" Illusion

Yet the chapter is not merely an exercise in the logic of capital categories. Its second, and polemically more explosive, objective is to dismantle the ideological form that this qualitative division breeds in the mind of the capitalist: the notion that profit of enterprise is really a wage — "wages of superintendence of labour," a higher wage paid for the capitalist's own managerial exertion, complex labour by a self-paying employer. Marx's task here is to demonstrate that this "wage" theory of profit is not a description of economic reality but a fetish generated by the antithetical form the two parts of profit assume vis-à-vis one another. Because interest appears as the fruit of mere ownership (property in capital abstracted from any function), the residual — profit of enterprise — appears as the fruit of function alone, of the capitalist's labour. From there it is a short, ideologically laden step to the claim that the capitalist earns his reward as a worker, indeed as "complex" labour commanding a higher wage. Marx systematically exposes this as a rationalization whose apologetic function is completed by two historical proofs: the slaveholder's use of the identical argument (O'Conor's "just compensation for the labour and talent employed in governing"), and the empirical fact that cooperative factories and joint-stock companies separate managerial wages from profit of enterprise — demonstrating that the capitalist as proprietor is superfluous to production.

Placement within the Broader Theoretical Framework

The chapter belongs to the larger architecture of Volume 3, in which Marx analyzes how surplus-value — the single, undifferentiated social total extracted from living labour — assumes the "transformed forms" of profit, average profit, rent, and interest. Chapter 23 is the hinge between the treatment of credit and interest-bearing capital (Chapters 21–22) and the treatment of the credit system, banking, and the historical genesis of interest-bearing capital (Chapters 24 onward). It presupposes the theory of the rate of profit derived from surplus-value, the equalization of profit rates through competition, and — crucially — the distinction between "capital as property" and "capital as function." It also depends on the historical observation that interest-bearing capital is older than industrial capital: it exists "as a ready-made form handed down" long before the capitalist mode of production, which is precisely why interest so readily appears to the popular mind as "capital as such, capital par excellence." The chapter thereby bridges Marx's analytical theory of profit with his historical and ideological critique of political economy (Massie, Hume, Ramsay, Tooke, Bosanquet, Smith, Ure, Hodgskin, Mill), and sets the stage for understanding how the credit system's later development makes "the capitalist vanish from the production process as someone superfluous."

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 Re-establishing the Premise: Interest as a Share of Profit, and the Functioning Capitalist's Inability to Dispose of Capital

Marx opens by restating the governing premise carried over from the two preceding chapters. Interest, he insists, "originally appears, originally is, and remains in reality nothing but a part of the profit, i.e. the surplus-value," which the functioning capitalist (industrial or merchant) must pay to the owner-lender whenever the capital used is borrowed. When the capitalist works with his own capital, there is no division at all — the entire profit belongs to him. Marx draws a crucial inference: "in so far as the owners of capital use it themselves in the reproduction process, they do not compete together to determine the interest rate," and thus "the category of interest — which is impossible without the establishment of a rate of interest — lies outside the movement of industrial capital itself."

This is a methodological bombshell. Interest, for Marx, is not an intrinsic "department" of industrial capital's own motion. It emerges only from a social division of the capitalist class itself into money capitalists and industrial capitalists. He reinforces this with a quotation from Thomas Tooke (History of Prices, 1838): the rate of interest is a proportional sum the lender is content to receive and borrower to pay; but when the owner employs his capital actively in reproduction, "he does not come under the head of those capitalists, the proportion of whom, to the number of borrowers, determines the rate of interest." Hence Marx's formula: it is only the division of capitalists into money capitalists and industrial capitalists that transforms a part of profit into interest and creates the category of interest at all; and only the competition between these two kinds of capitalist that creates the rate of interest.

He then deepens the point by examining a subtle datum: the functioning capitalist, even when he owns his capital, does not have at his disposal the capital itself as a private person — only the profit he can spend as revenue. As long as his capital functions, it belongs to the reproduction process and is "tied down in it." Ownership does not grant him the freedom to dispose of it otherwise so long as it is used as capital for the exploitation of labour. The same holds symmetrically for the money capitalist: as long as his capital is lent out and operates as money capital bearing interest, he cannot dispose of the principal. Marx makes this vivid — if he lends for a year he receives interest at fixed dates without repayment of principal; but even repayment makes no difference, because to continue operating as capital it "must always be lent out afresh." While it sits in his hands it bears no interest and does not operate as capital; once it bears interest it is no longer in his hands. From this follows "the possibility of lending capital in perpetuity."

2.2 The Tooke–Bosanquet Quarrel: The Falsity of "Repayment" as a Barrier to Convergence

This theoretical point about perpetual lending becomes the ground on which Marx adjudicates a real controversy among his contemporaries. Bosanquet (Metallic, Paper, and Credit Currency, 1842) had claimed that if the rate of interest fell as low as 1 per cent, "capital borrowed would be placed nearly on a par with capital possessed." Tooke objected that Bosanquet overlooked "the condition of repayment" — a borrower must eventually return the principal, so borrowed capital can never truly equal possessed capital.

Marx's verdict is blunt: Tooke is "completely false." Marx's reasoning is elegant. If interest were zero, the industrial capitalist who borrowed capital would be in the same position as the one working with his own capital; both would pocket the same average profit and their capital would operate as capital only by producing a profit. The requirement of repayment would make no difference here. And the closer the rate of interest approaches zero — say 1 per cent — the more borrowed capital stands "on the same level" as capital actually owned. As long as money capital is to continue existing as money capital, it must be lent out afresh at the prevailing rate to the same class of industrial and commercial capitalists. The distinction between the borrower and the owner — as long as both function as capitalists — reduces to a single quantitative difference: one pockets the whole profit p; the other pockets p − i (profit minus interest). As i → 0, p − i → p. Moreover, the objection "one has to pay back his capital and borrow anew" is void, because the owner equally "must similarly advance it afresh each time to the production process, and has no control over it that is independent of this process." The sole remaining distinction is the legal one: "one is the proprietor of his capital and the other is not."

Here we can isolate the chapter's working mathematical notation, which recurs throughout: p = gross profit; i = interest; p − i = profit of enterprise. The central dialectical operation the chapter performs is to show that this purely arithmetical subtraction is pregnant with a qualitative form.

2.3 The Central Question and the Counter-Example of "Associates"

Marx now poses the question explicitly (see §1 above) and immediately guards against a false generalization. Not every quantitative division of profit becomes a qualitative one. Consider several industrial capitalists who form an association, pursue a business jointly, and divide the profit under legally established provisions. These associates reckon their shares only as individual shares — they do not split the total into "my individual profit" plus "company profit for my non-existent associates." The mere multiplication of legal owners (a "quantitative division when the owner happens to consist of several legal persons") produces no qualitative split. The division must therefore have a different origin than the simple plurality of claimants.

2.4 The Real Starting Point: The Double Movement of the Same Capital

To find that origin, Marx insists we must "proceed from the assumption that the money capitalist and productive capitalist actually do come face to face — not just as legally separate persons but as persons who play quite different roles in the reproduction process, or in whose hands the same capital really does go through a double and completely different movement." One merely lends the capital; the other applies it productively. This is the real starting point [Ausgangspunkt] of interest formation. The same sum of value undergoes two distinct "movements" — a lending movement (outside production, bearing interest) and a functioning movement (inside production, yielding a profit) — and this objective bifurcation of the movement of one capital, when it is held by two different agents, is what can generate a qualitative distinction.

2.5 How Gross Profit Breaks Down for the Borrower: Interest as Presupposition

For the productive capitalist working with borrowed capital, the gross profit breaks into (a) the interest he must pay the lender and (b) the excess above that interest, which forms his own share. Here Marx introduces a critical causal asymmetry: if the general rate of profit is given, the borrower's share is determined by the rate of interest; if the rate of interest is given, it is determined by the profit rate. More decisively, however much the gross profit may diverge from the average profit in any single case, the lender's share is fixed by the general rate of interest — and, crucially, this is presupposed in advance, before the production process begins, i.e. before its result (the gross profit) is obtained. Interest is therefore a contractually and socially pre-given magnitude; profit of enterprise is a residual. This temporal and logical priority of interest over the resulting profit is the seed of its "qualitative" appearance.

2.6 The Genesis of Unternehmergewinn (Profit of Enterprise)

Marx then traces the subjective form this objective structure generates. The specific product of capital is surplus-value, and at a further remove, profit. But for the capitalist working with borrowed capital, the part of profit remaining after interest is not profit — it is "profit minus interest." It is this residual that "necessarily appears to him as the product of capital in its actual functioning." And this is not mere appearance: it is so for him, because he represents capital only as functioning capital — "he is its personification in so far as it functions," which it does when profitably invested and when he performs the operations the business prescribes. In opposition to the interest he must pay, the remaining profit "necessarily assumes the form of industrial or commercial profit" — or, using the German expression that embraces both, profit of enterprise [Unternehmergewinn].

Its determination: if gross profit equals average profit, the size of profit of enterprise is exclusively determined by the rate of interest. If gross profit diverges from average profit, the divergence (after interest is deducted on both sides) is determined by "all the conjunctures that give rise to such a temporary divergence" — differences in a particular sphere's profit rate versus the general rate, or an individual capitalist's profit versus his sphere's average.

2.7 The Conjunctural Sources of Divergence and the Role of "Cunning and Perseverance"

Marx enlarges on the determinants of the profit rate within production: it depends not only on surplus-value but on the purchase prices of means of production, on methods more than averagely productive, on economizing constant capital, etc. And, leaving aside the price of production, it depends on the state of the trade cycle and, "with each individual business deal," on "the greater or lesser cunning and perseverance of the capitalist," whether and how far he buys or sells below or above the production price and thereby appropriates a greater or lesser share of the total surplus-value in the circulation process. In each case the quantitative division of gross profit is transformed into a qualitative one — "all the more so in that the quantitative division itself depends on what is to be divided, how the active capitalist looks after his capital and what gross profit it yields him as functioning capital." Here Marx quietly introduces an important variable: the borrower's own activity (his entrepreneurship) shapes the divisible magnitude itself, reinforcing the sense that the residual reward attaches to his function.

2.8 The Objective Basis of the Qualitative Distinction

Assuming the functioning capitalist is not the owner, property in capital is represented to him by the lender. The interest he pays thus appears as "a part of the gross profit that accrues to property in capital as such." By contrast, the part falling to the active capitalist, as profit of enterprise, appears to derive "exclusively from the operations or functions that he performs with the capital in the reproduction process, especially therefore the functions that he performs as an entrepreneur in industry or trade." To him, then:

  • Interest = the mere fruit of property in capital, of "capital in itself, abstracted from the reproduction process in so far as it does not 'work'."
  • Profit of enterprise = the exclusive fruit of functions, of "capital's movement and process," which appears "as his own activity, in contrast to the non-activity and non-participation of the money capitalist."

Crucially, Marx stresses that this qualitative separation is not merely the subjective conception of either party. "It rests on objective fact": interest accrues to the money capitalist, "who is simply the owner of the capital and thus does represent mere property in capital before the production process and outside it," while profit of enterprise accrues to the merely functioning capitalist, "who is not the owner of capital." This objectivity is the linchpin holding the entire analysis together — and, as we shall see, it is also what makes the resulting ideology so powerful.

2.9 Ossification and Autonomization: "Mutual Ossification" and the Generalized Split

Marx now generalizes. For both the industrial capitalist working with borrowed capital and the money capitalist who does not apply his capital himself, the merely quantitative division between two persons with different legal titles "turns into a qualitative distinction." One part of profit now appears "in and of itself" as the fruit accruing to capital in one capacity — as interest; the other appears as "a specific fruit of capital in an opposite capacity" — as profit of enterprise: "one as the simple fruit of property in capital, the other as the fruit of merely functioning with capital, as the fruit of capital as capital in process."

Marx calls this "mutual ossification and autonomization" — the two parts harden into thing-like forms, "as if they derived from two essentially separate sources." And this must now "be fixed for the entire capitalist class and the total capital." The decisive extension follows: this holds irrespective of whether the functioning capitalist's capital is borrowed or not, and irrespective of whether the money capitalist uses his own capital himself. The capitalist who works with his own capital also divides his gross profit into: - interest accruing to him "as owner, as lender of his own capital to himself," and - profit of enterprise, accruing to him "as an active, functioning capitalist."

It becomes "a matter of indifference, as far as this division is concerned, whether the capitalist really does have to share with another or not." The owner-operator "breaks down into two persons, the mere owner of capital and its user"; and his capital itself breaks down into "owned capital, capital outside the production process, which yields an interest, and capital in the production process, which yields profit of enterprise as capital in process." This is the heart of the chapter's transformation thesis: the single self-identical capital is internally doubled into "capital as property" and "capital as function."

2.10 Why the Division Becomes Qualitative for the Whole Capitalist Class: The Three (and a Fourth) Grounds

Marx pauses to enumerate why this qualitative division generalizes.

First ground — the empirical prevalence of mixed financing. The majority of industrial capitalists operate with both their own and borrowed capital, in changing ratios across periods. Because nearly everyone straddles the line, the interest/profit-of-enterprise distinction is a lived, everyday reality for the class as a whole.

Second ground — the antithetical constitution of profit of enterprise by interest. Once a part of gross profit is ossified as interest, "the transformation of a part of the gross profit into the form of interest transforms its other part into profit of enterprise." Profit of enterprise is, "in point of fact, only the antithetical form that the excess of gross profit over interest assumes as soon as the latter exists as a category of its own." The general question — how gross profit differentiates into interest and profit of enterprise — thus reduces to the question: how does a part of gross profit become invariably ossified and autonomized as interest? Here Marx makes a momentous historical observation: interest-bearing capital "exists as a ready-made form handed down," and interest "as a ready-made subordinate form of the surplus-value produced by capital, long before the capitalist mode of production and the conceptions of capital and profit corresponding to it come into existence." In the popular mind, therefore, money capital / interest-bearing capital "is still seen as capital as such, capital par excellence." Hence the belief (prevailing "down to Massie's time") that it is money as such that is paid interest. The fact that loan capital yields interest whether or not it is applied as capital — even if borrowed only for consumption — confirms the conception of its total independence. The "best proof" of interest's independent form vs. profit, and of interest-bearing capital vs. industrial capital, in the earliest periods of the capitalist mode, is that it was discovered only in the middle of the eighteenth century — by Massie and subsequently Hume — that interest is just one part of gross profit. That such a "discovery" was needed at all attests to how thoroughly interest had already been reified.

Third ground — the independent existence of the class of money capitalists. Whether the industrial capitalist uses his own or borrowed capital, "the class of money capitalists confronts him as a special kind of capitalist, money capital as an autonomous kind of capital, and interest as the separate form of surplus-value that corresponds to this specific capital."

Fourth ground — (a gap in the manuscript). Marx left the fourth point blank. The chapter records only "(a gap in the manuscript)" — a telling editorial trace of the work-in-progress character of Volume 3 (the manuscript was edited by Engels, whose footnotes appear as "F.E.").

2.11 The Qualitative and Quantitative Definitions of Interest

Marx crystallizes interest on two axes. Qualitatively, interest is "the surplus-value supplied by capital as simple ownership, which capital yields in itself, even if its owner remains outside the reproduction process; surplus-value therefore yielded in separation from its process." Quantitatively, the part of profit forming interest "seems to be related not to industrial and commercial capital as such but rather to money capital," and the interest rate confirms this relationship. This confirmation is twofold: (1) the rate of interest, "despite its dependence on the general rate of profit, is separately determined"; (2) it appears — "just like the market price of commodities" — "as something hard and fast, for all its changes: a palpable and always given relationship as opposed to the intangible rate of profit." This solidity of the palpable interest rate, contrasted with the elusive, abstract profit rate, is what fixes interest in consciousness as a self-standing thing. Marx then states the negative condition of the whole category: "If all capital were to be found in the hands of industrial capitalists, there would be no interest and no rate of interest." The independent form that the quantitative division assumes is precisely what produces the qualitative distinction.

Comparing the industrial capitalist to the money capitalist: the industrialist is distinguished by profit of enterprise — the surplus of gross profit over average interest — and the rate of interest makes this appear as "an empirically given quantity." Comparing him to the industrial capitalist operating with his own capital, the latter differs from him "as a money capitalist simply in so far as he pockets the interest himself instead of paying it out." From both sides, the part of gross profit distinct from interest appears to him as profit of enterprise, and "interest itself appears as a surplus-value that capital yields in and of itself and which it would therefore yield even without productive application."

2.12 The Individual-Capitalist Validity vs. the Social Absurdity

Marx then performs one of his characteristic moves: he affirms the individual capitalist's viewpoint as practically correct for him, then annihilates it when elevated to the whole social capital. For the individual capitalist, it is "in practice correct": he genuinely has "the choice between lending his capital out as interest-bearing capital or valorizing it himself as productive capital," regardless of whether it begins as money capital or must first be transformed into it.

Taken generally — applied to total social capital — the notion is "of course quite absurd." It is "utter nonsense," Marx thunders, "to suggest that all capital could be transformed into money capital without the presence of people to buy and valorize the means of production, i.e. the form in which the entire capital exists," apart from the relatively small part actually in money. Concealed is an even greater absurdity: "that capital could yield interest on the basis of the capitalist mode of production without functioning as productive capital, i.e. without creating surplus-value, of which interest is simply one part; that the capitalist mode of production could proceed on its course without capitalist production." He adds a dynamic refutation: if an inappropriately large number of capitalists sought to transform their capital into money capital, the result would be "a tremendous devaluation of money capital and a tremendous fall in the rate of interest," and many would find themselves unable to live on interest, "compelled to turn themselves back into industrial capitalists." These vulgar economists (whom Marx castigates for taking the individual's calculus as the basis of profit itself) thereby commit the fallacy of composition in its purest form. Yet — and this is the dialectical double-edge — "for the individual capitalist this is in fact how it is." Hence even the self-financing capitalist necessarily treats the portion of his average profit equal to average interest as "the fruit of his capital as such, leaving aside the production process," and treats the excess as profit of enterprise. Schema (with p = average profit, i = average interest, e = profit of enterprise):

p = i + e → the capitalist experiences i as "property's yield" and e = p − i as "enterprise's yield."

2.13 Recapitulation and the Relational Nature of the Two Forms

Marx recapitulates: the part of profit the functioning capitalist pays to the mere owner becomes, through generalization, the separate form (interest) that all capital yields as such, borrowed or not. Its magnitude depends on the average rate of interest. Its origin is visible only in the fact that the functioning capitalist, insofar as he is owner of his own capital, "does not compete — at least not actively — in determining the rate of interest." The purely quantitative division between two persons with different legal titles has become "a qualitative distinction that seems to arise from the very nature of capital and profit." And the mechanism is relational: as soon as a part of profit assumes the form of interest, the difference between average profit and interest is transformed into the antithetical form of profit of enterprise. Marx is emphatic that these two forms "exist only in their antithesis." They are "neither of them related to the surplus-value, of which they are simply parts, under different categories, titles or names, but rather related to each other." Thus: "It is because one part of profit has been turned into interest that the other part accordingly appears as profit of enterprise." This is a functionalist, relational account of economic forms — forms defined positionally, not by intrinsic content.

Marx inserts a methodological note: by "profit" he always means average profit, since divergences (individual profit, or profit across spheres — "variations in the division of the average profit or surplus-value that swing back and forth with the competitive struggle") are "quite immaterial" to the investigation. This bracketing allows the analysis to isolate form from fluctuation.

2.14 Interest as "Net Profit" (Ramsay) and the Ontology of Capital-as-Property

Interest, Marx says, following Ramsay, is the "net profit" yielded by property in capital as such — whether to the mere lender outside reproduction or to the owner who employs his capital productively himself. But the latter receives it "not in so far as he is a functioning capitalist but rather as a money capitalist, the lender of his own capital as interest-bearing capital to himself as functioning capitalist." This reflexive formulation — capital lending to itself in its two capacities — is the conceptual core of the autonomous doubling.

Marx then embeds interest in his deepest ontological claim about capital. Just as the transformation of money and value into capital is the constant result of the capitalist production process, so its existence as capital is the constant presupposition of that process. Through its capacity to be transformed into means of production, capital "always commands unpaid labour and hence transforms the production and circulation process of commodities into the production of surplus-value for its possessor." Therefore interest "simply expresses the fact that value in general — objectified labour in its general social form — value that assumes the form of means of production in the actual production process, confronts living labour-power as an autonomous power and is the means of appropriating unpaid labour; and that it is this power in so far as it confronts the worker as the property of another." This is the social essence of capital as a class relation, now sedimented in the form of interest.

2.15 The Obliteration of the Antithesis to Wage-Labour in the Form of Interest

Here is one of the chapter's most profound dialectical points. In the form of interest, the antithesis to wage-labour is obliterated. Why? Because interest-bearing capital as such "does not have wage-labour as its opposite but rather functioning capital." The lending capitalist directly confronts "the capitalist actually functioning in the reproduction process," not "the wage-labourer who is expropriated from the means of production precisely on the basis of capitalist production." Marx's formula: "Interest-bearing capital is capital as property as against capital as function. But if capital does not function, it does not exploit workers and does not come into opposition with labour." The exploitation relation—the beating heart of capital—is displaced out of the interest form entirely. In interest, capital appears as a relation between capitalists, with no visible connection to labour.

2.16 Profit of Enterprise: Antithesis with Interest, Not with Labour

Symmetrically, profit of enterprise does not form an antithesis with wage-labour but with interest. Marx gives two grounds:

First, taking average profit as given, the rate of profit of enterprise is determined "not by wages but rather by the rate of interest" — high or low in inverse proportion to the latter. He footnotes Ramsay (Essay on the Distribution of Wealth, p. 214): "The profits of enterprise depend upon the net profits of capital, not the latter upon the former." Marx glosses: "For Ramsay, net profits always means interest." (We can formalize: given p, then e = p − i, hence ∂e/∂i < 0 — profit of enterprise varies inversely with interest.)

Second, the functioning capitalist derives his claim to profit of enterprise — and profit of enterprise itself — "not from his ownership of capital but rather from the function of capital as opposed to the capacity in which it exists only as inert property." This antithesis "directly emerges as soon as he operates with borrowed capital," where interest and profit of enterprise accrue to two different persons. Profit of enterprise arises "from the function of capital in the reproduction process, i.e. as a result of the operations and activity by which the functioning capitalist mediates these functions of industrial and commercial capital."

But Marx insists: "it is no sinecure to be a representative of functioning capital, unlike the case with interest-bearing capital." On the basis of capitalist production, "the capitalist directs both the production process and the circulation process. The exploitation of productive labour takes effort, whether he does this himself or has it done in his name by others." Therefore — and this is the ideological pivot — in opposition to interest, his profit of enterprise presents itself to him "as independent of his property in capital and rather as the result of his functions as non-owner, as a worker."

2.17 The Birth of the "Wages of Superintendence" Illusion

The capitalist "inevitably gets the idea into his head" that his profit of enterprise — "very far from forming any antithesis with wage-labour and being only the unpaid labour of others — is rather itself a wage, 'wages of superintendence of labour,' a higher wage than that of the ordinary wage-labourer," for two reasons: (1) because it is complex labour, and (2) because he himself pays the wages. Marx underscores the forgetting involved: "That his function as a capitalist consists in producing surplus-value, i.e. unpaid labour, and in the most economical conditions at that, is completely forgotten." Why? Because "interest accrues to the capitalist even if he does not perform any function as capitalist, but is simply the owner of capital; while profit of enterprise, on the other hand, accrues to the functioning capitalist even if he is not the owner of the capital with which he functions." In the face of this antithetical form, "it is forgotten that both are simply parts of surplus-value and that such a division can in no way change its nature, its origin and its conditions of existence."

2.18 The Real Relationship: Capital Against Labour, Displaced

Marx restores the suppressed truth. In the reproduction process, the functioning capitalist "represents capital against the wage-labourers as the property of others," and the money capitalist "participates in the exploitation of labour as represented by the functioning capitalist." The active capitalist can exercise his function — have workers work for him, have means of production function as capital — only as representative of the means of production vis-à-vis the workers. But this foundational truth "is forgotten in the face of the opposition between the function of capital in the reproduction process and mere ownership of capital outside the reproduction process."

He then states the general principle: the form the two parts of profit assume as interest and profit of enterprise "does not express any relationship with labour, because this relationship exists only between labour and profit, or rather surplus-value, as the sum of these two parts, their whole and their unity." The division presupposes profit as ready-made. If the capitalist is the actual owner, he pockets the entire profit; "it is all the same for the worker whether this is what he does or whether he has to pay one part to a third party as the legal proprietor." The basis for the division of profit between two kinds of capitalist "is thus transformed imperceptibly into the basis of existence of the profit or surplus-value to be divided" — a classic case of a distributional relationship being mistaken for a generative one. Marx's lament: "From the fact that interest confronts profit of enterprise, and vice versa, but neither confronts labour, it seems to follow that profit of enterprise plus interest, i.e. profit and consequently surplus-value, is derived — from what? From the antithetical form of its two parts!" But profit "is produced before this division takes place."

2.19 The Potentiality Analogy: Capital and Labour-Power

Marx anticipates an objection: does the fact that interest-bearing capital proves itself only when the money lent is really transformed into capital and produces surplus-value rule out interest being "its inherent property, independent of the production process"? He answers via analogy. Labour-power proves its value-creating property only if it is activated and realized in the labour process; but "this does not exclude it being potentially in itself already value-creating activity as a capacity, and as such it does not just arise from the process but is rather presupposed by it." It is bought as the ability to create value, and can be bought without being productively used (for personal services, etc.). "The same with capital." It belongs to its borrower whether or not he uses it as capital; what he pays for is "the surplus-value inherently contained in the commodity of capital as a potentiality." This is a subtle defense: interest is the price of a potentiality embodied in a commodity-relation, structurally parallel to the wage as the price of labour-power's capacity.

2.20 A Closer Look at Profit of Enterprise: The Production Process as "Labour Process in General"

Marx now descends into the ideological mechanics. As "capital ownership which possesses the capacity of command over the labour of others" becomes fixed in interest, the other part — profit of enterprise — "necessarily appears as if it does not derive from capital as capital, but rather from the production process independently of its specific social determination." But "the production process, when separated from capital, is simply the labour process in general." Therefore the industrial capitalist, as distinct from the owner, "appears therefore not as functioning capital but rather as a functionary independent of capital, as a simple bearer of the labour process in general; as a worker, and a wage-worker at that."

Contrast: interest expresses "precisely the existence of the conditions of labour as capital, in their social antithesis to labour and their transformation into personal powers vis-à-vis labour and over labour." Interest "represents mere ownership of capital as a means of appropriating the product of other people's labour." But — the crucial twist — "it represents this character of capital as something that falls to it outside the production process and is in no way the result of the specifically capitalist character of this production process itself." It presents capital's exploitative character "not in direct antithesis to labour, but, on the contrary, with no relationship to labour at all, merely as a relationship between one capitalist and another." Thus interest is "a capacity that is external and indifferent to the actual relationship between capital and labour." In interest, capital's antithetical character "acquires an autonomous expression" — but "in such a way that this antithesis is completely obliterated in this expression and completely abstracted from it. Interest is a relationship between two capitalists, not between capitalist and worker."

The mirror image: this form of interest gives the other part of profit "the qualitative form of profit of enterprise, and subsequently of wages of superintendence." The capitalist's specific functions "fall to his part precisely as distinct from the workers and in opposition to them," yet are "presented as simply functions of labour." He obtains surplus-value "not because he works as a capitalist but rather because, leaving aside his capacity as a capitalist, he also works." Thus "this part of surplus-value is therefore no longer surplus-value at all, but rather its opposite, the equivalent for labour performed." Marx's stunning formulation: "Since the estranged character of capital, its antithesis to labour, is shifted outside the actual process of exploitation, i.e. into interest-bearing capital, this process of exploitation itself appears as simply a labour process, in which the functioning capitalist simply performs different work from that of the workers. The labour of exploiting and the labour exploited are identical, both being labour." The social form of capital devolves on interest (neutral, indifferent form); the economic function of capital devolves on profit of enterprise, stripped of its specifically capitalist character. The reader should note the structural parallel Marx draws: exactly the same mystification occurs here "in the consciousness of the capitalist" as with the "grounds of compensation" discussed earlier (Part Two) regarding the equalization of the average profit — where grounds determining the division of surplus-value are turned into grounds for the existence and justification of profit as such.

2.21 The Manager: Where a Part of Profit Genuinely Separates Off as Wages

The "wages of superintendence" idea finds "further support" in a real fact: "one part of the profit actually can be separated off as wages, and really does separate off; or rather, a part of wages, conversely... appears as an integral component of profit." As Adam Smith already rightly realized, this part presents itself in pure, independent form — separate both from profit in general and from profit of enterprise — in the payment of a manager in branches where scale permits sufficient division of labour for a special salary. Marx footnotes Say's sayings and generally positions Smith as having grasped this genuinely.

2.22 The Two Forms of Supervisory Labour

Marx distinguishes two forms of supervision/management labour:

First — the cooperative/technical form. "In all labour where many individuals cooperate, the interconnection and unity of the process is necessarily represented in a governing will, and in functions that concern not the detailed work but rather the workplace and its activity as a whole, as with the conductor of an orchestra." This is "productive labour that has to be performed in any combined mode of production." It is independent of the specifically capitalist, exploitative relation.

Second — the antagonistic/domination form. "Quite apart from the commercial department," this work of supervision "necessarily arises in all modes of production that are based on opposition between the worker as direct producer and the proprietor of the means of production." The greater the opposition, the greater the role supervision plays. "It reaches its high point in the slave system." Marx cites Cairnes (The Slave Power, 1862): "Superintendence is here [the peasant proprietor] completely dispensed with" — and, for dispersed slave labour, "the number of overseers... will be proportionately increased." But supervision is also "indispensable in the capitalist mode of production, since here too the production process is at the same time a process of consumption of labour-power by the capitalist."

2.23 The Political Analogy: Despotic States

Marx extends the analogy to politics: "In despotic states, too, the work of supervision and all-round intervention of the government involves both aspects: the performance of those common tasks that arise from the nature of all communities, and the specific functions that arise from the opposition between the government and the mass of the people." The writers of antiquity, having the slave system in mind, found the two aspects "coincide as inseparably as they do with the modern economists who view the capitalist mode of production as the absolute mode." And — the polemical sting — "the apologists of the modern slave system know just as well how to use supervisory work as a justificatory ground for slavery as other economists do for the wage-labour system."

2.24 Historical Evidence I: The Villicus of Cato's Time

Marx documents the ancient rationalization of supervision. Drawing on Mommsen (Römische Geschichte, 1856), he describes the villicus (from villa) — the manager of the familia rustica, who "had to take and spend, buy and sell; he received his instructions from the master, and gave both orders and punishment in his absence." The villicus "of course had more freedom than the other slaves." The Magonian books (Carthaginian agricultural treatises, translated into Latin by order of the Roman senate, serving as official model for rational slave-based agriculture) advised he be permitted to marry and raise children and keep his own money; Cato said he should be married to the female manager. "He alone had any prospect of obtaining his freedom, as a reward from the master for good behaviour." Every slave, including the manager, "received his necessities at the master's expense at definite intervals and in fixed amounts." And: "The quantity depended on the work, so that the manager, for instance, whose work was lighter than the other slaves, received a smaller ration." Note the inverted logic: the supervisory "wage" is smaller in kind because the work is lighter — a datum Marx deploys against the "wages of superintendence" as reward for superior/heavier labour.

2.25 Historical Evidence II: Aristotle on Domination and Supervision

Marx quotes Aristotle (De republica, Book I, ch. 7) in Greek with translation: "For the master" — Marx glosses: the capitalist — "proves himself such not by obtaining slaves" — ownership of capital, which gives him the power to buy labour — "but by employing slaves" — using labourers, nowadays wage-labourers, in the production process. "There is nothing great or sublime about this science... but whatever the slave is to perform, the master must be able to order." And: "Whenever the masters are not compelled to plague themselves with supervision, the overseer assumes this honour, while the masters pursue public affairs or philosophy." Marx's interpretation: Aristotle says "in very blunt terms" that domination, "in the economic domain as well as in the political, imposes on those in power the functions of dominating," so that in the economic domain "they must know how to consume labour-power." And Aristotle adds "that this supervisory work is not a matter of very great moment, which is why the master leaves the 'honour' of this drudgery to an overseer as soon as he is wealthy enough." Marx thus turns Aristotle into a witness against the pretension that supervision is great or sublime labour.

2.26 The Ideological Function: Making Exploitation Into a "Wage"

Marx draws the general conclusion. This work of management and supervision, "in so far as it is not simply a particular function arising from the nature of all combined social labour, but arises rather from the opposition between the owner of the means of production and the owner of mere labour-power" — whether the labour-power is bought with the worker (slavery) or the worker sells it himself, so that the production process "appears at the same time as a process of consumption of labour by capital" — "this function arising from the servitude of the direct producer is made often enough into a justification of that relationship itself, and the exploitation and appropriation of the unpaid labour of others is just as often presented as the wage due to the owner of capital."

2.27 Historical Evidence III: O'Conor's Pro-Slavery Speech

Marx supplies the most damning example. The lawyer O'Conor, at a New York meeting on 19 December 1859 under the slogan "Justice for the South," declared amid "thunderous applause": "to that condition of bondage the Negro is assigned by Nature... He has strength, and has the power to labour; but the Nature which created the power denied to him either the intellect to govern, or willingness to work." (Applause.) "Both were denied to him. And that Nature which deprived him of the will to labour, gave him a master to coerce that will, and to make him a useful... servant... I maintain that it is not injustice to leave the Negro in the condition in which Nature placed him, to give him a master to govern him... nor is it depriving him of any of his rights to compel him to labour in return, and afford to that master just compensation for the labour and talent employed in governing him and rendering him useful to himself and to the society." Marx footnotes that he himself was a regular contributor to the New York Daily Tribune (1852–62), which then held strongly democratic, anti-slavery views — a self-referential verification of his source.

Marx's cutting conclusion: "Now the wage-labourer, just like the slave, must have a master, to make him work and govern him." And once this "relationship of domination and servitude is assumed," it is "quite in order" for the wage-labourer to be compelled to produce, besides his own wages, "also the wages of supervision, a compensation for the work of dominating and supervising him, or 'just compensation for the labour and talent employed in governing him and rendering him useful to himself and to the society'." The O'Conor quote is thus the slavery-form of the very same apologetic that the "wages of superintendence" theory performs for wage-labour — proving the apologetic structure is invariant across modes of exploitation.

2.28 Historical Evidence IV: The Epitropos and Régisseur

Marx notes that the supervisory work, arising from the antagonistic character (domination of capital over labour), is "directly and inseparably fused, under the capitalist system, with the productive functions that all combined social labour assigns to particular individuals as their special work." The wages of an epitropos — or régisseur as he was known in feudal France — "become completely separated from profit and even take the form of wages for skilled labour, as soon as the business is conducted on a sufficiently large scale for such a manager to be paid" — "even though our industrial capitalists are still a long way from 'pursuing public affairs or philosophy'."

2.29 Ure and the Managers as "the Soul of Our Industrial System"

Marx cites Andrew Ure (Philosophy of Manufactures, 1836): "it is not the industrial capitalists but rather the industrial managers who are 'the soul of our industrial system'." Marx's footnote adds that "this Pindar of the manufacturers also testifies that most of the latter have not the slightest understanding of the machinery they use" — a jab at the redundancy of the capitalist's technical contribution. And for the commercial side, he refers back to the previous Part.

2.30 The Superfluity of the Capitalist: Cooperative Factories as Proof

Marx's theoretical payoff: "Capitalist production has itself brought it about that the work of supervision is readily available, quite independent of the ownership of capital." It has therefore become "superfluous for this work of supervision to be performed by the capitalist." His orchestra-conductor analogy returns: "A musical conductor need in no way be the owner of the instruments in his orchestra, nor does it form part of his function as a conductor that he should have any part in paying the 'wages' of the other musicians." Cooperative factories provide the proof that "the capitalist has become just as superfluous as a functionary in production as he himself, from his superior vantage-point, finds the large landlord." Marx concedes that in so far as this function "does not arise from the production process simply as a capitalist process" and "is not confined to the function of exploiting the labour of others" — i.e. in so far as it arises from "the form of labour as social labour, from the combination and cooperation of many to a common result" — it is "just as independent of capital as is this form itself, once it has burst its capitalist shell." To say that this labour, as capitalist labour, is necessarily the function of the capitalist "means nothing more than that the vulgus cannot conceive that forms developed in the womb of the capitalist mode of production may be separated and liberated from their antithetical capitalist character."

His decisive formulation: "Vis-à-vis the money capitalist, the industrial capitalist is a worker, but his work is that of a capitalist, i.e. an exploiter of the labour of others." The "wage" he claims "is precisely the quantity of others' labour that is appropriated, and depends directly upon the rate of exploitation of this labour, as long as he makes the effort required for this exploitation. It does not depend on the amount of effort this exploitation costs him, which can be devolved on a manager against moderate payment." Marx then gives an empirical illustration: "After every crisis one can see many ex-manufacturers in the English factory districts who are now supervising their own former factories as managers for the new owners, often their creditors, in return for a modest wage." An Engels footnote (F.E.) records a personal-knowledge case: a manufacturer whose business failed in the crisis of 1868 became "the paid employee of his own former workers" after the bankrupt factory was taken over by a workers' cooperative.

2.31 Managers' Wages: Constant Separation in Cooperatives and Joint-Stock Companies

Marx generalizes: the wages of management, "both commercial and industrial, appear as completely separate from profit of enterprise both in the workers' cooperative factories and in capitalist joint-stock companies." What appears "accidental" in other cases is "here a constant factor." In the cooperative factory, "the antithetical character of the supervisory work disappears, since the manager is paid by the workers instead of representing capital in opposition to them." Joint-stock companies in general (developed with the credit system) "have the tendency to separate this function of managerial work more and more from the possession of capital, whether one's own or borrowed" — just as, with the development of bourgeois society, judicial and administrative functions separated from landed property, to which they were attributes in the feudal period.

The dialectical climax: since (on one hand) the functioning capitalist confronts the mere owner (money capitalist), and with the development of credit this money capital itself assumes a social character, "being concentrated in banks and loaned out by these, no longer by its direct proprietors"; and since (on the other hand) the mere manager, "who does not possess capital under any title, neither by loan nor in any other way," takes care of all real functions of the functioning capitalist — "there remains only the functionary, and the capitalist vanishes from the production process as someone superfluous." This is the culminating thesis of the chapter's second half.

2.32 Empirical Confirmation: Cooperative Factory Accounts

Marx cites the published accounts (footnote: "The accounts referred to here only go up to 1864, since the above was written in 1865. — F.E.") of English cooperative factories. After deducting the manager's wages (which "form a part of the variable capital laid out, just like the wages of the other workers"), their profit "was greater than the average, even though they sometimes paid a much higher interest than private factories did." The reason: "in all these cases a greater economy in the use of constant capital." What matters for Marx: "here the average profit (= interest + profit of enterprise) presents itself palpably and in actual fact as a magnitude completely separate from the wages of management. Since profit here was higher than average, so profit of enterprise was also higher than elsewhere." This is a crucial empirical hinge: the separation of managerial wage from profit is not a conceptual abstraction but a measurable accounting fact.

2.33 Empirical Confirmation: Joint-Stock Banks

The same appears in certain capitalist joint-stock undertakings, e.g. joint-stock banks. The London and Westminster Bank paid an annual dividend of 30 per cent in 1863; the Union Bank of London and others paid 15 per cent. "In addition to the managers' salaries, the interest paid on deposits is deducted from gross profit." The high profit is explained "by the small proportion of the paid-up capital in relation to deposits." Marx gives figures: London and Westminster Bank, 1863 — paid-up capital £1,000,000, deposits £14,540,275; Union Bank of London, 1863 — paid-up capital £600,000, deposits £12,384,173. This demonstrates the enormous leverage of a small ownership base over borrowed/deposited funds — the quantitative disproportion that produces outsized profit of enterprise for shareholders and managers.

2.34 The Historical Genesis and Apologetic Purpose of the Confusion

Marx then narrates the history of the idea. "The confusion between profit of enterprise and the wages of supervision or management originally arose from the antithetical form that the surplus of profit over interest assumes in opposition to this interest. It was subsequently developed with the apologetic intention of presenting profit not as surplus-value, i.e. as unpaid labour, but rather as the wage that the capitalist himself receives for the work he performs." Marx here makes a sharp distinction between origin (a spontaneous categorical confusion generated by the antithetical form) and subsequent development (a deliberate apologetic weaponization of that confusion).

2.35 The Socialists' Demand and Its Uncomfortable Reception

"The socialists then raised the demand that profit should be reduced in practice to what it claimed to be in theory, i.e. simply to the wages of supervision." This demand "came up against the theoretical embellishment still more uncomfortably" as two processes unfolded: 1. The wages of superintendence "found their particular level and market price, just like every other wage, with the formation of a numerous class of industrial and commercial managers" (footnote 78: Hodgskin, Labour Defended against the Claims of Capital, 1825 — "Masters are labourers as well as their journeymen. In this character their interest is precisely the same as that of their men. But they are also either capitalists, or the agents of the capitalists, and in this respect their interest is decidedly opposed to the interests of the workmen"; and "The wide spread of education among the journeymen mechanics of this country diminishes daily the value of the labour and skill of almost all masters and employers by increasing the number of persons who possess their peculiar knowledge."); 2. They "fell on the other hand, just like wages for skilled labour in general, with the general development that reduces the costs of production of labour-power with special training" (footnote 79: J. S. Mill, Principles of Political Economy, 2nd edn, 1849 — "The general relaxation of conventional barriers, the increased facilities of education tend to bring down the wages of skilled labour instead of raising those of the unskilled").

Thus the "wage of superintendence," once admitted as a real category, turns out — by the very laws the apologists invoke for all wages — to fall as the supply of qualified managers rises. The apologetic theory refutes itself when taken literally as a wage theory.

2.36 The Last Pretext Removed: Cooperatives and Joint-Stock Companies

"With the development of cooperatives on the workers' part, and joint-stock companies on the part of the bourgeoisie, the last pretext for confusing profit of enterprise with the wages of management was removed, and profit came to appear in practice as what it undeniably was in theory: mere surplus-value, value for which no equivalent was paid, realized unpaid labour; so that the functioning capitalist really exploits labour, and the fruits of his exploitation, if he operates with borrowed capital, are divided into interest and profit of enterprise, the surplus of the profit over the interest."

2.37 The "New Swindle" of Joint-Stock Governance

Marx closes with a final invidious twist. On the basis of capitalist production, "a new swindle with the wages of management develops in connection with joint-stock companies": over and above the actual managing director, "a number of governing and supervisory boards arise, for which management and supervision are in fact a mere pretext for the robbery of shareholders and their own enrichment." He cites The City or the Physiology of London Business; with Sketches on 'Change, and the Coffee Houses, London, 1845: a private balance sheet of Mr Timothy Abraham Curtis, presented to the Court of Bankruptcy on his failure, showed income netted from directorship "between £800 and £900 a year." Curtis's association with the Bank of England and East India House made him "quite a plum for a public company to acquire... in the boardroom." Marx notes: "The remuneration of these company directors is at least one guinea (21 shillings) for each weekly meeting." And his final line: "Hearings before the bankruptcy court show that the wages of supervision are in inverse proportion, as a rule, to the actual supervision exercised by these nominal directors." The chapter thus ends by fully inverting the "wages of superintendence" apologetic: the reward rises as the actual work falls — proving the payment is a tribute of ownership, not compensation for labour.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

3.1 Fundamental Principles and Systemic Laws Revealed

First, the primacy of form over quantity in the analysis of capital. The chapter's governing law is that a purely quantitative division of a magnitude can, under determinate social conditions — the division of the capitalist class into owners and functionaries, and the objective separation of the "double movement" of the same capital — harden into a qualitative, autonomous distinction. This is a methodological maxim of Marx's mature method: economic forms are not merely labels for quantitative shares but possess their own objectivity and causal power, feeding back upon the consciousness and behavior of economic agents. The formula p = i + e (average profit = interest + profit of enterprise) is not a neutral accounting identity; it encodes an entire social ontology in which one portion of value is attributed to "property" and another to "function."

Second, the relational constitution of economic categories. Interest and profit of enterprise "exist only in their antithesis." Each is defined by its relation to the other, not by any intrinsic content. Interest is not "naturally" the yield of ownership; it becomes so only once profit of enterprise exists as its opposite, and vice versa. This is a thoroughly dialectical result: categories are positionally, not substantively, constituted — a principle with broad reach across Marx's theory of value forms.

Third, the illusion-generating power of "capital as property" vs. "capital as function." The separation of the exploitative essence of capital from the visibility of exploitation is the chapter's deepest insight. Because interest appears as a relation between capitalists, with no reference to labour, the exploitation relation itself gets displaced into a form that erases its own origin. The social antagonism between capital and labour is preserved (the profit is still unpaid labour) but its form of appearance is neutralized. This is the fetishism of capital in its most refined form.

Fourth, the ideological function of an apologetic category and its self-refutation. The "wages of superintendence" theory is shown to be (a) generated spontaneously by the antithetical form of profit; (b) subsequently weaponized apologetically; and (c) empirically self-destroying once taken literally — managerial wages fall with the growth of a managerial class; supervisory "wages" vary inversely with actual supervision; cooperative and joint-stock forms separate wages from profit as a "constant factor." Marx thereby demonstrates that an ideology can be refuted not merely by counter-argument but by the internal logic of its own claims and by the actual development of social forms.

Fifth, the historical priority of interest-bearing capital and the "reification lag." That it required a discovery (Massie, Hume, mid-18th century) that interest is part of gross profit reveals how deeply pre-capitalist money-lending forms had sedimented in popular consciousness. The category of interest is historically older than industrial capital — a fact that explains its apparent self-evidence as "capital par excellence." Theoretical categories, Marx teaches, carry their history within them.

Sixth, the tendency toward the "superfluity" of the capitalist. The joint-stock company, the credit system, the cooperative, and the salaried managerial class together dissolve the functional necessity of the proprietary capitalist: "there remains only the functionary, and the capitalist vanishes from the production process as someone superfluous." This is a structural, historically dynamic tendency — a foreshadowing of the "abolition" logic that runs through Marx's critique of political economy.

3.2 Broader Practical, Theoretical, and Philosophical Implications

Theoretically, the chapter completes the architecture of Volume 3 by showing how the single social surplus-value, already distributed into profit, rent, and industrial/commercial categories, undergoes yet further subdivision into interest and profit of enterprise — and how each subdivision generates its own ideological "justification." It bridges the abstract theory of the rate of profit to the concrete institutions of credit, banking, and joint-stock capital that Marx develops in the following chapters, and it supplies the conceptual tools for understanding financialization as a distinct form of surplus appropriation.

Practically, the analysis bears directly on the political economy of "shareholder value" and managerial compensation. The apology that profit is a wage for "supervision" is shown to be structurally identical to the slaveholder's "just compensation for the labour and talent employed in governing" — a continuity Marx underscores by placing O'Conor's speech next to the modern economists' defense of the wage system. The designation of directorship fees as "wages of supervision" that vary in inverse proportion to actual supervision exposes such remuneration as a claim grounded in ownership, not work. Philosophically, the chapter is a study in the dialectic of appearance and essence — how a real, objective social structure generates a false appearance that is nonetheless practically true for the individuals enmeshed in it (the individual capitalist's "choice" is genuinely available to him), while being absurd when universalized to the whole social capital. Marx's refusal to reduce ideology to mere "error" — his insistence that the apologetic form arises from the real antithetical structure of the categories — is the enduring methodological lesson: social illusions are not accidents of thought but necessary forms of a contradictory reality.

The chapter ultimately teaches that within capital, the identity "capital = claim to surplus-value" is internally split into two personas — the owner and the functionary — and that this split, while purely formal in its arithmetic origin, becomes materially decisive in law, consciousness, institutions, and class struggle. To understand capitalism's financial surface, one must grasp that the apparently neutral relation "one capitalist to another" (interest) is the disguised form of the relation "capital to labour" — and that the more that disguise is perfected, the more the underlying exploitation is enabled to continue unobserved.
