Volume 3 • Chapter 39: First Form of Differential Rent (Differential Rent I)

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem

Chapter 39 of Volume III of Capital opens a new territory in Marx's analysis: the transformation of surplus profit into ground-rent. Up to this point in the volume, Marx has traced the formation of the general rate of profit, the equalization of profit across branches of production, and the emergence of commercial and interest-bearing capital. Now he confronts a form of revenue that appears, on the surface, to arise from the soil itself — from nature's bounty rather than from the exploitation of labour. The chapter's task is to dissolve that appearance and demonstrate that ground-rent, in its first and simplest form, is nothing but a differential surplus profit — the excess of the profit realized on better land over the profit realized on the worst land in cultivation, which the landowner appropriates by virtue of the legal monopoly of landed property.

The chapter's core thesis is that differential rent is not a payment for the productive power of the soil as such, but a social relation masquerading as a natural one. It arises whenever equal amounts of capital and labour are applied to equal areas of land with unequal results, provided that (a) the worst soil in cultivation governs the market price, and (b) landed property exists as a form that can intercept the resulting surplus. The chapter thus performs a double operation: it simultaneously explains the economic mechanics of differential rent (a rigorously worked-out system of numerical tables) and exposes the ideological illusion that rent is a natural tribute rather than a socially determined appropriation.

The Intellectual Objective

Marx has three interlocking objectives. First, he must correct and generalize Ricardo's law of rent. Ricardo had defined rent as the difference between the produce obtained by the employment of two equal quantities of capital and labour — but, as Marx immediately notes, Ricardo omitted the crucial clause "on equal quantities of land." Without that qualification, the statement confuses ground-rent with surplus profit in general. Marx's correction is not pedantic; it isolates the precise condition under which surplus profit becomes rent: the simultaneous application of equal capitals to equal land areas.

Second, Marx must demolish the "Malthusian" narrative — shared by West, Malthus, and Ricardo alike — that rent necessarily arises from a progression to ever-worse soil and therefore expresses a declining natural fertility of agriculture. Against this, Marx demonstrates through three systematically varied tables that differential rent can arise under an ascending progression (worse to better soil), a descending progression (better to worse), or a criss-cross movement, and can coexist with falling prices, stationary prices, or rising prices. He also — crucially — demonstrates that rent can rise while prices fall, and can fall per acre while the total rental rises.

Third, and most profoundly, Marx demonstrates that differential rent rests on a "false social value" — a market value imposed by the competition of capitals on the basis of the capitalist mode of production, which is above the actual labour-time embodied in the agricultural product. This opens the way to the chapter's most radical conclusion: that the economic basis for a class of landowners would disappear under a consciously planned association of producers, because such a society would not pay 2½ times the labour-time actually contained in the grain.

The Chapter's Place in the Broader Framework

This chapter belongs to Marx's systematic refutation of the trinity formula — the ideological representation of capitalist society as consisting of three independent sources of revenue: land yields rent, capital yields profit, labour yields wages. By demonstrating that ground-rent in its first form is merely a transformed surplus profit — a portion of surplus value already generated by the exploitation of wage-labour — Marx severs the apparent link between the soil and rent. The soil is a condition of agricultural production, but it is not a source of value. Only labour creates value; the fertility of the soil merely determines how that value is distributed among farmers and landlords, and how much the consuming society is made to overpay.

The chapter is also a bridge. It explicitly announces itself as preparing "differential rent II" — the case where successive doses of capital are invested on the same land rather than on different lands. The extensive analysis of Tables Ia, Ib, and Ic — in which Marx holds rent per acre constant while varying the proportions in which different soil types compose the total cultivated area — functions as the transition. It reveals a variable that Marx claims had been "completely overlooked, in a quite striking fashion": the composition of the cultivated area as a determinant of the average rent and the rate of rent, entirely independent of the price of grain or the differential fertility of any particular soil.

Finally, the chapter's closing sections — on the price of untilled land, on colonial agriculture, on the historical examples of English grain prices from the seventeenth to the nineteenth century — extend the analysis into the domains of land speculation, imperial and colonial political economy, and economic history. The discussion of Michigan and New York, the Erie Canal, and the export of wheat from superficially cultivated prairie soil, anticipates the twentieth-century debates over the "frontier," land rent in settler colonies, and the specific form of agricultural surplus in the world market. In this respect the chapter is not merely a technical treatise on rent; it is a demonstration of the historical and geographical variability of the conditions under which differential rent arises — and therefore a refutation of any attempt to elevate Ricardo's law into a universal natural law.

The intellectual objective, in sum, is to show that differential rent is a historically specific, socially produced magnitude whose level depends not on any single "law of nature," but on the interaction of: the chemical and mechanical development of agriculture; the pattern in which cultivation extends across soil types; the distribution of total capital among those types; the level of demand; the level of the market price as governed by the worst soil in cultivation; and the legal existence of landed property as a claim on surplus profit. The chapter is thus simultaneously an exercise in exact economic modelling and a critique of political economy's naturalization of social relations.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 Opening Corrections to Ricardo: Rent as Differential Product on Equal Areas

Marx opens the chapter by quoting Ricardo's Principles: "Rent is always the difference between the produce obtained by the employment of two equal quantities of capital and labour." He calls this statement "completely correct" — but only within a specific restriction. Ricardo, Marx explains, is here assuming that no other rent exists besides differential rent (that is, no absolute rent, no monopoly rent). Marx immediately supplies the correction that Ricardo himself failed to make: the statement should read "on equal quantities of land," since Ricardo is dealing with ground-rent and not with surplus profit in general.

This distinction is foundational. Surplus profit, Marx defines, is — when produced in normal conditions and not as a by-product of fortuitous circumstances in circulation — "always produced as the difference between the product of two equal amounts of capital and labour." But surplus profit becomes ground-rent only when two equal amounts of capital and labour are employed on equal areas of land with unequal results. The specificity of the agricultural case lies in the equality of area and the inequality of yield. This double condition is what allows landed property to intervene and appropriate the excess as rent.

Marx then adds an important qualification that complicates the simple Ricardian picture. It is "by no means necessarily required" that the surplus profit should arise from unequal results of equal capitals. Capitals of different size could also be employed in different investments, and this is even the general assumption. What is essential is that equal proportionate parts — for instance £100 of each — give unequal results; that is, the rate of profit varies. This is the general precondition for the existence of surplus profit in any sphere of capital investment whatsoever. The second thing, Marx insists, is the transformation of this surplus profit into the form of ground-rent — "of rent in general, as a form distinct from profit." And this transformation is not automatic; it must be investigated: "when, how and under what circumstances this transformation takes place."

The conceptual architecture being laid down here is crucial for everything that follows. There are two analytically separable questions: (1) the origin of surplus profit (unequal profitability of equal capital advances in different spheres or on different lands), and (2) the form of ground-rent (the legal-economic mechanism by which landed property intercepts that surplus). The chapter's whole argument depends on keeping these distinct. The existence of differential surplus profit does not by itself produce rent; what converts it into rent is the existence of landed property as a barrier that prevents the surplus from being competed away.

2.2 The Second Ricardo Quote and Marx's Expanded List of Causes of Fertility Inequality

Marx next turns to a second statement of Ricardo's, which he again endorses in so far as it is restricted to differential rent: "Whatever diminishes the inequality in the produce obtained from successive portions of capital on the same or on new land, tends to lower rent; and whatever increases that inequality, necessarily produces an opposite effect, and tends to raise it."

Marx then immediately expands the list of causes that produce such inequality. Ricardo, in the accepted reading, was limited to fertility and location. Marx, however, enumerates three additional causes:

(1) The distribution of taxes, according to whether this is uniform or not. In England, where taxation is not centralized and the tax is levied on the land itself and not on the rent, the resulting inequality is always present. A tax levied uniformly on land of different fertility will proportionally burden worse soil far more heavily than better soil, altering the pattern of profitability and thus the differential. A tax levied on rent, by contrast, would fall only on the surplus itself and would not (directly) alter the margin of cultivation.

(2) The inequalities resulting from the differential development of agriculture in different parts of the country. Agriculture, Marx explains, is — because of its traditional character — "more difficult to equalize than is manufacture." The implication is that in manufacture, competition and the mobility of capital tend to equalize conditions of production across regions far more rapidly than in agriculture, where local custom, technique, and soil-management practice diverge.

(3) The uneven way in which capital is divided among the farmers. Different farmers on different pieces of land possess different quantities and efficiencies of capital; this unevenness itself produces differential results.

Marx then adds a historical observation that is decisive for the argument: because "the capitalist mode of production's seizure of agriculture, the transformation of the independently operating peasant into a wage-labourer, is in fact the final conquest of this mode of production," these inequalities are greater here than in any other branch of industry. Agriculture is the last sector to be fully subsumed under capital; consequently, pre-capitalist residues of uneven development persist longest there. This is an important nuance: differential rent is not a "natural" constant but is historically intensified by the very backwardness of agriculture relative to manufacture.

Having laid these preliminaries, Marx announces his procedure: he will first "summarize quite briefly the particular features of my development in contrast with that of Ricardo, etc."

2.3 Fertility and Location: The Two Non-Capital Causes of Differential Product

Marx begins his own development by specifying the analytical starting point: the unequal products of equal amounts of capital applied to different lands of the same area — or, in the case of differently-sized lands, the products of equal acreages. He then identifies two general causes of such unequal products which are independent of capital:

(1) Fertility — to be discussed at length, including "what is meant by the natural fertility of land, and the different aspects this involves."

(2) Location — decisive in the case of colonies, and decisive everywhere for the sequence in which lands can be successively brought into cultivation.

The distinction between fertility and location is analytically clean but practically entangled. Marx notes immediately that these two bases for differential rent "can operate in opposite directions." A piece of land may be very well located but of very low fertility, and vice versa. This is an important fact, Marx says, because it explains why, when the land of a particular country is originally cultivated, it is possible to proceed from worse soil to better as well as the other way round. Location can override fertility, forcing cultivation of poorer land that happens to be closer to markets, rivers, or existing settlements, while more fertile land further away remains untouched.

Finally, Marx makes a dialectical observation about the historical trajectory of location as a basis for differential rent. On the one hand, the progress of social production has a levelling effect on location: it creates local markets and improves location by producing means of communication and transport. On the other hand, it increases the differences of geographical location, "by separating agriculture from manufacture and forming great centres of production, while also relatively isolating the countryside." So the development of capitalism cuts both ways: by integrating markets it reduces locational rents; by creating a sharp urban-rural divide, it intensifies them elsewhere. This dialectical formulation forestalls any simple teleological story about the "inevitable" decline or rise of locational differentials.

2.4 The Economic Character of "Natural" Fertility

Marx then sets location aside "for the moment" and analyses natural fertility in detail. This is one of the most philosophically sophisticated passages in the chapter, because it destabilizes the very concept of "natural" fertility.

Apart from climatic and similar aspects, differences in natural fertility are differences in the chemical composition of the soil — variations in the amount of nutrient elements for plants it contains. But even assuming identical chemical composition — and in this sense identical natural fertility — of two areas of land, their actual effective fertility will differ according to how far these nutrient elements occur in readily assimilable form, and can be directly used as plant foodstuffs. The extent to which the same natural fertility can actually be obtained on soils that are naturally equally fertile depends, therefore, in part on the chemical development of agriculture and in part on its mechanical development.

Marx draws the decisive conclusion: "Even though fertility is an objective property of the soil, it thus always involves an economic relation, a relation to the given chemical and mechanical level of agricultural development, and changes with this level of development." This is the crucial conceptual move. Fertility is not a pure datum of nature; it is a relational magnitude that shifts as the forces of production develop.

Marx gives concrete examples of this historical variability. By chemical means — liquid fertilizers on stiff clayey soil, or the calcination of heavy clayey soil — and by mechanical means — special ploughs for heavy soil, drainage — it is possible to remove obstacles that made soils of equal natural fertility less equal in practice. The sequence followed in bringing different types of soil into cultivation may also be changed: Marx notes that this happened between light sandy soil and heavy clayey soil at one period of English agricultural development, so that the historical order of cultivation was reversed by technical change.

He then generalizes: historically, in the successive course of cultivation, there can be both a transition from more fertile soil to less and vice versa. The same can happen as a result of artificially induced improvements in the composition of the soil, or of a mere change in the hierarchy of soil types when various sub-soil conditions come into play — once the subsoil also begins to be tilled and turned over into top layers. This is brought about partly by new agricultural methods (such as the cultivation of fodder grasses) and partly by mechanical means, which either turn the subsoil into the top layer or mix the two together, or else cultivate the subsoil without turning it up.

The upshot: "All these influences on the differential fertility of different soils boil down to the fact that as far as economic fertility is concerned, the level of labour productivity — in this case the ability of agriculture directly to exploit the natural fertility of the soil — an ability that varies with different stages of development — is just as much a factor in the so-called natural fertility of the soil as its chemical composition and other natural properties."

This is a compressed but powerful claim. The level of labour productivity is a constituent of what appears as the soil's "natural" fertility. The hierarchy of soil types — the very ordering A, B, C, D to which Marx will now turn — is thus not a fixed natural given but is calculated "in relation to this level of development." Marx adds that this is "of course always the case with simultaneous capital investments on different lands." The differential rent can therefore exhibit an increasing or decreasing series, for although the series is "given for the totality of lands actually cultivated," these have always been formed by a successive movement.

2.5 The Foundational Numerical Model: Table I and the Anatomy of Differential Rent

With the conceptual groundwork complete, Marx constructs the chapter's foundational model. He assumes four types of soil, A, B, C, D, and a price of wheat of £3 = 60 shillings per quarter. Crucially, since we are dealing only with differential rent, this price of 60 shillings per quarter is equal to the production costs on the worst soil — that is, equal to capital advanced plus average profit. Soil A is this worst soil.

The assumptions are: - A gives 1 quarter = 60s. for an outlay of 50s., i.e., a profit of 10s., or 20 per cent. - B yields 2 quarters = 120s. for the same outlay of 50s., so total profit = 70s., and the surplus profit (profit above the 10s. average) = 60s. - C yields 3 quarters = 180s. for 50s., total profit = 130s., surplus profit = 120s. - D yields 4 quarters = 240s. for 50s., total profit = 190s., surplus profit = 180s.

This yields Table I:

Type of soil Capital Product (Qrs) Product (Shillings) Profit (Qrs) Profit (Shillings) Rent (Qrs) Rent (Shillings)
A 50 1 60 1 10 — —
B 50 2 120 1½ 70 1 60
C 50 3 180 2½ 130 2 120
D 50 4 240 3½ 190 3 180
Total 10 qrs 600s. 6 qrs 360s.

The anatomy of rent is now fully visible. Marx computes the respective rents as: - For D: (190 − 10) shillings = 180s., the difference between D and A. - For C: (130 − 10) shillings = 120s., the difference between C and A. - For B: (70 − 10) shillings = 60s., the difference between B and A. - Total rent for B, C, and D = 6 quarters = 360 shillings, the sum of the differences D−A, C−A, and B−A.

Two structural points emerge immediately. First, the rent on each soil is not measured against zero profit but against the profit on the worst soil (A's 10s., or 1 quarter). This is essential: in differential rent, the worst soil pays no rent, and its profit is precisely the average profit (20 per cent on 50s.). Second, the total rent is the sum of the differences — it is inherently a relative magnitude, arising from the inequality of yields, not from any absolute property of the soil.

2.6 The Three Possible Paths of Formation: Descending, Ascending, Criss-Cross Series

Marx emphasizes that this series, "which represents a given product in a given condition," can occur in the abstract just as well as a decreasing series (from D down to A, from more fertile to ever less fertile soil) or as an increasing one (from A to D, from relatively infertile soil to ever more fertile soil). He also allows for a fluctuating, criss-cross movement — for example from D to C, C to A, and A to B. And he has already given reasons why this may also be the case in actual fact.

He then traces out the decreasing series in detail. The process is as follows. The price per quarter gradually rises from, say, 15 shillings to 60 shillings. Once the 4 quarters produced by D (which we may consider as millions) are no longer sufficient, the wheat price rises to the point at which the missing supply can be obtained from C — that is, the price would have to rise to 20 shillings per quarter. When the wheat price rises to 30 shillings per quarter, B can be brought into cultivation; and when it rises to 60 shillings per quarter, so can A, without any need for the capital applied to make do with a rate of profit lower than 20 per cent.

Under this descending sequence, the rent for D evolves dramatically: - First, 5 shillings per quarter = 20 shillings for the 4 quarters D produces. - Then 15 shillings per quarter = 60 shillings. - Finally 45 shillings per quarter = 180 shillings for 4 quarters.

Marx then introduces a subtle point about the profit rate. If the profit rate for D was also 20 per cent to start with, its total profit on the 4 quarters would be only 10 shillings, but this would represent more corn at a price of 15 shillings than at a price of 60 shillings. Since corn goes into the reproduction of labour-power, and one portion of each quarter must replace labour-power and another part constant capital, the surplus-value would therefore be higher under this assumption, and so too, with other factors remaining the same, would the rate of profit. Marx flags that "the question of the profit rate will have to be specially investigated in more detail" — a characteristic deferral that signals the interconnection of this analysis with the broader theory of the falling rate of profit.

He then traces the ascending sequence. If the process began with A, then as soon as new agricultural land had to be taken into cultivation, the price per quarter would rise above 60 shillings; but since the supply needed would come from B — a supply of 2 quarters — it would fall again to 60 shillings. For while B produces at 30 shillings per quarter, it sells at 60 shillings, since its supply is only just sufficient to meet the demand. A rent is thus formed for B which comes initially to 60 shillings, and similarly for C and D — still on the assumption that even though C and D supply at 20 shillings and 15 shillings per quarter respectively, the market price remains 60 shillings, since the supply of the one quarter that A provides is needed to satisfy the total demand.

Marx then clarifies the crucial difference between the two sequences in terms of how the cultivated area expands: "In this case the rise in demand above the supply that was satisfied first by A, and then by A and B, would not have meant that B, C and D could successively be cultivated, but simply that the overall cultivated area would be expanded, and it might so happen that the more fertile lands came under cultivation only later." In other words, the ascending sequence does not require that superior soils be "discovered" in order; it requires only the extension of cultivation, which may move erratically across soil types.

2.7 The Movement of the Profit Rate Under Rising vs Stationary Prices

Marx now draws out the divergent behaviour of the profit rate in the two sequences.

In the first (descending) sequence: rents rise with the increase in price, and the profit rate declines. This decline could be completely or partially offset by countervailing circumstances — a point Marx defers for detailed treatment later. But he issues a critical theoretical reminder: "the general rate of profit is not uniformly determined by the surplus-value in all spheres of production. It is not agricultural profit that determines industrial, but vice versa." This is a direct polemic against the Physiocratic and Ricardian tendency to treat agriculture as the "primitive" sphere whose profitability sets the tone for the economy. Marx's position is the reverse: agriculture is subordinated to the general rate of profit set in industry.

In the second (ascending) sequence: the profit rate on the capital invested remains the same. The mass of profit is represented by less corn, but the relative price of corn compared with other commodities will have risen. It is just that the increase in profit — where there is such an increase — "instead of flowing into the pockets of the industrialist farmers as a growing profit, separates off from profit in the form of rent." The price of corn remains stationary under the assumptions made here. In other words, in the ascending sequence the differential surplus that emerges is not absorbed into the general profit rate; it is siphoned off as rent. This is a vivid illustration of how landed property functions as a barrier to the equalization of the rate of profit in agriculture.

Marx concludes this stage of the argument with a summary: "The development and growth of differential rent remains the same, both when prices remain the same and when they rise, and both when there is a steady progression from worse soil to better and when there is a steady regression from better soil to worse." The pattern of formation is indifferent to the direction of movement; what matters is the inequality of soils.

2.8 Table II: Falling Prices, Criss-Cross Cultivation, and the Counter-Intuitive Fall in Money Rent

Marx now sets aside his earlier simplifying assumptions — (1) that the price rises in the one sequence and remains stationary in the other, and (2) that there is a steady progression from better soil to worse or conversely. He introduces a new and much more complex scenario.

Assume the demand for grain rises from the original 10 quarters to 17 quarters. Further assume that the worst soil A is displaced by another soil which yields 1½ quarters for a production cost of 60 shillings (50s. costs plus 10s. representing a profit of 20 per cent), its production price per quarter thus being 45s. Alternatively, that the old soil A has been improved as a result of rational cultivation, or has been cultivated more productively at constant costs — for example by the introduction of clover — so that its product rises to 1½ quarters for the same advance of capital. (Note: Marx offers here two distinct mechanisms for the same result — displacement by a new soil, or improvement of the old one.)

Further assume that soil types B, C, and D supply the same product afterwards as before, but that new types of soil come into cultivation: A' with a fertility between A and B, and B' and B" with fertilities between B and C.

Under these assumptions, three phenomena occur:

First: the production price of a quarter of wheat, or its governing market price, would fall from 60s. to 45s., or by 25 per cent.

Second: there would be at the same time a progression both from more fertile to less fertile soil and from less fertile soil to more fertile. Soil A' is more fertile than A, but less fertile than B, C, and D, which were cultivated previously; and B', B" are more fertile than A, A', and B but less fertile than C and D. The sequence thus takes on a criss-cross pattern. The progression is not towards soil that was absolutely less fertile compared with the formerly most fertile soil types C and D; on the other hand it is not towards absolutely more fertile soil, but simply to soil that is relatively more fertile compared with the previously least fertile A, or A and B.

Third: the rent on B would fall, and similarly the rent on C and D, but the total rental in corn would have risen from 6 qrs to 11 qrs (or, per the table, to 11⅔ qrs). The amount of cultivated and rent-bearing land would have increased, and the total product would increase from 10 qrs to 17. Profit, if it remained the same for A, would have risen when expressed in corn; but the profit rate itself might have risen, since relative surplus-value had done so. In this case, wages and thus the outlay on variable capital would have fallen, on account of the cheapening of the means of subsistence, and so too therefore would the total outlay. The total rental in money would have fallen from 360s. to 345s.

Here is the counter-intuitive result that Marx wishes to establish: money rent can fall while corn rent rises, and rents on the superior soils can fall while new rent-bearing soils are added, and all this can happen simultaneously with a falling price of grain. This is entirely impossible within the West-Malthus-Ricardo framework, which posited a necessary connection between rising rents and worsening cultivation.

2.9 Table III: Absolute Improvement of All Soils and the Rising Differential

Marx now presents a third scenario. Here, only the soil types A, B, C, and D are cultivated as before, but their productivity has risen in such a way that A produces 2 quarters instead of 1; B, 4 quarters instead of 2; C, 7 quarters instead of 3; and D, 10 quarters instead of 4. The same causes have operated differently on the different soil types. Total production rises from 10 quarters to 23. Assuming demand has absorbed these 23 quarters as a result of a rise in population and a fall in price, Marx arrives at Table III:

Type of soil Capital invested Product (Qrs) Product (Shillings) Price of production per quarter Profit (Qrs) Profit (Shillings) Rent (Qrs) Rent (Shillings)
A 50 2 60 30s. ⅓ 10 0 0
B 50 4 120 15s. 2¼ 70 2 60
C 50 7 210 8 4/7 s. 5⅓ 160 5 150
D 50 10 300 6s. 8⅓ 250 8 240
Total 23 15 qrs 450s.

Marx notes, with characteristic honesty, that "the numerical ratios are as arbitrary here as in all the other tables, but the assumptions are completely reasonable." He then specifies the two major assumptions on which Table III rests.

First and major assumption: the improvement in agriculture has differing effects on different types of soil, and in this case a greater effect on the better soil types C and D than on A and B. "Experience shows that this is the general rule, even if the opposite case is also possible." Marx draws the crucial counterfactual: if the improvement had a greater effect on the worse soils than on the better, the rent on the latter would fall instead of rising. With the absolute growth in fertility of all types of soil, the table presupposes a growth in the higher relative fertility of the better soil types C and D — hence a growth in the difference in products from the same capital investment and a rise in differential rent. This is entirely consistent with the Ricardian principle as Marx has reformulated it: rent depends on inequality, and the inequality can increase even as absolute fertility rises for all.

Second assumption: total demand keeps pace with the growing total product. Marx defends this assumption robustly against several objections:

  1. It is not necessary to consider the growth as happening suddenly; series III can be considered as arising gradually.
  2. "Secondly, it is wrong to maintain that the consumption of necessary means of subsistence does not grow when these become cheaper." The abolition of the Corn Laws in England (Marx cites Newman's Lectures on Political Economy) proved the opposite. The contrary conception arose only because major sudden harvest variations — themselves due simply to the weather — produce sudden disproportionate rises or falls in corn prices. If in this case the sudden and short-lived cheapening does not last long enough to exert its full effect in expanding consumption, the opposite is the case when the fall in price results from a fall in the governing production price itself, and is thus of longer duration.
  3. Part of the grain can be consumed in the form of spirits or beer, and the growth in consumption of these two articles "is in no way confined within narrow limits."
  4. The matter depends partly on population growth; and the country may also be a corn-exporting one — as England still was until past the middle of the eighteenth century — so that demand is not governed by the limits of national consumption alone.
  5. The increase and cheapening of wheat production may make wheat into the main staple foodstuff of the mass of the people instead of rye or oats, which already leads to a growth in the market for wheat. (The reverse case may arise with a declining product and increasing price.)

On these assumptions and figures, series III yields the following results: the price per quarter falls from 60s. to 30s., i.e. by 50 per cent; production grows from 10 qrs to 23, i.e. by 130 per cent; the rent on soil B remains the same; on soil C it rises by 25 per cent; on D by 33⅓ per cent; the total rental thus rises from £18 to £22½, i.e. by 25 per cent. So here is the decisive result: falling prices, rising production, and a rising total rental, combined with a rising rent on the best soils and stationary rent on the middling soil.

2.10 The Five General Results of the Three Tables

Marx now consolidates. The three tables — in which series I should be taken twice, rising from A to D and falling from D to A, and which may be interpreted either as representing distinctions within a given state of society (e.g. alongside one another in three different countries) or as succeeding one another at various points in the development of the same country — give five results.

(1) The sequence, when complete, always appears as a decreasing one, whatever the course of its formation might have been; for in considering rents, one will always first proceed from the soil that bears the maximum rent and only come last to that which yields no rent. This is an important methodological point: the appearance of rent analysis is always descending because analytic exposition begins with the highest rent; but this appearance must not be confused with the actual historical order of cultivation.

(2) The production price of the worst soil that yields no rent is always the governing market price, although, considering Table I where it is taken as an increasing sequence, this production price would remain stationary only if ever better land were cultivated. In this case, the price of the corn produced on the best soil is the governing one, in so far as the extent to which soil A remains the price-governing soil depends on the quantity produced on the best soil. If B, C, and D produce more than is demanded, A ceases to govern. Marx notes that Storch "has a vague idea of this when he makes the best type of soil the governing type." This mechanism explains why "American grain prices govern English ones" — the abundant production of superior American soils displaces the English margin as the price-governing locus.

(3) The differential rent arises from the difference in the natural fertility of soil types that is given for the level of agriculture found in existence at the time (leaving aside location), i.e. it arises from the limited extent of the best lands and from the fact that the same capital has to be applied to unequal types of soil, which thus yield unequal products for the same capital.

(4) The existence of a differential rent and a graduated differential rent can be based just as well on a declining scale (from better soil to worse) as on an ascending scale (from worse soil to better); or it can arise in an alternating criss-cross pattern. The former case may be formed by a progression either from D to A or from A to D. The latter involves both kinds of movement.

(5) According to its mode of formation, differential rent can develop along with a stationary, rising, or falling price of the agricultural product. In the case of a falling price, the total production and the total rental may rise, so that rent is formed on what was previously non-rent-bearing land, even though the worst soil A has been displaced by better, or has itself been improved, and even though the rent on other better types of soil, and even on the best types, falls (Table II); this process can also be linked with a fall in the total rental (in money). Finally, in the case of falling prices which result from a general improvement in cultivation, so that both the product of the worst soil and its price fall, rent on a portion of the better types of soil may remain the same or fall, while rising on the best types of soil.

Marx then formalizes the relative logic of rent carefully: the differential rent on any soil, compared with the worst soil, depends on the price of wheat per quarter, if the difference in the quantity produced is given. But when the price is given, it depends on the difference in the amount produced; and in the case of an increasing absolute fertility of all soil, the fertility of the better types rises relatively more than that of the worse soils, so that the size of this difference also grows. Given a price of 60s., therefore (Table I), the rent on D is determined by its differential product vis-à-vis A — i.e., by the excess of 3 qrs — so the rent is 3 × 60 = 180s. In Table III, however, where the price is 30s., it is determined by the amount of D's excess product over A, = 8 qrs; but 8 × 30 = 240s. The arithmetic is trivially simple, but the conceptual point is profound: rent is the product of two independently variable magnitudes — the price and the differential quantity — and can therefore move in either direction or be stationary even when one component moves.

2.11 The Refutation of West, Malthus, and Ricardo: The Law of Diminishing Returns

Marx now draws the great polemical conclusion. "In this way we can abandon the erroneous conception of differential rent which still prevailed with West, Malthus and Ricardo and which assumed a necessary progression to ever worse soil, or an ever declining agricultural fertility."

The refutation is comprehensive. Differential rent can arise with a progression to ever better soil (the ascending series). It can arise if a better soil takes the lowest place instead of that which was formerly the worst (displacement of A by A' or improvement of A). It can be linked with a steady advance in agriculture (the case of Table III). Its only precondition is the inequality of types of soil. In so far as the development of productivity is involved, it assumes that the rise in the absolute fertility of the total acreage does not abolish this inequality, but that it either increases it, leaves it stationary, or simply reduces it.

This is the theoretical core of the chapter's critical contribution. The Ricardian law of rent is not wrong in its mechanism (rent is differential), but it was falsely entangled with the historical thesis of declining agricultural fertility. Marx separates the two: the mechanism is general, the historical narrative is false.

It should be noted — and this is a nuance which the chapter implies but does not fully develop here — that the "law of diminishing returns," which West, Malthus, and Ricardo had elevated to a natural law, becomes under Marx's treatment a historically contingent and socially mediated phenomenon. What appears as diminishing returns in a given agricultural system may reflect the level of development of agricultural chemistry and mechanics, the institutional barriers to capital investment, or the class relations of landed property — not any inherent niggardliness of nature.

2.12 The Historical Illustrations: English Grain Prices and Rents

Marx now supplies three historical illustrations drawn from English experience, "in which series I should be taken twice," corresponding to distinct phases.

Phase 1 (start of the sixteenth century to the middle of the eighteenth century): England saw a steady fall in grain prices, despite the falling price of gold and silver, alongside a simultaneous growth in rents (taking the period as a whole), in the total rental, in the extent of land cultivated, in agricultural production, and in population. This corresponds to Table I combined with Table II in an upward direction — but in such a way that the worst soil A is either improved or taken out of cereal cultivation; which, however, does not mean it ceases to be used for other agricultural or industrial purposes.

Phase 2 (beginning of the nineteenth century until 1815): a continuous rise in grain prices, with a steady growth in rent, rental, the extent of land cultivated, agricultural production, and population. This corresponds to Table I in a downward direction. (Marx notes that references to the cultivation of worse lands at that time are to be supplied.)

Phase 3 (the time of Petty and Davenant): complaints of country people and landowners about improvements and the ploughing up of commons; fall in rents on better lands; rise in the total rental by extension of rent-bearing land.

These historical vignettes are not decorative. They prove two things. First, that the pattern of rent formation varies dramatically across historical epochs and cannot be reduced to a single "law." Second, that the social forces which produce falling rents on better lands (as in the Petty-Davenant era) can coexist with a rising total rental — a phenomenon Marx has already explained structurally in the discussion of the composition of the cultivated area which follows.

2.13 Market Value, "False Social Value," and the Landed Class: The Anti-Physiocratic Core

Before turning to the extensive cultivation question, Marx makes one of the chapter's most important theoretical statements. "In connection with differential rent in general, it should be noted that the market value is always above the total production price for the overall quantity produced."

Consider Table I. The total product of 10 qrs is sold for 600s., since the market price is determined by the production price of A, which comes to 60s. per qr. The actual production price, however, is: - A: 1 qr = 60s. (i.e., 1 qr per 60s.) - B: 2 qrs = 60s. (i.e., 1 qr per 30s.) - C: 3 qrs = 60s. (i.e., 1 qr per 20s.) - D: 4 qrs = 60s. (i.e., 1 qr per 15s.) - 10 qrs = 240s. total; average 1 qr = 24s.

So the real production price of the 10 qrs is 240s.; they are sold for 600s., 250 per cent too much. The real average price for 1 qr is 24s.; the market price 60s., similarly 250 per cent too much.

Marx's interpretation is decisive: "This is determination by a market value brought about by competition on the basis of the capitalist mode of production; it is competition that produces a false social value. This results from the law of market value to which agricultural products are subjected. The determination of the market value of products, i.e. also of products of the soil, is a social act, even if performed by society unconsciously and unintentionally, and it is based necessarily on the exchange-value of the product and not on the soil and the differences in its fertility."

This passage repays careful reading. The market value of grain — determined, under capitalist competition, by the most expensive (worst-soil) production price that is needed to satisfy demand — is a social determination. It has nothing intrinsically to do with the soil. Yet because the price is socially established at the level of the worst land, the products of the better land command a price that is above their own production price — and the difference accrues to the landowner. The soil is thus the occasion for the surplus, but not its source. The source is the social act of valuation.

Marx then draws the radical implication. If we imagine that the capitalist form of society has been abolished and that society has been organized as a conscious association working according to a plan, the 10 qrs represent a quantity of autonomous labour-time equal to that contained in 240s. Society would therefore not purchase this product at 2½ times the actual labour-time contained in it; the basis for a class of landowners would thereby disappear — which would have the same effect as a cheapening of the product to the same amount by foreign imports.

This is a subtle and important argument. Marx distinguishes two propositions: - Correct: keeping to the present mode of production, but assuming differential rent accrued to the state (as opposed to private landlords), the prices of agricultural products would remain the same, if other factors did too. (Nationalization of land would not by itself alter the market price governed by the worst soil.) - Wrong: to say that the value of these products would remain the same if capitalist production were replaced by association. Under association, the social labour-time contained in the grain would be recognized directly, and the "false social value" would vanish.

The distinction between price and value here is crucial. Under capitalism, the price is fixed by competition irrespective of who receives the rent. But the value — measured by social labour-time — is less than the price; the difference is a social overpayment. The fact that "commodities of the same kind have an identical market price is the way in which the social character of value is realized on the basis of the capitalist mode of production, and in general of production depending on commodity exchange between individuals."

The class-theoretic conclusion is stated with precision: "Where society, considered as a consumer, pays too much for agricultural products, this is a minus for the realization of its labour-time in agricultural production, but it forms a plus for one portion of society, the landowners." Rent is thus a transfer — not a creation of value. It is a portion of society's labour-time appropriated by a class that contributes nothing to production. This is the anti-Physiocratic core: the soil does not create value; the social form of valuation does, and landed property intercepts the surplus thereby generated.

2.14 The Transition to Differential Rent II: Extensive Cultivation and the Rate of Rent

Marx now introduces a "second circumstance, important for what will be presented in the next chapter as differential rent II." Up to this point, the analysis has been about rent per acre and the difference between individual and general production prices per acre. The new consideration is: how many acres of each type of soil are under cultivation. This bears directly on the size of the rental (the total rent for the whole cultivated area), but Marx says it also serves as a transition to the discussion of a rise in the rate of rent, even when prices do not rise, or the differences in the relative fertility of the soil types do not rise when prices fall.

He restates Table I with the explicit addition of the acreage column:

Marx now constructs three variants on Table I by varying the number of acres of each soil type.

Table Ia — all four types double in acreage:

Type of soil Acres Price of production per acre Total Product Rent in grain Rent in money

Total 12 acres £36 36 qrs 24 qrs £72

Marx's analysis of these variants begins with the fundamental point: in all four cases (I, Ia, Ib, Ic), the rent per acre remains the same, because the product of the same quantity of capital on each acre of the same type of soil is unchanged. He is assuming only two things: (i) that the various types of soil share the total cultivated area in a definite ratio in any country at a particular time; and (ii) that the ratio in which the total cultivated area is divided between them changes over time (or differs between countries).

Ia vs Ib comparison: When cultivation in all four classes grows in the same proportion (Ia), a doubling of the number of acres doubles the total production and similarly the corn and money rent. This is trivially proportional.

Ib and Ic vs I: In both cases, the area under cultivation triples (from 4 to 12 acres). But the composition of the expansion differs radically.

In Ib, classes A and B take the major share of the growth: of the 8 newly cultivated acres, 3 each fall into A and B — a total of 6 — while only 1 each, a total of 2, fall into C and D. So three-quarters of the increase takes place on A and B, and only one-quarter on C and D. On this assumption, the tripling of the expanse under cultivation does not involve a tripling of the product: it rises not from 10 to 30, but only to 26. On the other hand, because a substantial part of the growth takes place on A, which yields no rent, and because of the growth on the better lands the major part is on class B, the corn rent rises only from 6 qrs to 14, and the money rent from £18 to £42.

In Ic, by contrast, the non-rent-paying land does not increase its extent at all, while land yielding the minimal rent exhibits only a weak increase (B goes from 1 to 2 acres); the bulk of the growth accrues to C and D. Here, with the tripling of the cultivated area, production rises from 10 qrs to 36, i.e. by more than three times; the corn rent from 6 qrs to 24, or four times; and the money rent similarly from £18 to £72.

The general rule Marx extracts: In all these cases, the price of the agricultural product remains stationary, "in the nature of things"; in each case the total rental grows with the expansion of cultivation, as long as this does not take place exclusively on the worst lands, which pay no rent. But the growth varies. To the extent that the expansion takes place on the better types of soil, so that the quantity produced does not just grow in proportion to the expansion of the land cultivated but more steeply, the corn and money rent grows. To the extent that the worst soil and the adjacent categories take the principal share of the increase (which assumes that the worst soil is a constant category), the total rental does not rise in proportion to the expansion of cultivation.

From this, Marx derives a proportional law: given two countries in which land A, which yields no rent, is of the same quality, the rental stands in inverse proportion to the aliquot part of the total cultivated area composed by the worst and less good soil types, and hence also in inverse proportion to the quantities produced by identical capital investments on equal areas. The greater the share of the cultivated area occupied by inferior soils, the lower the total rental relative to the acreage — and vice versa.

He then states a critical dual-aspect conclusion: The proportion between the amount of the worst cultivated soil and that of the better in the total area of a country thus has an effect on the total rental which is opposite to the effect that the relation between the quality of the worst cultivated soil and that of the better and best has on the rent per acre, and hence, with other circumstances remaining the same, also on the rental. "The confusion between these two aspects has given rise to all kinds of confused objections against differential rent." This is a masterful piece of analytical housekeeping — Marx is showing how apparently conflicting data about rents can be reconciled once the two distinct variables (quality ratio vs. quantity composition) are disentangled.

2.16 The Structure of Average Rent and the Rate of Rent: The "Overlooked Factor"

Marx now introduces two derived magnitudes which will be central to the rest of the chapter.

Average rent per acre = total rental ÷ total number of acres tilled. (This is what is generally used in statistical works.)

Rate of rent = total rental ÷ total capital invested in the soil.

He then compares the three cases.

Case I vs Case Ia: In I, the total area is 4 acres, the overall rental is £18, and hence the average rent — including the soil that bears no rent — is £4½ per acre. The total rental of £18 is produced by applying a capital of £10. The ratio between these two figures is the rate of rent: 180 per cent. In Ia, the same rate of rent occurs: 8 acres, total rental £36, capital £20, average rent £4½ per acre, rate of rent 180 per cent. So proportional expansion leaves both averages unchanged.

Case Ib: rent of £42 for 12 acres, i.e. an average rent of £3½ per acre. Total capital laid out is £30, i.e. a rate of rent of 140 per cent. So the average rent per acre has fallen from £4½ to £3½ (£1 less), and the rate of rent has fallen from 180 per cent to 140 per cent. This happens even though the rent on all soil types remains the same, whether reckoned per acre or on the capital invested. The reason: three-quarters of the increase occurred on soil A, which bears no rent, and soil B, which bears only the minimal rent.

Extreme sub-case of Ib: if the total expansion had taken place simply on soil A, we would have 9 acres of A, 1 of B, 1 of C, and 1 of D. The total rental would still be £18, giving an average rent per acre on these 12 acres of £1½; while £18 rent on a capital of £30 laid out gives a rate of rent of 60 per cent. "The average rent would have sharply declined, whether reckoned per acre or on the capital applied, while the total rental would not have grown."

Case Ic vs I and Ib: Compared with I, the acreage has tripled, and so has the capital laid out. The total rental is £72 for 12 acres, i.e. £6 per acre as against £4½ per acre in case I. The rate of rent on the capital laid out (£72 : £30) is 240 per cent instead of 180 per cent. The total product has risen from 10 qrs to 36. Compared with Ib — where the total acreage under cultivation, the capital applied, and the differences between the types of soil tilled all remain the same, though differently distributed — the product is 36 qrs instead of 26 qrs, the average rent per acre £6 instead of £3½, and the rate of rent on an equal total capital advanced is 240 per cent instead of 140 per cent.

Marx now states the general conclusions. Under the assumptions — a stationary price of grain because the yield on the worst, non-rent-bearing land remains the same; the same differences in fertility between the various categories of soil cultivated; an equal respective product from equal capital investment on equal aliquot parts (acres) of the area cultivated in each class of soil; and a constant ratio between rent per acre on each type of soil and an equal rate of rent on the capital invested in each piece of land of the same kind — we get:

Firstly: The rental always grows with an expansion of the cultivated area and therefore with an increased capital investment, except for the case when the entire growth falls on the non-rent-bearing soil.

Secondly: Both the average rent per acre (total rental ÷ total number of acres tilled) and the average rate of rent (total rental ÷ total capital invested) may vary very significantly; and even if both move in the same direction, they may still move in different ratios.

Leaving aside the case where the growth takes place simply on the non-rent-bearing soil A, the average rent per acre and the average rate of rent on the capital invested in agriculture depend on the proportionate shares that the various classes of soil make up within the total cultivated area — or, what comes to the same thing, they depend on the way in which the total capital applied is distributed over soil types of different fertility.

Whether much land is tilled or only a little — so that the total rental is larger or smaller (except for the case where the growth is solely on A) — the average rent per acre or the average rate of rent on the capital applied remains the same as long as the proportions in which the different types of soil participate in the total acreage remain constant. Despite a rise in the total rental as cultivation extends and more capital is invested, and even a major rise, the average rent per acre and the average rate of rent on capital fall, if the expansion of the rent-free lands and those that only bear a small differential rent is steeper than that of the better land. Conversely, the average rent per acre and the average rate of rent on capital rise to the extent that the better lands constitute a relatively larger share of the total area, and hence relatively more capital investment falls on them.

Marx now draws out the crucial statistical illusion. If we consider the average rent per acre of the total cultivated land — which is "what is generally done in statistical works," since either different countries are compared at the same time or different periods in the history of the same country — we see that the average level of rent per acre, and hence also the total rental, corresponds to a certain extent (without being identical: in fact the proportion tends to increase) not to the relative but to the absolute fertility of agriculture in a country, i.e. to the quantity of products supplied on average by a given area.

Why? "For the greater the share of the total area constituted by the better types of soil, the greater is the volume of products from the same capital investment and the same land area; and the greater, too, is the average rent per acre. And vice versa." This creates a statistical appearance: rent appears as determined not by the ratio of differential fertility but rather by the absolute fertility, which would refute the law of differential rent.

Marx's response is one of the chapter's most important methodological moves. "This is why certain phenomena are denied, or else the attempt is made to explain them in terms of non-existent distinctions in average grain prices and the differential fertilities of lands under cultivation, phenomena whose only basis is that the proportion of the total rental, either to the total area of land cultivated or to the total capital invested in the soil — given the same fertility for the non-rent-bearing soil and hence equal production prices, and given the same differences between the various soil types — is not determined only by the rent per acre or by the rate of rent on capital but just as much by the relative proportion of each soil type in the total acreage tilled; or, what comes to the same thing, by the distribution of the total capital applied among the various types of soil."

Marx then delivers the punchline: "Up till now, this factor has been completely overlooked, in a quite striking fashion." And he identifies the broader implication for the investigation: "It still shows, and this is important for the further course of our investigation, that the relative level of average rents per acre, and the average rate of rent or the ratio of the total rental to the total capital invested in the soil, may rise or fall even though prices, the difference in fertility of the lands under cultivation and the rent per acre or rate of rent for the capital invested per acre in each actual rent-bearing soil category, or for all actually rent-bearing capital, all remain the same, simply by an expansion of the cultivated area."

This is the chapter's most original technical contribution. Marx has isolated the composition of the cultivated area — the relative proportions of soil types — as an independent variable determining the average rent and the rate of rent, entirely separate from price movements or fertility differentials. This decoupling of statistical aggregates from the underlying per-acre determinants is what allows him to explain the apparent contradictions in rent data that orthodox political economy could not reconcile.

2.17 Additional Points (I): The Price of Untilled Land and the Capitalization of Future Rent

Marx introduces the remaining stages of the chapter with a note: "The following additional points have still to be made, in relation to the first form of differential rent, although they also apply in part to the second form."

Firstly, he extends the analysis to the price of land itself, including untilled land. He begins with the observation already established: the average rent per acre or the average rate of rent on capital may rise with the extension of cultivation, given stationary prices and unchanged differential fertility of the lands tilled. As soon as all the land in a country is appropriated, and capital investment on the land, agriculture, and population have all reached a certain level — factors taken for granted once the capitalist mode of production becomes dominant and takes control of agriculture too — the price of the untilled land of various qualities (assuming only differential rent) is determined by the price of land of equivalent quality and location that is tilled. The price is the same — after deducting the additional cost of ploughing up new land — even though this land does not bear any rent.

Marx then recalls a fundamental principle: "The price of land is of course nothing more than capitalized rent." Even in the case of cultivated lands, it is only future rents that are paid for in their price; for example, twenty years' rent is paid at one stroke if the determining interest rate is 5 per cent. As soon as land is sold, it is sold as rent-bearing; the prospective character of the rent (which is considered here as the fruit of the soil, something that it is only in surface appearance) does not distinguish the uncultivated land from the cultivated.

The striking consequence: "The price of untilled land, like its rent, which is what this contracted formula represents, is pure illusion as long as this land is not actually used. But it is determined a priori in this way, and is realized as soon as buyers are found." Thus, if the actual average rent in a country is determined by its actual average annual rental and the ratio of this rental to the total cultivated area, the price of the untilled portion is determined by the price of the tilled and is therefore simply a reflection of the capital investment in the tilled lands, and its results there. Since, with the exception of the worst land, all types of soil bear rent — and this rent, as we shall go on to see in the case of differential rent II, rises with the amount of capital and the corresponding intensity of cultivation — a nominal price is thereby formed for the untilled portions of land, so that these too become a commodity, a source of wealth for their owner.

Marx draws a vivid practical implication: "This explains at the same time why the price of land rises for the entire area, even for untilled land. (Opdyke.) Land speculation, e.g. in the United States, depends on this reflection which capital and labour cast on untilled land."

This passage extends the analysis of differential rent into the analysis of land as fictitious capital. The price of land is capitalized rent, and rent itself is a social claim on surplus profit; therefore, land speculation is not a natural phenomenon but an artifact of the social relations of production and the legal form of landed property. The reflection of capital investment on tilled lands onto the untilled land is the mechanism by which the appearance of a natural value of land is produced — an appearance that underwrites rentier speculation.

2.18 Additional Points (II): Location, Enclaves, and the Michigan/New York Examples

Secondly, Marx turns to the direction of extension of cultivation. All progress in the extension of cultivation takes place either towards worse soil or on the various given types of soil in different proportions, according to how these are to be found. Progression towards worse soil, of course, is never from free choice; taking the capitalist mode of production as given, it can only be the result of rising prices, and in any mode of production only the result of necessity. But this is not absolutely the case.

Why? Marx lists three conditions under which cultivation might extend to worse rather than better soil:

(a) Location: "Bad soil may be relatively preferred to better on account of its location, which is decisive for every extension of cultivation in new countries."

(b) Intermingling of soil types: "even though the soil formation in a certain region may be fertile on the whole, better and worse soil may be closely intermingled in some places, so that the inferior soil has to be cultivated simply because of its proximity to the better."

(c) Enclaves: "If the worse soil forms enclaves within the better, the better soil gives it the advantage of location as against more fertile land that is not yet part of the cultivated area or about to become so."

Marx illustrates with two examples. The state of Michigan was one of the first Western states to export corn. Its soil on the whole is poor. But its proximity to the state of New York and its water routes via the Great Lakes and the Erie Canal gave it at first an initial advantage over the states further west, though these were more fertile by nature.

The example of this state, in comparison with the state of New York, also shows the transition from better soil to worse. The soil of New York state, and particularly its western part, is of uneven fertility, particularly for wheat cultivation. Rapacious cultivation made this fertile soil infertile, and then the Michigan soil appeared more fertile. Note the crucial phrase: "rapacious cultivation" — the depletion of the soil through a particular mode of agricultural exploitation, not natural exhaustion, produced the apparent switch in fertility ranking. This is a vivid illustration of Marx's earlier conceptual point about the historicity of "natural" fertility.

He cites J. W. Johnston (Notes on North America, London, 1851, I, pp. 220–23): "In 1838, wheaten flour was shipped at Buffalo for the West; and the wheat-region of New York, with that of Upper Canada, were the main sources of its supply. Now after only twelve years, an enormous supply of wheat and flour is brought from the West, along Lake Erie, and shipped upon the Erie Canal for the East, at Buffalo and the adjoining port of Blackrock... The effect of these large arrivals from the Western States — which were unnaturally stimulated during the years of European famine — has been to render wheat less valuable in western New York, to make the wheat culture less remunerative, and to turn the attention of the New York farmers more to grazing and dairy husbandry, fruit culture, and other branches of rural economy, in which they think the North-West will be unable so directly to compete with them."

This is a rich historical case. Note the multiple variables at work: the water routes (location); the "unnatural stimulation" of European famine demand (external demand); the rapid shift of western New York farmers out of wheat and into grazing, dairy, and fruit (specialization and differentiation of agriculture); and the resulting downward pressure on wheat prices and rents in the older region. The example demonstrates that the "inevitable" progression from better to worse soil is not inevitable at all — location, transport infrastructure, and market demand can produce the appearance of declining natural fertility when what is actually declining is the profitability of the former best soils under changed market conditions.

2.19 Additional Points (III): The Illusion of Colonial Fertility and the Nature of Colonial Surplus

Thirdly, Marx attacks a widely held error: "It is false to assume that the soil in those colonies and other new countries that can export corn at cheaper prices is therefore necessarily of greater natural fertility." In this case — where colonial grain undersells the older countries — grain is sold "not only below its value, but also below its price of production, i.e. below the production price determined by the average rate of profit in the older countries."

This is a crucial nuance. The low price of colonial grain is not proof of superior fertility. It may be proof that colonial conditions permit sale below the normal production price — that is, temporarily below the value-governing price of the world market.

Marx quotes Johnston again: we "are accustomed to attach the idea of great natural productiveness and of boundless tracts of rich land, to those new States from which come the large supplies of wheat that are annually poured into the port of Buffalo." But this appearance "depends first of all on economic conditions."

Marx lists the economic conditions:

(1) One-sided specialization in agriculture. "The entire population of a state such as this, e.g. Michigan, begins by being almost exclusively engaged in agriculture, and particularly in the mass crops which alone can be exchanged for industrial goods and tropical products. Their entire surplus product thus takes the shape of corn." This "fundamentally distinguishes the colonial states founded on the basis of the modern world market from those of earlier times, and particularly those of antiquity." The colonies "receive ready-made, through the world market, products that they would otherwise have to produce themselves, such as clothing, tools, etc." It is only on this basis that the Southern states of the Union could make cotton into their principal product. The division of labour on the world market permits this.

Hence Marx's conclusion: "if, considering their newness and their relatively small population, they appear to produce a very large surplus product, this is not due to the fertility of their soil or to the productiveness of their labour, but rather to the one-sided form of this labour and thus of the surplus product in which it is expressed."

This is a brilliant application of world-systems reasoning avant la lettre. The appearance of super-abundant colonial production is a specialization effect in the international division of labour, not a fertility effect. It anticipates the analysis of comparative advantage as a product of the world market rather than a natural endowment.

(2) Virgin soil accumulates easily assimilated plant nutrients. "Besides, relatively less fertile soil which is tilled for the first time and has never been touched by agriculture before has accumulated so much in the way of easily assimilated plant nutrients, at least in its top layers, that it will yield harvests for a quite long period without any fertilizer — as long as the climatic conditions are not completely unfavourable — even on quite superficial tilling." In the Western prairies, "a further factor is that scarcely any clearing costs are required, since nature has made them already arable."

(3) The surplus arises from the acreage, not the yield per acre. "In less fertile regions of this kind the surplus comes not from the high fertility of the soil, i.e. from the yield per acre, but rather from the great acreage that can be cultivated superficially, since this land costs the tiller nothing, or at least only an infinitesimal amount compared with the older countries." This is true for share-cropping, as in parts of New York, Michigan, Canada. A family tills, say, 100 acres superficially; the product per acre is not large, but the product of 100 acres provides a sizable surplus for sale. Cattle, etc., can be grazed almost cost-free on natural pasture, without any need for artificial meadows. "The decisive thing here is not the quality of the soil but the quantity."

The possibility of superficial cultivation is "of course more or less rapidly exhausted in inverse proportion to the fertility of the new soil and in direct proportion to the export of its product." Johnston: "And yet such a country will give excellent crops, even of wheat, and will supply to those who skim the first cream off the country, a large surplus of this grain to send to market" (op. cit., p. 225).

Marx then makes a sharp historical-structural contrast: "In countries where agriculture is older, any such kind of extensive farming is made impossible by the property relations, the price of uncultivated land as determined by that of cultivated, etc." Here the earlier discussion of the price of untilled land returns with direct bearing on agricultural practice: the appropriation of land and the pricing of untilled land at capitalized future rent prevent the kind of extensive, low-intensity farming that is possible on the frontier.

Empirical evidence: In the state of Michigan, 465,900 acres were sown to wheat in 1848, producing 4,739,300 bushels, an average of 10½ bushels per acre — less than 9 bushels per acre after deducting the seed-corn. Out of twenty-nine counties in the state, two produced an average of 7 bushels; three an average of 8; two 9; seven 10; six 11; three 12; four 13; a single county 16; and one 18 bushels per acre (op. cit., p. 225). This dispersion shows enormous variability across counties — evidence, again, that "fertility" is not uniform, and that the surplus comes from quantity of acreage rather than exceptional yield.

Marx's conclusion is aesthetic as well as analytical: "As far as practical agriculture is concerned, higher fertility of the soil is the same thing as a greater possibility of immediately exploiting its fertility. Immediate exploitation may be more possible with a naturally poor soil than with a naturally rich one; and this is the kind of soil which a colonist will take up first, and must take up when capital is scarce."

This is a reversal, in the domain of colonial agriculture, of the Ricardian presumption. A colonist with scarce capital does not choose the most fertile soil as such; he chooses the soil whose fertility can be immediately exploited with minimal capital outlay. A naturally poor soil that requires no clearing and yields immediately may be preferred to a naturally richer soil that requires investment to bring into production. Thus the hierarchy of soils under colonial conditions is determined by the availability of capital, not by natural fertility considered in the abstract.

2.20 Additional Points (IV): Relative Overproduction and the Autonomy of Capital Accumulation

Finally, Marx turns to a decisive point about the dynamic of agricultural expansion. "The extension of cultivation to larger areas (apart from the case just considered, in which resort has to be had to a worse soil than that formerly tilled), on the various soil types from A to D, for example, and therefore the tilling of greater areas of B and C, in no way depends on a previous rise in grain prices, any more than the anticipatory annual expansion of a cotton spinning-mill, for instance, depends on a continuous rise in the price of yarn."

The analogy is crucial. Capitalist production expands in anticipation of demand, not in response to already-realized price increases. A cotton spinning-mill expands its capacity before prices rise, in the expectation that demand will absorb the new production; in the same way, agricultural capital extends cultivation onto new land of whatever soil type is available, without waiting for a price rise.

Even though a major rise or fall in market price does have an effect on the scale of production, "there is still, apart from this — and even given average prices, whose level neither inhibits production nor gives it an exceptional boost — the same perpetual relative overproduction in agriculture which is inherently identical with accumulation and which in the case of other modes of production is directly caused by the increase in population, and in the colonies by a steady immigration." Demand steadily grows, and with this prospect new capital is continuously invested in new land, even though this happens for different crops and according to particular circumstances. "The formation of new capitals brings this about automatically."

Marx then notes the subjective dimension on the side of the individual capitalist: "But as far as the individual capitalist is concerned, he measures the scale of his production by the capital he has available, to the extent that he can still control it himself. His intention is to take as big a share of the market as possible. If there is overproduction, he blames it on his competitors, not on himself. The individual capitalist can extend his production just as much by appropriating a greater aliquot share of the given market as by expanding this market itself."

This final passage is a gem of Marx's method. It shows how the aggregate phenomenon — the "perpetual relative overproduction" of agriculture, which is inherently identical with accumulation — arises from the uncoordinated decisions of individual capitals, each seeking to maximize its market share. The "overproduction" is relative (relative to the purchasing power of the market, not to need), and it is the ordinary form of capital accumulation in a sector characterized by many competing capitals. It also disposes of any "Say's Law" intuition that production automatically matches demand; in agriculture, as in other branches of capitalist production, expansion is anticipatory, competitive, and perpetually outrunning realized demand.

The chapter ends on this note — with the demonstration that the extension of cultivation onto new soil is not a function of some natural tendency toward "diminishing returns," but of the autonomous logic of capital accumulation in a sector where the pricing mechanism (governed by the worst soil) guarantees a surplus profit to the better soils, and where landed property stands ready to appropriate that surplus as rent.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

3.1 Fundamental Principles Revealed

The chapter yields several foundational principles that extend well beyond the technical analysis of rent.

Principle 1: Rent is a social relation, not a natural fact. The entire chapter is organized around the demonstration that differential rent arises from the social act of market valuation, not from the inherent productive power of the soil. Fertility is a relational concept — an "objective property of the soil" that "always involves an economic relation" to a given chemical and mechanical level of agricultural development. Location, likewise, is a function of the social transportation and communications infrastructure. The "natural" bases for differential rent — fertility and location — are therefore historically variable and socially constituted. Marx's conceptual move prefigures the modern critique of "natural" categories in economics: what appears as a natural given is often a frozen social relation.

Principle 2: Rent is differential, and differential rent is relative. The rent on any soil is its surplus profit over the worst soil in cultivation. It is not a positive attribute of the soil but a difference. This means the abolition of differential rent would require abolishing the inequality among soils (impossible in full) or abolishing the social form (landed property and commodity pricing) that converts inequality into revenue. It also means that a fall in the rent on the best soils, or on the middling soils, is entirely compatible with a rise in the total rental — depending on the composition of the cultivated area and the distribution of capital among soil types.

Principle 3: The quantity and composition of the cultivated area is an independent determinant of aggregate rent. This is Marx's most original technical contribution in the chapter. The average rent per acre and the rate of rent on capital can rise or fall with prices, fertility differentials, and per-acre rents held constant, simply because the relative proportions of different soil types in the total cultivated area change. Statistical averages therefore carry no direct information about the underlying law of rent. Marx warns that the "confusion between these two aspects has given rise to all kinds of confused objections against differential rent" — a warning that remains highly relevant to empirical work on agricultural rents and land values.

Principle 4: The Ricardian law of diminishing returns is a historical artifact, not a natural law. By showing that differential rent can arise under ascending, descending, or criss-cross sequences of cultivation, and can coexist with rising, stationary, or falling prices, Marx dissolves the connection between rent and declining agricultural fertility. The only precondition of differential rent is the inequality of soils; the direction of cultivation is determined by contingent historical circumstances — location, capital availability, technical change, demand, transport infrastructure.

Principle 5: The price of land is capitalized rent, and therefore a form of fictitious capital. The price of untilled land is determined by the price of tilled land of equivalent quality and location. It is therefore a reflection of past and anticipated capital investment in agriculture, not a natural value of the soil. The land market, and therefore land speculation, rests on this reflection. This principle establishes the continuity between the analysis of ground-rent and the analysis of fictitious capital (State debt, joint-stock equity, etc.) which occupies other parts of Volume III.

Principle 6: Colonial "fertility" is an illusion generated by the world market. The apparent natural richness of new lands — the boundless prairies of the American West — is produced by one-sided specialization in the international division of labour (which permits the entire surplus product to take the form of corn), by the virgin accumulation of easily assimilated plant nutrients, by the absence of clearing costs, and by the free or near-free appropriation of extensive acreage. The decisive factor is quantity of acreage, not quality of soil. This principle anticipates later analyses of settler-colonial economies and the specific forms of ground-rent they generate.

Principle 7: Agriculture exhibits "perpetual relative overproduction" inherent in accumulation. The extension of cultivation does not wait for price rises; it precedes them, driven by the individual capitalist's drive for market share. This "relative overproduction" is the ordinary form of accumulation in agriculture, just as it is in industry. It is not a pathology but a structural feature of competitive capitalism.

3.2 Broader Theoretical and Practical Implications

The chapter's implications ramify far beyond rent theory narrowly conceived.

For the theory of value and distribution, the chapter demonstrates that rent is a transfer of surplus value, not a creation of value. The "false social value" of agricultural products — their market value above their total production price — is a social overpayment that benefits a class contributing nothing to production. Under a consciously planned association of producers, this overpayment would disappear. This insight is central to Marx's broader project of demonstrating that all revenue forms (profit, interest, rent) are ultimately portions of the surplus value extracted from wage-labour.

For the critique of political economy, the chapter is a sustained demonstration of the pitfalls of treating historically specific economic forms as natural categories. West, Malthus, and Ricardo had conflated the mechanism of differential rent (which Marx accepts and refines) with a historical narrative of declining natural fertility (which Marx refutes). The conflation served an apologetic function: it treated the rentier's income as a natural tribute to the niggardliness of nature, rather than as a social claim on the surplus product of labour. Marx's decomposition of the two — mechanism vs. historical narrative — is a model of critical method.

For economic history and empirical analysis, the chapter provides a toolkit for understanding puzzles in rent statistics. The apparent paradox — that average rents can fall while total rentals rise, or rise while prices fall — is resolved once the composition of the cultivated area is treated as an independent variable. This toolkit has been applied by later scholars to historical data on agricultural rents in England, Prussia, Ireland, and the United States.

For the analysis of land and finance, the discussion of the price of untilled land and land speculation establishes the connection between ground-rent and fictitious capital. The land market is one of the earliest and most important forms of capitalized future income; Marx's analysis here is continuous with later analyses of credit, joint-stock capital, and the financialization of land and real estate.

For political strategy, the chapter's demonstration that land nationalization would not, by itself, alter agricultural prices (since price is governed by the worst soil in cultivation, independent of who receives the rent) has important implications. It implies that the abolition of rent as a private revenue requires not merely state appropriation of rent, but a transformation of the mode of production itself — the replacement of commodity pricing (and the "false social value" it generates) by a system of consciously planned social labour-time accounting. This is the deepest political implication of the chapter: the basis of a class of landowners can only disappear in a society organized as a conscious association of producers.

For ecological economics, the chapter's insistence that "natural" fertility is a relational and historically variable magnitude anticipates contemporary discussions of soil fertility as a function of agricultural technique and investment. The observation that "rapacious cultivation" can exhaust a naturally fertile soil while superficially cultivated virgin land appears temporarily more fertile is an early recognition of what is now called "soil mining" or "land degradation" — the exhaustion of accumulated natural capital through unsustainable exploitation.

3.3 The Chapter's Place in Marx's System

Chapter 39 is the opening move in Marx's demolition of the trinity formula — the ideological representation of capitalist society as composed of three independent sources of revenue. By demonstrating that differential rent in its first form is entirely a transformed surplus profit, Marx prepares the ground for showing that the second form (differential rent II, on successive investments of capital on the same land) and absolute rent (arising from the lower organic composition of capital in agriculture) are similarly derived, not original, forms of surplus value. The chapter thus belongs to the systematic critique of the "fetishism" that attributes revenue to things (land, capital) rather than to social relations (the exploitation of wage-labour).

The chapter is also, in its own way, a model of dialectical exposition. Marx begins with an apparently simple Ricardian formula, corrects it, decomposes it, complicates it via multiple numerical scenarios, and arrives at a conclusion far richer and more nuanced than the original formula. Each new table is not merely a new illustration but a new dimension of the same phenomenon — price level, direction of cultivation, absolute fertility, composition of cultivated area, capital distribution — until the phenomenon is fully characterized. The apparent randomness of the initial "three tables" gives way to the systematic isolation of independent variables. And the historical illustrations — England 1600–1750, England 1800–1815, Petty and Davenant, Michigan, New York — are not decorative but are the empirical confirmation of the theoretical possibilities laid out in the tables.

The final message, if we are to distill it, is this: the rent of land, that most ancient and apparently most natural of revenues, is in fact a modern, social, and historically specific phenomenon. It arises from the social act of valuation under competitive capitalism, it varies with the composition of the cultivated area and the distribution of capital, it supports a class that contributes nothing to production, and its basis would disappear in a society that organized its production consciously rather than through the blind interplay of commodity exchange. The chapter is thus both an exercise in exact economic analysis and a manifesto for the supersession of the social relations that produce rent at all.

In sum, Chapter 39 establishes the analytic foundations for the theory of ground-rent that will occupy the remainder of Part VI, while simultaneously demonstrating — in its own movement from Ricardian formula to socialist conclusion — the method of critique that Marx had elaborated throughout Capital: the relentless dissolution of naturalized social forms into the historically specific relations of production from which they arise.
