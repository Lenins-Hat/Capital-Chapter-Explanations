Volume 3 • Chapter 50: Illusions Created by Competition

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem

Chapter 50 of Capital, Volume III, closes the unfinished Part VII, "The Revenues and Their Sources," with what is in effect the epistemological capstone of Marx's entire critique of political economy. Having constructed the machinery of value, surplus-value, the rate of profit, the transformation of values into prices of production, and the various forms of revenue across the preceding chapters, Marx now turns to a question that is not technical but phenomenological and ideological: why does the real underlying causal structure of capitalist production inevitably present itself to its own agents — and to the economists who theorize them — in an inverted form?

The chapter's title states its thesis in miniature: there is an illusion, and it is created by competition — or, more precisely, by the surface movement through which the actually determined value magnitudes of the total social product reappear as mutually independent, self-determining "sources" of value. The intellectual objective is to demonstrate that the appearance according to which wages, profit, and rent are independent constituent elements whose sum produces, limits, and determines the price of commodities is not merely a logical mistake but a necessary inversion generated by the way the capitalist mode of production continually reproduces itself out of its own premises. The illusion is the structure of appearances itself, and it has a social cause, not merely a cognitive one.

The central dialectical proposition of the chapter can be stated in one sentence: commodity value is the prius, the original quantitative unity, and wages/profit/rent are parts into which it is dissolved; the illusion consists in reversing this, so that the parts become the premises and the value becomes their sum. Everything else in the chapter — the numerical tables, the critique of the wage circularity, the five "reasons why the quid pro quo is necessary" — elaborates and defends this single inversion.

The Chapter's Place in the Theoretical Framework

This chapter is the practical conclusion of a long build. In Chapter 48 (the "trinity formula," capital–profit/interest, land–rent, labour–wages) Marx had shown that the "sources" of revenue correspond to the "sources of wealth" only in a mystified way. In Chapter 49 he had investigated why the value of the total product dissolves into wages, profit, and rent in the immediate production process itself. Chapter 50 pushes one decisive step further: it asks how this dissolution appears on the surface, and how that appearance hardens into an apparently self-evident theory of value as the sum of its parts.

It belongs, therefore, to the family of chapters in Volume III that address not the quantitative mechanics of distribution but the forms in which those mechanics must represent themselves: the fetishism of capital, the appearance that land yields rent and capital yields profit as natural properties. Chapter 50 provides the most general and most sustained treatment of what one may call — in Marx's own vocabulary — the ideological categories of the economic agents, or, as the chapter itself phrases it, the mode in which the actual determinations are "represented on the immediately visible surface of capitalist production, and hence in the minds of the agents trapped within it, in a distorted way."

Two consequences follow. First, this chapter is one of the most important bridge-texts between Marx's economic theory and his theory of consciousness/ideology, since it grounds the categories of bourgeois economic thought (the very categories through which everyone, worker and capitalist alike, "experiences" the economy) in the reality of the reproduction process itself, not in error or deception. Second, because it is the final substantive chapter of the manuscript edited by Engels, it functions as an implicit methodological verdict on the whole tradition of classical political economy — Adam Smith's "adding-up" theory of value, Say's traité, and the vulgar economists' reliance on "competition" as an explanatory principle. Ricardo is mainly spared here (footnote 56 allies Marx with him), but the "components" theory is demolished.

The Methodological Stakes

Marx's move is methodologically sharp in a way that is easy to miss. He does not argue that the components-view is false because empirically contradicted. He argues that (a) it is conceptually self-dissolving — it collapses into circularity, tautology, and the disappearance of any concept of value; and (b) it is nonetheless practically necessary — it is what the agents of production must think, because that is how their social relations reproduce themselves in their daily contracts, account books, and price calculations. The dialectical climax is the sentence: "Its result thus constantly appears as its premise, and its premises as its results." The illusion is not an optical error that could be corrected by better knowledge; it is a real inversion, a structural feature of a system that "constantly reproduces not only the material product but also the socio-economic relations, the formal economic determinants of its formation."

The chapter thus belongs at the intersection of three of Marx's lasting contributions: the value-form analysis, the theory of the transformation of values into prices of production, and the critique of ideology as socially necessary appearance.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Opening Recapitulation: The Three Components of Commodity Value (pp. 992–993)

Marx opens not with a definition but with a résumé of everything already established, because the entire illusion he is about to expose depends on the reader keeping this prior analysis firmly in view. The value of commodities — or, what governs it in developed capitalism, the price of production — can be resolved into three components:

  1. A constant capital component (c), which replaces constant capital. This is the value representing labour already past, spent as means of production. Crucially, its magnitude is fixed by the value (or price) at which these means of production enter the production process. It is a "replace[ment]" — a mere restitution of what was consumed, transferring the value of the consumed means of production into the new product without creating new value.

  2. A variable capital component (v), which measures the worker's income and becomes wages. This is the value component the worker has reproduced — it represents "the paid portion of the labour freshly added" to the constant portion.

  3. Surplus-value (s) — the value component in which unpaid or surplus labour is expressed. This third component then assumes independent revenue forms: profit on capital (which itself splits into interest on capital as such, and profit of enterprise on functioning capital), and ground-rent, falling to the landowner.

A decisive qualification is inserted immediately: when Marx says "commodity," he means commodity capital — the product of capital over a definite period (e.g. one year), of which the individual commodity is only one element. The individual commodity's value breaks down into "the same analogous components," but the aggregation matters, because the laws of distribution and the illusion of independence operate at the level of the total social product and its periodic reproduction, not only at the level of the single item.

The key discriminator, stated explicitly, is that components (2) and (3) contain the whole of the value in which the labour freshly added is objectified. That is: the fresh labour is exhausted within v + s. There is no leftover. This is the pivot of everything that follows — the sum v + s is a fixed, given magnitude (in Marx's notation below, 250), and it is this fixed magnitude that is then divided. The constant component (1) is different in kind: it is old value transferred, not new value created.

2.2 The Founding Distinction: Components of Value vs. Constituent Elements of Value (p. 993)

Here Marx draws the distinction without which the entire chapter is unintelligible. Leaving the constant component aside, "the value of a commodity, in so far as this represents freshly added labour, is always reducible to three elements, wages, profit and rent, which constitute the three forms of revenue," while the respective value magnitudes (the aliquot parts) are determined by different, specific laws that have already been developed.

But — and this is the crucial wrong inference — "It would be wrong however to say that the value of wages, the rate of profit and the rate of rent are independent constituent elements of value, with the value of the commodity, minus its constant component, arising from their combination." In other words:

  • TRUE: the fresh value is divided into wages, profit, and rent.
  • FALSE: wages, profit, and rent are independent magnitudes that combine to produce the fresh value.

The distinction is between a dissolution (a given unity splitting into parts) and a composition (given parts adding up into a unity). These are not the same operation read in opposite directions, because in the first case the whole is determined first and bounds the parts; in the second, the parts are determined first and no bound on the whole exists. Marx will spend the chapter demonstrating that the composition-view, taken seriously, is incoherent.

Notice that Marx adds the important qualifier that the aliquot parts are determined "by different, specific laws." This heterogeneity of causation is itself one of the reasons the illusion is generated: because the magnitudes of wages, profit, and rent obey different laws, they do not present themselves as parts of one common magnitude but as entities governed by separate, unrelated principles.

2.3 The First Numerical Model: 400c + 100v + 150s = 650 (pp. 993–994)

Marx constructs a simple, controlled numerical example to make the argument concrete. Let the value produced by a capital of 500 be:

400c + 100v + 150s = 650

with the surplus-value of 150 further divided into 75 profit + 75 rent. He assumes, "in order to avoid needless difficulties," a capital of average organic composition, so that its value and its price of production coincide (value = price of production whenever the individual capital is a proportionate sample of total social capital).

He then reads out the derived proportions:

  • Wages = v = 100, measured against the capital advanced (500), make up 20% of the capital advanced.
  • Surplus-value reckoned on total capital = 150/500 = 30% (15% profit + 15% rent).
  • The total new value component of the commodity = 100v + 150s = 250.

And here is the analytical hinge: "Its total amount is independent of its division into wages, profit and rent." The 250 is fixed by the amount of labour objectified in it; the further split of that 250 into 100/75/75 is a secondary determination that cannot alter the 250. This is the "quantitative premise" the chapter will repeatedly defend.

Marx then performs three interpretive conversions on the same numbers:

(a) The ratio of paid to unpaid labour. The £100 laid out for labour-power has set in motion a quantum of labour expressed in £250. Therefore the worker has performed 1½ times as much surplus labour as labour for himself (since 150/100 = 1.5). He makes this vivid: if the working day is 10 hours, the worker works 4 hours for himself and 6 hours for the capitalist. The "labour of the workers who are paid £100 is expressed therefore in a money value of £250."

(b) The absolute limit. "Apart from this value of £250, there is nothing left to be shared between worker and capitalist, or capitalist and landowner." The £250, added to the £400 transferred from the means of production, gives the commodity value £650. "The commodity value of £250 thus produced, and determined by the amount of labour objectified in it, sets the limit to the dividends that worker, capitalist and landlord can draw."

(c) The class-distribution implication. This is not a mathematical curiosity; it says that whatever the wage-bargain, the profit-rate, or the rent-level, all parties are drawing on one bounded pool. The whole point of the chapter is the systematic obscuring of this boundedness.

2.4 The Second Model and the Marginal Arithmetic of the Wage Shift (pp. 994–995)

Marx now changes one variable and holds everything else constant to isolate its effect. Suppose the same capital of the same organic composition must pay £150 instead of £100 for the same labour-power, while the constant capital remains £400 and the quantity of labour set in motion is unchanged. Then:

400c + 150v + 100s = 650, with the surplus-value of 100 now split (in the same proportion as before) into 45 profit + 55 rent.

The derived proportions are transformed:

  • Wages = 150/550 = 27 3/11% of the capital advanced.
  • Profit = 45/550 = 8 2/11% on capital advanced.
  • Rent = 55/550 = 10% on capital advanced.
  • Total surplus-value = 100/550 ≈ 18 2/11% on capital advanced.

And again the interpretive conversion of the labour day: with s/v = 66⅔%, the worker now works 6 hours for himself and only 4 for the capitalist. The surplus labour has fallen.

Marx then distils the differential behaviour of the three rates, which he tabulates:

Value of the product New value Rate of surplus-value Gross rate of profit
First case 400c + 100v + 150s = 650 250 150% 30%
Second case 400c + 150v + 100s = 650 250 66⅔% 18 2/11%

Here "gross rate of profit" is defined explicitly by Marx as the ratio of the total surplus-value to the total capital advanced — i.e., here, s/(c+v) = 150/500 and 100/550 respectively. (This is what is elsewhere called simply the rate of profit.)

The comparison yields three precise claims:

  1. The mass of surplus-value falls by one-third (150 → 100).
  2. The rate of profit falls proportionately more than the mass of surplus-value (from 30% to ~18%), "since the diminished surplus-value has to be reckoned against an increased total capital advanced" — the denominator has grown from 500 to 550.
  3. But the rate of profit falls less than the rate of surplus-value. The rate of surplus-value falls from 150% to 66⅔% (a fall of 5/9, or 83⅓ percentage points); the rate of profit falls only from 30% to 18 2/11% (a fall of about 2/5). Therefore: rate of profit falls proportionately more than the mass of surplus-value, though less than the rate of surplus-value.

This is a precise, non-trivial result about the elasticities of the various profit measures, and it matters because it is exactly the heterogeneity of these movements that experience cannot decode. The agents observe the profit rate falling; they do not observe why the fall is neither proportional to the fall in surplus-value nor to the fall in the rate of surplus-value.

Marx adds an economic observation about the expansion of the capital advanced: an increased variable component of capital makes a new business harder to open (more capital required), but for reproduction as a whole, "an increase in variable capital means nothing more than that a greater part of the value newly created by the freshly added labour is transformed into wages, and hence first of all into variable capital, instead of into surplus-value and surplus product." The value of the product is unchanged because it is defined by (i) the £400 constant value and (ii) the £250 fresh value — both unchanged. And if that product re-entered constant capital, it would still represent the same amount of use-value within the same amount of value.

This is the substance of the dissolution-not-composition claim: raising wages does not raise the value of the product; it reallocates a given quantity of value.

2.5 The Cause/Effect Inversion: Wage Rises, Productivity, and the "Dearness" Illusion (p. 996)

Marx now introduces a critical distinction between two different causes of a wage rise, which lead to opposite conclusions about the product's value:

Case A — the worker keeps a greater part of his own labour (distributional wage rise). If the worker keeps a greater share, the value of the product is unchanged (the analysis of §2.4).

Case B — the productivity of labour has declined (productivity-driven wage rise). Here the worker keeps a greater part of his own labour because a unit of labour now yields less product. "The total value in which the same labour was expressed, i.e. paid and unpaid, would remain the same; but this quantity of labour would be expressed in a diminished product, i.e. the price of each aliquot part of the product would rise, since each part represented more labour." Concretely: the increased wage of £150 represents a product no larger than £100 did before; the diminished surplus-value of £100 now represents only two-thirds the former product — 66⅔% of the use-values previously expressed in £100. The constant capital would also become more expensive insofar as this (now-dearer) product enters it.

The crucial analytical payoff: "The illusion arises that the rise in wages has made the product dearer; but here this is not cause but consequence of a change in the commodity's value, resulting from the diminished productivity of labour." That is, in Case B, the rise in wages is the effect, not the cause, of the changed value of the commodity. The appearance on the surface (wages rose, so prices rose) inverts the real causal arrow. This is the first appearance of the chapter's central methodological theme — the systematic inversion of cause and effect on the surface of capitalist production.

2.6 Constant Capital Value Movements and the Asymmetry of Compensation (pp. 996–997)

Marx now turns to changes in the value of constant capital to isolate a second asymmetry. Working with the same amount of labour (still expressed in 250) but a different value of means of production:

  • 450c + 100v + 150s = 700 (from 400c: +50)
  • 350c + 100v + 150s = 600 (from 400c: −50)

Thus, "If the capital advanced for putting the same amount of labour in motion grows or declines, the value of the product rises or falls, conditions being otherwise identical, as long as the increase or decrease in the capital advanced derives from a change in the value magnitude of the constant capital component."

But — and this is the sharply asymmetric result — "It remains unaffected, on the other hand, if the increase or decrease in the capital advanced derives from a change in the value magnitude of the variable capital component, with labour productivity remaining the same."

The reason for the asymmetry:

  • Constant capital: an increase in its value is not compensated by any movement in the opposite direction. The value added transfers in full to the product, so a rise in c raises the product's value one-for-one.
  • Variable capital: an increase in its value is compensated by an opposite movement in surplus-value, so that v + s is unchanged. Since v + s is exactly what labour newly adds, the total value added to the means of production is constant.

This produces an observable "law of motion" of the surface: when the source is constant capital, an increased capital advanced is matched by a higher product value; when the source is variable capital, it is not. Note the qualification: this holds so long as labour productivity is constant. If the change in the value of variable capital stems from a change in commodity prices (i.e., from a change in productivity), then the value of the product is affected — but then, as established in §2.5, the wage movement is effect, not cause.

Marx then offers a specific illustration from agriculture: if the rise of variable capital from 100v to 150v is caused not by any decline in cotton-spinning productivity but by a decline in agriculture (the sector supplying the workers' sustenance, hence raising the price of provisions), the value of the product remains unchanged. The £650 is still "expressed in the same amount of cotton yarn as before." The greater part of the fresh value simply flows into wages rather than surplus-value; the yarn itself is unchanged in value and quantity. This is a strong, testable claim: general wage changes alter distribution but not the value of a given commodity produced by given labour with given means of production.

2.7 The Economy-in-Constant-Capital Case and the General Laws of the Profit Rate (pp. 997–998)

Marx now extends the analysis to a third mechanism — economies in the use of constant capital. If the reduction in outlay on constant capital occurs "in branches of production whose products go into the workers' consumption," this cheapens the workers' means of subsistence and may thereby reduce wages, in the same way as a direct increase in the productivity of the labour immediately applied.

The profit rate then rises for two reasons: 1. Because the value of the constant capital has declined (smaller denominator); 2. Because surplus-value has increased (larger numerator, via lower wages and hence a larger surplus share of the given new value).

Marx adds a crucial methodological note reconciling this with preceding chapters: in the analysis of the transformation of surplus-value into profit, he had assumed wages constant in order to isolate profit-rate fluctuations independent of changes in the rate of surplus-value. But "the laws developed there, however, are general ones, and apply also for capital investments where the products do not go into the consumption of the workers, so that changes in the value of the product are without influence on wages." The increase in labour productivity applied directly raises surplus-value regardless of whether that sector's product enters workers' consumption; it is only the indirect effect (via the wage-goods basket) that requires the product to be a worker-consumption good.

This §2.7 functions as a stress-test of the earlier, more restrictive model and establishes that the general laws survive when wages are released from the constant-wage ceteris paribus.

2.8 The Starred Section: The Absolute Limits of Revenue Division (pp. 998–1001)

The asterisk marks a fresh, self-contained sub-argument — arguably the philosophical core of the chapter. It re-establishes, now at the level of the annual social product, the quantitative boundedness of all revenue.

(a) The given sum and its arbitrary decomposition. The value newly added each year by fresh labour can be "separated out and resolved into the different revenue forms." This "in no way alters the limits of the value itself, the sum of value that is divided." A given figure of 100 "always remains the same, whether it is broken down into 50 + 50, or 20 + 70 + 10, or 40 + 30 + 30." What is given first is the mass of commodity values to be divided; the absolute limit to the sum of the value portions is therefore given.

(b) Wages as the base. "In this delimitation, wages form the basis." Wages are governed by a natural law: their minimum limit is the physical minimum of means of subsistence "that the worker must receive in order to maintain and reproduce his labour-power." The value of these commodities is determined by the labour-time required for their reproduction — i.e., by the portion of the working day the worker needs to produce an equivalent for his subsistence. If average subsistence costs 6 hours of average labour, the worker must spend about 6 hours working for himself.

Marx immediately adds the historical qualification that saves this from crude physiologism: "The actual value of his labour-power diverges from this physical minimum; it differs according to climate and the level of social development; it depends not only on physical needs but also on historically developed social needs, which become second nature." But "in each country, this governing average wage is a given quantity at a given time." This is the classical Marxian doctrine of the value of labour-power as a historically and socially determined magnitude, here doing decisive work: it establishes wages as a bounded but non-physical baseline from which the other revenues are derived as remainder.

(c) The absolute limits of surplus-value, profit, and rent. All other revenues have a limit: it is "the value embodying the total working day … minus that part of it embodied in wages." The limit is the value representing unpaid labour. Symmetrically: - The wage has its ultimate barrier in the physical minimum of the working day spent on necessary labour. - The value component expressing surplus-value has its barrier in the physical maximum of the working day — the total daily labour-time the worker can provide while maintaining and reproducing labour-power.

Since "what we are dealing with here is the distribution of the value in which the total labour freshly added each year is expressed," the working day is taken as a constant quantity (though it may depart from its physical maximum). We therefore have an absolute limit for the surplus-value component, determined by "the excess of the unpaid portion of the working day over its paid portion."

(d) Profit as surplus-value reckoned on total capital. If this limit-bounded surplus-value is called profit when calculated on total advanced capital, then profit in absolute amount equals the surplus-value and is "just as regularly determined in its limits." Profit is defined as the ratio between total surplus-value and total social capital advanced in production. Illustration: if capital = 500 (which may be millions) and surplus-value = 100, the absolute limit to the rate of profit is 20%.

(e) The transformation to prices of production. The division of this social profit as measured by this rate, among capitals in different spheres, produces prices of production which diverge from commodity values and which are the actual governing averages of market prices. But — the crucial claim — "this divergence from values abolishes neither the determination of prices by values nor the limits imposed on profit by our laws." A commodity's value is not equal to the capital consumed in it plus the surplus-value it contains; its price of production is the capital k consumed plus the surplus-value that falls to it by virtue of the general rate of profit (say 20%) on the capital advanced, whether consumed or merely applied. But that 20% surcharge is itself determined by the surplus-value created by the total social capital and its proportion to that capital — "and this is why it is 20 per cent and not 10 per cent or 100 per cent." The transformation redistributes the social surplus-value among particular capitals "evenly, in proportion as they form value components of this total capital" — it does not abolish the limits.

(f) Market prices and Quetelet's law. Market prices rise above or fall below production prices, but their fluctuations balance out. Marx asserts — with the surprising claim that it is "surprising" — "how narrow the limits of these divergences are and how regularly they are balanced out." He invokes Adolphe Quetelet (1796–1874), the Belgian mathematician who applied statistical methods to social phenomena (especially in On Man and the Development of his Faculties, 1835; English 1842), and affirms that "the same rule of governing averages is found here as Quetelet demonstrated in connection with social phenomena."

Note the critical footnote: Marx's attitude toward Quetelet is ambivalent. The regularities Quetelet demonstrates "are ingenious, but not particularly significant" — and the editor directs the reader to Marx's fuller, sardonic remarks in "Parties and Cliques" in Surveys from Exile (p. 279). The use of Quetelet here is thus largely illustrative, not a philosophical endorsement: Marx is not adopting statistical positivism, but using the fact of statistical regularity to underline that underlying value determination imposes narrow bounds on market-price wanderings.

(g) Rent's three limits. Marx then subdivides rent: - Differential rent: When the adjustment of commodity values to production prices encounters no obstacles, "rent is reduced to differential rent, i.e. it is restricted to the cancellation of the surplus profits that the governing prices of production would give to one section of capitalists, these now being appropriated by the landowners." Its limits lie in the divergences among individual profit rates arising from the general rate governing production prices. - Absolute rent: When landed property blocks this adjustment and appropriates an absolute rent, it is "limited by the excess value of agricultural products over and above their price of production, i.e. by the excess surplus-value contained in them over and above the profits that capitals receive by virtue of the general rate of profit." Its limit is a specific portion of the given surplus-value. - Monopoly price: If monopolies (natural or artificial, especially landed property) make a monopoly price possible above both price of production and value, this "does not mean that the limits fixed by commodity value are abolished. A monopoly price for certain commodities simply transfers a portion of the profit made by the other commodity producers to the commodities with the monopoly price." It causes a local disturbance in the distribution of surplus-value, but "leaves unaffected the limit of the surplus-value itself."

Marx's calculus of monopoly price then becomes precise: if the monopoly commodity is a worker's necessary consumption good, it raises wages and reduces surplus-value (as long as workers receive the value of their labour-power). It could press wages down below the value of labour-power, but "only if they previously stood above the physical minimum." In that case the monopoly price is paid "by deduction from real wages (i.e., from the amount of use-values that the worker receives for the same amount of labour) and from the profit of other capitalists." Crucially: "The limits within which monopoly price affects the normal regulation of commodity prices are firmly determined and can be precisely calculated."

(h) Summary of the starred section's logic. The division of the fresh value into wages and surplus-value is bounded by the necessary/surplus labour proportion; the division of surplus-value into profit and rent is bounded by the laws of profit-rate equalization; the division into interest and profit of enterprise is bounded only by competition (see §2.9); and even monopoly price, far from abolishing limits, merely redistributes a bounded surplus-value. The entire architecture is one of nested determinations of a bounded magnitude.

2.9 The Division of Average Profit: Interest vs. Profit of Enterprise (pp. 1001–1002)

Marx now makes the sharpest contrast in the chapter. If the division of the fresh value into wages/profit/rent is bounded by laws, and the division of surplus-value into profit/rent is bounded by profit-rate equalization, then:

"With the division into interest and profit of enterprise, the average profit itself sets the limit for the two together. It supplies the given amount of value they have to share between them, and this is all they have to share. The specific ratio of this division is accidental here, i.e., it is determined exclusively by relations of competition."

The logic is dialectically important: normally, market prices cease to diverge from their governing averages when supply and demand match — competition's effect is abolished at equilibrium, and thus competition cannot be the determinant of the equilibrium level. But here, uniquely, competition is the sole determining factor. Why? Because "the same factor of production, capital, has to share the portion of surplus-value accruing to it between two owners of this same production factor." Since both interest and profit of enterprise accrue to capital as such (one as ownership of loanable capital, one as functioning capital), there is no immanent law that can discriminate between them. Two owners of the same factor divide the single pool.

The key caveat: even here, "if the division of average profit has in this case no determining limit as imposed by the laws we have developed, that does not abolish this limit as a portion of commodity value." Marx's analogy: "as little as when two partners in a company share their profit unequally, because of different external circumstances, this in any way affects the limits of this profit." The size of the pie is law-determined; the cut between two owners may be purely the result of bargaining.

2.10 The False Conception Stated Formally: Independent Value Magnitudes (p. 1002)

Marx now restates the illusion in its sharpest form so as to refute it. If the value component representing fresh labour "breaks down into different portions, which assume mutually independent shapes in the form of revenues, this does not in any way mean that wages, profit and ground-rent are now to be considered as the constituent elements, with the governing price (natural price, prix nécessaire) of commodities itself arising from their combination or sum."

Characterizing the false view, Marx says: it holds that "it would not be the commodity value, after deduction of the constant value component, that was the original unity and breaks down into these three components, but the price of each of these three components was rather determined independently, and the commodity's price formed only from the addition of these three independent magnitudes."

Stated as a contrast:

  • Marx's view (real): commodity value is the quantitative premise — the sum total value of wages, profit, and rent — whatever their relative magnitudes.
  • The false view: wages, profit, and rent are three independent value magnitudes, whose total "produces, limits and determines the magnitude of commodity value."

Marx notes immediately that this false view, if generalized, must extend also to the constant value component — since if three components composed prices, the same logic would apply to the input commodities that make up constant capital. This reveals that the false view "also involves a denial of the existence of such a constant value component." In other words, the composition-view is not merely quantitatively wrong; it dissolves the whole category of constant capital, and with it any non-circular conception of value.

2.11 The Dissolution of Value and Money — and the Wage Circularity (pp. 1002–1004)

Marx deepens the critique: on the composition-view, "any concept of value would inevitably disappear here. All that was left would be the idea of price, in the sense that a certain amount of money is paid to the owners of labour-power, capital and land."

But "what is money? Money is not a thing but a particular form of value, so that it again presupposes value." Gold and silver are themselves commodities; their price is therefore also determined by wages, profit, and rent. So you cannot determine wages, profit, and rent by equating them with a certain quantity of gold or silver, since the value of that gold and silver, "as the equivalent in which they are to be assessed, is precisely supposed to be determined by wages, profit and rent independently of gold and silver." "To say that the value of wages, profit and rent consists in their being equal to a certain amount of gold and silver is thus simply to say that they are equal to a certain amount of wages, profit and rent." This is a devastating identity: the alleged measure measures itself.

Marx then turns to the wage determination in detail (the longest single reductio in the chapter):

  1. Start from labour (as even the composition-view must).
  2. The governing (natural) wage is set by the supply and demand for labour-power.
  3. But whose demand? Capital's demand. So the demand for labour is the supply of capital.
  4. To speak of the supply of capital, we must know what capital is: its simplest form is money and commodities; money is simply a form of commodity; so capital = commodities.
  5. But the value of commodities is determined, in the first instance, on this same assumption, by the price of the labour producing them — wages — which is presupposed.
  6. Therefore, on this view, the price of commodities is determined by the proportion of labour on offer to capital, capital's demand for labour = the supply of capital = the supply of a sum of commodities whose price is governed, in the first instance, by the price of labour. Circle closed. "The determination of wages cannot start from capital, for wages are themselves a factor entering into the determination of the value of capital."

Marx then forecloses the escape route through competition: competition can make market prices of labour rise or fall, but if we assume demand and supply match, competition ceases to be determinant — "its effect is abolished by the achievement of equilibrium between its two counteracting forces." And the whole point is to find the natural price of labour — "a price of labour which is not governed by competition, but on the contrary is what governs competition." Nothing remains but to determine the necessary price of labour by the worker's necessary means of subsistence. But these are commodities with a price. So the price of labour is determined by the price of the necessary means of subsistence, and the price of the means of subsistence, like that of all other commodities, is determined in the first place by the price of labour. Therefore "the price of labour is determined by itself. In other words, we do not know how the price of labour is determined." And crucially: "in order to speak of the price of labour, we must know what price in general is. But this procedure does not tell us anything about price in general."

2.12 Competition as a Pseudo-Explanation (pp. 1004–1005)

Marx now performs the same reduction on profit. The average profit must be determined by an average rate of profit; how is that determined? By competition among capitalists. But competition already assumes profit. It assumes different rates and different profits in the same branch and across branches. "Competition can act on the profit rate only by acting on the price of commodities." It can only: - make producers in the same sphere sell at the same price; - make producers in different spheres sell at prices yielding the same proportionate surcharge.

"Hence competition can only even out inequalities in the rate of profit." It cannot create profit. It "makes it higher or lower, but it does not create the level that is present as soon as this equalization has taken place." The average rate of profit appears when the competing forces balance; competition can produce the balance, but not the rate which appears when the balance is given. And the decisive forensic question: why is the general rate of profit 10, 20, or 100 per cent? Competition cannot answer, for it has abolished the causes that led to departures from those levels. "All competition does is persistently reduce all divergences to this level. One person competes with another, and competition forces him to sell his commodity at the same price as the next man. But why is this price 10 or 20 or 100? There is nothing left for it, then, but to declare that the rate of profit and hence profit itself is a surcharge, determined in an incomprehensible way, on the partial price of a commodity as determined by wages."

This culminates in one of Marx's most pungent methodological verdicts:

"Competition, in other words, is burdened with explaining all the economists' irrationalities, whereas it is supposed to be the economists who explain competition."

Ground-rent needs no separate retracing. "We can already see that if it is carried through in any consistent way, it makes profit and rent appear as mere surcharges, determined by incomprehensible laws, on top of a commodity price determined in the first place by wages."

2.13 The Nominal-Surcharge Detour (pp. 1005–1006)

Marx now reduces the "surcharge" theory of profit and rent to its absurd consequence. Suppose commodity price = 100 (determined by wages); rate of profit = 10% on wages; rent = 15% on wages. Commodity price = 110 + 16.5... no — the calculation given is price = 125 (100 + 10 + 15). The surcharge of 25 cannot derive from the sale of the commodity: "if everyone who sells to someone else sells what cost 100 at 125, it is the same thing as if they had all sold at 100." The operation must therefore be considered independently of the circulation process — a restatement of Marx's fundamental thesis that circulation "can never yield anything that was not previously put into it."

The detour is then laid bare. If the commodity actually costs 125 and the three elements are divided within it:

  • The worker, nominally receiving 100, actually receives 4/5 of the value and product (100/125 = 4/5).
  • The capitalist receives 2/25 of value and product (10/125).
  • The landlord receives 3/25 (15/125).

Since the capitalist sells at 125 instead of 100, he gives the worker only 4/5 of the product in which his labour is expressed. "It would be the same thing therefore if he gave the worker 80 and kept back 20, with 8 of this accruing to him and 12 to the landlord." (Check: 80 + 8 + 12 = 100; profit = 10% of 80 = 8; rent = 15% of 80 = 12.) The capitalist has then sold the commodity at its value, since in fact the "price surcharges" are independent of the commodity's value, which on this assumption is determined by the value of wages.

The upshot is that 'wages' in this conception = 100 = the value of the product, i.e., the sum of money in which a particular amount of labour is expressed; but this value is different again from real wages, and hence leaves a surplus. The "surplus" is now brought about via a nominal surcharge: if wages are 110 instead of 100, profit = 11, ground-rent = 16½, price = 137½. The proportions are unchanged.

The decisive observation: "since the division is always obtained by a nominal surcharge of a certain percentage on wages, the price rises and falls with wages. First wages are posited as equal to the value of the commodity, and then they are again divorced from the value of the commodity." Wage = value, then wage ≠ value — the theory oscillates incoherently. "In fact, however, it all comes down, by way of this irrational detour, to the determination of the value of the commodity by the amount of labour contained in it, while the value of wages is determined by the price of the necessary means of subsistence, and the excess of the value over and above wages forms profit and rent."

In other words, the surcharge theory, unfolded consistently, covertly reproduces the correct result — labour-determined value with a distributional surplus — but through an "irrational detour." The correct content is smuggled back in under a false form.

2.14 The Quid Pro Quo and Reason One: The Reification of Revenue-Forms (pp. 1006–1007)

Marx introduces the central term: the quid pro quo — the substitution of the wrong causal order. At the immediately visible surface of production, the dissolution of the given quantum of labour into wages/profit/rent is represented "in a distorted way." He sets it up with a simple schema: if total commodity value = 300, of which 200 = constant capital consumed, the new value remaining for division = 100. Let wages = x, profit = y, rent = z: x + y + z = 100 always. But "in the minds of the industrialists, merchants and bankers, and the vulgar economists as well," the order is reversed: x, y, z are each independently determined, and the magnitude of the commodity's value results from their sum — which may be greater or less than 100.

Marx then gives the first of five reasons why this quid pro quo is "necessary":

Reason One — Revenue-forms are personified by three separate agents, and property appears as the source of value. The components "confront one another as independent revenues, which are related as such to three completely separate agents of production, labour, capital and the earth, and appear therefore to arise from these." Property in labour-power, capital, and the earth is the reason why the different value components fall to their respective proprietors, transforming them into revenues. "But value does not arise from a transformation into revenue, it must rather be already in existence before it can be transformed into revenue and assume this form." The appearance is reinforced because the relative sizes of the three parts are governed by different laws, and their relationship to — and limitation by — commodity value is not indicated on the surface at all.

The implication is important: the distributional relationship of persons to things (property titles) is mistaken for the causal relationship of things to value. Because every income has an owner attached, income seems to be produced by ownership rather than merely appropriated through ownership.

2.15 Reason Two: Experience, the Inversion of Cause and Effect, and the "Confirmed" Illusion (pp. 1007–1009)

Reason Two — the surface regularities of profit-rate movements confirm the wage-determines-price appearance. Marx gives three concrete mechanisms:

(a) General wage rise/fall with opposite profit-rate movement alters production prices. When wages rise generally, the general rate of profit moves oppositely, other things equal, and this alters production prices — raising some, lowering others — depending on the average composition of capital in each sphere. In some spheres, therefore, "experience shows that the average commodity price rises because wages have risen and falls because they have fallen." What is not experienced is "the secret regulation of these changes by a commodity value independent of wages."

(b) Local wage rises. If the rise is local — particular to certain spheres due to specific circumstances — there may be a corresponding nominal rise in the price of these commodities. This price rise is a reaction to the local disturbance of the uniform distribution of surplus-value, a means of adjusting particular profit rates to the general rate. "Experience" again shows price determined by wages; the "hidden basis" (the uniform division of social surplus-value) is not experienced.

(c) Wage as production price of labour-power. The average price of labour, i.e., the value of labour-power, is determined by the production price of the necessary means of subsistence; if that rises or falls, so does the price of labour. So there is a real, experienced correlation between wages and commodity prices — but "the cause may present itself as effect, and the effect as cause," just as with market-price movements, where a wage rise above average corresponds to the market-price rise characteristic of prosperity, and the subsequent wage fall below average corresponds to the market-price fall below production prices in the downturn.

(d) The crucial falsification. Marx then signals an observation incompatible with the surface appearance: the profit rate may be affected independently of wage movements by movements in the value of constant capital — so that wages and the profit rate may rise or fall in the same direction, instead of opposite ones. "If the rate of surplus-value directly coincided with the rate of profit, this would not be possible." Moreover, even if wages rise due to higher subsistence prices, the profit rate may remain the same or rise as a result of greater labour intensity or the prolongation of the working day.

The chapter's diagnosis: "All these experiences confirm the illusion produced by the independent, distorted form of the value components, as if the value of commodities was determined either by wages alone, or by wages and profit together." And once this seems true for wages — once the price of labour seems to coincide with the value labour creates — it follows "self-evidently" for profit and rent too, whose money expressions "must then be governed independently of labour and the value it produces." The illusion propagates by analogy.

2.16 Reason Three: Presupposition, Reproduction, and the Constant-Capital Tautology (pp. 1009–1012)

Reason Three is the most theoretically profound. Marx asks: suppose we remove competition entirely and assume perfect constancy and coincidence. Would the illusion disappear? No.

He stipulates three highly favourable assumptions: 1. Values or production prices always coincide at the phenomenal level with market prices; 2. Reproduction takes place under constant conditions — labour productivity constant for all elements of capital; 3. The fresh value component always breaks down into wages, profit, and rent in the same proportions, so that actually paid wages, actually realized profits, and actual rent coincide directly with the value of labour-power, with the share of total surplus-value accruing under the average rate of profit, and with the normal limits of ground-rent.

Then: "Under these assumptions, then, with the values of commodities being and appearing constant… the real movement would necessarily appear in a distorted form." Even with competition abstracted away, the actual appearance is not "the dissolution of a value magnitude given in advance into three parts" but rather "the formation of this value magnitude from the sum of the component elements of wages, profit and ground-rent, taken as determined independently and separately."

Why? Because "in the real movement of individual capitals and their commodity product it is not the value of commodities that appears the premise of its own dissolution but, on the contrary, the components into which it can be dissolved function as the premises for a commodity's value."

Marx elaborates four structural features:

(i) Cost price. For each capitalist, the cost price appears as a given quantity and constantly presents itself as such in the production process. Cost price = value of constant capital + value of labour-power — though labour-power's value appears in the "irrational form of the price of labour," so that wages too appear as the worker's revenue.

(ii) Wages are contractually fixed before the equivalent is produced. "The average price of labour is a given magnitude, since the value of labour-power, like that of any other commodity, is determined by the labour-time necessary for its reproduction." But the value component that resolves into wages does not arise from the fact that it takes the form of wages; it arises from the worker producing an equivalent. However, "wages are stipulated by contract before the value equivalent corresponding to them is produced. And since they are a price element whose magnitude is given before the commodity and its value are produced, a component of the cost price, wages appear not as a part separated off from the total value of the commodity in an independent form, but rather the reverse, as a given magnitude that determines the total value in advance, i.e., a formative element of price or value." This is a temporal/practical fact — the wage contract — that has the epistemic consequence of making the part appear as the premise.

(iii) Average profit as a regulating element. Average profit plays a role in the price of production analogous to wages in the cost price. Price of production = cost price + average profit on capital advanced. Average profit has a practical bearing in the mind and accounting of the capitalist himself, as a regulating element: it determines the transfer of capital between spheres and all sales and contracts over long periods. But insofar as it has this practical bearing, it is a magnitude fixed in advance, independent of the value and surplus-value produced in any particular sphere, and even more independent of any individual capital investment. "Instead of being the result of a division in value, it rather presents the appearance of a magnitude independent of the value of the commodity product, given in advance in the commodity's production process and itself determining the average price of the commodities; it presents the appearance in other words of a formative element of value."

(iv) Interest and ground-rent enter the cost price as given magnitudes. One part of average profit, in the form of interest, confronts the functioning capitalist "from an independent position as an element already presupposed in the production of commodities and their value." The amount of interest fluctuates, but at any moment it is a given magnitude that "enters into the cost price of the commodities he produces." Similarly ground-rent, in the form of contractually fixed lease-money paid by the agricultural capitalist, or rent for business space. These parts into which surplus-value can be resolved, "since as elements of the cost price they are given for the individual capitalist, appear upside-down, as formative elements of surplus-value; forming one portion of commodity price in the way that wages form the other."

(v) The dialectical climax — the reproduction of the relations. Marx then states the deepest reason:

"The secret reason why these products of the dissolution of commodity value constantly appear as the premises of value formation itself is simply that the capitalist mode of production, like every other, constantly reproduces not only the material product but also the socio-economic relations, the formal economic determinants of its formation. Its result thus constantly appears as its premise, and its premises as its results. And it is this constant reproduction of the same relationships which the individual capitalist anticipates as self-evident, as an indubitable fact."

Because wages, profit, interest, and rent are constantly reproduced as the form in which income flows, they are built into all contracts, and so are presupposed. "The specific shape in which the value components confront one another is presupposed because it is constantly reproduced, and it is constantly reproduced because it is constantly presupposed." This mutual constitution of presupposition and reproduction is the social mechanism behind the epistemic illusion.

(vi) Market prices confirm the "given-ness." "Experience and appearance also show that market prices… are in no way dependent on these anticipations as far as their level goes; they are not affected by whether interest or rent is fixed high or low." Market prices constantly change, and their longer-period average "is precisely what gives rise to the respective averages of wages, profit and rent, as the constant quantities that therefore ultimately govern market prices." So it looks as if market prices are indifferent to the "given" magnitudes and the magnitudes are the "given" governing realities.

(vii) The constant-capital tautology. The chapter now strips away the last possible defence:

"It seems very simple, on the other hand, to reflect that if wages, profit and rent are formative elements of value, because they appear as presupposed in value production, and are presupposed for the individual capitalist in the cost price and price of production, then the constant capital component, whose value is given in the production of any commodity, is also a value-forming element. But the constant capital component is nothing but a sum of commodities and hence commodity values. We would thus get the absurd tautology that commodity value forms and causes commodity value."

This is a reductio ad absurdum. If whatever appears as given in advance is thereby a value-forming element, then the constant capital component — which is every bit as "given in advance" for the individual capitalist as wages, interest, and rent — must also be value-forming. But constant capital is itself a sum of commodity values. So the view collapses into the tautology that value causes value. Marx notes that the capitalist, driven by "his own self-interest and the resulting motives," may nevertheless be led to extend the components-view to constant capital via the same circular logic: he sees his product entering other spheres as constant capital, and theirs entering his, and so he can reduce the price of constant capital "in the last instance, even if in a way that cannot be entirely fathomed, to the sum of value that results from the addition of independent value elements governed by different laws and formed from different sources: wages, profit and rent."

2.17 Reason Four: The Practical Indifference of the Individual Capitalist (pp. 1012–1015)

Reason Four — for the individual capitalist, the whole determination of value is immaterial.

Marx's argument: "It is completely immaterial for the individual capitalist whether commodities are sold at their values or not, and so therefore is the whole determination of value. Right from the start, this is something that goes on behind his back." The governing average prices in each sphere are not values but prices of production that differ from values. Value determination "as such" interests and affects the individual capitalist only in one narrow way: insofar as a rise or fall in the productivity of labour changes how much labour is required — either allowing him an extra profit at existing market prices, or compelling him to raise his price because more wages, more constant capital, and hence more interest fall to each unit produced.

Wages, interest and rent appear to him as governing limits — not only to the price at which he can realize his profit of enterprise, but to the price at which he must sell if reproduction is to continue. "It is a matter of complete indifference to him whether he realizes the value and surplus-value contained in the commodity on its sale or not, as long as he extracts from the price the customary profit of enterprise, or a greater profit, above the cost price as individually given for him by wages, interest and rent."

Moreover, this indifference has practical consequences that make the view functional: if the capitalist can drive wages below the value of labour-power, or obtain capital at lower interest, or pay sub-normal rent or sub-production-price raw materials, he doesn't mind selling below value "or even below the general price of production, i.e., parting with a portion of the surplus labour contained in the commodity for nothing." "His profit of enterprise can remain the same, and even grow, as long as the excess of the commodity price above the elements of it that must be paid for… remains the same or grows."

So the individual capitalist's business accounting treats wages, interest, and rent (plus the value of means of production going into his commodities) as the price-determining elements of his commodity. Profit of enterprise from this standpoint appears either as: - determined by an excess of market price (resulting from chance relations of competition) over the immanent value of commodities as determined by those elements; or - insofar as it is included in the market price as a determining element, as itself dependent on competition among buyers and sellers.

World-market extension. "Both in competition between the individual capitalists and in competition on the world market, given and presupposed amounts for wages, interest and rent go into the account as constant and governing quantities" — "constant not in the sense that they do not change, but rather in that they are given in any one particular case and constantly set the limit for the ever fluctuating market price." Marx gives a concrete example: world-market competition is a question of whether, with the given levels of wages, interest, and rent, a commodity can profitably be sold at or below the general market price. If wages and land prices are low but interest is high in one country (because the capitalist mode of production is not fully developed), while wages and land are nominally high and interest is low in another, then the first country's capitalist uses more land and labour, the second's relatively more capital. These factors are the determining elements in competition. "Experience shows here in theory, and the self-interested calculation of the capitalist shows in practice, that commodity prices are determined by wages, interest and rent, by the prices of labour, capital and land, and that these price elements are in fact the governing elements of price formation."

The residual "fourth element." Marx concedes one thing: "There still of course remains one element that is not assumed in advance but results from the market price of commodities, namely the excess over the cost price formed from the addition of these elements, wages, interest and rent. This fourth element appears in each individual case as determined by competition, and in the average case by the average profit, which is again governed by the same competition, simply over a longer period." So profit of enterprise, precisely the residual, is the only element not "given in advance" for the individual capitalist — and it is the one that competes hardest to be seen as pure result, while the others are seen as causes. This contrast is the practical-mind's version of the whole illusion.

2.18 Reason Five: Subsumption by Analogy and the Naturalization of Capital (p. 1015)

Reason Five — the categories are applied even where their conditions of existence are absent, by analogy.

Marx's case: "If an independent worker labours for himself and sells his own product — we may take a small peasant, since in this case all three forms of revenue can be used — he is first of all considered as his own employer (capitalist), employing himself as a worker, and as his own landowner, using himself as his own farmer. He pays himself wages as a worker, lays claim to profit as a capitalist and pays himself rent as a landowner."

Marx concedes that, once the capitalist mode of production is the general social basis, this subsumption is "correct in as much as" — for the peasant is able to appropriate his own surplus labour through possession of means of production (necessarily taken to have the form of capital), and his product is a commodity whose price is estimated against the general rate of profit; any excess beyond the ordinary quota depends on ownership of land. But the consequence is essential:

"Because a form of production that does not correspond to the capitalist mode of production can be subsumed under its forms of revenue (and up to a certain point this is not incorrect), the illusion that capitalist relationships are the natural condition of any mode of production is further reinforced."

This is the ideological payoff of the whole chapter: the categories of capitalist distribution (wages, profit, rent) become so pervasive that they are projected onto non-capitalist producers, and this projection is what makes capitalist relations appear natural and eternal. The chapter does not treat the illusion as a merely conceptual error; it treats it as a social process of naturalization.

2.19 The Coda: Stripping Away the Capitalist Form and the Feudal Analogy (pp. 1015–1016)

The chapter's final paragraph is a flash of the post-capitalist horizon. Marx asks what remains if the specifically capitalist character of wages and surplus-value is stripped away:

  • Wages reduced to their general basis: that portion of the product of labour going into the worker's own individual consumption; this share, "freed from its capitalist limit and expanded to the scale of consumption that is both permitted by the existing social productivity… and required for the full development of individuality."
  • Surplus labour and surplus product reduced to the degree needed, under given conditions of production, on the one hand "to form an insurance and reserve fund," on the other hand "for the constant expansion of reproduction in the degree determined by social need."
  • Both necessary labour and surplus labour taken to include the labour "that those capable of work must always perform for those members of society not yet capable, or no longer capable of working."

"Then nothing of these forms remains, but simply those foundations of the forms that are common to all social modes of production." This is a specification of the universal economic content — labour, necessary consumption, insurance, reproduction, provision for the young and old — beneath the historically specific capitalist forms of wages, profit, and rent. The illusion is that the forms are eternal; the reality is that the forms are historical, while the underlying content (labour and its allocation) is trans-historical.

The closing sentence seals the historical point with a brisk analogy:

"This kind of subsumption, incidentally, is also characteristic of modes of production previously dominant, e.g., the feudal. Relations of production that in no way corresponded to it, standing completely outside it, were subsumed under feudal relationships; e.g., 'tenures in common socage' in England (as opposed to 'tenures on knight's service'), which simply involved monetary obligations and were feudal only in name."

The move completes the chapter's critical trajectory: just as socage tenures were "feudal only in name," so the wage/profit/rent categories applied to a self-employed peasant are "capitalist only in name." Every mode of production, Marx implies, projects its own categories onto alien relations and thereby mistakes itself for the natural form of human economy. The critique of the illusion created by competition is therefore at once a critique of political economy's central category and a contribution to the theory of historical forms of social consciousness.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

3.1 The Fundamental Principle: Bounded Unity vs. Free Composition

The chapter's core systemic law can be stated with maximum precision: every quantum of new value added by labour is a bounded unity (v + s), and the division of that bounded unity into revenue forms obeys specific, heterogeneous laws, but the sum is invariant with respect to the division. The composition-view — wages, profit, and rent as independent inputs whose sum makes commodity value — is exposed as logically self-dissolving on three connected grounds:

  1. The regress/tautology argument. If constant capital is itself a "given" and is thus value-forming, then commodity value forms commodity value (absurd). If constant capital is excluded from value-formation, one has simply denied a component of value that empirically exists.
  2. The money-circularity argument. On the composition-view, "value" reduces to "a certain quantity of money," but money is itself a commodity, so the money magnitude must be determined by the same components it was invoked to determine. Wages = wages; value = value.
  3. The competition argument. Competition equalizes rates and prices but cannot create the level to which it equalizes. If the equilibrium rate of profit cannot be explained by competition (since at equilibrium competition's effect is abolished), then competition is being invoked to conceal a theoretical void.

These three arguments are logically independent but converge on the same result: the composition-view substitutes the form of appearance for the form of determination.

3.2 The Theory of Necessary Appearance (Ideology as Social Fact)

Perhaps the most important theoretical lesson of the chapter is its methodology of appearance. Marx does not treat the illusion as an error of the ignorant. He states that the quid pro quo is "necessary" and gives five structural reasons. This is a template for a materialist theory of ideology:

  • Appearance is generated by the reproduction of social relations, not by deception or stupidity.
  • Conceptual categories (wages, profit, rent) are true at the level of distribution — they are real forms that really govern real transactions — but false at the level of determination, where they conceal the unity of value.
  • The truth of distribution and the falsity of its self-understanding coexist because the system reproduces itself in the form of its own appearance: "its result thus constantly appears as its premise, and its premises as its results."

This is a sophisticated position. The categories of bourgeois economics are not fiction; they are practically valid forms of thought whose validity is strictly bounded by the surface they describe. The illusion is thus socially necessary, and only the theoretically reconstructed order — value → surplus-value → profit → rent → prices of production — can break it.

3.3 The Unity of the Value Theory

The chapter also functions as a defence of the labour theory of value against both Ricardo and the vulgar economists. Against the "adding-up" theorists, Marx shows that the components do not add up to value, since the components presuppose value. Against a naive reading of Ricardo, note that Marx here uses Ricardo's own correct claim (footnote 56: the value of raw produce, manufactures, and metals "depend[s] not on the rate of profits, nor on the rate of wages, nor on the rent paid for mines, but on the total quantity of labour necessary") as a fixed point. Marx's chapter is a systematic demonstration that when you try to construct value from its revenue-parts, you end up retrieving Ricardo's own conclusion by an irrational detour — the surcharge exercise (100 + 10 + 15 = 125, alternatively 80 + 8 + 12 = 100) shows that the components-view can only be made consistent by covertly presupposing labour-value.

The chapter therefore exhibits the superiority of Marx's architecture: it preserves both the determination of value by labour-time and the entire apparatus of distribution, transformation, and appearance, without collapsing either into the other. The key to this preservation is the strict distinction between (i) labour-time as the determinative substance of value, (ii) value as the quantitative premise of the fresh labour added, and (iii) the division of that premise into the historically specific revenue forms, the relative sizes of which are governed by social laws (the value of labour-power, the rate of surplus-value, profit-rate equalization, landed property, competition).

3.4 The Theory of the Working Day and the Limits of Class Conflict

The starred section (§2.8) draws a striking downward limit-chain:

  • The necessary part of the working day is limited from below by the physical minimum (interpreted historically and socially).
  • The surplus part is limited from above by the physical maximum of the working day.
  • Therefore the total share of profit and rent is absolutely bounded.

This has a political implication that is theoretical rather than moralizing: the class struggle over the length of the working day, the intensity of labour, and wages is a struggle over the division of a bounded value, not over the production of value. Competition between capitalists, no matter how fierce, cannot increase the total social surplus-value; it can only redistribute it and enforce its extraction. This is why the chapter repeatedly uses the phrase that there is "nothing left" beyond what labour has produced — a statement that is at once analytic and historically fateful.

3.5 Interest and Profit of Enterprise: The Indeterminacy of Rentier Capital

The treatment of the interest/profit-of-enterprise split is one of the chapter's most subtle contributions: it identifies the one division that is law-indeterminate, i.e., purely competition-determined. This exception proves the rule of the chapter: the only portion of the distributional apparatus that is genuinely open to pure bargaining is the share between two owners of the same factor (capital). Every other division has a law. The implication is that the rentier position is theoretically and legally distinct without being economically decisive: the level of profit as such is law-determined; the split is contingent. This has implications for theories of finance that one can still see echoed in later Marxian work.

3.6 Monopoly Price: Redistribution, Not Transgression

The chapter's handling of monopoly price is important methodologically. Marx insists that monopoly price does not create value or abolish limits; it transfers surplus-value from other producers and, where monopoly goods enter worker consumption, may redistribute the burden between workers (via real wages) and other capitalists. "The limits within which monopoly price affects the normal regulation of commodity prices are firmly determined and can be precisely calculated." The method is therefore: explain the anomaly by locating it within the bounded social surplus-value, not by abandoning the law. This is a generally applicable analytical principle — to explain apparently free-floating prices by the movement of a bounded underlying magnitude.

3.7 Naturalization as the Political Function of the Illusion

Reason Five is arguably the most politically consequential section of the chapter. By showing that the revenue-forms are extended, "by way of analogy," to the independent small producer, Marx identifies the mechanism by which capitalist relations are made to appear natural and universal. The category "employer of himself," "his own landlord," is a miniature of the ideological operation of an entire mode of production: the projection of historically specific categories onto relations that are not capitalist at all, and thereby the transmutation of historical forms into natural kinds. The closing image of socage tenures — "feudal only in name" — completes the move: every historical mode of production produces the same kind of projection, which is why each one believes itself eternal.

3.8 The Post-Capitalist Horizon

The closing paragraph's enumeration — consumption scaled to "the full development of individuality," insurance/reserve funds, reproduction expanded "in the degree determined by social need," and provision for the young and old — outlines a rational core that survives the abolition of the specifically capitalist forms. The essential methodological point is that these "foundations of the forms that are common to all social modes of production" are recognizable precisely because the illusion has been dispelled. The critique of the illusion is thus not merely negative but revelatory: the same analytical labour that dissolves the composition-view also reveals the trans-historical content that capitalism distorts. This is the positive upshot of the entire Part VII argument and the implicit bridge from Capital to Marx's broader project of a post-capitalist political economy.

3.9 Practical and Theoretical Summary

In summary, Chapter 50 establishes five durable propositions:

  1. Value is logically prior to its division. Commodity value, less constant capital, is the quantitative premise out of which wages, profit, and rent are dissolved; the reverse order is incoherent.
  2. The illusion of composition is generated by the surface and by practice, not by error. It is produced by the contractual, accounting, and market forms through which the system reproduces itself; hence "its result constantly appears as its premise."
  3. Competition is a mechanism of equalization, not a source of magnitude. The level to which it equalizes must be explained by something else — here, by the total social surplus-value and its ratio to total social capital.
  4. Every revenue form has a limit; some limits are law-determined, one (interest/profit of enterprise) is competition-determined, and all are bounded by the surplus-value produced. Monopoly price redistributes but does not escape the bounds.
  5. The revenue categories are historically specific and are illegitimately projected onto non-capitalist relations, thereby producing the appearance of the eternity of capitalism. The forms are historical; the underlying foundations of labour, consumption, insurance, and reproduction are common to all social modes.

The chapter is therefore not merely the closing section of a technical treatise. It is Marx's most systematic account of how a mode of production necessarily misunderstands itself — and of what critical theory must therefore be: not a correction of isolated errors, but the reconstruction of the real order beneath the inverted appearance. Its methodological lesson — that socially necessary appearance must be explained from the structure that produces it, not dismissed as false — remains one of the most powerful and portable insights of the Marxian tradition, and its analytic core (the bounded unity of fresh value, the heterogeneity of the laws dividing it, the distinction between determination and distribution) remains essential for any serious theory of distribution under capitalism.
