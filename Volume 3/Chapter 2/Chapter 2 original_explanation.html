Volume 3 • Chapter 2: The Rate of Profit

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

This is Chapter 2 of Volume 3 of Capital, the opening chapter of the section entitled "The Transformation of Surplus-Value into Profit." Its title — "The Rate of Profit" — appears disarmingly narrow, but the chapter is in fact the hinge upon which the entire argument of Volume 3 turns. Its central thesis is at once simple and revolutionary: profit is not a magnitude different from surplus-value; it is the same surplus-value appearing in a transformed form — a form in which its origin in the exploitation of living labour is veiled, and in which capital seems to reproduce itself out of its own hidden productive qualities. Correspondingly, the rate of profit is not a new economic quantity but a second, alternative measure of the surplus-value already measured by the rate of surplus-value. The two rates are "two different standards for measuring the same quantity," and precisely because of this they "express the different relations in which the same quantity may stand."

The chapter's central problem is therefore not arithmetic but philosophical: why and how does this transformation occur, and why is it necessary? Marx's answer is that the transformation of surplus-value into profit is the subjective reflex — the "transposed consciousness" — of an objective inversion that already occurs in the production process itself, and that is further developed by the circulation process. The chapter is thus an exercise in what Marx calls the sifting of appearance from essence: surplus-value and the rate of surplus-value are "the invisible essence to be investigated," while profit and the rate of profit are "visible surface phenomena." Yet Marx insists — against any naive reading of this essence/appearance divide — that it is not a case of error to be corrected by demonstrating the truth. The transformation is a real necessity of the capitalist mode of production, and the mystification it produces is not a contingent illusion but a socially objective form.

The chapter's intellectual objective, then, is threefold. First, to formally derive the rate of profit p′ = s/C from the rate of surplus-value s′ = s/v, showing that the numerator (s) is identical while the denominator changes. Second, to demonstrate that the direction of derivation is one-way — the rate of-profit is derived from the rate of surplus-value and not the reverse — while simultaneously acknowledging the historical priority of the profit rate (it is "the historical starting-point"). Third, and most ambitiously, to explain the necessity of mystification: why the capitalist, and the capitalist's theoretical spokesmen (Ramsay, Malthus, Senior, Torrens, and the Ricardian school), necessarily fail to see the extraction of surplus labour behind the surface form of profit, and why the "further we trace out the valorization process of capital, the more is the capital relationship mystified."

Fitting this into the broader framework: Volume 1 revealed exploitation within the immediate process of production; Volume 2 traced the circuit of capital through circulation. Volume 3 must confront the forms in which capital appears to the participants — profit, interest, rent, the "revenue" forms — and demonstrate their dependence on surplus-value. Chapter 2 is the decisive first step in this confrontation: it establishes that the stuff of profit is surplus-value and that its form is a concealment. It also sets the programme for what follows: in this Part, profit and surplus-value are treated as "numerically identical magnitudes, different only in form"; in the next Part, Marx will show how profit comes to appear as distinct from surplus-value "in a numerical respect as well." The chapter thus knowingly sets up its own supersession.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Starting Point: M–C–M′ and the Capitalist's Undifferentiated Advance of Capital

Marx opens not with the rate of profit but with the general formula for capital, M–C–M′: "a sum of value is cast into circulation in order to extract a greater sum." This is the logical point of departure because the entire problematic of the chapter grows out of the capitalist's own standpoint. The capitalist does not produce commodities for their use-value or for personal consumption; "the product in which the capitalist is really interested is not the palpable product itself, but rather the excess in the value of the product over and above the value of the capital consumed in it." The category of profit is thus born from the standpoint of the advancing capitalist, not from the anatomy of production.

The crucial analytical move is that the capitalist "advances the capital as a whole without considering the different roles that its components will play in the production of surplus-value." This is the seed of the entire mystification. He advances all components equally — not only to reproduce the advanced capital but to produce an excess over and above it. Here Marx establishes the conditions of exploitation: the capitalist can convert value into a higher value "only by exchanging it with living labour, by the exploitation of living labour." But exploitation is possible only if he simultaneously advances the conditions of this labour — "means and object of labour, machinery and raw materials" — i.e., only by transforming a sum of value into the form of the conditions of production. The capitalist is a capitalist at all only because he confronts the worker as proprietor of the conditions of labour, while the worker owns only labour-power. The footnote to Volume 1 (pp. 270–74, 874–6) anchors this in the earlier analysis of "primitive accumulation" and the separation of the producer from the means of production. So at the very outset the chapter reminds us that the social relation of capital (the property relation) is what makes the material elements of production function as capital — a point that will later be suppressed in the surface form.

The Equivalence of the Two Viewpoints and the Move from s/v to s/C

Marx now introduces a rhetorical symmetry that dissolves into a real identity: "It makes no difference to the capitalist whether we see him as advancing the constant capital to make a profit out of his variable capital, or advancing the variable capital in order to valorize the constant." On the one side, the capitalist lays out money on wages "in order to give machines and raw material a higher value"; on the other, he advances money in machinery and raw material "in order to exploit labour." This is not merely a matter of perspective — it expresses a real reciprocity: "it is only the variable part of capital that creates surplus-value, [but] it does so only under the condition that the other parts are advanced as well." Each presupposes the other, and "these are both one and the same in his eyes."

The decisive consequence is then stated: "the actual degree of his profit is determined in relation not to his variable capital but to his total capital; not by the rate of surplus-value but by the rate of profit." We should pause on the phrase "may remain the same while expressing different rates of surplus-value" — this is Marx's signal that the rate of profit is a lossy measure, one that can hide radical differences in exploitation. Two capitals with the same profit rate can rest on very different rates of surplus-value, depending on their organic composition. This single remark contains the germ of the later analysis of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall and the equalization of profit rates across branches.

Cost Price, Value, and the Locus of Profit

Having established the capitalist's standpoint, Marx defines the costs of the product: "all the components of its value which the capitalist has paid for, or for which he has cast an equivalent into the production process." These costs must be replaced "even if his capital is to do no more than maintain itself, reproduce its original magnitude." This is the cost price (what later editions will designate k), and it is strictly less than the value of the commodity.

The distinction is now drawn with precision. "The value contained in a commodity is equal to the labour-time taken in making it, and this consists of both paid and unpaid labour." The value, in other words, is the total labour objectified: c + v + s. But "the costs of the commodity for the capitalist … include only the part of the labour objectified in it for which he has actually paid" — that is, c + v (with the caveat, elaborated later, that c enters in varying degrees depending on fixed/circulating form). The surplus labour contained in the commodity costs the capitalist nothing, even though it costs the worker labour, every bit as much as the paid labour does. Both paid and unpaid labour create value and enter the commodity as elements of value formation; but only the paid portion enters the capitalist's cost accounting.

From this emerges the chapter's most quotable formulation of profit: "The capitalist's profit, therefore, comes from the fact that he has something to sell for which he has not paid." Surplus-value or profit "consists precisely in the excess of commodity value over its cost price, i.e. in the excess of the total sum of labour contained in the commodity over the sum of labour that is actually paid for." And from wherever it may derive, surplus-value is "an excess over and above the total capital advanced." This excess "stands in a certain ratio to the total capital, as expressed by the fraction s/C, where C stands for the total capital." Hence:

  • Rate of profit: p′ = s/C (surplus-value over total capital advanced)
  • Rate of surplus-value: s′ = s/v (surplus-value over variable capital)

The text's OCR renders these as garbled glyphs, but the intention is unambiguous: the rate of profit is the surplus measured against the whole C = c + v; the rate of surplus-value is the same surplus measured against v alone.

Two Standards, One Quantity: The Realm of Measurement

Marx now crystallizes the point: "The rate of surplus-value, as measured against the variable capital, is known as the rate of surplus-value; the rate of surplus-value, as measured against the total capital, is known as the rate of profit." (Here the second "rate of surplus-value" should be read as the same quantity, surplus-value, measured against total capital.) The pair are "two different standards for measuring the same quantity, and as a result are able to express the different relations in which the same quantity may stand." This is a subtle and important formal point: unlike the difference between, say, velocity and acceleration, the difference between the two rates is only a difference of denominator. The numerator is identical. This identity of substance under a difference of measure is what allows Marx to say later that profit and surplus-value are "numerically identical magnitudes, different only in form."

The Direction of Derivation: Essence versus Surface

Next comes a methodological claim of the first order: "It is the transformation of surplus-value into profit that is derived from the transformation of the rate of surplus-value into the profit rate, not the other way round." But Marx immediately complicates this by adding that "in actual fact, the rate of profit is the historical starting-point." The relation of logical derivation and the relation of historical appearance run in opposite directions. This is the classic Marxian structure of essence and appearance: "Surplus-value and the rate of surplus-value are, relative to this, the invisible essence to be investigated, whereas the rate of profit and hence the form of surplus-value as profit are visible surface phenomena." The analyst begins from what is visible (profit) and works back to what is hidden (surplus-value); but the explanatory derivation runs from essence to appearance. Any attempt to invert this explanatory order — to read the laws of surplus-value directly off the laws of profit — is, Marx will later say, "completely wrong-headed."

The Capitalist's Interest in Disguise

Marx now ties the epistemological problem to a material interest. "As far as the individual capitalist is concerned, it is evident enough that the only thing that interests him is the ratio of the surplus-value … to the total capital advanced." Not only do the specific ratios of the excess to the particular components, and its "inner connections with them," fail to interest him — "it is actually in his interest to disguise these particular ratios and inner connections." This is a critical move: the mystification is not merely a cognitive error but is functionally motivated. The capitalist has a practical stake in not seeing the extraction of surplus labour as such. This anticipates the later analysis of "the trinity formula" and the fetishism of capital.

Realization in Circulation and the Appearance of Mutual Cheating

The next beat locates the second source of mystification. The excess value "arises in the immediate process of production," but "it is only in the circulation process that it is realized." Because it is realized there, and "in the world as it actually is, the world of competition, i.e. on the market, it depends on market conditions whether or not this excess is realized and to what extent," it "appears all the more readily to derive from the circulation process." Marx is careful to contain this: "if a commodity is sold above or below its value, there is simply a different distribution of the surplus-value," and this redistribution "in no way affects either the magnitude or the character of the surplus-value itself." But from the individual capitalist's vantage, this containment is invisible. The circulation process is the "scene of those transformations considered in Volume 2," and these coincide with "actual competition, the purchase and sale of commodities above or below their value, so that … the surplus-value that he realizes depends just as much on this mutual cheating as on the direct exploitation of labour."

Here we meet a crucial nuance: Marx does not deny that realized surplus-value varies with market conditions; he denies that this affects the magnitude or character of surplus-value as such. The individual's experience is not simply false — it is a partial, abstract truth that, when taken as the whole, becomes the source of a systematic illusion. Circulation time, moreover, "restrict[s] the surplus-value that can be realized in a certain period," and other aspects deriving from circulation "react with decisive effect on the immediate process of production itself" (e.g., turnover time forcing speed-up, or lengthening the working day). The two processes "constantly run into one another and intertwine, and in this way their distinguishing features are continuously blurred."

The Stepping-Out of Capital into External Relations

Marx now offers an almost dramatic description of the passage from the "inner organic life" of capital to its "external relations": in circulation, "it is not capital and labour that confront one another, but on the one hand capital and capital, and on the other hand individuals as simple buyers and sellers once again." Circulation time and working time "cut across each other's paths, and both appear to determine surplus-value in the same way." The consequence is that the "original form in which capital and wage-labour confront one another is disguised by the intervention of relations that seem to be independent of this." Surplus-value no longer appears "as having been produced by the appropriation of labour-time, but as the excess of the sale price of commodities over their cost price." And this cost price "readily present[s] itself therefore as their proper value (valeur intrinseque), so that profit appears as an excess of the sale price of commodities over their immanent value." The introduction of the French term valeur intrinseque (intrinsic value) is pointed: it names the physiocratic/pre-classical notion of a value inherent in the commodity (land, or matter), against which the sale price may be above or below — a notion Marx is here showing to be the natural self-understanding of the circulation standpoint.

The Twofold Reinforcement of Mystification: Evanescence and the Cost Rubric

Marx now confronts a possible objection: does the capitalist not, after all, know that profit comes from the labour-time of others, given "his greed for the labour-time of others"? Yes — but this inkling is systematically dissolved by two mechanisms, enumerated as (1) and (2).

(1) The evanescence of the production process. "The immediate process of production is itself simply an evanescent moment, which is constantly passing over into the process of circulation, and vice versa." Thus any insight into the source of profit that dawns in production "appears at the most as an equally valid moment alongside the notion that the excess … derives from the sphere of circulation, a movement therefore that capital possesses independently of its relation to labour." Marx then names the economists who elevate this moment into theory: Ramsay, Malthus, Senior, Torrens, who adduce "these phenomena of circulation … as direct proofs that capital in its mere material existence, independently of its social relation to labour (which is precisely how it comes to be capital), is an autonomous source of surplus-value alongside labour and independent of it." This is a crucial nuance: Marx is not simply dismissing these economists as foolish. He is showing that their error has a real foundation in the appearances generated by the circulation process. Their theory is the conceptual reflex of a real mystification.

(2) The cost-price rubric. "Under the heading of costs, which include not only wages but also the price of raw material, the depreciation of the machinery, etc., the extortion of unpaid labour appears simply as an economy in the payment for one of the articles that comprise these costs." That is, surplus labour is booked as if it were merely a cheap purchase of an input — "similar to that made when raw material is bought more cheaply or the wear and tear of machinery is reduced." The specific character of surplus labour is thereby lost; "its specific relationship to surplus-value is obscured." And this obscuring is "greatly furthered and facilitated by the representation of the value of labour-power in the form of wages" (Volume 1, Part Six, Chapter 19). In other words, because wages appear as the price of labour (rather than the price of labour-power), the unpaid portion of the working day has no separate line in the accounts; it appears as a saving on the wage bill rather than as the very substance of profit. Here Marx connects the form of the wage — analyzed in Volume 1 — to the form of profit analyzed here.

The combined result: "Since all sections of capital equally appear as sources of the excess value (profit), the capital relation is mystified."

The Inversion of Subject and Object

Marx now deepens the diagnosis by connecting it to a more fundamental inversion already established in Volume 1. "The way that surplus-value is transformed into the form of profit, by way of the rate of profit, is only a further extension of that inversion of subject and object which already occurs in the course of the production process itself." In production, "all the subjective productive forces of labour present themselves as productive forces of capital" (footnote to Volume 1, pp. 450–53). On one side, "value, i.e. the past labour that dominates living labour, is personified into the capitalist"; on the other, "the worker conversely appears as mere objectified labour-power, as a commodity." This inverted real relationship "necessarily gives rise" to "a correspondingly inverted conception of the situation, a transposed consciousness, which is further developed by the transformations and modifications of the circulation process proper." This "transposed consciousness" (verkehrtes Bewußtsein) is a structural category, not a psychological failing. The mystification is the necessary subjective form of an objectively inverted relation.

The Ricardian Error and the Prohibition on Collapsing the Two Rates

The chapter now issues a direct warning against a specific theoretical mistake: "As can be studied in the case of the Ricardian school, it is completely wrong-headed to seek directly to present the laws of the profit rate as laws of the rate of surplus-value, or vice versa." The Ricardians — attempting to explain profit directly by the labour theory of value — collapsed the two rates, and thereby ran into insoluble contradictions (e.g., how equal profit rates coexist with unequal rates of surplus-value across branches). Marx's point is that the profit rate is a genuinely different object of law from the surplus-value rate: the surplus-value rate has laws (its determination by the working day, intensity, productivity), and the profit rate has its own additional laws (determination by organic composition and turnover). To reduce one to the other is to lose the very phenomena one means to explain.

He then specifies what s/C does and does not express. In the capitalist's mind, "these things are of course not distinguished." The expression s/C "measures surplus-value against the value of the total capital advanced for its production, of which one part is completely consumed in this production, while another part is simply applied." Thus s/C "expresses the degree of valorization of the whole capital advanced; i.e., viewed in accordance with the conceptual, inner connection and the actual nature of surplus-value, it shows how the variation of the variable capital is related in magnitude to the total capital advanced." The rate of profit, correctly interpreted, still contains the secret — but only if one already knows to look for it.

The Technical Proportion and the Absence of Any Inner Relation Between Constant Capital and Surplus-Value

This is the chapter's most analytically dense passage. Marx argues: "In itself, the value of the total capital stands in no inner relationship to the amount of surplus-value, at least not directly." His proof proceeds by way of technical proportions. The constant capital, materially considered, "consists of the material conditions for the realization of labour — its materials and means." For a definite quantity of labour to be realized, a definite quantity of materials and means is required, a "definite technical proportion" between living labour and the mass of means of production, depending on the particular character of the labour. There is likewise "a definite proportion between the amount of surplus-value or surplus labour, and the mass of means of production."

He gives a numerical example. Suppose 6 hours of labour are needed to produce the worker's wage; the worker must then work 12 hours to perform 6 hours of surplus labour and create a surplus-value of 100 per cent. In 12 hours he consumes twice as much means of production as in 6. But — and this is the pivot — "this does not mean that the surplus-value he adds in 6 hours stands in any direct relationship to the value of the means of production that are used in these 6 or 12 hours. Their value is completely immaterial here; what matters is the amount technically needed."

Marx illustrates with the cotton example. If x lb. of cotton are spun per hour and cost y shillings, then in 12 hours 12x lb. (= 12y shillings) are spun. One can therefore compute the ratio of surplus-value to the value spun in 12 hours as well as in 6. But the ratio enters only "in as much as y shillings serves as the name for x lb. of cotton" — i.e., price functions here merely as an index of the quantity technically required, valid only so long as the price of cotton is unchanged. Hence: "If I know that in order to appropriate 6 hours' surplus labour I have to have the workers perform 12 hours' labour, I must have enough cotton ready for 12 hours, and if I know the price of this quantity … there exists in this roundabout way a certain relationship between the price of cotton … and the surplus-value." But I can never argue conversely from the price of the raw material to the quantity that can be spun in one hour. The relation is asymmetric and derivative, not intrinsic.

Conclusion: "There is thus no inner and necessary relationship between the value of the constant capital and the surplus-value, nor, hence, is there one between the value of the total capital (= c + v) and the surplus-value." This is the formal demolition of any attempt to derive profit from the value of capital advanced. The rate of profit is an inner-relationless measure — it correlates mathematically but expresses no causal internal connection. (Elsewhere Marx will show that it is the organic composition, not the price or value level of constant capital as such, that mediates the relation between s and c — but here he confines himself to denying the direct relation.)

Starting from the Rate of Profit: The Uniformity of Capital's Parts and Fixed vs. Circulating

Marx then confronts the epistemic asymmetry in practice. "If the rate of surplus-value and its absolute magnitude are both given, the rate of profit expresses no more than what it in fact is, i.e. an alternative measurement of surplus-value, its measurement in terms of the value of the total capital, instead of in terms of the value of that part of capital from which it directly derives." "In actuality, however, i.e. in the world of phenomena, things are the other way round." What is given in the market is the excess of sale price over cost price, and its ratio to total capital. The origin of the excess "remains a mystery" — from exploitation, mutual cheating, or both. And critically: "if we start from this rate of profit, we can never establish any specific relationship between the excess and the part of capital laid out on wages."

This is the heart of the argument. The rate of profit is, by construction, blind to the internal differentiation of capital. It shows "a uniform relationship of the excess to equally important parts of the capital, which from this point of view exhibits no internal distinctions apart from that between fixed and circulating." Marx then shows how even this residual distinction arises only retrospectively, from the two ways the excess is calculated: (a) as a simple quantity — the excess over cost price — in which circulating capital enters "in full" while fixed capital enters "only to the extent of its depreciation"; and (b) as the relationship of the excess to the total value of capital advanced, in which "the value of the entire fixed capital comes into the calculation as much as the value of the circulating capital." Hence circulating capital is treated the same way in both computations, while fixed capital is treated differently in the first from the second. "Thus the distinction between circulating and fixed capital suggests itself here to us as the only one." Even here, then, the surface form retains only a pale, derivative trace of the real internal distinctions (c versus v) that determine surplus-value.

This remarkable passage also shows that the fixed/circulating distinction, though real and important for turnover, is not the value-form distinction that matters for surplus-value. It is a distinction belonging to the appearance of profit accounting — and only here does it force itself upon us.

Marx adds an aside on Malthus: "We shall see in a later chapter the amusing capers Malthus cuts when he tries in this way to penetrate the secret of surplus-value and its specific relationship to the variable part of capital" (see Theories of Surplus-Value, Part III, pp. 31–4). This is an anticipatory dismissal of the attempt to reach the secret through the profit rate rather than through the analysis of surplus-value as such.

The Hegelian Reflection and Profit as Appearance

Marx now offers a deliberately Hegelian formulation: "the excess is reflected back into itself from the rate of profit … the excess, which is characterized more specifically by the rate of profit, appears as an excess which the capital produces over and above its own value, either annually or in some definite period of circulation." Reflection-into-self (Reflexion in sich) is the Hegelian category of a determination that, having passed through otherness, returns to itself as its own ground. Here capital, having passed through production and circulation, returns as self-valorizing: the excess seems to be its own product, not the product of the appropriation of another's labour.

This yields the chapter's culminating statement of the form/content relation: "even if the rate of profit is numerically different from the rate of surplus-value, while surplus-value and profit are in fact the same and even numerically identical, profit is still for all that a transformed form of surplus-value, a form in which its origin and the secret of its existence are veiled and obliterated." Profit is the form of appearance (Erscheinungsform) of surplus-value; surplus-value "can be sifted out from the former only by analysis." In surplus-value, "the relationship between capital and labour is laid bare"; in profit, "capital appears as a relationship to itself, a relationship in which it is distinguished, as an original sum of value, from another new value that it posits." To consciousness, "it appears as if capital creates this new value in the course of its movement through the production and circulation processes." And crucially: "how this happens is now mystified, and appears to derive from hidden qualities that are inherent in capital itself." The mysterious "productive power of capital" — capital as a self-creating substance — is the theoretical horizon toward which the whole chapter drives.

The final sentence of the substantive argument states the general law of the argument: "The further we trace out the valorization process of capital, the more is the capital relationship mystified and the less are the secrets of its internal organization laid bare." This is a law of increasing obscurity: the closer we come to the surface of economic life (profit, interest, rent), the more opaque the underlying social relation becomes. It is a programmatic statement for the whole of Volume 3.

Looking Forward: The Plan for This Part and the Next

The chapter closes with its prospective statement: "In this Part, the rate of profit is taken as numerically different from the rate of surplus-value; profit and surplus-value on the other hand are treated as numerically identical magnitudes, different only in form." This is a precise statement of the abstraction level operative in Part 1 of Volume 3. Then: "In the following Part we shall observe the further development of the externalization by which profit presents itself as a magnitude distinct from surplus-value in a numerical respect as well." Marx signals that the mystification is not static but progressive — it deepens as we move through the equalization of profit rates (Part 2), the tendency of the rate of profit to fall (Part 3), merchant's capital, interest-bearing capital, and ground-rent. The next step, the formation of a general rate of profit, will make profit numerically diverge from surplus-value in each sphere, completing the appearance of capital as an autonomous source of value.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The fundamental principle revealed by this chapter is that of the necessarily mystifying form of capitalist economic categories. Profit is not an illusion in the sense of a false belief that could be dispelled by simply informing capitalists of the truth; it is the objective form of appearance that surplus-value necessarily assumes on the surface of capitalist society. Marx's argument is therefore a demonstration that appearance is real — that surface phenomena are not mere errors but structurally produced forms with their own necessity, regularity, and explanatory weight. This is the methodological core of Volume 3 as a whole: to trace the "forms of the process as a whole" (Gestaltungen des Gesamtprozesses) without either reducing them to the production process (the Ricardian error) or treating them as autonomous (the vulgar error).

The second lesson is the asymmetry of explanation. Essence explains appearance, but appearance does not explain essence — and yet, "in actuality … things are the other way round." The analyst begins from the surface (profit) because that is the historical and experiential starting point, but the scientific movement runs from surplus-value to profit. This is a general law of critical social science: the categories in which agents experience their world are the categories that conceal the social relations producing that world. Marx calls this a "transposed consciousness," and its roots lie not in the mind but in the "inversion of subject and object" of the production process itself, where the productive forces of labour are presented as those of capital.

The third lesson is the critical role of the denominator. The chapter demonstrates with mathematical precision that the entire difference between the rate of surplus-value and the rate of profit lies in the denominator — v versus C = c + v. This apparently minor formal shift has enormous consequences: it makes profit blind to exploitation intensity, blind to differences in organic composition, blind even to the c/v distinction (retaining only the fixed/circulating trace). The rate of profit thus functions as a systematic concealer. The absence of any "inner and necessary relationship" between the value of constant capital and surplus-value is the formal guarantee that no direct reading of exploitation off the profit rate is possible.

The fourth lesson concerns the material motivation of mystification. Marx does not treat the capitalist's blindness as mere stupidity; he shows "it is actually in his interest to disguise these particular ratios and inner connections." The form of profit corresponds to a real class interest in not seeing the source of profit in unpaid labour. This is an early and decisive instance of the Marxian critique of ideology as rooted in material position rather than in error.

The broader implications are considerable. For the interpretation of Volume 3 as a whole, the chapter establishes that the analysis of profit, interest, rent, and the "trinity formula" will be an anatomy of appearances — each form further from the essence than the last, and the whole movement characterized by the law that "the further we trace out the valorization process of capital, the more is the capital relationship mystified." For political economy as a critical project, the chapter establishes that "vulgar economy" is not simply wrong but is the conceptual reflex of the surface form — a demonstration that Marx's critique operates at a higher level than mere refutation of error. For the theory of crisis and of class struggle, the chapter explains why workers and capitalists alike experience the economy in terms of profitability rather than exploitation, and why the actual rate of surplus-value may rise even as the experienced rate of profit falls. And for the philosophy of social science, the chapter provides a rigorous model of the relation between essence and appearance, real abstraction, and the necessity of mystification — a model whose import extends far beyond the critique of political economy to any inquiry into how social structures generate the categories through which they are lived.

The chapter's final sentence thus functions as both conclusion and overture: the externalization of surplus-value into profit, here shown in its form, will in the following Part be shown in its numerical divergence — and the veil, already thick, will grow thicker still. What Marx establishes here, once and for all, is that the project of Volume 3 is not to deny the reality of profit but to explain the necessity of its misrecognized form — and thereby to strip from the capital relation the last semblance of its self-creating autonomy.
