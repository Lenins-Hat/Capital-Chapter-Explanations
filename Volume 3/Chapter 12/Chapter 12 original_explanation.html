Volume 3 • Chapter 12: Supplementary Remarks on Prices of Production

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 12 of Capital Volume 3 carries the unassuming title "Supplementary Remarks." The label conceals one of the most methodologically explosive short chapters in the entire work. It is not a fresh theory but a mopping-up operation: Marx here closes three specific loopholes left open by the preceding analysis of the transformation of values into prices of production, and in closing them he lays bare his full position on the relationship between the visible surface of capitalist economy and its concealed inner core.

The central problem is this. Marx has just argued that commodities do not exchange at their values but at prices of production, defined as k + p — the cost price plus average profit. This creates three embarrassments. First, if prices of production are their own law of motion, what governs their changes, and can that law still be traced back to value? Second, for capitals of average composition Marx has claimed the special privilege that price of production equals value — but the inputs of those very capitals are bought at prices of production, not values. Does this not introduce a circularity that empties the claim of content? Third, and deepest: competition — the only thing the actors themselves see — displays average profits that seem independent of the organic composition of capital, and prices that rise when wages fall (a phenomenon that appears to flatly contradict the labour theory of value). The phenomenal world therefore seems to refute the theory that is supposed to explain it.

The core thesis is a triple claim. (a) Every change in a price of production can ultimately be reduced to a change in value, but not conversely: value changes may fail to register in prices of production because the latter are determined not by the value of the single commodity but by the total value of all commodities. (b) The average-composition case survives the input-price circularity because what determines surplus-value — hence average profit — is not the magnitude of the cost price but the ratio of v to (c + v); the figures 80c + 20v need not be value-figures, only properly proportional ones. (c) The "grounds for compensation" that capitalists experience as the source of their profit — risk, slow turnover, distant markets, cyclical fluctuations — are nothing but the mechanisms through which total social surplus-value is redistributed, a system of shareholding in the collective social capital. The capitalist's consciousness inverts this: he sees compensation as creating profit.

The intellectual objective, then, is not merely technical repair. It is to demonstrate that the phenomenal forms of competition are not incomplete or imperfect representations of the essence but its "very reverse and antithesis" — a systematic inversion that therefore cannot be corrected by gazing harder at appearances, but only by a critical science that reconstructs the concealed inner core.

Systemic placement. The chapter sits at the hinge of Volume 3, Part II. It follows the demonstration of the general rate of profit and precedes Part III (the tendency of the rate of profit to fall) and Parts IV–VII (commercial capital, interest-bearing capital, ground rent). Its closing gesture — the "common booty," the "whole social enterprise," the "inverted configuration" — prepares the terrain for the later analysis in which merchant's profit, interest, and rent will appear to the actors as autonomous sources of revenue, each with its own "justification." The chapter is the epistemological antechamber to the "trinity formula" that Marx will dismantle at the book's end.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Two Doors Through Which a Price of Production Can Move

Marx opens with a ruthless dichotomization. "The price of production of a commodity can vary for only two reasons": either (1) the general rate of profit changes, or (2) the general rate of profit remains unaltered. This exhausts logical space because the price of production is a composed magnitude — k plus the profit accrued on k at the general rate — so any movement in it must originate either in the general rate that scales profit or in the cost price/value base itself. The first door leads outside the individual commodity, into the economy-wide determinants of profitability; the second door leads inside, into the value of the commodity's own reproduction. Everything that follows unpacks these two doors.

The first door immediately generates a question: what could cause the general rate of profit to change? Marx decomposes this further. The general rate r is, definitionally, the mass of surplus-value divided by the total social capital advanced — structurally r = s/(c + v), which can be rewritten as (s/v) / [(c/v) + 1]. Therefore r can change only if either the average rate of surplus-value s/v changes, or — holding s/v fixed — the ratio of the total capital's constant to variable components (its value-composition) changes. Marx's opening paragraph is nothing more than this algebraic truism, but he immediately begins converting algebra into social causation by asking what, in the world of production, could make either numerator or denominator move.

The General Rate of Profit Traces Back to Productivity and Value

Consider the first sub-case: a change in the average rate of surplus-value. Marx immediately brackets two "movements" as inadmissible: a depression of wages below their "normal level" or a rise above it. These, he insists, "are never more than oscillations" — temporary deviations from the value of labour-power, not changes in the value of labour-power itself. This bracketing is crucial and methodological: Marx refuses to let the accidental wobbles of the labour market masquerade as explanations of structural change. Once they are set aside, a sustained change in the rate of surplus-value "can occur only because the value of labour-power has either fallen or risen."

But a change in the value of labour-power is itself a derived phenomenon. It depends on "the value of the commodities that are consumed by the worker," and those values in turn depend on "the productivity of that labour which produces the means of subsistence." Here is the causal chain compressed into a single beat: a change in the rate of surplus-value → a change in the value of labour-power → a change in the value of wage goods → a change in the productivity of labour in the wage-goods sector. The entire movement of the first sub-case is thus traceable, at its root, to a change in the productivity of labour and therefore to a change in commodity values. Nothing in the movement of the rate of profit originates outside the value-determining process.

Now the second sub-case: the ratio between the sum of surplus-value appropriated and the total social capital advanced changes, while the rate of surplus-value remains fixed. Since this change "does not arise from the rate of surplus-value," it "must proceed from the total capital, and moreover from its constant part" — that is, from c. Marx's reasoning proceeds in three steps. (i) The technical mass of constant capital is functionally proportional to the labour-power bought by variable capital; more workers require more raw material, more instruments, more auxiliary materials. (ii) The value of that constant capital rises and falls with its technical mass. (iii) Therefore the mass of constant capital rises and falls "similarly in proportion to the sum of value of the variable capital." But this proportionality is not a fixed constant — it is the level of productivity. "If the same labour sets more constant capital in motion, it has become more productive, and vice versa." The conclusion therefore follows directly: a change in the composition of capital at a fixed rate of surplus-value is nothing other than a change in the productivity of labour, and "a change must have occurred in the value of certain commodities."

The importance of this passage can hardly be overstated. It closes the first door from the inside: any movement of the general rate of profit, whether through the numerator (s/v) or the denominator (c/v), routes back through productivity and hence through value. The rate of profit is not an autonomous force; it is a surface trace of underlying movements in the productivity of labour and the values of commodities. This is the labour theory of value operating not as a price-formation formula (which Marx explicitly denies it is, in the aggregate) but as a theory of the causal structure of the capitalist economy.

The Master Law: Value May Change Without Necessarily Changing Price

Marx now fuses both sub-cases into a single law: "if the production price of a commodity changes as the result of a change in the general rate of profit, its own value may well remain unaffected. However, there must have been a change in its value relative to other commodities." This is subtle and worth unpacking carefully. Under a change in the general rate of profit, a commodity might have undergone no change at all in its own value — the same labour-time still reproduces it — and yet its price of production moves, because the profit added to its cost price has changed, or because the total value configuration that fixes the general rate has changed. What must have changed is its value relative to other commodities: some other commodity's value must have shifted, producing the shift in the general rate, and hence in this commodity's price. Relative value is the relational ground of the movement; absolute individual value may be untouched.

This is the first statement of the chapter's governing asymmetry: prices of production are social-relational magnitudes, not individual-value magnitudes. A commodity's price of production is not a private fact about that commodity but a position in a system.

The Second Door: A Change in the Commodity's Own Value

Now case (2): "The general rate of profit remains unaltered." Here, Marx says, the production price of a commodity "can change only because its value has altered; because more or less labour is required for its actual reproduction." Note "actual reproduction" — Marx is careful to include not only the labour that directly produces the good in its final form but also "the labour producing those commodities that go towards producing it," i.e. the labour embodied in the means of production. This is the point on which the entire second section will turn.

The illustration is economical: the price of production of cotton yarn may fall "either because raw cotton is produced more cheaply, or because the work of spinning has become more productive as a result of better machinery." One path alters the value of an input; the other alters the value contributed by the final stage. Both are internal to value — no wages movement, no profit-rate movement, no market accident is required.

The Formula k + p = k + kp′ and the Fundamental Asymmetry

Marx then formalizes the price of production: "Price of production, as we have already shown, is k + p, cost price plus profit. But this = k + kp′, where k, the cost price, is a magnitude which varies according to the different spheres of production and is everywhere equal to the value of the constant and variable capital used up to produce the commodity, while p′ is the average rate of profit calculated as a percentage."

Unpack the notation. k is the cost price — the sum of the values (or, by the argument of Section II, at least the cost-price sums) of the constant and variable capital consumed in production. p′ is the average rate of profit expressed as a fraction or percentage of the total capital advanced (here of k, understood as the capital used up). The second equality, p = kp′, expresses profit as a percentage mark-up on cost — which is precisely how the individual capitalist, accounting on his ledger, experiences it. Marx then walks through a numerical case: "If k = 200 and p′ = 20 per cent, the price of production k + kp′ = 200 + 200 × 20/100 = 200 + 40 = 240."

The purpose of the arithmetic is to exhibit a logical possibility that will shortly prove decisive: "It is evident that this price of production may remain the same even though the value of the commodity changes." Because the price of production is a function of k and p′ — not of the commodity's value per se — a change in value that leaves k (the value of the capital consumed expressed as cost) and the general rate of profit unchanged will leave the price untouched. The chain value → price of production is therefore a conditional one: value change is necessary but not sufficient for price change.

Marx draws the full asymmetry with unmistakable clarity: "All changes in the price of production of a commodity can be ultimately reduced to a change in value, but not all changes in the value of a commodity need find expression in a change in the price of production, since this is not determined simply by the value of the particular commodity in question, but rather by the total value of all commodities. A change in commodity A, therefore, may be balanced by an opposite change in commodity B, so that the general proportion remains the same." This is the chapter's first substantive thesis, and it is a totality thesis: prices of production are the gravitational centres of a system in which value-change in A can be systematically cancelled by value-change in B before it ever reaches the price of A. The individual commodity is not a closed monad; it is a node in a network whose equilibrium properties are determined at the level of the whole.

The Two Reasons Price of Production Diverges From Value (Section II)

Having disposed of the dynamics of price of production, Marx turns to a residual worry about its static definition. Section II opens by recalling the two reasons "the divergence of price of production from value" arises. The first is the classical one: "because the average profit is added to the cost price of a commodity, rather than the surplus-value contained in it." Where a capital's composition is above the social average, its own surplus-value is less than average profit; the price of production must exceed value. Where composition is below the social average, the reverse. This is the familiar sectoral redistribution.

But the second reason is more insidious and it is the real subject of the section: "because the price of production of a commodity that diverges in this way from its value enters as an element into the cost price of other commodities." The inputs that a producer buys are themselves bought at production prices, not values. There is, therefore, "a divergence from the value of the means of production consumed … already contained in the cost price, quite apart from the divergence that may arise for the commodity itself from the difference between average profit and surplus-value." The divergence is second-order and self-referential — a divergence inside the cost price, generated upstream in the production chain before the commodity in question has even been priced.

This is the earliest explicit statement in Marx of what later economists would call the input-price transformation problem — the fact that if outputs are priced at production prices, then inputs (which are yesterday's outputs) are too, and the entire system is one of mutually determined prices, not a one-way mapping from values to prices.

Inputs Priced at Prices of Production: The Circularity Objection

Marx now asks the sharp question: "It is quite possible, accordingly, for the cost price to diverge from the value sum of the elements of which this component of the price of production is composed, even in the case of commodities that are produced by capitals of average composition." This is the danger. The special privilege of average-composition capitals — price of production = value — was supposed to be the anchor of the whole doctrine, the point at which value and price visibly coincide. If even in those capitals the cost price k is not a value sum, has the anchor been cut loose?

Marx works the case concretely. "Let us assume that the average composition is 80c + 20v." Here c and v are the constant and variable capital components of the capital advanced. Marx's point is that, for actual capitals that happen to be composed in this way, the "80c may be greater or less than the value of c, the constant capital, since this c is composed of commodities whose prices of production are different from their values." The 80 units of constant capital are a bundle of commodities — machinery, raw materials, fuel — each of which was itself sold at a production price, not a value. Hence the 80 is 80 in price terms, not 80 in value terms.

The same applies to v: "The 20v can similarly diverge from its value, if the spending of wages on consumption involves commodities whose prices of production are different from their values." Wage goods, too, are priced at production prices. And this has a real, not merely accounting, consequence: "The workers must work for a greater or lesser amount of time in order to buy back these commodities (to replace them) and must therefore perform more or less necessary labour than would be needed if the prices of production of their necessary means of subsistence did coincide with their values." Here Marx is being rigorous: a divergence in the prices of wage goods translates directly into a divergence in the quantity of necessary labour, which is the substance of the value of labour-power. The circularity cuts deep.

The Ratio, Not the Magnitude: Why Average Composition Still Holds

The resolution is the intellectual heart of the chapter. "Yet this possibility in no way affects the correctness of the principles put forward for commodities of average composition." Why not?

The argument turns on what determines surplus-value. For the capital composed 80c + 20v, Marx says: "the important thing as far as the determination of surplus-value is concerned is not whether these figures are the expression of actual values, but rather what their mutual relationship is; i.e. that v is one-fifth of the total capital and c is four-fifths. As soon as this is the case, as assumed above, the surplus-value v produces is equal to the average profit."

Consider what this says. Surplus-value is produced by the labour-power purchased by v. If v is one-fifth of the total capital in whatever units we are working — value units, price units, or cost-price monetary units — then the surplus-value produced relative to total capital advanced matches the social average rate, because the social average rate was itself defined as the total surplus-value divided by the total social capital, and the composition of this particular capital equals the composition of the average. The ratio of v to (c + v), not its absolute numerical magnitude, is the operative determinant. What matters is that the capital stands at the average axis of composition — not that its figures are literal value quantities. Marx is implicitly saying: the magnitudes in the two-aggregate equalities (total value = total price of production; total surplus-value = total profit) are consistent under any choice of numéraire, and it is the relations that carry the theoretical content.

Two identities follow immediately. First, because the composition is average, the surplus-value contained in the commodity equals the average profit. Second, "because it is equal to the average profit, the price of production = cost price + profit = k + p = k + s, which is equal in practice to the commodity's value." Note the "=" between s and p: for an average-composition capital, surplus-value and profit coincide because there is no redistributive gap to bridge. The price of production therefore coincides with value "in practice." The phrase is important: it is not an exact mathematical identity in a world of mutual price determination; it is a practical identity, an identity of result, which suffices for the theoretical claim.

The Consistency Test: Why Wage Changes Cannot Move Prices in Average Spheres

Marx now performs a decisive consistency test, which doubles as an indirect proof of the value principle. "In other words, an increase or decrease in wages in this case leaves k + p unaffected, just as it would leave the commodity's value unaffected, and simply brings about a corresponding converse movement, a decrease or increase, on the side of the profit rate."

This is the crucial connection with the appearance catalogued later in Section III, where "rises and falls in the prices of production as a result of changes in the wage level" seem to contradict the value relationship. Marx is here establishing that, for the benchmark case, the appearance is consistent with the essence: a wage change moves p′, not k + p, precisely because k + p = k + s and s is produced by v's labour — a wage increase reduces s one-for-one, hence reduces profit one-for-one, with no effect on price. This exactly mirrors the behaviour of value under a wage change: value depends on labour-time, not on its division into paid and unpaid parts. Prices of production in average spheres behave from the wage side as values would.

The proof proceeds by reductio ad absurdum from the equilibrium condition: "If an increase or decrease in wages did affect the price of commodities in this case, the profit rate in these spheres of average composition would come to stand below or above its level in the other spheres." Average spheres would be knocked off the general average — which is a contradiction, since they are average. "It is only in so far as their prices remain unaltered that the spheres of average composition maintain the same level of profit as the others. The same thing thus takes place in practice as if the products of these spheres were sold at their actual values."

The phrase "as if" (in practice) is not a concession of weakness. It is a statement of Marx's method: what the theory predicts for average-composition capitals (price = value in practical effect) is exactly what the equilibrating mechanism of competition must produce, on pain of inconsistency. Section II closes by reinforcing the general principle with respect to wages and values: "if commodities are sold at their actual values, it is clear that with other circumstances remaining the same, a rise or fall in wages provokes a corresponding fall or rise in profit but no change in the commodity's value, and … in no circumstances can a rise or fall in wages ever affect the value of commodities, but only the size of the surplus-value." This is the classical Ricardian result, here re-established within Marx's own price-of-production framework.

The Real Movement of Capital and the Levelling of Profit (Section III)

Section III pivots from the analytic to the descriptive-epistemological. Marx restates the mechanism he has already established: "competition equalizes profit rates between the different spheres of production to produce an average rate of profit, and … this is precisely the way in which the values of products from these various spheres are transformed into prices of production." The mechanism is "the continual transfer of capital from one sphere to another, where profit stands above the average for the time being."

Three refinements immediately follow. First, the temporal dimension: the "cycle of fat and lean years that follow one another in a given branch of industry over a particular period of time, and the fluctuations in profit that these involve" must be included in the account. The uninterrupted emigration and immigration of capitals between spheres "produces rising and falling movements in the profit rate which more or less balance one another out and thus tend to reduce the profit rate everywhere to the same common and general level." Equalization is not instantaneous; it operates through a multi-year averaging over the boom-bust cycle.

Second, the causal trigger: "This movement of capitals is always brought about in the first place by the state of market prices, which raise profits above the general average level in one place, and reduce it below the average in another." Marx explicitly sets commercial capital aside "for the time being" — a limitation he flags — but observes that speculation in favoured articles can "withdraw masses of capital from one line of business with extraordinary rapidity and fling them just as suddenly into another."

Third, the frictions: "In every sphere of actual production, however, industry, agriculture, mining, etc., the transfer of capital from one sector to another presents significant difficulties, particularly on account of the fixed capital involved." The mobility that equalization assumes is constrained by the immobility of installed plant. Yet the empirical result holds over the cycle: "Experience shows … that if one branch of industry, e.g. cotton, yields extraordinarily high profits at one time, it may bring in very low profits at another, or even run a loss, so that in a particular cycle of years the average profit is more or less the same as in other branches. Capital soon learns to reckon with this experience."

That last clause — "capital soon learns to reckon with this experience" — is the hinge into the epistemological section. The levelling is not merely an objective process imposed on passive capitals; it is a process that capitals learn to anticipate.

What Competition Does Not Show: The Three Inverted Appearances

Now Marx isolates precisely what competition fails to reveal. "What competition does not show, however, is the determination of values that governs the movement of production; that it is values that stand behind the prices of production and ultimately determine them." Competition displays effects whose causes remain invisible. He enumerates three surface phenomena, each of which seems to contradict the value principle:

  1. "average profits that are independent of the organic composition of capital in the various spheres of production, i.e. independent of the mass of living labour appropriated in a given sphere of exploitation." The observable world looks as if profit had nothing to do with how much living labour a capital employs — a fact that seems to refute the labour theory of surplus-value directly. (Underneath, of course, is the equalization mechanism: profits are proportional to capital, not to labour, at the surface — because surplus-value has been redistributed.)

  2. "rises and falls in the prices of production as a result of changes in the wage level — a phenomenon which at first sight seems completely to contradict the value relationship of commodities." Marx has just shown in Section II that this is precisely what value should produce in average spheres (wage rise → profit fall, no price change there); but in non-average spheres the visible movement of prices will look like pure wage-determined pricing.

  3. "fluctuations in market prices that reduce the average market price of a commodity over a given period of time, not to its market value but rather to a market price of production that diverges from this market value and is something very different."

Marx's conclusion is famously direct: "All these phenomena seem to contradict both the determination of value by labour-time and the nature of surplus-value as consisting of unpaid surplus labour. In competition, therefore, everything appears upside down."

The Finished Configuration vs. the Concealed Inner Core

This gives rise to the chapter's most quoted theoretical articulation of Marx's epistemology of the capitalist mode of production:

"The finished configuration of economic relations, as these are visible on the surface, in their actual existence, and therefore also in the notions with which the bearers and agents of these relations seek to gain an understanding of them, is very different from the configuration of their inner core, which is essential but concealed, and the concept corresponding to it. It is in fact the very reverse and antithesis of this."

Unpack the vocabulary. "Finished configuration" (fertige Gestalt) is the shape economic relations assume when fully developed and observed from outside — the world of competitive prices, of uniform profit rates, of wage-cost pricing. "Their actual existence" grounds the appearance in reality, not illusion: the surface is not a fantasy. "The notions with which the bearers and agents of these relations seek to gain an understanding" — that is, the spontaneous consciousness of capitalists, workers, bankers, landlords, as it crystallises around the visible forms. "Inner core" (innerer Zusammenhang): the connective structure of value, surplus-value, and their transformation. It is "essential but concealed" (wesentlich aber verhüllt). And crucially, the relation is not merely one of hiddenness but of inversion: the surface is "the very reverse and antithesis" of the core. This is the theoretical warrant for the whole critique of political economy: if the surface were merely incomplete, common sense and ordinary observation would suffice; because it is systematically inverted, a critical science is required to invert the inversion.

The Developed Consciousness and the Grounds for Compensation

Marx now arrives at what gives Section III its title. Once capitalist production is developed, the equalisation of profit rates no longer operates only through the blind attraction-and-repulsion of capital via market prices. "Once average prices and the market prices corresponding to them have been established for a certain length of time, the various individual capitalists become conscious that certain differences are balanced out in this equalisation, and so they take these into account in their calculations among themselves." The mechanism becomes internalised. "These differences are actively present in the capitalists' view of things and are taken into account by them as grounds for compensation."

"Grounds for compensation" (Kompensationsgründe) is the operative concept. It designates any circumstance which would otherwise make one investment abnormally unprofitable or profitable relative to others and which is neutralised, in the capitalist's own pricing calculus, by a compensating price adjustment. The mechanism of equalisation has migrated from the market's invisible hand into the business calculation itself.

"The basic notion in this connection is that of average profit itself, the idea that capitals of equal size must yield equal profits in the same period of time." From this maxim, Marx derives the capitalist ideological form: "This is based in turn on the idea that capital in each sphere of production has to participate according to its size in the total surplus-value extorted from the workers by the total social capital; or that each particular capital should be viewed simply as a fragment of the total capital and each capitalist in fact as a shareholder in the whole social enterprise, partaking in the overall profit in proportion to the size of his share of capital."

This is a striking formulation. The capitalist does not, in his own consciousness, see himself as a shareholder in a social enterprise: but his practice — his calculation of average profit, his expectation of equal returns on equal capitals regardless of the labour employed — presupposes exactly such a shareholding arrangement. Marx is claiming that the operative logic of capitalist competition, whether or not the individual recognises it, is the logic of equitable distribution of a common pool — the "common booty" of total surplus-value, whose title is proportionality to capital advanced.

Average Profit as Shareholding in the Social Enterprise

Marx now illustrates the shareholding logic with concrete business calculations. First, turnover time: a capital "that turns over more slowly, either because the commodity in question remains in the production process for a longer period or because it has to be sold on distant markets, still charges the profit it would otherwise lose by raising its price and compensates itself in this way." A slow-turning capital would, at the social average rate p′, earn less per unit time than a fast-turning one; the market, through the producer's own pricing, restores proportionality by raising k + p. Second, risk: "capital investments that are exposed to greater risk, as in shipping, for instance, receive compensation through increased prices."

Marx then makes a pointed argument against the risk theory of profit — anticipating, by three-quarters of a century, Frank Knight's and others' attempts to derive profit from uncertainty. "Once capitalist production is properly developed, and with it the insurance system, the risk is in fact the same for all spheres of production (see Corbet); those more endangered simply pay higher insurance premiums and receive these back in the price of their commodities." The footnote cites An Inquiry into the Causes and Modes of the Wealth of Individuals (London, 1841), pp. 100–102 — an obscure work from which Marx takes the proposition that insurance premiums equalise the cost of risk across all spheres. The theoretical point is decisive: risk-bearing is a cost that enters k, not a source of profit above the average; and once insurance exists, differential risk is converted into a differential premium, recovered through price, with no surplus-value created. Risk can justify a price adder, but it cannot generate profit in the Marxian sense — profit remains a transformed form of surplus-value.

Marx articulates the general form: "any circumstance that makes one capital investment less profitable and another one more so (and all these investments are taken as equally necessary, within certain limits) is invariably taken into account as a valid reason for compensation, without there being any need for the constant repetition of the activities of competition in order to demonstrate the justification for including such motives or factors in the capitalist's calculations."

The Fetishism of Compensation: Profit as an Addition to Cost Price

The paragraph that follows is the section's culmination, and it merits the closest reading.

The capitalist "simply forgets (or rather he no longer sees it, since competition does not show it to him) that all these grounds for compensation that make themselves mutually felt in the reciprocal calculation of commodity prices by the capitalists in different branches of production are simply related to the fact that they all have an equal claim on the common booty, the total surplus-value, in proportion to their capital."

Here is the whole dialectic compressed. The grounds for compensation — slow turnover, distance, risk, cyclical fluctuation — are the form in which the equal claim on total surplus-value is asserted and enforced. They are distributive mechanisms. But the capitalist sees them differently: "It appears to them, rather, that the profit which they pocket is something different from the surplus-value they extort; that the grounds for compensation do not simply equalise their participation in the total surplus-value, but that they actually create profit itself, since profit seems to derive simply from the addition to the cost price made with one justification or another."

The inversion is complete. What in truth is a redistribution of already-extorted surplus-value appears on the surface as the creation of fresh profit out of nothing more than a justified mark-up on cost. This is the precise form of profit-fetishism in the sphere of average profit. It is structurally identical to, though distinct from, the surplus-value fetishism analysed in Volume 1 Chapter 1, Section 4: the attribution to things (capital, risk, time, distance) of a social power that properly belongs to a relation between persons (the appropriation of unpaid labour).

Continuity With Chapter 7: The Capitalist's Conception of Surplus-Value

Marx closes by invoking his earlier analysis. "Finally, what was said in Chapter 7, p. 236, about the capitalist's ideas as to the source of surplus-value applies also to the average profit." Chapter 7 of Volume 3 discussed the capitalist's spontaneous conception of surplus-value — the way in which he attributes profit to the productive power of his capital, to the sale of the commodity above its cost, and so on. Marx's point here is systematic analogy: the fetishistic inversion identified at the level of the individual capital (surplus-value appears to come from the capital itself) reappears at the level of the average profit, where it appears to come from the various grounds for compensation.

But Marx adds one differentiation: "The only way in which the situation looks different in this second case is that for a given market price and a given level of exploitation of labour, savings on the cost price depend on individual talent, attention, etc." That is, at the level of the individual firm operating at an established market price and social rate of exploitation, whatever surplus the capitalist pockets over the average can depend on the personal efficiency of his management. This empirical iridescence of individual talent reinforces the ideological misrecognition: it supplies an apparent, tangible proof that profit is earned by personal contribution, and thereby obscures the fact that the total profit available is a magnitude fixed by the extraction of surplus-value from the working class, whatever particular capitalist manages to appropriate what share of it.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter yields a compact set of fundamental principles.

The asymmetry principle. Value change is a necessary but not sufficient condition for a change in the production price of any given commodity; price-of-production change is, however, a sufficient indicator that some value has changed somewhere. This is not a technical footnote but a philosophical position about the direction of explanation: prices are surface traces of underlying value movements, and the theory runs from value to price, not the reverse, even though the observation runs from price to value.

The totality principle. A commodity's price of production is determined "not simply by the value of the particular commodity in question, but rather by the total value of all commodities." Value in Marx is therefore a systemic category — it is defined across the whole social product — and this is why individual value changes may be invisible at the level of individual prices. The economy is a network whose equilibrium is holistic.

The ratio-not-magnitude principle. For capitals at the average composition, the identity price-of-production = value survives the mutual dependency of prices, because the operative determinant is the proportional structure of the capital (v as one-fifth of the total) rather than the absolute numerical value of k. This dissolves the input-price circularity objection in the special case that anchors the whole theory, and it does so by shifting the theoretical content from numerical magnitudes to relational structure. This is a template for all subsequent stages of Marx's analysis of revenue forms.

The compensation principle. The grounds for compensation — risk, turnover-time, market distance, cyclical fluctuation — do not create profit; they redistribute the total social surplus-value in proportion to capital advanced. They are the concrete mechanisms through which the "shareholding in the whole social enterprise" is enforced.

The inversion principle. The finished configuration of economic relations, visible to the actors themselves, is not merely an incomplete picture of the inner core — it is its "reverse and antithesis." Because the phenomenal form is systematically inverted, ordinary perception and the spontaneous consciousness of economic agents are systematically misleading. The critique of political economy is therefore not optional refinement but a necessary corrective — the only mode of access to the concealed inner core.

The broader theoretical implications radiate in several directions. First, the chapter forecloses the risk-of-capital theory of profit in advance: risk-bearing is a cost recovered through price, whose differential incidence is equalised by insurance. Any theory that treats entrepreneurial risk as a source of surplus fails to distinguish between distribution and production. Second, the "shareholder in the whole social enterprise" formulation directly prepares Parts IV–VII of Volume 3: commercial profit, interest, and ground rent will all be shown to be claim-tickets on the same total surplus-value, held by different classes of capital-owners (merchants, financiers, landlords), each with its own apparent "justification." The trinity formula — Capital–Profit, Land–Rent, Labour–Wages — is the fully developed form of the same inversion the current chapter isolates at the initial stage of compensation. Third, Marx here anticipates the substantive problem subsequently formalised by Bortkiewicz, Sraffa, and the "transformation problem" literature: he acknowledges the second-order divergence of cost prices from value sums, and he resolves it — for the benchmark case — by the ratio argument, of which he says "in practice" it is equivalent to selling at values. Whether one takes this as a genuine solution or as a strategic sidestep, the chapter establishes that Marx himself was aware of the mutual dependence of prices and thought it did not undermine the priority of value.

Practically, the chapter remains a sharp tool for dismantling contemporary ideological forms. Whenever the "cost of capital" is presented as a justification for the price of a product; whenever "risk premiums," "turnover costs," or "industry-specific returns" are invoked as if they generated value; whenever the accountant's mark-up on cost is treated as the reality of profit — Marx's argument commands: these are distributive titles to social surplus-value, not its source. The chapter's closing observation that profit appears to depend on "individual talent, attention, etc." names the micro-ideological supplement that makes the misrecognition persuasive at the level of the firm, and thereby names the precise mechanism by which capitalist competition manufactures the appearance of earned profit.

Philosophically, the chapter is perhaps Marx's most explicit statement of the relation between science and the phenomenal forms of social life. If the surface of society were merely incomplete, ordinary observation would gradually complete it; because the surface is the systematic antithesis of the essence, science must be built by inverting the inversion. This is the epistemological core that will be radicalised by Lukács's account of reification, by Althusser's reading of Marx's "symptomatic" method, and by every subsequent critical theory that insists on the necessary illusions of bourgeois society. "In competition, therefore, everything appears upside down" — and the task of theory is not to describe how the world looks, but to reconstruct how it is, and why it must look that way.
