Volume 3 • Chapter 7: Supplementary Remarks

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 7, "Supplementary Remarks," is the closing chapter of Part One of Capital, Volume 3 — the Part devoted to "The Transformation of Surplus-Value into Profit." It is exactly what its title announces: not a new theoretical construction, but a set of three tightly compressed addenda, separated by asterisks in the manuscript, that consolidate, qualify, and defend the results the preceding chapters have produced. Its intellectual objective is double. First, it must explain a puzzle of consciousness: why does the bourgeois systematically refuse to identify profit with surplus-value, even though the whole of Part One has demonstrated that identity? Second, it must settle a technical controversy: does a change in the magnitude of the capital advanced necessarily leave the rate of profit untouched, as Rodbertus claimed? Marx's answer is a precise, two-case exception to a general rule, followed by a closing taxonomy of the only channels through which a profit rate can move.

The chapter's core thesis can be stated in three propositions. (i) The identity of profit and surplus-value holds at the level of the sphere of production in aggregate, but it is systematically obscured at the level of the individual capital and in the process of circulation — and this obscurity is not mere ignorance but a necessary appearance generated by the ordinary functioning of the capitalist economy. (ii) The rate of profit is a ratio, and a ratio is not a magnitude; the same mass of surplus-value, extracted from the same workers under identical exploitation, can be expressed in radically different rates of profit depending on the size and the composition of the capital against which it is measured. (iii) Consequently, changes in the magnitude of capital advanced leave the profit rate unaffected in only two cases — a purely monetary change in the value of the money commodity, and a real change in capital magnitude that preserves the capital's organic composition — plus a further special case, developed at the chapter's close, in which a proportional revaluation of all components of capital scales numerator and denominator alike.

The framing assumption, explicitly carried over from the rest of Part One, is that "the mass of profit appropriated in each particular sphere of production is equal to the sum of the surplus-value produced in this sphere by the total capital applied." This is a distributional premise: no value is transferred between spheres. It is the assumption that will be suspended in the next Part of the volume, where profit rates are equalized into a general rate of profit. Chapter 7 therefore sits precisely on the hinge between the analysis of surplus-value production and the analysis of its distribution among competing capitals — while still, deliberately, on the near side of that hinge.

The chapter's intellectual stakes are therefore threefold. Analytically, it establishes the exact conditions of invariance and variance for the profit rate. Critically, it refutes Rodbertus's proportionality thesis and, through the footnote Engels appends, situates that refutation within Marx's long-running polemic against Rodbertus on rent, profit, and "state socialism." Ideologically, it offers what is in effect a micro-theory of bourgeois economic consciousness: an account of how the real dispersion of profit rates, rooted in the real technical and commercial differences among firms, necessarily produces the belief that profit springs partly from the capitalist's "own individual action" rather than from unpaid surplus labour. The chapter thus does not merely report results; it explains why those results cannot be seen from the standpoint of the individual capitalist.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The chapter unfolds in three asterisk-divided movements. The first (reasons 1 and 2) explains the bourgeois's misrecognition of profit. The second (the Rodbertus critique) isolates the two exceptional cases in which capital magnitude is neutral for the profit rate. The third (the closing law and its sub-cases) states the general theorem governing any increase in the profit rate, then traces the last remaining channel of autonomous fluctuation — the revaluation of capital through changes in the labour-time socially necessary for its reproduction.

2.1 The Opening Assumption: What Part One Has Already Settled

Marx opens with an explicit continuity clause: "We continue to assume, as throughout this Part, that the mass of profit appropriated in each particular sphere of production is equal to the sum of the surplus-value produced in this sphere by the total capital applied." Every term here is load-bearing.

A sphere of production is a branch of industry — a set of capitals producing a particular class of commodity under broadly comparable technical conditions. The assumption is stated at the level of the sphere, not the firm, and it uses two different verbs: surplus-value is produced by the living labour set in motion; profit is appropriated by the capital that owns the product. The equality says that within a sphere, the profit actually pocketed by the owners of capital equals the surplus-value actually generated by the workers of that sphere — nothing leaks in from outside, nothing is drained away to another branch. This is precisely the abstraction that a theory of the general rate of profit will later be forced to abandon. Here, however, the abstraction is retained so that the profit rate of a sphere can be read off directly from its own rate of surplus-value and its own value composition.

Two consequences follow immediately and should be registered before the argument proper begins. First, the denominator of the profit rate is the total capital applied — constant capital plus variable capital, the whole advance, not merely the wages bill. Second, because the equality is sphere-level, it leaves untouched the possibility that within a sphere, individual firms may earn very different rates of profit. And it is exactly that possibility — the dispersion of profit rates among individual capitals under conditions of identical exploitation — that the chapter now proceeds to exploit.

2.2 First Source of Misrecognition: Circulation Forgets Production

The first of the two reasons Marx gives for the bourgeois's refusal to identify profit with surplus-value is a statement about consciousness as a function of the circuit of capital: "In the process of circulation, he forgets the production process. The realization of commodity value — including the realization of surplus-value — he takes as the making of this surplus-value."

The terms must be kept strictly apart. Production of surplus-value is the extraction of unpaid surplus labour in the labour process: workers work longer than the labour-time required to reproduce their own labour-power, and the excess is embodied in the product. Realization, by contrast, is the sale of the commodities at their value — the conversion of already-produced value and surplus-value into money. Realization presupposes production; there is nothing to realize that has not already been produced. Yet from the vantage point of the individual capitalist's practical experience, the sequence runs the other way. His money goes out as capital, comes back as more money, and the increment appears at the moment of sale, in the market, at the hand of the buyer. The circuit M–C–M′ presents the surplus as a surplus of money over money advanced — and money-over-money is a phenomenon of exchange, not of the workshop.

This is not, in Marx's account, a personal failure of memory. The word "forgets" names a structural inversion: the form in which the process appears to its agent systematically occludes the process that generates it. Engels's editorial note — "(A blank in the manuscript here indicates that Marx intended to develop this point in more detail. — F.E.)" — is a valuable trace of the chapter's provisional character: Marx recognized that the conflation of realization with creation deserved fuller treatment, and left the space unfilled. The reader should therefore treat this first reason as a signpost rather than a completed argument: it announces that the circulation form of capital is one independent source of the profit/surplus-value illusion, distinct from and prior to the second reason that follows.

Note also the difference in logical status between the two reasons. Reason (1) is universal — it applies to every capitalist in every branch, because every capitalist moves through the circuit of capital. Reason (2) is differential — it explains how the observable variety of profit rates among firms feeds the same illusion. The two reasons are complementary: one explains why the illusion is unavoidable in general, the other explains why the observable facts seem to confirm it in particular.

2.3 Second Source: The Real Dispersion of Profit Rates Among Individual Capitals

The second reason is introduced by a crucial act of bracketing. Marx specifies that "rates of profit can be very different" even assuming the same degree of exploitation of labour, and "ignoring all modifications introduced by the credit system," and ignoring "all mutual swindling and cheating among the capitalists themselves," and ignoring "all favourable selections of the market."

Each exclusion removes a candidate explanation that a defender of the harmonious view might invoke. Credit lets firms operate on borrowed funds, altering the return on the capitalist's own money as distinct from the return on the total capital employed; Marx sets this aside. Mutual swindling and cheating — fraud, sharp dealing, transfers of value from one capitalist to another — can enrich one firm at another's expense, but redistributes existing surplus-value rather than creating it; it is set aside. Favourable selections of the market — luck in timing, location, or customer base — is likewise set aside. The methodological point is sharp: even after removing redistribution, fraud, and luck, dispersion of profit rates remains. It must therefore have structural roots.

Marx then enumerates those roots: raw materials "purchased cheaply or less cheaply, with more or less specialist knowledge"; machinery that is "productive, suitable and cheap"; the "overall arrangement of the production process in its various stages" being "more or less satisfactory"; "wastage of material avoided"; and "management and supervision simple and effective." Two distinct families of cause are at work here, and they should be separated. Some listed factors operate on the value of the inputs — buying raw materials or machinery cheaply lowers the value of the constant capital advanced per unit of output, without any change in the physical production process. Others operate on the technical and organizational efficiency of production — avoiding wastage, arranging the stages of production well, supervising effectively, choosing appropriate machinery — and thereby change the quantity of constant capital effectively consumed per unit of output. Both families show up in the accounts in the same place: as a difference in the magnitude of constant capital set against a given wage bill.

The decisive formulation follows: "given the surplus-value that accrues to a certain variable capital, it still depends very much on the business acumen of the individual, either the capitalist himself or his managers and salespeople, whether this same surplus-value is expressed in a higher or lower rate of profit." The verb is expressed, and it is doing all the work. Acumen does not create the surplus-value; it determines the measure against which that surplus-value is reckoned. The capitalist's skill operates on the denominator of the ratio, not on its numerator.

A textual caveat is warranted here. The sentence continues: "and therefore whether it delivers a greater or lesser amount of profit." Read strictly against the illustration that follows, this is loose drafting. In the example, the amounts of profit are identical in both businesses — £1,000 each — while only the rates differ. Either the phrase should be read as "a greater or lesser amount of profit per unit of capital advanced," or it is a slip in which "profit" stands in for "rate of profit," or it anticipates a comparison in which the same rate applied to capitals of different size yields different masses. The reader should hold onto the analytical point (acumen changes the measure, not the measured surplus) rather than the loose phrase.

2.4 The Numerical Illustration: Same Exploitation, Different Profit Rates

Marx now makes the point arithmetically. "The same surplus-value of £1,000, the product of £1,000 in wages, may involve £9,000 of constant capital in business A, and £11,000 in business B."

Reconstructing the two cases with the standard symbols — c for constant capital, v for variable capital, s for surplus-value, C = c + v for the total capital advanced, s′ = s/v for the rate of surplus-value, and p′ = s/C for the rate of profit:

Business A: c = £9,000; v = £1,000; s = £1,000; therefore C = £10,000; s′ = 1,000/1,000 = 100%; and p′ = 1,000/10,000 = 10 per cent.

Business B: c = £11,000; v = £1,000; s = £1,000; therefore C = £12,000; s′ = 100% again; and p′ = 1,000/12,000 = 8⅓ per cent.

(The printed text garbles these fractions, rendering 1,000/10,000 as "/0,00Q04>" and 1,000/12,000 as "112.00°0°0" = 8⅓ per cent; the arithmetic is unambiguous once restored.)

What the illustration establishes is precise. The variable capital advanced is identical (£1,000); the surplus-value extracted is identical (£1,000); therefore the rate of surplus-value is identical, and — as Marx emphasizes — "there is thus in each case an equal exploitation of the same number of workers." Yet the rates of profit differ by a sixth. The difference has a single source: the constant capital is £2,000 larger in B. "In the first case the total capital produces relatively more profit than in the second" — the word relatively is essential, since the absolute profit is the same in both. The only thing that has changed is the ratio of that profit to the capital against which it is measured.

This result generalizes through the elementary decomposition implicit in the definitions:

p′ = s/C = s/(c + v) = s′ · v/(c + v) = s′ · (1 − c/C).

Every symbol retains its meaning: the profit rate equals the rate of surplus-value multiplied by the share of variable capital in the total advance. Equivalently, the gap between the two rates is s′ − p′ = s′ · c/C — the rate of surplus-value discounted by the weight of constant capital. So long as v and s are held fixed while c grows, p′ must fall, and it must fall in exact proportion to the growing weight of c in C. This is the algebraic skeleton of the chapter's entire subsequent argument, and the reader should keep it in view: everything Marx says about Rodbertus, about the two exceptions, and about the general theorem is an exploration of the conditions under which this formula does or does not change.

2.5 The Ideological Payoff: The Illusion of Individual Action

Having established the arithmetic, Marx draws the ideological consequence: "And this circumstance misleads the capitalist by convincing him that his profit is due not to the exploitation of labour, but at least in part also to other circumstances independent of this, and in particular his own individual action."

Three features of this sentence deserve emphasis. First, the illusion is partial — the capitalist is convinced that profit is only "at least in part" attributable to something other than exploitation. He has not abandoned the labour theory of value wholesale; he has appended an additional, independent source. This is the characteristic hybrid form of practical business ideology: profit is partly the reward of enterprise and skill, partly the return on the capital employed.

Second, the appearance that deceives him is real at the level at which he experiences it. From the standpoint of the individual capital, acumen genuinely does determine the rate of profit obtained from a given surplus-value. The illusion lies not in the observation but in the inference: from "my profit rate depends upon me" the capitalist concludes "my profit originates in me." Marx's analysis separates causation (the origin of surplus-value in unpaid labour) from expression (the rate against which it is measured) from appropriation (who pockets it). The error consists in reading the third and second back into the first.

Third, the illusion has a material basis, not merely a psychological one. It is generated by "this circumstance" — the actual, observable, structural dispersion of profit rates among firms operating under identical exploitation. Because the dispersion is real, the inference from it is not arbitrary; and because the inference is not arbitrary, the illusion cannot be dispelled by exhortation or by better information. It can only be dissolved by an analysis that shows how the rate of profit is constituted — which is precisely the analysis Part One has supplied and this chapter is consolidating.

2.6 The Rodbertus Problem: Does Capital Magnitude Move the Profit Rate?

A second asterisk marks a shift of terrain. "The arguments developed in this first Part show the errors of that view (Rodbertus) according to which (in distinction from ground-rent, where the land area can remain the same, for example, while the rent rises), even a large variation in the capital can remain without effect on the proportion between capital and profit, i.e. on the profit rate, because if the mass of profit grows, so does the mass of the capital on which it must be calculated, and vice versa."

The argument Marx attributes to Rodbertus is a proportionality fallacy, and it is instructive to see exactly how it is built. The analogy with ground-rent is its point of departure. In the case of land, the denominator — the acreage — is physically fixed. If rent rises while acreage stays constant, the ratio rent/land rises with the numerator. Rodbertus contrasts capital with land: capital has no fixed denominator, because any increase in the mass of profit is accompanied by an increase in the mass of capital on which it is computed. Numerator and denominator rise and fall together, and therefore — so the argument runs — the ratio between them can remain stable no matter how large the variation in the capital. The implicit model is a capital that scales up or down proportionally, like a photograph enlarged or reduced.

Marx's reply is a concession followed by a restriction: "This is true in only two cases." The concession is genuine — there are indeed circumstances in which capital magnitude changes without disturbing the profit rate. But Rodbertus has mistaken a pair of special cases for a universal law, and in doing so has made the profit rate a mere reflection of scale rather than a structure with laws of its own.

The footnote Engels attaches identifies the antagonist and the terrain of the dispute: "Johann Karl Rodbertus-Jagetzow (1805–75) was a Prussian landowner, and in his writings a 'state socialist', i.e. in fact a representative of agrarian capitalism who supported Bismarck's active intervention in economic management." The note cross-references Volume 1 of Capital (p. 669), Volume 2 (pp. 88–102), and Theories of Surplus-Value, Part II, Chapter VIII (pp. 15–114) and Chapter IX (pp. 127–61). What this apparatus makes clear is that the question is not pedantic. Rodbertus was a landowner-theorist whose analysis of rent, profit, and the state had a political program attached to it; Marx's refutation is part of a sustained and long-running critique, not an isolated remark. And the substantive stakes are exactly those articulated above: whether the profit rate is an autonomous ratio governed by value relations and composition, or a quantity that simply tracks the size of capital.

2.7 Exception One: A Change in the Value of the Money Commodity

The first case in which capital magnitude changes without moving the profit rate is a change in the value of money. "Firstly, if, other things being equal, and in particular the rate of surplus-value, there is a change in the value of the money commodity. (This is so even with a purely nominal change in value, the rise and fall of tokens of value, as long as other factors remain the same.)"

The illustration runs as follows. "Let the total capital be £100 and the profit £20, so that the rate of profit is 20 per cent." Then suppose the price of gold is halved. The same physical capital, which previously commanded £100, now commands £200 — because each unit of money is worth half as much, and twice as many units are needed to express the same value. Correspondingly, the profit "has a value of £40 instead of £20." Now suppose instead that the price of gold doubles: the same capital "falls to a value of £50, and the profit is now expressed in a product valued at £10." In both cases, Marx writes, "200:40 = 50:10 = 100:20 = 20 per cent."

Note first the notational point: in this passage Marx writes the profit rate as a ratio of capital to profit — C:π — rather than as s/C. The two are equivalent as a ratio expression, and the equality across the three cases is what matters. Note second the underlying logic. A £ in this passage is not an abstract unit of account hovering above the economy; it names a definite quantity of the money commodity. When the value of gold falls by half, the same real capital — the same machines, the same stock of materials, the same wage bill in real terms — is now denominated in twice as many pounds. The profit, being a claim on the same surplus product, is likewise denominated in twice as many pounds. Numerator and denominator are each multiplied by the same scalar; the ratio is untouched. "There would be no real change in the capital value in any case such as this, but simply a change in the monetary expression of the same value and surplus-value. The rate of profit ... could not be affected."

Marx's parenthesis extends the point beyond metallic money to "tokens of value" — inconvertible paper currency or coin whose nominal value rises and falls independently of its own labour-content. The requirement is only that "other factors remain the same," i.e., that the change in money is not simultaneously a change in real capital values or in the rate of surplus-value. The essential conceptual move is the separation of value from its monetary expression: a change of the latter alone is invisible to the profit rate. This exception thus has a somewhat different status from the second: it is not a case in which capital really changes magnitude at all, but only a case in which its measurement changes.

2.8 Exception Two: Real Change in Magnitude with Constant Composition

"The other case is when there is a real change in capital value, but this change is not accompanied by a change in the ratio v:c, i.e. when the rate of surplus-value is constant and the ratio of the capital invested in labour-power (the variable capital, taken as an index of the labour-power set in motion) to the capital invested in means of production remains the same."

Here the change is not merely nominal — the capital really is larger or smaller — but the proportion between its components is preserved. Marx sets out the arithmetic: "if we take C or nC or C/n, e.g. 1,000 or 2,000 or 500, the total profit will be in the first case 200, in the second case 400 and in the third case 100, but 200/1000 = 400/2000 = 100/500 = 20 per cent." (The printed page renders these fractions mutilated — "1788-o = 24:88-o = ~gg" — but the intended equalities are transparent.) Scaling the total capital by a factor n scales the profit by the same factor n; the ratio is invariant.

Three analytical points should be extracted. First, the condition is stated in terms of the ratio v:c, and the parenthetical gloss is exact: variable capital is here "taken as an index of the labour-power set in motion." The variable capital is not valuable for its own sake in this argument; it is a proxy for the quantity of living labour employed. Constant composition means: for every unit of labour-power set in motion, the same amount of means of production is required. Second, the invariance result follows directly from the formula developed earlier: p′ = s′ · (1 − c/C). If s′ is constant and c/C is constant, then p′ is constant, whatever the absolute size of C. Third, Marx draws the interpretive conclusion: "Hence the increase or decrease in the mass of profit simply indicates an increase or decrease in the size of the capital applied." Under constant composition, the profit mass is a faithful index of scale — and nothing more. Rodbertus's proportional-scaling model is, so to speak, the model of this second case, mistakenly universalized.

The distinction between the two exceptions is then stated with care: "In the first case, therefore, there is simply an apparent change in magnitude of the capital applied; in the second case there is a real change in magnitude, but no change in the capital's organic composition, in the proportion between its variable and its constant parts." Note that the chapter has now provided its own gloss on the term organic composition: it is "the proportion between its variable and its constant parts," i.e., the value proportion v:c. This gloss will matter when the closing paragraph speaks of a change in organic composition brought about by revaluation. Note also the asymmetry: the first exception involves no real change at all; the second involves a real change that happens to be composition-neutral. Rodbertus needs both, and both are special.

2.9 The Residual Cases: Why Magnitude Change Normally Moves the Profit Rate

Having isolated the two exceptions, Marx characterizes what happens in every other case: "a change in the magnitude of capital applied is either the result of a change in the value of one of its components, and thus a change in their relative magnitude (as long as the surplus-value does not itself change with the variable capital); or else this change in magnitude is the cause of a change in the relative magnitude of its two organic components (as with large-scale operations, the introduction of new machinery, etc.)."

Two distinct mechanisms are identified. In the first, the change in the magnitude of the capital is an effect: one of its components changes in value, and the aggregate therefore changes — but so do the proportions among the components. A rise in the value of raw materials, for example, enlarges c, enlarges C, and raises c/v, all at once. The change in magnitude is never isolated from a change in composition. In the second, the change in magnitude is a cause: expanding the scale of operations, introducing new machinery, and similar measures change the relative magnitude of the two organic components. Larger-scale production is more capital-intensive per worker; new machinery raises c relative to v. Here again, the magnitude change and the composition change come as a package.

The parenthetical caveat in the first case — "as long as the surplus-value does not itself change with the variable capital" — registers a genuine complication rather than resolving it. If the value of labour-power falls, then, with a given working day, the surplus-value does change, since less of the day is needed to reproduce the worker. Marx brackets this knock-on effect in order to isolate the pure composition mechanism, but the reader should note that the bracketed case is not exotic; it is the ordinary mechanism by which a cheapening of wage goods raises both s′ and p′. The caveat marks the boundary of the present argument, not a claim that such cases are rare.

The conclusion is unambiguous: "In all these cases, therefore, a change in the magnitude of the capital applied must be accompanied by a simultaneous change in the rate of profit, as long as other things remain equal." The force of the "must" derives from the fact that, outside the two exceptions, capital magnitude is never a free-standing variable. Under capitalist production, a capital is always a definite structure of value relations and technical proportions; to change its size is, in general, to change that structure. Rodbertus's error was to treat magnitude as if it could vary while everything else stood still — which is possible only in the two cases Marx has enumerated, and in the further special case to be developed at the chapter's close.

2.10 The General Theorem: The Two Sources of a Rising Profit Rate

A third asterisk opens the chapter's final movement with a general law: "An increase in the rate of profit always stems from a relative or absolute increase in the surplus-value in relation to its costs of production, i.e. to the total capital advanced, or from a reduction in the difference between the rate of profit and the rate of surplus-value."

A terminological clarification is necessary before the law can be assessed. "Costs of production" is used here, as Marx immediately specifies, to mean the total capital advanced — the full denominator C = c + v — not the narrower "cost price" of the commodity, which would exclude the surplus-value but also, in the standard Volume 3 usage, treat the whole capital as consumed. The identification is made explicit in the apposition: "i.e. to the total capital advanced."

The law then identifies two branches. The first is an increase in surplus-value relative to the total capital advanced. This can be absolute — more surplus-value in the same total capital, as when the rate of exploitation rises or the working day lengthens; or relative — the surplus-value holds its ground while the total capital shrinks, as would occur if constant capital were cheapened. In either direction, the numerator of p′ = s/C rises without an offsetting rise in the denominator. The second branch is "a reduction in the difference between the rate of profit and the rate of surplus-value." Since p′ = s′ · (1 − c/C), the gap between the two rates is s′ − p′ = s′ · c/C, which is precisely the rate of surplus-value discounted by the weight of constant capital in the total advance. To reduce that gap is to reduce the weight of constant capital in the total — that is, to move the composition in the direction of labour. The two branches are therefore not independent causes but two descriptions of one underlying relation: the profit rate rises when surplus-value gains on the capital against which it is measured, whether by growing or by shrinking the measure.

This is the theorem Rodbertus's view denies. If the profit rate could vary only with capital magnitude, and if magnitude changes always moved numerator and denominator in step, then the profit rate would have no determinants of its own and could never systematically rise or fall. Marx's two branches identify exactly where the movement comes from: from the relation between surplus-value and the total advance, which is a relation between value magnitudes and value proportions, not a matter of scale.

2.11 Autonomous Fluctuations: Reproduction Cost and the Revaluation of Capital

The final paragraph of the body of the argument addresses a residual question: can the profit rate fluctuate without any change in composition and without any change in absolute magnitude? Marx's answer supplies the last channel. "Fluctuations in the rate of profit that are independent of changes in either the capital's organic components or its absolute magnitude are possible only if the value of the capital advanced, whatever might be the form — fixed or circulating — in which it exists, rises or falls as a result of an increase or decrease in the labour-time necessary for its reproduction, an increase or decrease that is independent of the capital already in existence."

The phrase "whatever might be the form — fixed or circulating" extends the mechanism to the whole capital stock, whether it is tied up in machinery and buildings or in materials and wages. The condition "independent of the capital already in existence" is decisive: the change in value does not come from anything the capital itself does; it comes from outside, from changed conditions of production in the branches that supply the capital's components.

Marx then states the general principle of value that licenses this: "The value of any commodity — and thus also of the commodities which capital consists of — is determined not by the necessary labour-time that it itself contains, but by the socially necessary labour-time required for its reproduction." This is the distinction between embodied and reproduction labour-time, and it is a foundational claim of Marx's theory, not a local observation. The value of a machine is not fixed at the moment it is produced and thereafter immutable; it is what it would cost, in socially necessary labour-time, to produce that machine now. "This reproduction may differ from the conditions of its original production by taking place under easier or more difficult circumstances."

The illustration is stark. "If the changed circumstances mean that twice as much time, or alternatively only half as much, is required for the same physical capital to be reproduced, then given an unchanged value of money, this capital, if it was previously worth £100, would now be worth £200, or alternatively £50." The physical capital is untouched: same machines, same buildings, same inventories. Only the labour-time socially necessary to reproduce them has changed — and with it, the value of the capital on the books.

The consequence for capital as a stock is significant and should be registered explicitly. Capital is continuously exposed to revaluation. A rise in productivity in the branches producing means of production devalues existing constant capital; a rise in the difficulty of extraction or production revalues it upward. The capitalist's "capital advanced" is therefore not a fixed quantity deposited once and retrieved unchanged; it is a value magnitude perpetually at the mercy of social productivity. This is the mechanism — here presented in its abstract form — that later analysis calls the moral depreciation of fixed capital. The present paragraph establishes the general principle from which that result follows: value is a social relation in the present tense, not a fossilized record of past labour.

2.12 The Closing Reconciliation: Proportional versus Differential Revaluation

The chapter ends by distinguishing two sub-cases within the revaluation channel, and in doing so returns to the point from which the Rodbertus discussion began. "If this increase or decrease in value affects all components of the capital equally, the profit is also expressed accordingly in twice or only half the monetary sum." A uniform doubling of the value of all components — means of production and means of subsistence alike — doubles the money expression of the capital advanced. But because the means of subsistence also double in value, the value of labour-power doubles, so v doubles in money terms while the real wage is unchanged; the working day and the surplus labour time are unchanged, so the surplus product's value likewise doubles in money terms. Numerator and denominator are scaled together; the organic composition, in the chapter's own gloss, is untouched; "the profit rate then remains unchanged." This case, though it involves a real change in the value of capital rather than a merely monetary one, is formally parallel to Exception One: proportionality in revaluation is neutral to the profit rate.

"But if it involves a change in the organic composition of the capital, the ratio between the variable and the constant portions of the capital, then, if other circumstances remain the same, the profit rate will rise with a relatively rising share of variable capital and fall with a relatively falling share." This is the general result of §2.4 restated in dynamic dress: the profit rate moves inversely with the weight of constant capital. A differential revaluation — one that raises the value of constant capital relative to variable capital, or vice versa — alters c/v even if the physical capital and the scale of operations are unchanged, and the profit rate moves accordingly. Note that the chapter's own definition of organic composition ("the proportion between its variable and its constant parts") is what allows this to be stated cleanly: a differential revaluation is a change in organic composition, even where the use-value basis of production is unaltered.

The final sentence closes the loop with the first exception: "If it is only the money value that rises or falls (as a result of a change in the value of money), the monetary expression of the surplus-value rises or falls in the same proportion. The profit rate then remains unchanged." Money-value changes scale the expression of surplus-value uniformly; real, proportional revaluations scale capital and profit uniformly; only differential revaluations — and composition changes more broadly — move the rate.

The chapter thus closes with a compact taxonomy. Two invariance conditions: a composition-neutral change in real capital magnitude (Exception Two), and a proportional revaluation of all components, whether of money or of capital goods (Exception One, extended). One mover: any change in the ratio of constant to variable capital, whether arising from technical change, from changes in the relative values of the components, or from differential revaluation. Rodbertus's proportional-scaling picture is contained within the taxonomy as one of its special cases — and thereby deprived of its claim to universality.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Four fundamental principles emerge from this short chapter, and each has consequences well beyond its immediate occasion.

First: the profit rate is a relation, not a magnitude. The chapter's central analytic achievement is to insist that the same surplus-value, extracted under identical exploitation from identical numbers of workers, is compatible with widely different profit rates. The formula p′ = s′ · (1 − c/C) makes the dependence explicit. Every practical consequence follows from this: absolute profit and profit rate can move in opposite directions; a firm can earn the same profit as its competitor while reporting a lower rate; the "efficiency" of a capitalist and the "exploitation" of his workers are conceptually distinct magnitudes that only appear fused in the ratio. The rate of profit is the surplus-value discounted by the weight of the capital against which it is measured — and that discount factor is a social and technical fact, not an individual virtue.

Second: the value of capital is a present-tense social relation. The chapter's invocation of socially necessary reproduction labour-time against embodied labour-time is a foundational claim, not a local technicality. Capital advanced is not a conserved reservoir of past labour; it is a value magnitude continuously exposed to revaluation by changes in social productivity elsewhere in the economy. This has direct implications for any accounting of profitability over time: rates of profit can move without any change in the physical capital stock or in the scale of operations, purely because the labour-time required to reproduce that stock has changed.

Third: ideology is generated structurally, not psychologically. The bourgeois's refusal to identify profit with surplus-value is grounded in two real features of the system — the inversion of production and realization in the circuit of capital, and the genuine dispersion of profit rates among firms. Because the dispersion is real, the inference from "my rate depends on my acumen" to "my profit originates in my acumen" is not a random error. It is a necessary appearance, one that can only be dissolved by an analysis demonstrating how the rate of profit is constituted. This is a general model for how the surface forms of a social system produce the categories through which its agents understand it — and it explains why critique must be theoretical rather than merely informational.

Fourth: methodological — the critique of the proportionality fallacy. Rodbertus's error is not a factual mistake but a modelling mistake: he treats capital magnitude as a variable that can change while all else is held constant, and thereby converts a special case into a general law. Marx's reply is the classic critical move: enumerate the exact conditions under which the claim holds, show that they are exceptions, and identify the mechanism that operates in every other case. This is the method of the chapter as a whole, and it is worth naming: the general law is established by rigorously delimiting the space of its exceptions.

The broader implications follow from these principles. Analytically, the chapter defines the limits of the sphere-level identity of profit and surplus-value and thereby marks exactly what remains to be explained once the assumption is relaxed — the equalization of profit rates across spheres into a general rate of profit. Methodologically, it demonstrates that the theory of the profit rate cannot be reduced to the theory of the scale of capital: the profit rate has its own determinants, in the composition of capital and the value relations among its components. Ideologically, it supplies the micro-foundations of a critique that remains pertinent wherever profit is explained by entrepreneurial skill, cost discipline, or managerial excellence — the chapter's point being that these are determinants of how much capital is measured against, not of how much surplus-value is produced. And philosophically, it offers one of Marx's clearest statements of the dialectic of essence and appearance: the identity of profit and surplus-value is true of the system as a whole and invisible from any vantage point within it, and the task of theory is not to deny the appearance but to reconstruct the totality in which it is grounded.
