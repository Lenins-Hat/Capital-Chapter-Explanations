Volume 3 • Chapter 17: Commercial Profit

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 17 of Capital, Volume 3, "Commercial Profit," is the opening and foundational chapter in Marx's account of "merchant's capital"—capital that operates exclusively in the sphere of circulation, buying in order to sell, and that nonetheless extracts an average profit from surplus-value created elsewhere. Its central problem is stark and at first glance paradoxical.

Marx begins by recalling a result already established in Volume 2, Chapter 5. The "pure functions of capital in the circulation sphere" —the operations the industrial capitalist must perform (a) to realize the value of his commodities and (b) to transform this value back into the commodity-elements of production, i.e., the acts of sale and purchase that effect the metamorphoses of commodity capital C'-M-C—create neither value nor surplus-value. The time these operations require sets an objective and subjective limit on the formation of value and surplus-value. If these operations produce no value when performed by the industrial capitalist himself, they cannot suddenly acquire productive power when delegated to a "special section of capitalists" or made "the exclusive function of one portion of the money capital." The metamorphosis C'-M-C is sale and purchase; if it creates no value for industrial capitalists, it cannot do so when performed by others. Likewise, the money capital that must always be on hand to keep reproduction continuous cannot acquire value-creating properties merely by being put into circulation by a specialized division of capitalists rather than by the industrial capitalist.

Yet—and this is the pivot on which the entire chapter turns—"since the circulation phase of industrial capital forms just as much a phase in the reproduction process as production does, the capital that functions independently in the circulation process must yield the average profit just as much as the capital that functions in the various branches of production." If commercial capital yielded a higher average profit than industrial capital, part of industrial capital would convert into commercial capital; if lower, the reverse would occur. No species of capital is more mobile, more able to "change its function and designation," than commercial capital. The general rate of profit therefore imposes itself on commercial capital as a hard constraint.

This generates the chapter's guiding question: Since commercial capital does not itself produce surplus-value, the surplus-value that accrues to it as average profit must be a portion of the surplus-value produced by productive capital as a whole—so how does commercial capital attract that share?

Marx's answer unfolds through three interlocking movements:

First, he demolishes the surface illusion—natural to the merchant's own standpoint—that commercial profit is a nominal addition to price, a selling of commodities above their value. He exposes this as an illusion by showing that, under the dominance of the capitalist mode of production, the merchant buys commodities below their value and sells them at their value (or real price of production). The merchant's sale price exceeds his purchase price not because it rises above total value, but because his purchase price lies below that total value.

Second, he constructs a rigorous numerical model to prove this. With total industrial capital 720c + 180v = 900, s' = 100%, the product C has a value of 1,080 and a profit rate of 20%. Introducing an independent commercial capital of 100 changes the total capital sharing in surplus-value to 1,000; the general rate of profit falls to 18%. The industrial capitalist now sells to the merchant at 720c + 180v + 162s = 1,062—below the commodity's value. The merchant adds 18% to his own 100 and sells at 1,062 + 18 = 1,080, i.e., at value. He pockets the difference precisely because industrial capital omitted that portion from its own sale price.

Third, Marx reformulates the very definition of "price of production." Hitherto, price of production = cost (k) + average profit, computed on productive capital alone (k + 20%). Henceforth, the average profit is computed on total productive and commercial capital together (k + 18%). "The real value or production price of the total commodity capital is therefore k + p + m" (where m is commercial profit). The price at which the industrial capitalist sells as such is less than the real production price of the commodity; the price at which the industrial capitalist class as a whole sells is less than value.

Two consequences are drawn: (1) The larger commercial capital is relative to industrial capital, the lower the rate of industrial profit, and vice versa. (2) The rate of profit always understates the rate of surplus-value—further exacerbated when commercial capital dilutes the average. The average rate of profit makes exploitation appear smaller than it is.

Marx then inserts a historical–logical inversion: in the order of scientific analysis, the general rate of profit appears to originate in industrial capitals and their competition, and only later is it "rectified, supplemented and modified" by commercial capital. In the order of historical development, exactly the reverse holds. Commercial capital is chronologically prior: it first fixes commodities' prices more or less according to their values, and the circulation sphere mediates the reproduction process in which a general rate of profit first forms. Commercial profit originally determines industrial profit; only once the capitalist mode of production prevails does commercial profit get reduced to an aliquot share of the total surplus-value.

The chapter then extends to the merchant's costs of circulation. He needs additional capital beyond his purchasing money capital. The merchant's money capital behaves like the industrial capitalist's fixed capital: insofar as it is not consumed, its value does not enter the commodity's value. Purely commercial circulation costs (accounting, book-keeping, correspondence, offices, paper, postage, commercial wages) proceed from "the economic form of the product as a commodity"; they realize value but create none. Yet this entire additional capital still enters the formation of the general rate of profit, thereby lowering it. Marx illustrates this with a further modification—adding 50 to the commercial capital to cover such costs reduces the average profit to 17⅓%—and argues the division between commercial and industrial capital permits a centralization and reduction of trading costs.

The most concentrated sub-problem is the merchant's variable capital, b. Marx poses the dilemma with surgical precision: if b is not counted as capital advanced, the law of the equalization of the profit rate is violated (no capitalist would advance 150 if only 100 counted); if b is counted, the very nature of commercial capital seems contradicted, since it functions not by setting the labour of others in motion (as industrial capital does) but by itself performing buying and selling and thereby transferring to itself a portion of the surplus-value industrial capital created.

His resolution is methodological: reduce commercial capital back to its origin in industrial capital. Commercial capital is nothing but the form in which a portion of industrial capital functioning in circulation has become autonomous. The b at issue must be investigated "on the spot"—first of all in the commercial office of the industrial capitalist himself. That office is initially "infinitesimally small" relative to the workshop, yet commercial operations grow with production. The outlay on commercial workers increases the capital advanced without directly increasing surplus-value: s stays constant, but C rises to C + ΔC, so s/C becomes s/(C + ΔC). Hence industrial capital behaves toward its commercial employees differently from productive wage-labourers: more productive workers increase surplus-value; more commercial workers are an effect of the value to be realized, never its cause.

Marx then develops the commercial worker's position: a wage-labourer like any other (his labour bought with variable capital; his wage determined by the value of his labour-power), yet fundamentally different, because he creates no surplus-value. His unpaid labour, however, does something critical—it "creates his ability to appropriate surplus-value." For the merchant, this is the source of profit. "Just as the unpaid labour of the worker creates surplus-value for productive capital directly, so also does the unpaid labour of the commercial employee create a share in that surplus-value for commercial capital."

The chapter closes with the concept of duplication: the functions of commodity capital and money capital are general formal determinations of industrial capital, and simultaneously become the exclusive domain of special capitals and a special class of capitalists—functions that develop into "special spheres for the valorization of capital." Only with commercial capital do circulation costs acquire autonomy. The cost of circulation is, for industrial capital, an expense (which it is); for the merchant, this same cost is the source of his profit—proportional to its size. For commercial capital, circulation outlays are a productive investment, and the commercial labour it buys is, for it, directly productive.

In sum, the chapter completes the transformation of surplus-value into profit by integrating independent circulation capital that shares in profit without generating surplus-value. It fills a gap left open when Marx first derived the general rate of profit, and it sets the stage for the later treatment of money-dealing capital, interest, and the division of surplus-value among the trinity of profit, rent, and wages.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Inherited Premise: Circulation Is Value-Neutral

Marx opens by anchoring the entire chapter to a result he claims to have established in Volume 2, Chapter 5: the "pure functions of capital in the circulation sphere create neither value nor surplus-value." The footnote explicitly points the reader there. These pure functions are the operations the industrial capitalist must undertake: first, to realize the value of his commodities; second, to transform this value back into the commodity-elements of production. In a formula, they are the operations that effect the metamorphoses of commodity capital C'-M-C—the acts of sale and purchase. Note the strong claim here: these operations produce nothing in the way of new value. And not only that: "the time these operations require sets limits to the formation of value and surplus-value"—objectively with respect to the commodities and subjectively with respect to the capitalist. Time spent selling is time not spent producing; therefore circulation time is a negative determinant of value formation, measured both in objective interruptions and in the capitalist's personal loss of productive capacity.

Marx immediately extends the claim by the most important inferential step in the chapter's opening: what holds for the metamorphosis of commodity capital as such is "naturally not changed in any way" when part of that capital takes the form of commercial, commodity-dealing capital and the operations that effect the metamorphosis come to appear as "the special business of a special section of capitalists, or as the exclusive function of one portion of the money capital." He offers two complementary arguments. First, if sale and purchase create no value for industrial capitalists, they cannot do so when performed by others—a non-value-creating function cannot become value-creating merely by changing hands. Second, if the money capital that must always exist to keep reproduction continuous creates no value when circulated by the industrial capitalist, it cannot acquire such properties simply because a specialized division of capitalists now circulates it. This is a "conservation" argument: value properties cannot be conjured by institutional reallocation of functions.

Commercial Capital Defined by Its "True Function"

Marx then performs an act of methodological stripping. Commercial capital, "stripped of all the heterogeneous functions that may be linked to it, such as storage, dispatch, transport, distribution and retailing," and "confined to its true function of buying in order to sell, creates neither value nor surplus-value, but simply facilitates their realization." This is a critical boundary-drawing move. Marx isolates the pure commercial core (buying to sell) so as to analyze it in isolation. The heterogenous functions—storage, dispatch, transport, distribution, retailing—are set aside because they belong to a different conceptual register (some are belated production processes; some are hybrid). The pure function facilitates the actual exchange of commodities, their transfer "from one hand to another"—"society's metabolic process." Even as it facilitates this, it creates nothing.

The consequence is posed as a rigorous necessity: "since the circulation phase of industrial capital forms just as much a phase in the reproduction process as production does, the capital that functions independently in the circulation process must yield the average profit just as much as the capital that functions in the various branches of production." Here is the equalization argument in its purest form. Marx does not argue by analogy; he argues by competitive mobility. If commercial capital yielded a higher average profit than industrial, part of industrial capital would change into commercial capital. If it yielded a lower average profit, the opposite process would occur. And he adds a sociological observation that matters for the whole theory of capital mobility: "No species of capital finds it easier than commercial capital to change its function and designation." Commercial capital's low specificity—its lack of fixed, specialized productive equipment—makes it the most mobile form of capital, and therefore the most immediately subject to the equalization mechanism. It is the availability of this escape route that ensures the average rate of profit binds commercial capital as tightly as it binds industrial capital.

The Fundamental Question: How Does Commercial Capital Attract Its Share?

From the fact that commercial capital produces no surplus-value, two things follow: (1) the surplus-value accruing to it as average profit is a portion of the surplus-value produced by productive capital as a whole; (2) the question becomes a transmission question, not a creation question. Marx states it exactly:

"How does commercial capital attract the part of the surplus-value or profit produced by productive capital that falls to its share?"

Everything that follows is an answer to this question.

The Illusion of Nominal Price Increase

Marx first states what the answer is not: it is "a mere semblance that commercial profit is just a supplement, a nominal increase in the price of commodities above their value." Yet he immediately acknowledges the surface plausibility of this illusion. The merchant can obtain his profit only from the price of the commodities he sells; and this profit must equal the difference between his purchase price and his sale price. If additional costs of circulation enter after purchase and before sale, the excess of sale price over purchase price doesn't purely represent profit—but Marx temporarily abstracts from such costs ("To simplify the investigation, however, we assume to start with that no costs of this nature are involved").

He then contrasts the form of profit for the two kinds of capitalist. For the industrial capitalist, the difference between sale price and purchase price is the difference between the price of production and the cost price—or, at social level, between the value of commodities and their cost price for the capitalists, resolvable into the difference between total objectified labour and paid labour. Crucially, before the commodities the industrial capitalist buys go back on the market as commodities for sale, "they pass through the production process, and it is here alone that the component of their price that will later be realized as profit is produced."

The commercial dealer is different: "He has commodities in his possession only as long as they are in their circulation process." He merely continues the sale begun by the productive capitalist, the realization of their price; he makes them undergo "no intervening process in which they might absorb new surplus-value." Note the neat distinction Marx draws: the industrial capitalist realizes surplus-value in circulation; the merchant, by contrast, "does not merely realize his profit in and through circulation, he also makes it there." This making it there is what needs explanation.

Marx gives the surface appearance of how this is done: the merchant sells commodities bought at their prices of production at prices above their prices of production, or, taking commodity capital as a whole, at prices above their value—"making a nominal addition to their prices," pocketing "the difference between their nominal value and their real value, i.e. sells them dearer than they are."

The Linen Illustration and Its Interpretation

Marx then works the appearance through a small example, whose figures are partly garbled by OCR but whose logical content is clear:

Say a yard of linen costs 2 shillings. To make 10% profit on resale, the merchant adds one-tenth: he sells at 2s. 2⅖d. (the text shows "2td," an OCR corruption of 2s 2⅖d, i.e., 2.2 shillings). The difference between actual price of production and sale price is 2⅖d—a 10% profit on 2 shillings. The merchant sells a yard of linen at a price "that is really the price of 1⅒ yards." Equivalently (and here the text garbles "H yards" and "1⅒," reconciling to the reciprocal 10/11), it is "just as if I sold the buyer only 10/11 of a yard for 2 shillings and kept 1/11 for myself." Or: the merchant can buy back 1/11 of a yard with the 2⅖d at the new price. This is "simply a roundabout way of sharing in the surplus-value and surplus product by making a nominal increase in the prices of commodities."

Marx's point in presenting this example is diagnostic, not advocated. He notes: "This is the realization of commercial profit by an addition to the price of commodities, as it presents itself at first sight." And: "the whole idea that profit is derived from a nominal increase in commodity prices, or by selling them above their value, arises from the viewpoint of commercial capital." So the illusion is standpoint-dependent—it is the natural ideology of the merchant.

The Refutation: The Illusion Dissolved

Marx's decisive move: "When we look more closely, however, we soon see that this is just an illusion." But he adds a methodological caveat and bracket: "assuming the predominance of the capitalist mode of production, this is not the way commercial profit is realized. (What we are dealing with here is always the average, and not individual cases.)" The assumption of average and of developed capitalism matters—edge cases and undeveloped conditions may look otherwise.

His argument: Why did we assume the merchant can only realize 10% by selling 10% above production price? Because we assumed the producer—the industrial capitalist, who faces the world as "the producer"—had sold them to the merchant at their price of production. If the merchant's purchase price equals the production price (ultimately value), then the excess of his sale price over his purchase price—his only profit source—must be an excess of commercial price over production price; at social level, the merchant sells all commodities above their value.

But then Marx unearths the hidden premise: "But why did we assume that the industrial capitalist sold commodities to the merchant at their prices of production?... That merchant's capital... does not enter into the formation of the general rate of profit." In the earlier derivation of the general rate of profit, this assumption was necessary—first because merchant's capital did not yet exist for the analysis, and second because the average profit had to be developed as an equalization of the profits actually produced by industrial capitals in different spheres. But commercial capital is "a capital that takes a share in profit without participating in production." Therefore the earlier presentation must be supplemented. This is an unusually self-aware methodological moment: Marx flags that the previous treatment was an abstraction that now needs lifting.

Marx's Numerical Model: The Central Proof

Marx lays out the correction numerically.

Let total industrial capital advanced during the year be: - constant capital c = 720 - variable capital v = 180 - total = 900 (in millions of pounds sterling, as he stipulates) - rate of surplus-value s' = 100%, so surplus-value s = 180

The product (commodity capital C) = 720c + 180v + 180s = 1,080. Its value or price of production (the two coincide when we take the totality of commodities) equals 1,080. The rate of profit on the total capital of 900 is 180/900 = 20%. Marx notes explicitly that this 20% is the average rate of profit, because the surplus-value is here reckoned not on any particular capital of specific composition but on the total industrial capital with average composition.

Now introduce an independent commercial capital of 100. Total capital becomes 1,000. This commercial capital takes a proportionate share in profit according to its size—one-tenth of the total. It therefore takes a one-tenth share in the total surplus-value of 180, which is 18; and this corresponds to a profit rate of 18% on the commercial capital. The profit to be divided among the remaining nine-tenths (the industrial capital of 900) is now only 180 − 18 = 162, or 18% on 900. The price at which C is sold to the merchants by the industrial capitalists is therefore:

720c + 180v + 162s = 1,062

The merchant, adding the average profit of 18% to his capital of 100, sells the commodities at:

1,062 + 18 = 1,080

—i.e., at their price of production or, taking commodity capital as a whole, at their value. And yet, crucially, the merchant "only makes his profit in and through circulation and only by the excess of his sale price over and above his purchase price." The apparent contradiction is dissolved: if he still does not sell above value or price of production, this is "precisely because he bought them from the industrial capitalists below their value or price of production."

Reformulating the Price of Production

This numerical result obliges Marx to revise a core concept. Commercial capital contributes to the general rate of profit "according to the proportion it forms in the total capital." If the average rate of profit is 18% in the case considered, it would have been 20% if one-tenth of the total capital had not been commercial and the general rate had not consequently been reduced by one-tenth.

Marx then gives the "stricter and more accurate definition of the production price":

  • As before, the price of production = cost (the value of constant and variable capital contained) + average profit on this.
  • But now, the average profit is determined by the total profit the total productive capital produces, yet it is not calculated just on this total productive capital alone. If productive capital is 900 and profit is 180, the earlier rate would be 180/900 = 20%. Now it is calculated on the total productive and commercial capital together: 180/(900+100) = 180/1,000 = 18%.
  • Hence price of production = k + 18%, not k + 20%.

And then the final formula: "The real value or production price of the total commodity capital is therefore k + p + m (where m is commercial profit)." The price of production—the price at which the industrial capitalist sells as such—is less than the real production price of the commodity; or, in aggregate, the price at which the industrial capitalist class sells is less than value. In the example: 900 (cost) + 18% of 900 = 900 + 162 = 1,062.

Since the merchant sells at 118 what cost him 100, he "still adds 18%"; but because the commodities he bought at 100 are worth 118, he does not sell them above their value. In future, Marx says, we keep the expression "price of production" for this more exact sense. The editor's footnote here (F.E.) clarifies the terminological tangle: "price of production" now means the price at which the industrial capitalist sells to the merchant, not, as Marx originally developed the concept, the price at which the industrial capitalist sells when no independent commercial capital intervenes. The footnote confesses it is "rather confusing" that Marx still refers to the latter as the "real price of production," but it is presumably because it is at that price that the commodity is finally sold. This is an important editorial nuance: the reader must track which of two distinct prices "price of production" designates at each moment.

Marx then states the three-part decomposition with great clarity: - The industrial capitalist's profit = the excess of the production price of his commodity over its cost price. - Commercial profit = the excess of the sale price over the commodity's production price (the merchant's purchase price). - The real price of the commodity = its production price + the commercial profit.

And the symmetry: just as industrial capital only realizes profit already contained in the commodity's value as surplus-value, so commercial capital does so only because the whole of the surplus-value is not yet realized in the price of the commodity as realized by industrial capital. Note the footnote citing John Bellers (Essays about the Poor, Manufactures, Trade, Plantations, and Immorality, London, 1699, p. 10)—a historical anchor for the idea that merchant profit is a deduction, a citing of a pre-classical source who already intimated the mechanism.

The essential formula for the merchant: "The merchant's sale price is higher than his purchase price not because it is above the total value, but rather because his purchase price is below this total value."

Commercial Capital Inside the Equalization Process

Marx summarizes: "Commercial capital is involved in the equalization of surplus-value that forms average profit, therefore, even though it is not involved in the production of this surplus-value." The general rate of profit "thus already takes account of the deduction from the surplus-value which falls to commercial capital, i.e. a deduction from the profit of industrial capital." This is a structural claim about the rate of profit, not merely an accounting convention: the general rate is not produced by industrial capital and then modified; commercial capital is constitutive of the rate from the start, once it exists.

Two Consequences and the Dilution Effect

Marx draws two consequences:

(1) Inverse relation. "The bigger commercial capital is in comparison with industrial capital, the smaller the rate of industrial profit, and vice versa." This is a direct corollary of the denominator in the average profit rate. It means that the growth of the commercial apparatus of capitalism is a systematic depressant on the industrial profit rate—a point of substantial importance for the theory of capitalist development.

(2) The rate of profit understates exploitation. Marx recalls that in Part One it was shown that the rate of profit is always expressed in a lower figure than the rate of actual surplus-value—it "underestimates the exploitation of labour." In the present case: 720c + 180v + 180s yields s' = 100% but a profit rate of only 20%. With commercial capital, this difference is still greater: the average profit, taking into account the share accruing to commercial capital, is here 18% instead of 20%. "The average rate of profit for the directly exploiting capitalist thus makes the rate of profit appear smaller than it actually is." The veil of the profit form is thickened by commercial capital's participation.

The Relative Size of Commercial Capital and Turnover

Marx posits a regular relation: "Assuming that all other circumstances are the same, the relative size of commercial capital (though retail traders, a hybrid species, form an exception) will be in inverse proportion to the speed of its turnover, i.e. in inverse proportion to the overall vigour of the reproduction process." This is an elegant general law: the faster circulation performs its function, the less commercial capital is required relative to industrial capital. Retail traders form an explicit exception—a caveat Marx flags without full development here (retailers operate with high turnover of small capitals and myriad transactions, so the general rule doesn't apply cleanly).

The Historical–Logical Inversion

This is one of the chapter's most philosophically weighty passages. Marx distinguishes two orders:

  • In scientific analysis: "The formation of the general rate of profit appears to proceed from industrial capitals and the competition between them, being only later rectified, supplemented and modified by the intervention of commercial capital."
  • In historical development: "The situation is exactly the reverse."

He spells out the historical sequence. It is commercial capital that first fixes the prices of commodities "more or less according to their values"; it is the sphere of circulation that mediates the reproduction process in which a general rate of profit is first formed. "Commercial profit originally determines industrial profit." Only when the capitalist mode of production has come to prevail, and the producer has himself become a merchant, is commercial profit "reduced to the aliquot share of the total surplus-value that accrues to commercial capital as an aliquot part of the total capital concerned in the process of social reproduction."

This is a specific application of Marx's wider method: the logical order of presentation (industrial → commercial) is the inverse of the historical order (commercial → industrial). The abstraction that builds from production first is scientifically correct precisely because it isolates the essential relation (exploitation of labour) that the historically prior commercial form actually presupposes without yet expressing it. The nuance is significant: the deduced primacy of industrial capital is a logical primacy, not a chronicle.

The Merchant's Money Capital and the Analogy to Fixed Capital

Marx returns to the "supplementary equalization of profits brought about by the intervention of commercial capital" to make a further analytical point. No additional element goes into the value of the commodity for the money capital the merchant advances. The addition to price whereby the merchant makes his profit is "simply the portion of commodity value that productive capital has not included in the production price of the commodity, and has in fact left out."

He then draws a striking analogy: the case of this money capital "is similar to that of the industrial capitalist's fixed capital. In so far as it is not consumed, its value does not constitute an element of the commodity's value." This is a subtle point about recycling rather than consumption. The merchant's money capital is repeatedly advanced and recovered; it is not consumed into the commodity (as raw materials are), so it does not enter value. In the price the merchant pays, he replaces its production price, = M, in money. His sale price = M + ΔM, where ΔM is the addition to commodity price determined by the general rate of profit. On selling, he recovers both the original money capital and ΔM.

Crucially: "his money capital is nothing more than the commodity capital of the industrial capitalist turned into money capital, which can no more affect the value of this commodity capital than if the latter were sold directly to the final consumer instead of to the merchant." All that actually happens is that the final consumer's payment is anticipated. But—and here Marx introduces the next qualification—"this is correct only if, as we have so far assumed, the merchant has no expenses," i.e., if he advances no other capital, circulating or fixed, besides the money capital for purchasing commodities.

The Costs of Circulation Enter the Analysis

The assumption of zero expenses must now be dropped: "This is not the case, however, as we saw in discussing the costs of circulation (Volume 2, Chapter 6)." These costs are of two types: (a) costs the merchant must reclaim from other agents of circulation; (b) costs arising directly from his specific business.

Whatever their kind—whether they belong to the merchant's specific circulation costs, or represent charges arising from "belated production processes that are inserted within the circulation process" (dispatch, transport, storage, etc.)—they always require, beyond the money capital advanced for commodity purchase, an additional capital advanced to purchase and pay for these means of circulation.

Marx then distinguishes by the type of capital involved: - Circulating component: goes completely into the sale price as an additional element. - Fixed component: goes in according to the degree of its depreciation. - In so far as these are purely commercial costs of circulation: this element forms "only a nominal value and not a real addition to commodity value." - But regardless of circulating or fixed: "this entire additional capital goes into the formation of the general rate of profit."

This last clause is the essential one: even costs that add only nominal value to commodities still raise the capital base on which the general rate of profit is computed, thereby depressing it. This is a structural parallel to Marx's treatment of constant capital in the productive sphere.

Purely Commercial Costs: Definition and Content

Marx now isolates the "purely commercial costs of circulation" (excluding dispatch, transport, storage). They are the costs "necessary to realize the value of the commodity, whether transforming it from commodity into money or from money into commodity—to effect its exchange."

He carefully limits the scope: he ignores eventual production processes that continue during circulation and can exist separately from commerce as such—e.g., the transport industry proper and dispatch of goods "can be completely separate from trade, as branches of industry, and actually are so." Goods may be kept in docks and public storage areas, with costs charged to the merchant by third parties, in so far as he advances them. All this is found in wholesale trade proper, "where commercial capital appears in its purest form, hardly mixed up at all with other functions." And then the memorable line: "The haulier, the railway director and the shipowner are not 'merchants.'" This is a boundary-setting statement—it keeps the concept of commercial capital disciplined.

The costs considered are those of buying and selling. They break down into: - accounting, book-keeping; - marketing, correspondence, etc.

Their constant capital consists of offices, paper, postage, etc. Their remaining costs are reducible to variable capital advanced for commercial employees. (Marx parenthetically notes that dispatch charges, transport costs, advances of customs duties, etc. may partly be considered as advanced by the merchant in the purchase of commodities, and hence go into his purchase price.)

Marx then asserts a decisive characterization: "All these costs are incurred not in the production of the commodities' use-value, but rather in the realization of their value; they are pure costs of circulation." They don't enter the immediate production process but do enter the circulation process and hence the overall process of reproduction. Only the part laid out as variable capital concerns us at this point, and Marx lists three further questions requiring investigation: (1) how the law that only necessary labour goes into commodity value applies in the circulation process; (2) how accumulation appears in the case of commercial capital; (3) how commercial capital functions in the actual overall process of social reproduction. This signals that the present chapter is programmatic, not exhaustive.

He concludes the definition with a crucial genetic statement: "These costs arise from the economic form of the product as a commodity." They are not technically necessary costs of making the thing; they are historically necessary costs of making the thing as a commodity, i.e., of validating it through exchange.

Labour-Time in Circulation: The Continuity Argument

Marx now disposes of a possible objection by a continuity argument. If the labour-time the industrial capitalists lose in selling commodities directly to one another—which is, in objective terms, the circulation time of the commodities—does not add value, then this labour-time "does not change its character by devolving on the merchant instead of the industrial capitalist." The transformation of commodities (products) into money and money into commodities (means of production) is a necessary function of industrial capital, hence a necessary operation for the capitalist, who is simply "personified capital, capital endowed with its own consciousness and will." But these functions neither increase value nor create surplus-value.

The merchant, performing these operations and carrying on the functions of capital in circulation after the productive capitalist has ceased to do so, "simply takes the place of the industrial capitalist." The labour-time these operations cost is employed on necessary operations in the reproduction process of capital, "but it does not add any extra value."

Marx then draws a direct consequence for the merchant's own income. If the merchant did not perform these operations, and so did not spend the labour-time they require, "he would not be using his capital as a circulation agent of industrial capital; he would not be continuing the function that the industrial capitalist has abandoned, and hence would not share as a capitalist, and in proportion to the capital he advanced, in the mass of profit produced by the industrial capitalist class." The merchant's claim on surplus-value derives from performing a necessary social function—not from creating value. The legitimating basis of his income is functional, not productive.

Therefore: "the merchant capitalist does not need to employ any wage-labourers in order to take a share in this mass of surplus-value, to valorize his advance as capital." If his business and capital are small, he may himself be the only worker employed. He is paid out of the part of profit that accrues to him from the difference between his purchase price and the real price of production.

And, in a passage that anticipates the later theory of the commercial petty bourgeoisie: "if the capital advanced by the merchant is small, the profit he realizes may not be any greater than the wage of a better-paid skilled worker; it may even be less than this." Functioning alongside him are the direct commercial agents of productive capitalists—buyers, salesmen, commercial travellers—receiving the same income or higher, whether as wage or as a share in profit (commission, percentage). In one case the merchant pockets commercial profit as an independent capitalist; in the other, the direct employee of the industrial capitalist is paid a part of the profit as wage or proportionate share, and the principal pockets both the industrial and the commercial profit. But in all cases, "even though the income the circulation agent receives may appear to him as a simple wage, as payment for the work he has performed," and even if the size of his profit may only equal a better-paid worker's wage, "this income still derives solely from the commercial profit. This results from the fact that his labour is not value-creating labour." This is a pivotal claim for understanding the ideological form of commercial income as "wages of work."

Time-Costs for the Industrial Capitalist and the Division-of-Function Advantage

Marx now examines the consequences from the standpoint of industrial capital. A prolonged circulation operation means for the industrial capitalist: (1) a personal loss of time, insofar as he is prevented from performing his function as director of production; (2) an extended stay of his product, in money or commodity form, in the circulation process, where it is not valorized and the immediate process of production is interrupted. If production is not to be interrupted, either production must be cut back, or additional money capital must be advanced so production can continue on the same scale. Either way: "either a smaller profit is made with the former capital or additional money capital has to be advanced in order to make the previous profit."

The same logic holds when the merchant replaces the industrial capitalist. Instead of the industrial capitalist spending more time in circulation, the merchant now spends the time; instead of the industrial capitalist advancing additional capital for circulation, the merchant advances it; or (the same thing), "whereas previously a substantial portion of the industrial capital was constantly entering and leaving the circulation process, now the merchant's capital is cooped up there permanently." Previously the industrial capitalist made a smaller profit; now he must abandon a part of his profit completely to the merchant.

But here Marx states the advantage of the division of function: in so far as commercial capital remains confined to the limits within which it is necessary, "the distinction is simply that this division of capital's function enables less time to be devoted exclusively to the circulation process, and less additional capital advanced for it, so that the reduction in the total profit which now takes the form of commercial profit is less than it would be otherwise."

He gives a numerical variant: In the earlier example (720c + 180v + 180s, plus commercial capital 100), the industrial capitalist is left 162, i.e., 18%, a deduction of 18. But if he had to conduct without the independent operations of commercial capital, the additional capital needed might be 200, so the total advance would be 1,100 instead of 900, and the surplus-value of 180 would represent a profit rate of only 16 4/11%. The comparison (18% deduction vs. 16 4/11% profit rate) is not apples-to-apples (one is a deduction, one is a rate), but the direction is clear: the division of functions reduces the total loss of profit to industrial capital.

Additional Capital for Circulation Costs Taken Over by the Merchant

Marx extends: if the industrial capitalist who is his own merchant has advanced—besides the additional capital to buy new commodities before the product in circulation has been transformed into money—still further capital to realize the value of his commodity capital (office expenses and wages for commercial employees), then this certainly forms additional capital but "does not form any more surplus-value." It must be replaced out of the commodities' value; a portion of this value must be reconverted into these circulation costs, even though no additional surplus-value is formed. As far as the total social capital is concerned, "a part of this capital is required for secondary operations that are not a part of the valorization process, and this part of the social capital has to be constantly reproduced for this purpose." In this way, the profit rate for both individual capitalists and the industrial capitalist class as a whole is reduced—"a result that follows from every injection of additional capital in so far as this is required to set the same amount of variable capital in motion."

If these additional costs are taken over by the merchant, there is still a reduction in the profit rate, "but to a lesser extent and in a different manner." The merchant now advances more capital than would be necessary if these costs did not exist; the profit on this additional capital raises the total commercial profit; commercial capital enters the equalization of the average rate of profit "on a greater scale," and the average profit falls.

Numerical illustration: If a further additional capital of 50 is advanced for these costs, besides the commercial capital of 100, the total surplus-value of 180 is distributed between a productive capital of 900 and a commercial capital of 150, making a total of 1,050. The average rate of profit falls to 180/1,050 = 17 1/7%. The industrial capitalist sells to the merchant at 900 + 154 2/7 = 1,054 2/7; the merchant sells for 1,130 (1,080 + 50 for expenses he must recover). Marx adds the assumption that the division between commercial and industrial capital "involves a centralization of trading costs and a consequent reduction in them." So the merchant's larger scale compensates partially for the dilution.

The Position of the Commercial Wage-Labourer

The chapter now turns to the commercial employees employed by the merchant capitalist. Marx offers a two-sided characterization. From one point of view, a commercial employee is "a wage-labourer like any other":

  • First, his labour is bought with the merchant's variable capital, not with money spent as revenue; it is bought "not for a personal service but for the purpose of valorizing the capital advanced in it." (This is the general criterion distinguishing productive from unproductive consumption of labour-power within capitalist production.)
  • Second, the value of his labour-power, and therefore his wage, is determined, like all wage-labourers', by the production and reproduction costs of this particular labour-power, "and not by the product of his labour."

But there is necessarily a difference, "the same difference between him and the workers directly employed by industrial capital as there is between industrial capital and commercial capital, and consequently between the industrial capitalist and the merchant." Since the merchant, as pure circulation agent, produces neither value nor surplus-value (the additional value he adds is reducible to the addition of previously existing value, though Marx concedes the question here of how he maintains and conserves the value of this constant capital still remains to be examined), "the commercial workers whom he employs in these same functions cannot possibly create surplus-value for him directly."

Marx is careful to add the standard abstraction: just as with productive workers, we assume wages are determined by the value of labour-power, so the merchant "does not enrich himself by a deduction from wages," and in reckoning his costs "does not put down an advance for labour that he only pays in part." So Marx explicitly excludes cheating on wages from the analysis to isolate the structural mechanism. This is the same methodological bracket applied to productive workers.

The Resolution of the Apparent Paradox: How Commercial Workers Yield Profit Without Creating Surplus-Value

The "problem" that commercial workers seem to pose—how they produce profit for employers even though they produce no surplus-value—Marx declares already resolved by the general analysis of commercial profit. The mechanism is a transfer within the total surplus-value, not a creation of new value:

"Just as industrial capital makes its profit by selling labour that is already contained and realized in the commodity, labour for which it has not paid an equivalent, so commercial capital makes a profit by not paying productive capital in full for the unpaid labour contained in the commodity (in so far as the capital laid out in order to produce the commodity functions as an aliquot part of the total industrial capital), and, as against this, itself receiving the additional portion which it has not paid for once the commodity has been sold."

The relation to surplus-value differs between the two capitals: - "The latter [industrial] produces surplus-value by directly appropriating the unpaid labour of others." - "The former [commercial] appropriates a portion of this surplus-value by getting it transferred from industrial capital to itself."

So the merchant's claim is a redistribution claim. He gets a share because he performs a function that industrial capital needs done and pays for by accepting a lower price.

The Source of the Individual Merchant's Profit: Unpaid Commercial Labour

Marx then specifies how the individual merchant's profit expands. "It is only by way of its function in the realization of values that commercial capital functions as capital in the reproduction process, and therefore draws, as functioning capital, on the surplus-value that the total capital produces." For the individual merchant, the amount of his profit "depends on the amount of capital that he can employ in this process," and he can employ all the more capital in buying and selling "the greater the unpaid labour of his clerks."

This is the key mechanism: the merchant's appropriation of surplus-value is scaled by the unpaid labour of his employees. "The very function by virtue of which the commercial capitalist's money is capital is performed in large measure by his employees, on his instructions." Their unpaid labour, "even though it does not create surplus-value, does create his ability to appropriate surplus-value, which, as far as this capital is concerned, gives exactly the same result; i.e. it is its source of profit." Otherwise, "the business of commerce could never be conducted in the capitalist manner, or on a large scale."

Note the double negative patience of Marx's formulation. He is careful not to say that commercial labour creates surplus-value. He says it creates the capacity to appropriate it. The distinction is analytically crucial—and it is precisely here that Marx distinguishes his own theory from the vulgar view that all wage labour equally produces profit. The symmetry he asserts is structural, not substantive: "Just as the unpaid labour of the worker creates surplus-value for productive capital directly, so also does the unpaid labour of the commercial employee create a share in that surplus-value for commercial capital."

The Remaining Difficulty: The Nature and Limits of b

Now Marx states the real remaining difficulty. Since the merchant's own labour-time is not value-creating even though it procures him a share in surplus-value already produced, what is the situation with the variable capital he lays out on the purchase of commercial labour-power? Two horns:

  • If b is NOT included in the cost of the commercial capital advanced: this would seem to contradict the law of equalization of the profit rate—"what capitalist would advance 150, if he could reckon only 100 of it as capital advanced?"
  • If b IS included: this would seem to contradict the very nature of commercial capital, since this kind of capital "does not function as capital by setting the labour of others in motion, in the manner of industrial capital, but rather by itself working, i.e. itself performing the functions of buying and selling, and it is precisely in this way that it transfers to itself a part of the surplus-value the industrial capital has created."

Marx then enumerates the further questions this opens (merchant's variable capital; the law of necessary labour in the circulation sphere; how the work of the merchant maintains the value of his constant capital; the role of commercial capital in overall reproduction; and the division of commodity capital / money capital on the one hand and commercial capital / money-dealing capital on the other). These are deferred.

The Fragmentation Argument

Before resolving the b problem formally, Marx develops its dialectical material basis. If each merchant possessed only the capital he was personally able to turn over by his own work, "there would be an infinite fragmentation of commercial capital." And this fragmentation would necessarily increase with the progress of the capitalist mode of production—because productive capital produces on larger and larger scales, the disproportion between productive and commercial capital would grow. "To the same extent as capital was centralized in the sphere of production, it would be decentralized in the sphere of circulation." The industrial capitalist would then have to deal with 100 or 1,000 different merchants; the advantages of the autonomous position of commercial capital would be lost; and other circulation costs (grading, dispatch, etc.) would also grow.

Then, considering commercial capital itself: commercial work proper takes no more time to reckon with large figures than small; it takes ten times longer to make ten purchases of £100 than one purchase of £1,000; ten times the correspondence work, paper, and postage to write to ten small merchants as to one big one. A division of labour in the commercial office—one person keeps books, another the cash-box, a third writes letters, one buys, another sells, another travels—"spares a tremendous amount of labour-time," so the number of workers in wholesale trade is "in no way proportionate to the comparative scale of the transactions." Marx's conclusion: "In commerce, in fact, far more than in industry, the same function takes the same amount of labour-time whether it is performed on a large or small scale." This is a claim of returns to scale (or, more precisely, of near-constant unit labour-time and hence strong scale economies). "Thus concentration historically appears in commerce earlier than in the industrial workshop."

Added to this are the expenses for constant capital: a hundred small offices cost infinitely more than one big one; a hundred small warehouses more than a big one; transport costs grow with fragmentation.

So: the industrial capitalist would spend more labour and incur greater circulation costs on the commercial side of his business. The same commercial capital, divided among many small merchants, would require many more workers to carry out its functions and "a larger commercial capital would be required to turn over the same commodity capital."

Symbolic Formulation and the "Limits of b"

Marx now introduces formal notation:

  • B = the total commercial capital directly invested in the buying and selling of commodities.
  • b = the corresponding variable capital laid out for payment of commercial assistants.

Then: B + b will necessarily be less than the total commercial capital B would be if each merchant struggled by without assistance (i.e., if one portion was not invested as b). The hiring of commercial assistants reduces the total commercial capital needed, even as it adds b, because of the scale economies just described.

But the problem is not finished. The price at which commodities are sold must be sufficient: 1. "to pay the average profit on B + b." This is already explained by the fact that B + b is always a reduction on the original B and represents a smaller commercial capital than would be needed without the b. 2. "to replace, besides the seemingly additional profit on b, also the wages paid, the merchant's actual variable capital = b."

The second requirement creates the difficulty. Does b form a new component of the price, or is it "simply a part of the profit made with B + b that appears as wages as far as the commercial employees are concerned and appears to the merchant himself as simply the replacement of his variable capital"?

  • If the latter: the profit the merchant makes on B + b would be equal simply to the profit accruing to B, per the general rate—b being paid as wages, but without this yielding any profit.
  • "It all boils down to finding the limits of b (in the mathematical sense)."

Marx then sharpens the notation: - B = capital directly laid out on buying and selling commodities. - K = the constant capital utilized for this function (the material expenses involved). - b = the variable capital the merchant lays out.

The Replacement of B and K

Replacement of B presents no difficulty. For the merchant, B is simply the realized purchase price, or the price of production for the manufacturer. The merchant pays this price, and on resale he receives B back as a portion of his sale price, plus the profit on B. Example: commodity costs £100, profit 10%; sold at 110. The commodity "still costs 100, as before," so the commercial capital of 100 "only adds 10 to it"—i.e., B is simply passed through, and the profit on B is the only increment to price.

Replacement of K: K is at most as large as, though in actual fact smaller than, the portion of constant capital the producer would need for selling and buying (in addition to the constant capital he uses directly in production). This part must always be recovered in the price of the commodity, or, equivalently, a corresponding part of the commodity must always be spent and reproduced in this form, taking the total social capital as a whole. This portion of constant capital advanced would have "the same constricting effect on the profit rate as does all constant capital directly invested in production." Insofar as the industrial capitalist hands over the commercial side of his business to the merchant, he doesn't need to advance this portion—the merchant does.

But Marx notes "this is really only an advance in name," in as much as the merchant "neither produces nor reproduces the constant capital that he uses (his material expenses)." The production of these appears therefore as a separate business of certain industrial capitalists (or a part of their business), so that these "play the same role as those supplying constant capital to the producers of means of subsistence." The merchant thus receives first the replacement for this constant capital, and second the profit on it. On both counts, the profit of the industrial capitalist is reduced. But because of the concentration and economy that results from the division of labour, this reduction is less than it would be if he had to advance this capital himself. "The reduction in the profit rate is less, because the capital advanced in this way is less."

The Sale Price with b Included

The comprehensive formula. Formerly the sale price was B + K + profit on (B + K). Now:

Sale price = B + K + b + profit on (B + K) + profit on b.

  • B only replaces the purchase price; it adds nothing to this price besides the profit on B.
  • K adds not only the profit on K but also K itself. But K + the profit on K—the portion of circulation costs advanced as constant capital + the corresponding average profit—"would be greater in the hands of the industrial capitalist than in the hands of the commercial capitalist." The reduction in the average profit takes this form: the full average profit is calculated after the deduction of B + K from the industrial capital advanced. This deduction from the average profit for B + K is paid to the merchant, so that it appears as the profit of a special capital, commercial capital.
  • b + profit on b: "this is where the real difficulty lies."

What the Merchant Buys with b

Marx restates: What the merchant buys with b is "merely commercial labour," i.e., labour needed for the functions of capital circulation, C-M and M-C. Commercial labour is the labour always necessary for a capital to function as commercial capital, for it to mediate the transformation of commodities into money and money into commodities. "It is labour that realizes values but does not create any." And only in so far as a capital performs these functions—only in so far as a capitalist performs these operations and this labour with his capital—does the capital function as commercial capital and take part in settling the general rate of profit, drawing dividends from total profit.

But in (b + profit on b) it seems that: (1) first the labour is paid (since it comes to the same thing whether the industrial capitalist pays the merchant for his own labour or for that of his employees); and (2) secondly, the profit on the payment for this labour that the merchant himself would have had to perform. Commercial capital seems to receive both the repayment of b and the profit on it—because it first gets paid for the labour by which it functions as commercial capital, and secondly gets the profit because it functions as capital, i.e., in its capacity as a functioning capital, it performs labour that is paid for in profit. "This is the question which we have to resolve."

The Numerical Decomposition of the b Problem

Marx sets up the calculation. Let:

  • B = 100
  • b = 10
  • rate of profit p' = 10%
  • K = 0 (to avoid unnecessarily reintroducing an element of the purchase price already dealt with)

Then:

Purchase price = (B + profit on B) + (b + profit on b) = B + Bp' + b + bp' = 100 + 10 + 10 + 1 = 121.

Now the counterfactual: if the merchant did not lay out this b on wages—since b is paid simply for commercial labour, i.e., labour needed to realize the value of the commodity capital industrial capital puts on the market—the situation would be as follows. To buy or sell B = 100, the merchant gives up his time, and we assume this is the only time available to him. The commercial labour represented by b = 10, if it were not paid as wages but rather by way of profit, would presuppose another commercial capital of 100, since 10% of this gives b = 10. This second B = 100 would not go additionally into the price of the commodity, but the 10% certainly would. There would therefore be two operations at 100, giving 200, buying commodities for 200 + 20 = 220.

This is a subtle argument. Marx shows that the profit-based compensation for commercial labour requires a doubling of the merchant's capital (from 100 to 200)—because his own labour-time can only turn over B = 100, and the extra commercial labour must be recruited either by wages (b = 10) or by a second merchant's capital of 100 receiving 10% profit (= 10). If recruited by wages, the price is 121; if recruited by a second capital, the price would be 220 for double the goods, i.e., 110 per single unit—which reveals that the wage-based route requires only b = 10 as an additional price component, not an additional capital of 100. This is precisely why commercial employees are necessary for large-scale commerce: hiring b-labour is cheaper (to industrial capital) than duplicating merchant capital. And this explains the "limits of b": b is bounded by the cost of the alternative (a second B), and hence b enters the price as a cost like any other, and also earns profit as part of the total commercial capital advanced.

The Methodological Reduction: Commercial Capital as a Branch of Industrial Capital

Marx states the governing methodological principle of the whole resolution:

"Since commercial capital is nothing at all but the form in which a part of the industrial capital functioning in the circulation process has become autonomous, all questions relating to it must be resolved in this way: the problem must at the outset be put in the form in which the phenomena peculiar to commercial capital do not yet appear independently but are still in direct connection with industrial capital, of which commercial capital is a branch."

So the b at issue "must firstly be investigated on the spot, in the commercial office of the industrial capitalist himself." This is the analytical equivalent of rewinding the tape to the moment before the commercial function became autonomous, and examining the wage payments for commercial labour as they appear inside industrial capital.

The Commercial Office of the Industrial Capitalist

"Right from the beginning, this office is always infinitesimally small in relation to the industrial workshop." Yet it is evident that as production expands, the commercial operations required by the circulation of industrial capital increase—both those required to sell the product as commodity capital and those required to transform the money obtained back into means of production, plus keeping the accounts for the whole process. Price calculation, book-keeping, fund management, and correspondence are all part of this. "The more the scale of production grows, the greater are industrial capital's commercial operations, although the increase is by no means in the same proportion" (again the scale-economy qualification), "and the greater also the labour and other circulation costs involved in the realization of value and surplus-value."

It is necessary therefore to employ commercial workers "who make up a proper commercial office." The expenditure on this, "even though incurred in the form of wages, is distinct from the variable capital laid out on the purchase of productive labour." Note the crucial distinction: this is wages, but not variable capital in the sense of capital that purchases productive labour. It increases the outlays of the industrial capitalist, the mass of capital he has to advance, without directly increasing surplus-value: "For this is an outlay for labour employed simply in realizing values already created." Like other outlays of the same kind, this reduces the rate of profit, because the capital advanced grows, but not the surplus-value.

Formal statement: The surplus-value s remains constant, but the capital advanced C grows from C to C + ΔC, so the profit rate s/C is replaced by s/(C + ΔC). (The text's notation "~o" and "c /,... c" is OCR-garbled; the intended relation is s/C → s/(C + ΔC).) The industrial capitalist therefore attempts to keep these circulation costs to a minimum, just as he does his outlay on constant capital.

The Asymmetry Between Commercial and Productive Wage-Labour

The crucial consequence: "Industrial capital therefore does not behave towards its commercial employees as it does to its productive wage-labourers." The more productive workers are employed, other things being equal, "the more massive is production and the greater the surplus-value or profit." By contrast, the greater the scale of production and the greater the value and surplus-value to be realized—and therefore the greater the commodity capital produced—"the more, accordingly, do office expenses grow in absolute terms, even if not relatively," and the more occasion there is for a particular kind of division of labour.

The dependency runs one way: profit is the prerequisite for these outlays, not the consequence. Marx observes this is shown by the way commercial salaries are often paid partly as a percentage of profit—evidence that the salary is a share of a pre-existing fund, not a claim derived from value its recipient created. And note the essential asymmetry in a strong form:

"A labour that consists simply in intermediary operations, involving partly the calculation of values, partly their realization, and partly again the transformation of the money realized back into means of production, a labour whose scope thus depends on the magnitude of values produced and to be realized—that a labour of this kind functions not as the cause of the respective magnitudes and amounts of these values, as does directly productive labour, but is rather a consequence of them."

And he illustrates this with the parallel of circulation costs generally: "If there is much to be weighed, measured, packed and transported, there must be plenty there in the first place. The amount of packing and transport work, etc. depends on the mass of the commodities that are objects of this activity and not the other way round." This is an important dialectical point: the material dependent status of circulation labour is masked by its wage-form, which makes it look like any other labour.

The Commercial Worker's Wage and Its Tendency to Fall

Marx now consolidates the position of the commercial worker and develops a dynamic law of his wage. The commercial worker does not produce surplus-value directly. But the price of his labour is determined by the value of his labour-power, i.e., its cost of production, "although the exercise of this labour-power, the exertion, expenditure of energy and wear and tear it involves, is no more limited by the value of his labour-power than it is in the case of any other wage-labourer." This restates the standard distinction between the value of labour-power and its exercise (use-value as labour): the capitalist extracts as much labour as he can, but pays only for the reproductive cost of the labour-power.

His wage therefore does not stand in any necessary relationship to the amount of profit he helps the capitalist realize. "What he costs the capitalist and what he brings in for him are different quantities." What he brings in is "a function not of any direct creation of surplus-value but of his assistance in reducing the cost of realizing surplus-value, in so far as he performs labour (part of it unpaid)." The parenthetical "(part of it unpaid)" is critical—it names the appropriation relationship.

The commercial worker proper belongs to the better-paid class of wage-labourer—"one of those whose labour is skilled labour, above-average labour." But his wage has a tendency to fall, as the capitalist mode of production advances, even in relation to average labour.

Marx gives two causes:

First cause—the division of labour in the commercial office. Only a one-sided development of ability need be produced, and much of the cost of producing this ability is free for the capitalist, since the worker's skill is rather developed by the function itself—and indeed developed all the more quickly, the more one-sided the function becomes with the division of labour. The deskilling-then-reskilling dynamic here is peculiar: the worker acquires the skill on the job, at the capitalist's expense, and this acquisition time is unpaid overhead.

Second cause—the cheapening reproduction of basic skills. Basic skills, knowledge of commerce and languages, etc., are reproduced "ever more quickly, easily, generally and cheaply" the more the capitalist mode of production adapts teaching methods, etc., to practical purposes. The general extension of popular education permits this variety of labour "to be recruited from classes which were formerly excluded from it and were accustomed to a lower standard of living." This increases supply, and with it competition. So: "With a few exceptions, therefore, the labour-power of these people is devalued with the advance of capitalist production; their wages fall, whereas their working ability increases."

This is a striking and precise claim. The commercial worker is de-skilled by skill-diffusion, not by mechanization. The wage falls even as ability rises—a Veblen-like widening of the field of recruitment depresses value. The capitalist increases the number of these workers only if he has more value and profit to realize: "The increase in this labour is always an effect of the increase in surplus-value, and never a cause of it." The direction of causation is from surplus-value to commercial employment—never the reverse. And thus the chapter closes its treatment of the commercial worker.

The editor's footnote 39 (F.E.) provides a poignant empirical verification of "this prognosis, written in 1865, of the fate of the commercial proletariat since this time," in the form of "the hundreds of German clerks skilled in all commercial operations and in three or four languages, who are offering their services in vain in the City of London for a weekly wage of 25 shillings—well below the wage of a skilled mechanic." The footnote also reports a gap of two pages in the manuscript at this point, indicating that Marx intended to develop this point further, and refers back to Volume 2, Chapter 6 ("The Costs of Circulation"), pp. 207–14, already touching on various points pertinent here. So the editor's apparatus confirms the incompleteness of the chapter and its connection to the earlier analysis.

The Duplication: General Formal Determinations vs. Special Capitals

The chapter closes with a synthetic statement of the theoretical structure. Marx notes "a certain duplication":

  • On the one hand, the functions of commodity capital and money capital (and consequently also of commercial capital) are general formal determinations of industrial capital. That is, every industrial capital necessarily passes through the forms of commodity capital and money capital in the normal course of its circuit.
  • On the other hand, special capitals, and consequently a special set of capitalists, are exclusively engaged in these functions; and these functions develop into "special spheres for the valorization of capital."

This is an important theoretical synthesis: the economic form of a function can be universal (necessary to every industrial capital) and simultaneously become the exclusive occupation of a specialized capital. When the two coincide, the function becomes a distinct branch of capital in its own right.

The Autonomy of Commercial Functions and Circulation Costs

"It is only with commercial capital that the commercial functions and circulation costs acquire autonomy." For industrial capital, the aspect pertaining to circulation consists not only in its regular forms of commodity capital and money capital, but also in the commercial office alongside the workshop. But with commercial capital this acquires autonomy: for the latter, "the commercial office forms its only workshop." The part of capital applied in the form of circulation costs appears much greater with the wholesale merchant than with the industrialist—because besides the business office that goes with every industrial workshop, the part of capital that must be employed in this way by the overall class of industrial capitalists "is now concentrated in the hands of individual merchants." By taking charge of the circulation function, they also take over the circulation costs that arise from it. This is a concentration of overhead—which, as argued above, reduces the aggregate circulation costs and hence mitigates the dilution of the profit rate.

The Final Inversion: Expense vs. Source of Profit

The chapter's closing paragraph is a dialectical tour de force—the same object appears in opposite forms depending on the standpoint:

"To industrial capital, the costs of circulation appear as expenses, which they are. To the merchant, they appear as the source of his profit, which—on the assumption of a general rate of profit—stands in proportion to the size of these costs. The outlay that has to be made on these circulation costs is therefore a productive investment as far as commercial capital is concerned. For it, therefore, the commercial labour that it buys is also directly productive."

This final sentence requires careful reading. Marx is not saying that commercial labour creates value. He is saying that for commercial capital—i.e., from the standpoint of the merchant's particular capital—the labour it buys is "directly productive" of his profit, because his profit scales with the size of the circulation outlay he must make. The word "directly productive" is explicitly relative to "for it." From the standpoint of total social capital, commercial labour is unproductive of value. The chapter thus ends with a statement whose ambiguities would occupy Marxist scholarship for a century: the form of productive investment is attached, by the merchant's standpoint, to what is functionally unproductive—the same inversion of form that the chapter diagnosed at its opening.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Systemic Laws Revealed

Chapter 17 crystallizes several fundamental principles of Marx's mature economic theory.

First, the law of the equalization of profit rates applies to all capital, regardless of whether it produces surplus-value. Commercial capital, though it creates no surplus-value, is still governed by the general rate of profit reached through competition. The mechanism is competitive mobility—and commercial capital is the most mobile of all. This is a decisive theoretical move. It shows that the general rate of profit is not a productive phenomenon whose scope happens to include commercial capital; it is a distributive phenomenon in which all capitals, functionally necessary to the reproduction process, participate by right of their function. The rate of profit is a social rate, not a technical one.

Second, the general rate of profit is a composite rate, computed on the total social capital, not the productive capital alone. The chapter provides a strict correction to the earlier presentation: the formula is no longer s/(c + v) over productive capital, but s/(productive + commercial capital). This means that the more developed capitalist society becomes—the more the circulation apparatus grows—the more the industrial profit rate is depressed by the very existence of that apparatus. This is a profound point of development theory. The expansion of commerce, finance, and the service apparatus attendant to circulation is not a "friction" on the profit rate but a structural depressant of it.

Third, commercial profit is a transfer, not a creation. The chapter makes commercial profit structurally transparent: it is a deduction from surplus-value already produced, effected by the industrial capitalist selling below the real price of production and the merchant selling at the real price. The merchant's apparent markup above his own purchase price is simply the flip side of the industrial capitalist's lower sale price. The merchant is a redistributive agent, not a value-producer.

Fourth, the rate of profit systematically understates exploitation, and commercial capital deepens this understatement. In the example, s' = 100% but p' = 20%, then 18%. The veil of the profit form is denser in the presence of commercial capital. This has substantial ideological implications: the very category structure of capitalist accounting—the price of production, the profit rate—conceals the true degree of labour exploitation, and it does so by construction.

Fifth, the historical and logical orders are inverse. Marx's point that commercial profit historically determines industrial profit, while logically industrial profit is prior, is methodologically profound. It means the analytical primacy of production is not contradicted by the historic primacy of commerce; rather, the historically older commercial form can only be fully understood through the logically prior productive relation that it presupposes without yet expressing.

Broader Implications

For the theory of capitalist development: Marx's prediction that the commercial apparatus expands and that commercial wages tend to fall is empirically documented in the editor's footnote (the hundreds of German clerks in the City of London). This anticipates modern labour-market analysis of the "reserve army" in white-collar work, the effects of educational expansion on wage differentials, and the phenomenon of credential inflation. Marx's specific mechanism—that the extension of popular education cheapens the reproduction of commercial skills and thereby reduces wages—is a forerunner of later analyses of skill-reproduction and labour-market competition.

For the theory of the commercial petty bourgeoisie: Marx's observation that the small merchant "may himself be the only worker employed" and that his profit "may not be any greater than the wage of a better-paid skilled worker; it may even be less"—this anticipates the analysis of the "intermediate strata," the small-business owners whose income is effectively a wage but who bear capital risk. This is relevant to the modern theory of "the self-employed" and the differential between formal wage status and effective economic position.

For the theory of the wage form: Marx's insistence that the income of the circulation agent, whether merchant's profit or employee's wage, "derives solely from the commercial profit"—and that "his labour is not value-creating labour"—sets up a general critique of the wage form as a mask for appropriation. The wage form makes all labour look alike; but the source of the wage is different depending on the labour's relation to value creation. This is a central feature of Marx's critique of political economy: category forms conceal social relations.

For the theory of service and circulation in modern economies: The chapter's treatment of "pure costs of circulation" and their distinction from transport and storage anticipates the theoretical problems of defining productive vs. unproductive labour in complex economies. It shows that "productiveness" for Marx is a specific historical concept (production of surplus-value), not a general one (production of use-values).

For the trinity-formula critique: This chapter directly prepares the terrain for the theory of profit-sharing among commercial profit, interest, and rent. It establishes that commercial capital's share of total surplus-value is parasitic (in the technical sense: dependent on prior production). This sets up the critique of interest-bearing capital and the "fetishism" of the trinity formula that Marx will develop later: capital appearing to generate profit, land appearing to generate rent, labour appearing to generate wages.

Philosophical and Theoretical Implications

The standpoint-dependence of categories. Marx repeatedly emphasizes that the illusion of commercial profit (as a nominal markup) "arises from the viewpoint of commercial capital." This is an instance of Marx's general method: economic categories are not standpoint-neutral; their apparent meaning depends on the position from which the observer views the process. The merchant's view of his profit is a necessary illusion generated by his place in the system—not a mere mistake. This is a strong philosophical position about the relation between social position and cognitive form.

The methodological use of abstraction. The chapter moves back and forth between levels of abstraction: from the assumption of zero costs to the introduction of K and b; from the autonomy of commercial capital to the reduction of it to the commercial office of industrial capital. Marx is explicit that commercial capital must be analyzed first in its emergent form within industrial capital and only then in its autonomous form. The reduction of commercial capital to a "branch" of industrial capital—the methodological principle he states—is a model of his dialectical method: phenomena must be derived from their germinal form, not analyzed as if they had always existed in their developed shape.

The refutation of monetarism. The chapter's destruction of the "mark-up" theory of profit is simultaneously a refutation of the view that profit arises from sale itself. It is not the case, Marx argues, that profit is a price phenomenon. The merchant's selling above his purchase price is not an increase above social value. This is a direct attack on the "profit-upon-alienation" theory (the idea that profit comes from "selling dear"), and it connects to the broader theory of exploitation in Volume 1.

Essential Takeaways

  1. Function, not productiveness, grounds participation in average profit. Commercial capital earns the average profit by performing a necessary function (mediation of commodity metamorphosis), not by producing surplus-value.

  2. The general rate of profit is computed on all functioning capital. The entry of commercial capital into the equalization process reduces the industrial profit rate, and thereby deepens the concealment of labour exploitation.

  3. The merchant's profit is a redistribution of already-produced surplus-value, not value creation. The merchant buys below value and sells at value.

  4. Commercial labour realizes value but creates none; commercial workers' wages follow the value of labour-power, tend to fall with capitalist development, and are an effect of, never a cause of, surplus-value.

  5. Commercial capital is analytically derivative of industrial capital—its functions, its costs, and its wage relations must be derived from the corresponding moments inside industrial capital before being analyzed in their autonomous commercial form.

  6. The systematic illusion of commercial profit is the standpoint-ideology of the merchant, and this standpoint-dependence of economic categories is constitutive, not accidental.

  7. The historical primacy of commercial profit is compatible with the logical primacy of industrial capital—a paradigm case of Marx's method of uniting logical derivation with historical genesis.

The chapter, therefore, does not merely fill a gap; it re-organizes the entire theory of the profit rate around the total social capital, subsuming the circulation apparatus under the equalization mechanism, and laying the groundwork for the later analysis of the division of surplus-value among its various claimants—profit of enterprise, interest, rent. Its most enduring theoretical achievement is the demonstration that capital can earn average profit without producing surplus-value, provided it performs a necessary function in the social reproductive process—an insight whose implications extend far beyond the nineteenth-century textile merchant who stands at its center.
