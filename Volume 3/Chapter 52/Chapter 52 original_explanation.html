Volume 3 • Chapter 52: Classes

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 52 of Capital is, by any measure, the strangest chapter in the entire work: a fragment of roughly five hundred words that poses one of the most consequential questions in the history of social theory — what makes a class? — and then stops. The chapter is not an argument that unfolds; it is an argument that is interrupted at the precise moment when its destructive phase is complete and its constructive phase has not yet begun. The text that survives consists of an opening structural proposition (capitalist society is composed of three great classes defined by three property forms and three revenues), a geographical and methodological qualification (the pure articulation exists nowhere, not even in England), a compressed recapitulation of the developmental law that produces those three positions, a supporting quotation from Friedrich List on estate management, and then a two-step logical demolition of the most obvious answer to the class question — an answer Marx himself first states sympathetically before dismantling it. The final words of the chapter list vineyards, fields, forests, mines, and fisheries, end with "etc.," and are followed by the editorial note: "At this point the manuscript breaks off."

The core thesis the fragment advances — negatively and by implication — is that class cannot be defined by revenue or by the identity of revenue with revenue source. The most natural, most empirically seductive, and indeed the most textually prepared answer to the question of what constitutes a class is that a class is a group of people who derive their income from the same source. Marx grants this answer its full intuitive force: it is what appears "at first sight," it fits the three great classes perfectly, and it is supported by the analytic architecture of the whole volume, which has laboriously demonstrated the sources of wages, profit, and ground-rent. And then he destroys it with two counterexamples and a general principle. Doctors and government officials would also have to be classes. And the division of social labour fragments the very groups the criterion is supposed to unify into an "infinite" series — vineyard-owners, field-owners, forest-owners, mine-owners, fishery-owners — so that the criterion, applied consistently, yields not three classes but an unbounded plurality, and thereby abolishes the concept of class altogether.

The chapter's intellectual objective, then, is not to provide a definition of class but to clear the ground for one, by demonstrating that the definition cannot be found at the level of distribution, income, or the circulation of revenues, and must therefore be sought at the level of production — the level at which the three great groups are constituted as the "formative elements" of modern society. The text never states this positive answer, but the fragment's own opening analytical passages point unmistakably in that direction: the sentences that precede the pivot question describe a law of development that separates producers from the means of production and concentrates those means into large groups, i.e., transforms labour into wage-labour and the means of production into capital, and that independently divorces landed property from both capital and labour. The chapter thus supplies the raw material for the answer it never gives.

How the chapter fits its broader framework. The running head of the second page — "The Revenues and Their Sources" — identifies the chapter's location at the terminus of the volume's final analytical movement: the examination of the three great revenues (wages, profit, rent) as forms of income and, more fundamentally, as the forms in which surplus value is distributed among the classes that produce and appropriate it. The chapter therefore stands at the junction between the volume's theory of distribution and its implicit theory of social structure. It is the point at which the economic analysis of capital, landed property, and wage-labour would have been converted into a class analysis. Because the volume — and Marx's life — ended here, the conversion is never made. What we possess is the specification of the conditions under which it could not be made by the obvious route: the route that reads social classes directly off income streams.

Finally, the fragment's form matters as much as its content. Its textual status as a manuscript, its interruption, and the presence of the editor's note and editorial footnotes mean that the chapter is simultaneously Marx's argument and a document about the incompleteness of Marx's argument. Any rigorous reading must hold both facts at once: we have a decisive negative result — the distributive criterion fails — and an absent positive result, whose absence is itself the most cited lacuna in the Marxist theory of class.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Proposition: Three Property Forms, Three Revenues, Three Classes

The chapter opens with a single sentence that fuses two very different kinds of statement, and the entire subsequent difficulty of the fragment flows from that fusion. The sentence reads: "The owners of mere labour-power, the owners of capital and the landowners, whose respective sources of income are wages, profit and ground-rent — in other words wage-labourers, capitalists and landowners — form the three great classes of modern society based on the capitalist mode of production."

Consider what is doing the work here, clause by clause. The first clause identifies three social positions not by what their occupants do, nor by their status, nor by their culture, but by what they own. This is a deliberate and foundational move: the terminological thread that runs through all three phrases is the word "owners." The wage-labourer is introduced as the "owner of mere labour-power" — a formulation that is, at first glance, paradoxical, since the wage-labourer is the class that owns nothing in the ordinary sense. But Marx's point is precise and economically essential: the wage-labourer is an owner, of exactly one commodity, labour-power, and it is this ownership, and only this ownership, that distinguishes the proletarian as a legal and economic subject. The qualifier "mere" (bloße) carries the whole weight of the difference. It marks the labourer's property as bare — unaccompanied by any other means of production, by any land, by any capital. "Mere labour-power" is labour-power stripped of every complement, the residue that remains to a producer who has been separated from the means of production. The phrase is therefore not descriptive of a natural condition but expressive of a historical process; it presupposes precisely the separation that the chapter's third beat will name as the law of development.

The second and third phrases — "the owners of capital and the landowners" — name their positions by their characteristic property formations. Note the grammatical asymmetry: capital is treated as a possession ("the owners of capital"), while land is treated by way of its possessors ("the landowners"). This is not stylistic carelessness. Landed property is a class before it is a thing: the landowner is the personification of a property relation that historically preceded capitalism, whereas capital is a social relation that constitutes its owners as a class only within the capitalist mode of production. The formulation quietly encodes the historical asymmetry between the two propertied classes — an asymmetry whose consequences become explicit in the discussion of landed property's "independent divorce."

The middle clause — "whose respective sources of income are wages, profit and ground-rent" — introduces the distributional mapping. This is the pivot of the whole chapter, because it establishes a strict one-to-one correspondence: each of the three ownership positions has its own income stream, and each income stream has its own source. The mapping is exhaustive and reciprocal. There is no class without a revenue and no revenue without a class. This neatness is precisely what will be described as "at first sight" convincing, and precisely what will be undone.

The appositive clause — "in other words wage-labourers, capitalists and landowners" — converts the property-holders into the familiar social actors. The phrase "in other words" asserts an identity between "owner of mere labour-power" and "wage-labourer," and so on. This is a substantive claim, not a tautology: it asserts that the legal-economic position of ownership is the social position of class membership. That assertion is the target of the rest of the chapter.

The final clause — "form the three great classes of modern society based on the capitalist mode of production" — makes the structural claim. Two qualifications are essential here. First, the classes are "great": they are not merely three among many, but the three that are formative, the load-bearing categories of the structure. Any additional class or stratum must be understood in relation to them. Second, they belong to "modern society based on the capitalist mode of production." The "modern" and the "mode of production" qualifiers together impose a strict historical and structural limitation: this is not a general theory of social stratification, but an account of the class structure specific to one mode of production. Feudalism had its own classes; classical antiquity had its own; the three-part schema of wage-labourer, capitalist, and landowner is not a universal sociology but a historical, structurally generated result. This means that whatever "class" turns out to mean, it must be a relational and historically specific concept, not a permanent category of social description.

The opening sentence thus performs two operations simultaneously, and the tension between them is the chapter's engine: it describes classes distributionally (three property forms → three revenues) while asserting a structural claim (these three are the formative elements of a determinate kind of society). The question the fragment will pose is whether the distributional description can bear the structural weight placed on it. It cannot. But it takes the entire chapter to show why not.

The English Case and the Impurity of Class Articulation

The second movement of the chapter is a geographical and methodological qualification. "It is undeniably in England that this modern society and its economic articulation is most widely and most classically developed." Two terms deserve unpacking. "Economic articulation" designates the way the economic body is jointed — the way its parts are connected into an organized whole. The word choice suggests a structural, quasi-anatomical concern: what matters is not merely the presence of classes but the manner in which they are hinged together. England, as the site of the most advanced capitalist development, is the country where the joints are most clearly formed. "Most classically developed" introduces a further, stronger claim: England approximates the classical form, meaning that it approaches the conceptual type. The concept of capitalist society is not an abstraction imposed from outside; it is a reality that is at least approachable in its most advanced instance.

Then comes the decisive caveat, and it is more radical than it first appears: "Even here, though, this class articulation does not emerge in pure form. Here, too, middle and transitional levels always conceal the boundaries (although incomparably less so in the countryside than in the towns)."

This passage performs a crucial methodological operation. It denies that the conceptual purity of the three-class structure is ever empirically given. Pure class articulation is not an observed fact but a theoretical construction whose empirical realization is always obstructed. This is not an admission of failure; it is a statement about how social theory relates to its object. If the boundaries of classes were directly visible, no theory would be required to draw them. The fact that "middle and transitional levels" conceal them is what makes theoretical work necessary — and it is also what makes the concept of class a matter of judgment rather than measurement.

The word "always" is emphatic and should not be softened. It does not say that middle strata are remnants of a pre-capitalist past destined to disappear; it says that the concealment of boundaries is a permanent feature of the articulation. This explains why the chapter can speak of "middle and transitional levels" without suggesting that they are historically transient. Transitionality is structural, not merely chronological.

The parenthetical qualification — "although incomparably less so in the countryside than in the towns" — contains a claim that runs against most intuitive expectations and repays close scrutiny. One might assume that the countryside, with its small proprietors, its tenants, its farm servants, its village trades, is the most heterogeneous and least classically articulated region of a capitalist society, while the industrial town presents a stark polarization of employer and employee. Marx asserts the reverse: the boundaries are less concealed in the countryside. The only way to make sense of this within the fragment's own logic is by reference to the developmental law that follows and to the List quotation appended to it. In the English countryside, capitalist development had produced a particularly clean three-term structure: a landowner who owns but does not farm, a capitalist tenant farmer who farms but does not own, and an agricultural wage-labourer who neither owns nor commands. This is the triad in its purest agricultural form. In the towns, by contrast, the division of social labour produces an enormous proliferation of intermediate positions, functions, and trades — precisely what the third movement of the chapter will call the "infinite fragmentation of interests and positions." The countryside, having been reorganized by capitalism into three positions, exhibits a clean structural skeleton; the towns, being the site of the most intense division of labour, exhibit a dense mesh of specializations that obscures the same skeleton.

The methodological lesson is important: the purity of a class structure is not a function of its simplicity or backwardness but of the degree to which it has been subjected to the specific organizing process of capitalist development. The countryside, in Marx's England, is more classically capitalist in its articulation than the town.

The Law of Development: Divorce, Concentration, and the Double Transformation

The chapter's third movement supplies the genetic account. It begins with a reference backward: "We have seen how it is the constant tendency and law of development of the capitalist mode of production..." The phrase "We have seen" establishes that the account to follow is a summary of results established elsewhere in the volume rather than a new derivation. But as a summary it is extraordinarily compressed, and it must be unpacked clause by clause, because it is the fragment's only positive analytical content and therefore the only textual basis on which the missing answer to the class question can be reconstructed.

The first component of the law: "to divorce the means of production ever more from labour." The verb "divorce" (scheiden, trennen) denotes a separation of what had previously been joined — the producer from the instruments and materials of production. This separation is the defining historical rupture of capitalist development. The phrase "ever more" indicates that this is not an event but a process with a direction: the separation is progressive, cumulative, never complete, always intensifying. Crucially, the "divorce" is phrased in terms of means of production and labour, not in terms of wealth and poverty. It is a statement about the relation of producers to productive resources, not about the distribution of consumption. This distinction will become decisive for the class question.

The second component: "and to concentrate the fragmented means of production more and more into large groups." This is the other face of the same process. The means of production, once scattered among many small producers, aggregate into large, concentrated complexes. Note that the fragmentation of the producers and the concentration of the means are not two independent trends but a single movement described from two sides: as producers lose their grip on the instruments of production, those instruments collect into collective, large-scale units of command. The phrase "large groups" is deliberately non-committal about legal form; what matters is the scale and concentration of productive power as such.

The third component, which draws the definitional conclusion: "i.e. to transform labour into wage-labour and the means of production into capital." The "i.e." signals that this clause is not a further consequence but a restatement, at the level of social form, of what has just been described. And the restatement is a definition. Wage-labour is the form that labour takes when it has been divorced from the means of production: labour that must be sold in order to be exercised. Capital is the form that the means of production take when they confront labour as alien property: means of production that have become the vehicle for appropriating the product of others' labour. This is why the chapter insists on the phrase "owners of capital" rather than "owners of machines" or "owners of wealth." Capital is not a class of things; it is a social relation between persons expressed in the ownership of things. The sentence tells us that the two terms — wage-labour and capital — are correlative: neither can exist without the other, and both are constituted by the same act of separation. This is the fragment's most important positive contribution to the class question, even though the class question has not yet been posed: it establishes the relational, production-based character of the two principal classes.

The fourth component introduces the third class and gives it an analysis of its own: "And this tendency also corresponds to the independent divorce of all landed property from capital and labour." The word "independent" is doing serious work. It could mean either "separate, i.e., distinct from the capital–labour divorce," or "the divorce of landed property from both capital and labour, taking place as its own process." Either reading yields the same substantive point: landed property's separation is not a mere by-product of the capital–labour split. It is a sui generis transformation with its own logic, affecting both of the other classes: landed property is divorced from capital (the landowner ceases to be an operating capitalist farmer and becomes a pure rentier) and from labour (the landowner ceases to be a personal commander of agricultural workers, and the workers confront the land as alien property). The result is that the landowner is constituted as a class whose only economic function is the appropriation of a tribute — ground-rent — rather than the organization of production. The landowner's "independence" is thus both his separation from production and his distinctiveness as a structural position.

The fifth component states the outcome of that divergence: "or the transformation of all landed property into the form of landed property corresponding to the capitalist mode of production." The word "form" is emphatic, and so is the verb "transformation." Landed property is not abolished by capitalism; it is re-formed. Feudal, seigneurial, peasant, and communal forms of property in land give way to a distinctively capitalist form. That form is precisely the one that makes ground-rent — rather than the landlord's own farming profit — the landlord's characteristic revenue. In other words, the third "great class" of the opening sentence is not a survival from the past but a product of capitalist development. It is the class of those whose property has been transformed so that it can function as a claim on surplus value generated by others.

This sequence of five components constitutes the fragment's positive theory of how the three classes come into being. The remarkable fact is that nowhere in this sequence does income figure. The constituting process is described entirely in terms of the separation of producers from productive resources, the concentration of those resources, and the consequent reformation of economic forms. Only after the account of the law is complete does the chapter turn to revenues and to the question of class. The architecture of the fragment thus silently testifies to where Marx locates the constitutive principle — and it is not the revenue.

The List Footnote: Owner-Management as an Index of Backwardness

Attached to the phrase "the independent divorce of all landed property from capital and labour" is a footnote of considerable analytical interest, quoting Friedrich List's Die Ackerverfassung, die Zwergwirthschaft und die Auswanderung (1842): "The predominance of owner-management on large estates simply indicates inadequate civilization, means of communication, local industry and wealthy cities. This is why we find it all over Russia, Poland, Hungary and Mecklenburg. It was formerly predominant in England too, but with the arrival of trade and industry these estates were broken up into middle-sized farms and leased out."

The footnote functions as historical corroboration of the "independent divorce" thesis, but its content deserves careful exposition because it supplies a criterion that the chapter's main text leaves implicit. List's argument is that owner-management — the situation in which the owner of a large estate also manages and farms it — is a symptom, not a virtue. The four conditions listed as its cause are all marks of an undeveloped division of labour and an incomplete urban-industrial articulation: "inadequate civilization," in the specific sense of cultural and institutional development; "means of communication," i.e., transport infrastructure connecting farm regions to markets; "local industry," i.e., non-agricultural production in the vicinity; and "wealthy cities," i.e., concentrated urban demand able to absorb agricultural output.

This reverses a conventional hierarchy of value. In much agrarian ideology, the resident gentleman-farmer who personally oversees his acres is presented as the ideal of landed responsibility; the absentee rentier is the object of moral complaint. List, and Marx by adoption, treats the personal manager as the index of an economy too poorly developed to support a division of functions. This is not a moral judgment but a structural diagnostic: where markets, communications, and cities are absent, land must be consolidated under a single managing proprietor; where they are present, land can be let in farms to capitalistically organizing tenants.

The geographical enumeration — Russia, Poland, Hungary, Mecklenburg — names regions of the European east and northeast, characterized in the nineteenth century by large-scale estate agriculture with unfree or semi-free labour and by weak urban-industrial development. The English contrast — "It was formerly predominant in England too" — is instructive for two reasons. First, it historicizes the English case: owner-management, now largely superseded in England, was once the norm. The structure the chapter theorizes is not a natural consequence of large landownership but a historical achievement of capitalist development in a favourable environment. Second, the phrase "with the arrival of trade and industry" identifies the causal mechanism: commerce and manufacturing, not legal reform or ideology, dissolved the internally managed estate. The dissolution takes the specific form of "broken up into middle-sized farms and leased out."

These last two points are essential for the chapter's argument. "Broken up" and "leased out" describe two moments of the transformation that constitutes the third great class. The estate's scale is subdivided into farming units, and those units are handed over to tenants — a class of capitalist farmers, occupying the intermediate position between the landowner and the labourers. The result is the exact three-term structure that the chapter describes as "more classically" articulated in the countryside than in the towns: the landowner who owns and receives rent, the tenant farmer who farms and takes profit, the agricultural labourer who works and earns wages. The footnote thus supplies, in miniature, the empirical history of how the third of the three great classes acquired its characteristic form: by being driven out of production, and thereby constituted as the recipient of ground-rent alone.

It is worth noticing the ironic relation between List's politics and his evidence. The editorial gloss appended to the footnote explains who List was: "the most important German economist of the first half of the nineteenth century, closely represented the demands of the embryonic industrial bourgeoisie in Germany and is particularly remembered for his forceful arguments for protective tariffs." This note performs several services. It identifies List as a partisan — a spokesman of an industrial class — rather than a disinterested observer, and it identifies his program: tariffs, and by implication the development of domestic industry, trade, and urban markets. The irony is that the very program List advocated — the promotion of industry and commerce — is the dynamic whose effect he himself describes as dissolving owner-management and transforming estates into leased farms. List the advocate of industrialization and List the observer of agrarian transformation are the same figure, and Marx uses one against the political presuppositions of the other while accepting his factual testimony. The footnote is thus also a rhetorical lesson in how to use a witness: rely on what the witness saw, not on what he wanted.

Finally, the footnote's apparatus — numbered 58, with an asterisked editorial gloss — calls attention to the text's status as a posthumously edited manuscript. This is not a trivial matter. The chapter's footnotes are the editor's, the interruptions are the editor's, and the boundary between Marx's text and the editorial frame is itself part of the object we are reading. The List gloss tells us how the volume's editor expected readers to situate a nineteenth-century German economist; the closing note, "(At this point the manuscript breaks off. — F. E.)", will tell us that what we have is a fragment. The chapter is thus doubly mediated: by its incompleteness and by the editorial apparatus that frames the incompleteness.

The Pivot: "What Makes a Class?" and the Priority of the Formative Question

With the developmental account complete, the chapter turns abruptly to its central question: "The question to be answered next is: 'What makes a class?'" Every word of this short sentence repays attention. "The question to be answered next" presents the question not as an afterthought but as an item on a program: it is the next in a sequence of systematically ordered inquiries. The chapter is therefore the opening of a section, not a stray reflection. And the question itself — "What makes a class?" — is a question about constitution, not about description. It is not "How do we identify a class?" but "What produces a class?" The verb "makes" asks for a generative principle.

Immediately, Marx adds a second formulation and asserts a relation of derivation between the two: "and this arises automatically from answering another question: 'What makes wage-labourers, capitalists and landowners the formative elements of the three great social classes?'"

The claim that the first question "arises automatically" from the second is a compressed statement of methodological priority. Marx is saying that the general question about class is not prior to the specific question about these three groups; rather, the general concept is to be read off the explanation of why these particular groups are formative. This is a substantively important position. It implies that "class" is not a master category into which the three great groups are inserted, but a name for whatever it is that constitutes those groups as the structure-bearing elements of capitalist society. There is no transhistorical definition to be applied; there is a specific historical formation to be explained, and the general concept will be the conceptual form of that explanation.

The word "formative" is equally significant. The German term behind it designates what gives form or shape. The three great groups are not merely the largest or the most numerous; they are the elements that form the structure — the elements whose relations constitute the shape of society as a whole. This is why the criterion cannot be demographic, nor can it be a mere matter of size or wealth. To be a "formative element" is to occupy a position in the constitution of the social whole. When the chapter later criticizes the revenue-source criterion, the standard of judgment it invokes is precisely this one: only a criterion that could explain formative status would be adequate, and the revenue criterion cannot.

There is also a potential circularity in the passage, which is worth flagging as a nuance. The second question — what makes these three groups the formative classes? — appears to presume the very classification it is meant to explain. The opening sentence already asserted that they are the three great classes. How can the explanation of that assertion avoid circularity? The answer must lie in the developmental account already given: the three groups have been shown to arise from a single process of separation and reformation, and it is in that genesis, rather than in their classification, that the explanation must be sought. But the fragment does not complete the circle, and a careful reader should note that the "automatic" derivation Marx promises is never delivered.

The Answer "At First Sight": The Identity of Revenues and Revenue Sources

Marx next states the lay answer with full seriousness: "At first sight, the identity of revenues and revenue sources." He then unfolds it: "For these are three great social groups whose components, the individuals forming them, live respectively from wages, profit and ground-rent, from the valorization of their labour-power, capital and landed property."

Two levels are distinguished. The first is the level of livelihood: the individuals who compose each group "live respectively from" one of the three revenues. This is the everyday criterion — how do you make your living? The second level is that of sources: wages, profit, and ground-rent are described as returns from "the valorization of their labour-power, capital and landed property." The term "valorization" (Verwertung) is the technical term of the volume's analysis: the process by which value is put to work so as to yield a surplus, an increment. Thus labour-power is valorized when it is set to work and reproduces more value than it costs; capital is valorized when the advance returns with a profit; landed property is valorized when it yields ground-rent as the price of access to the soil.

The elegance of this schema cannot be overstated, and Marx's willingness to state it fully, without immediate qualification, is a mark of its explanatory attractiveness. Three property forms correspond to three valorization processes, which correspond to three revenues, which correspond to three social groups. It is a complete, reciprocal, and empirically applicable classification. Moreover, it is precisely the schema that the entire volume has been laboriously establishing. The reader who has just finished the analysis of wages, profit, and rent has been prepared to accept this schema as the natural summary of the whole work. The phrase "at first sight" is thus more than a conversational hedge: it marks the answer as belonging to the order of appearance — the level of the phenomenal forms in which the social relations of production present themselves to immediate observation.

This is a critical point for interpretation. Marx does not dismiss the distributive schema as false. He treats it as the surface form of the relations, a form that is not arbitrary but generated by the underlying structure. The three revenues do exist, and they do correspond to three propertied groups. What is at issue is whether this correspondence can serve as the definition of class — whether the appearance can be the concept.

The stage is now set for the reversal, and the reversal will not be based on external evidence but purely on the internal logic of the criterion: if the criterion is the identity of revenue with source, then the criterion must apply consistently, and consistency will destroy the triad.

The Counterexample: Doctors and Government Officials

"From this point of view, however, doctors and government officials would also form two classes, as they belong to two distinct social groups, the revenue of each group's members flowing from its own source."

The argument is a reductio ad absurdum, executed with surgical economy. The phrase "From this point of view" explicitly relativizes the argument to the criterion under examination: it is not Marx's own standpoint, but the standpoint of the revenue-identity criterion, that is being followed to its consequences. The consequence is immediate: doctors and government officials satisfy every element of the criterion. They constitute two distinct social groups; each group's members derive their income from a source peculiar to it; and therefore, by the criterion, they would be classes.

The force of the counterexample lies in the fact that it is so obviously not what anyone means by a class. Doctors and officials are professions or functions; the very suggestion that they constitute two of the great classes of modern society exposes the criterion as absurd. But the argument is not merely rhetorical. Consider what the criterion actually does. It equates classes with categories of income recipients. Any category of income recipients, however defined and however constituted, would then be a class. The class structure of society becomes a catalogue of income types — which is precisely the kind of accounting classification the criterion was apparently meant to transcend.

The choice of examples is also analytically pointed. Doctors and officials are not random; they are the two most conspicuous cases of income derived from labour services rendered to others (doctors) and from state revenue (officials). Both are structurally distinct from the three great classes, and both are, in the terminology the chapter has already used, part of the "middle and transitional levels" that conceal the boundaries. That the revenue criterion converts these middle levels into full-blown classes is a further indictment: the criterion not only overgenerates classes, it overgenerates them precisely at the point where the analysis of capitalist society most needs a way of distinguishing middle levels from formative classes. The criterion makes the problem of the middle strata unsolvable by treating them as coequal with the principal classes.

There is a deeper point in the counterexample that the text does not spell out but that its own logic presses upon the reader. The income of the officials — the "source" that flows "from its own source" — is, in the schema of the volume, not a primary revenue at all. It is a share of the surplus value already appropriated by others, redistributed through taxation. The text's characterization of official revenue as flowing from "its own source" quietly presupposes that an income stream can be treated as a source in its own right. Whether a revenue is a primary claim on production or a derived one is precisely the question that a rigorous class analysis would have to settle before the revenue criterion could be applied; and that question cannot be settled at the level of revenue at all. It requires the analysis of production and of the distribution of surplus value. So even in failing, the counterexample reveals what an adequate criterion would have to be able to do: distinguish primary from derived revenues, structural from incidental income streams, the population of functionaries from the formative classes. The revenue criterion cannot do any of this.

Finally, note the structure of the argument. Marx does not say that doctors and officials have similar revenues to workers or that they are really workers. He says the criterion, taken seriously, would have to call them classes. The argument is purely about the criterion's implications, not about the social reality of the groups in question. This is the hallmark of a properly philosophical refutation: it shows that a principle cannot be held without embracing consequences that its own adherents reject.

Infinite Fragmentation: Vineyards, Fields, Forests, Mines, Fisheries

The second and more radical criticism follows immediately: "The same would hold true for the infinite fragmentation of interests and positions into which the division of social labour splits not only workers but also capitalists and landowners — the latter, for instance, into vineyard-owners, field-owners, forest-owners, mine-owners, fishery-owners, etc."

Where the first counterexample attacked the criterion from outside the three great classes — showing that it overgenerates classes among non-class groups — the second attacks it from within, showing that it dissolves the classes it is supposed to define. If the criterion is "the identity of revenue and revenue source," then it must be applied inside each of the three great groups as well, for the division of social labour has differentiated those groups internally, giving rise to distinct interests and positions, and in many cases to distinct revenue streams.

The phrase "the infinite fragmentation of interests and positions" identifies two dimensions of division: "interests," i.e., the material stakes and aims of the groups in question; and "positions," i.e., their locations in the structure of social labour. Both are products of "the division of social labour." This is an important conceptual link, because it identifies the same mechanism that generates the abundance of urban middle levels mentioned earlier. The division of labour does not divide only labour; it divides all social positions. And the text emphasizes that it "splits not only workers but also capitalists and landowners." The qualification "not only workers" is essential. It answers any objection that fragmentation is the fate of the working class alone, and it generalizes the analytical problem: no class escapes the differentiating pressure of the division of labour.

The illustrative list is drawn entirely from the landowning class, and its items repay attention: "vineyard-owners, field-owners, forest-owners, mine-owners, fishery-owners." Several things are being communicated. First, the list reveals the enormous material breadth of what "landed property" designates. It is not merely agricultural land but vineyards, woodland, mineral deposits, and fisheries — a diversity of natural resources whose modes of valorization, markets, and technological requirements differ radically. Second, the list shows that even within the supposed unity of "landowners," there are distinct revenue streams and distinct social functions: viticulture, arable farming, forestry, mining, and fishing are different industries with different rhythms and different relations to the market. Third, the sequence moves from the agricultural to the extractive and maritime, demonstrating that the criterion would fragment the landlord class far beyond a simple agricultural/urban distinction. Fourth, the list ends with "etc.," a small but powerful rhetorical device: the enumeration is deliberately left open, enacting the infinitude of the fragmentation it describes. The "etc." is the criterion's death warrant, not a stylistic convenience.

The consequence for the concept of class is devastating. If every distinct interest and position generated by the division of social labour counts as a distinct class, then there are not three classes but an indefinite number. The concept of class becomes an instrument of infinite subdivision, and loses precisely what it was introduced to supply: a small set of structural categories adequate to the analysis of a determinate form of society. The criterion does not merely fail to define class; it abolishes the possibility of the concept.

There is a further, subtler implication. The passage makes the "infinite fragmentation" a property of all the great classes, not merely the landowners. The examples are drawn only from landowners because they are the most vivid, but the principle holds across the board. Consequently, any answer to the class question that relies on the internal homogeneity of a class must fail, because no such homogeneity exists. This raises the standard the positive answer would have to meet: it would have to explain how a class can be one despite internal differentiation — how vineyard-owners and fishery-owners and mine-owners can belong to a single class. That question, which the fragment poses by implication, can only be answered by identifying something that unites them despite the diversity of their interests and revenues: their common position in relation to the fundamental social relation of production — in relation to labour on the one side and to capital on the other. The fragment does not say this; but the critique it mounts is unintelligible unless some such unifying principle is available, and the only candidate the chapter's own preceding analysis supplies is the relation to the means of production and to the labour that works with them.

The Break-Off: The Fragment's Silence and Its Interpretive Weight

The chapter ends: "(At this point the manuscript breaks off. — F. E.)" The initials identify Friedrich Engels, the editor of the posthumous volumes. This parenthetical note is the last thing on the page, and it transforms the chapter from an argument into a document of an interrupted argument.

Everything that follows in the interpretation of this chapter must respect the break. We possess, in the surviving fragment, a negative result of the greatest importance and a set of positive materials — the developmental law, the relational definitions of wage-labour and capital, the reformation of landed property — that were evidently intended to be used in the constructive answer. We do not possess the constructive answer. Any reconstruction of the answer must be flagged as reconstruction, not as the text.

What is nevertheless secure from the fragment itself can be stated with precision. First, the distributive criterion of class is refuted, by two independent and mutually reinforcing arguments: it overgenerates classes among non-class groups, and it fragments the three great classes into an indefinite plurality. Second, the question of class has been given a specific shape: it asks after the formative elements of a determinate mode of production, and it expects the general concept of class to be derived from the specific explanation, not applied to it. Third, the chapter has, prior to posing the question, sketched a process — the separation of producers from the means of production, the concentration of those means, and the independent reformation of landed property — which constitutes precisely the three property forms named in the opening sentence. Fourth, and most tellingly, that process is described in a vocabulary that contains no reference to income whatsoever. The fragment's own architecture therefore indicates the direction of the missing answer.

There is an uncomfortable further possibility which must be acknowledged as a possibility and not asserted as a fact: that the chapter broke off not merely by accident but at a point of genuine theoretical difficulty. The problem of defining class at the level of production relations confronts the very "middle and transitional levels" that the chapter's second movement identified as always concealing the boundaries. If class membership is determined by relation to the means of production, what is the class position of the doctor, the official, the small shopkeeper, the self-employed professional? The fragment's critique shows that income cannot answer this; whether the intended production-based answer could have answered it definitively is something the surviving text does not show. The chapter's silence on this front is part of what it transmits to its readers.

Finally, the break-off gives the chapter a peculiar status in the argument of the volume. It is the point at which the economic analysis of the capitalist mode of production, complete in all its essential determinations, was to have been converted into an analysis of the classes that live within it. That conversion never occurs. What remains is an enacted demonstration that the conversion cannot be made by the easy route — that the theory of the three revenues, however complete, does not by itself yield a theory of the three classes. Anyone who wishes to move from the volume's economic analysis to a class analysis must therefore do work that the text does not do, and must do it under the constraint that the fragment imposes: the work cannot begin from income.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The deepest lesson of Chapter 52 is a lesson about the difference between the appearance and the constitution of social structure. The chapter begins with a schema — three properties, three revenues, three classes — that is at once empirically serviceable, theoretically well-prepared by the preceding analysis of the volume, and entirely inadequate as a definition. What makes it inadequate is not error but superficiality: it describes the way a class society distributes its product rather than the way it produces its positions. Income is what a class receives; it is not what a class is. And because distribution is downstream of production, a criterion drawn from distribution will always cut the social body along lines that cross the seams of its actual constitution — producing classes where there are only trades, and multiplying classes where there is one.

From this the fragment yields a series of connected principles.

First: the priority of production over distribution as the axis of class analysis. The chapter's own developmental passage establishes that classes are constituted by relations to the means of production: labour becomes wage-labour and means of production become capital in the same movement that divorces producers from the instruments of their work. The revenues — wages, profit, rent — are the forms in which those positions receive shares of the product. But the positions exist, and are what they are, prior to and independently of the revenues they receive. To define the class by the revenue is to mistake the effect for the cause. This is the systematic content of Marx's rejection of the "identity of revenues and revenue sources" as a definition of class.

Second: class is a relational and historically specific category, not a gradational one. The opening sentence's insistence that these are the classes of "modern society based on the capitalist mode of production" excludes any reading of the triad as a transhistorical scheme of stratification. Wage-labour and capital are correlative forms produced by a single historical process of separation and concentration; landed property in its classically capitalist form is likewise a produced, transformed relation. This relationality has an important consequence: the classes are not independently definable groups that happen to interact; they are mutually constituted positions whose very identities depend on their terms. This is why the internal fragmentation of a class — vineyard-owners and mine-owners and fishery-owners — is not fatal to its unity in the way the fragmentation argument would suggest if the class were merely an aggregate defined by income. A relational class can be internally differentiated in its interests and still be one, because its unity resides in its common position within the fundamental relation, not in the homogeneity of its revenues.

Third: the empirical inexistence of pure class structures is a permanent theoretical constraint, not a temporary imperfection. The chapter's "middle and transitional levels" that "always conceal the boundaries" are not residues awaiting the completion of capitalist development. They are the consequence of the division of social labour, which differentiates all positions without exception. Class theory must therefore always be an exercise in drawing boundaries that are not given in experience — an interpretive and structural operation, not a descriptive catalogue. This also supplies one of the fragment's most striking analytic results: the countryside, in an advanced capitalist country, may exhibit a cleaner class articulation than the town, because the reorganization of agriculture into owner, tenant capitalist, and labourer has produced a three-term structure, while the town's division of labour produces a dense mesh of intermediate positions. Purity of class structure is a function of capitalist reorganization, not of backwardness.

Fourth: concepts must be tested by their consequences, not by their plausibility. The chapter's refutation of the revenue criterion proceeds entirely by extrapolation. The criterion is not rejected because it fails to fit the facts — it fits the three great classes admirably. It is rejected because when carried through consistently it generates consequences that destroy its own purpose. This is a model of critical method: take the criterion seriously, follow it to its limits, and observe whether it can survive the journey. It cannot. Doctors and officials expose its overgeneration; vineyards and fisheries expose its self-dissolution. The lesson generalizes to every attempt to define fundamental social categories by their surface effects.

Fifth: the chapter's incompleteness is itself a substantive finding. The interruption of the manuscript at the exact moment between the demolition of the false answer and the construction of the true one means that the text transmits a fully formed question and only the materials of an answer. This has consequences for readers. It forbids the easy attribution to Marx of a completed theory of class derived from this chapter alone, and it requires anyone who would complete the fragment to work from the relational and production-based materials the chapter itself provides — the divorce of labour from the means of production, the correlative constitution of wage-labour and capital, the transformation of landed property into its capitalist form. The fragment is thus not a summary at the end of the volume but an assignment to its readers.

The broader implications are considerable. Politically, the chapter implies that the interests and alignments of social groups cannot be read off income distributions; the "middle levels" that dominate the visible surface of capitalist society are not, for that reason, self-standing classes, and their political behaviour cannot be inferred from their pay. Theoretically, the chapter sets an exacting standard for any theory of social structure: it must specify a constitutive mechanism, not a classificatory schema; it must be able to distinguish the formative from the merely statistical; and it must explain unity in the face of internal differentiation. Philosophically, finally, the fragment is a lesson about the relation between essence and appearance in social analysis. The three revenues are not illusions. They are real, and their correspondence to three groups of recipients is real. What is illusory is the belief that the real surface exhausts the real structure — that the face society shows to its members is the whole of what constitutes it. Marx's last chapter on class is thus, at bottom, a defence of the necessity of theory: the most convincing answer to the question "what makes a class?" is the one that, having been stated in full and given every advantage, cannot survive the company of a doctor and a government official, a vineyard and a fishery.
