Volume 3 • Chapter 9: Formation of a General Rate of Profit (Average Rate of Profit)

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Core Thesis

Chapter 9 constitutes one of the most decisive theoretical pivots in all of Marx's Capital — indeed, one of the decisive pivots in the entire history of economic thought. Its central thesis is that, under developed capitalist production, commodities are not sold at their values but at prices of production, and that these prices of production arise from the formation of a general (average) rate of profit. The chapter demonstrates that the different rates of profit initially prevailing in different spheres of production — rates determined by the differing organic compositions of the capitals there invested — are leveled out by competition into a single general rate of profit. Once this general rate exists, the profit that each individual capital appropriates is no longer the surplus-value it itself produces, but rather its aliquot share of the total social surplus-value, distributed in proportion to the size of the capital advanced. The immediate consequence is that the price of production of a commodity equals its cost price plus the average profit, and this price systematically diverges from the commodity's value — except where a sphere's capital composition happens to coincide with the social average.

The chapter is thus, at one and the same time, (a) a technical-mechanical derivation of how the average rate of profit is formed, (b) a resolution of the apparent contradiction between the labor theory of value and the equalization of profit rates under competition, and (c) a profound epistemological argument about how the appearance of the capitalist process (profit arising uniformly from the total capital) systematically conceals its essence (surplus-value arising exclusively from living labor). The chapter is simultaneously a work of economic science and a work of ideology critique.

The Central Problem

The problem the chapter confronts is precisely the "great contradiction" of the labor theory of value: if value is determined by labor-time, and if the surplus-value produced in each sphere depends on the quantity of living labor employed, then spheres with different organic compositions (different ratios of constant to variable capital) should have systematically different rates of profit. But under capitalist competition, capitals of equal size tend to earn equal profits regardless of their composition. How can both be true? Marx's answer — and this is the chapter's most important contribution — is that they are both true, but at different levels of abstraction. Value is the substance; price of production is its transformed form. The equality of profit rates is achieved precisely through the redistribution of the total social surplus-value pooled from all spheres, and this redistribution makes prices of production diverge from values in a systematic, mutually cancelling way. The total sum of prices of production equals the total sum of values; total surplus-value equals total profit. Value is not abolished but transformed — its determination "is now removed from view."

Intellectual Objective

The chapter's objective is threefold. First, to construct the average rate of profit from the differing organic compositions of capitals in different spheres, showing that it is nothing mystical but a mathematical resultant of these compositions weighted by the sizes of capitals invested. Second, to define the key categories — average profit, price of production, cost price in its modified significance — with precision, deriving the formula price of production = k + kp' (where k is cost price and p' is the general rate of profit). Third, and crucially, to draw out the ideological consequences: once a general rate of profit is established, profit and surplus-value diverge not merely in form but in magnitude; the origin of profit in the exploitation of living labor becomes entirely unrecognizable to the capitalist, who is "imprisoned in the competitive struggle"; and the determination of value by labor-time now "percolates through only in this crudified and naive form." The chapter thus sets the stage for the rest of Volume 3 (the falling rate of profit, merchant's capital, interest, rent) and for Volume 4 (Theories of Surplus-Value), where Marx will show that "all economics up till now" has either violently abstracted from these distinctions or abandoned any scientific foundation.

Fitting into the Broader Framework

This chapter is the hinge of Volume 3. In Volume 1, Marx developed the theory of value and surplus-value at the level of capital in general — assuming commodities sell at their values. In Volume 2, he developed the circuits and turnover of capital, still abstracting from the competitive formation of prices. Volume 3's "first Part" (Chapters 1–8) had already introduced the transformation of surplus-value into profit, and of the rate of surplus-value into the rate of profit — a change of form in which profit still equaled surplus-value in magnitude. Chapter 9 now introduces the quantitative divergence: with the general rate of profit, the profit a capital earns differs genuinely from the surplus-value it produces. This is the "second transformation" (after surplus-value → profit) that completes the move from essence to appearance, laying the groundwork for the analysis of commercial profit, interest, rent, and the tendency of the rate of profit to fall — all of which operate on the terrain of prices of production, not values.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Organic Composition of Capital and its Two Determinants

Marx opens by restating the determinants of the organic composition of capital — a concept central to everything that follows. At any given time, this composition depends on two factors: first, the technical proportion between labour-power and the means of production applied (how much machinery, raw material, etc., is set in motion by a given quantity of living labor); and second, the price of those means of production. This dual determination is crucial. The technical composition (a physical ratio) becomes a value composition only when it is weighted by the prices (values) of the elements involved. Marx's "organic composition" is precisely this unity of the technical and the value composition — the value composition insofar as it reflects the technical composition.

For analytical tractability, Marx insists this composition must be considered in percentage terms. He expresses a capital consisting of four-fifths constant and one-fifth variable capital by the formula 80c + 20v. He then assumes, "for the sake of comparison," an unchanged rate of surplus-value of 100 percent — noting "any rate will do." This assumption is a methodological device: by holding the rate of exploitation constant, Marx isolates the effect of varying organic composition on the rate of profit. The capital of 80c + 20v then yields a surplus-value of 20, which makes a rate of profit of 20 percent on the total capital (since profit is calculated on the total capital advanced, c + v = 100, not on v alone).

Marx then makes two simplifying assumptions whose immateriality he stresses. First, he brackets the question of fixed capital — the actual value of the product depends on how large the fixed portion of constant capital is and how much of it enters the product as depreciation. But "since this fact is completely immaterial as far as the rate of profit is concerned," he assumes all constant capital enters wholly into the annual product. Second, he assumes that capitals in different spheres realize the same amount of surplus-value in proportion to their variable components, and he ignores differences arising from variations in turnover time, promising to deal with this later. This bracketing of turnover is important: as we shall see, turnover time is later restored as a determinant of profit rates in different spheres.

The Five-Sphere Illustration: Setting Up Divergent Profit Rates

With these assumptions in place, Marx presents his foundational table of five different spheres of production, each with a different organic composition of capital invested in it. (The table appears across pages 1–2 of the text.) The five capitals are:

Capital Composition Rate of Surplus-Value Surplus-Value Value of Product Rate of Profit
I 80c + 20v 100% 20 120 20%
II 70c + 30v 100% 30 130 30%
III 60c + 40v 100% 40 140 40%
IV 85c + 15v 100% 15 115 15%
V 95c + 5v 100% 5 105 5%

The mechanics here are transparent: with a 100 percent rate of surplus-value, surplus-value equals the variable capital (v), so surplus-value is 20, 30, 40, 15, and 5 respectively. The value of the product is c + v + s (assuming all c enters the product): 80 + 20 + 20 = 120, and so on. The rate of profit is s divided by the total capital (c + v = 100 in each case), yielding 20%, 30%, 40%, 15%, and 5%. The result is "very different rates of profit in different spheres of production with a uniform exploitation of labour, rates which correspond to the differing organic composition of the capitals involved." This is the essential problem: equal exploitation, unequal profit rates — because profit is measured against total capital while surplus-value is produced only by the variable part.

Note the crucial counter-intuitive feature Marx is highlighting: the higher the organic composition (the more constant relative to variable capital, i.e., capital III with 60c + 40v has the lowest composition and the highest profit rate), the lower the rate of profit. Capital V (95c + 5v), the most "advanced" or mechanized, has the lowest profit rate (5%). The sphere with the lowest composition (III, 60c + 40v) has the highest (40%). This sets up the illusion — which Marx will destroy — that living labor is not the source of profit, since more dead labor seems to yield more profit.

The Aggregation as a Single Capital: Forming the Average

Marx now performs the decisive aggregation. The total sum of capitals applied in the five spheres is 500 (five capitals of 100 each). The total surplus-value they produce is 110 (20 + 30 + 40 + 15 + 5). The total value of the commodities they produce is 610 (120 + 130 + 140 + 115 + 105). Now Marx asks us to treat the 500 as one single capital, "with I–V simply forming different portions of it." He offers a vivid analogy: just as a cotton mill has different proportions between variable and constant capital in its various departments (carding, combing, spinning, weaving shops), and "the average proportion has to be calculated for the entire factory," so too the social capital has different compositions in its different branches, and an average must be calculated.

The average composition of the capital of 500 works out to 390c + 110v, or in percentages 78c + 22v. How? Total constant capital = 80 + 70 + 60 + 85 + 95 = 390. Total variable capital = 20 + 30 + 40 + 15 + 5 = 110. So 390c + 110v, or as percentages: 390/500 = 78%, 110/500 = 22%. Treating each of the five capitals of 100 as "simply a fifth of the total capital," its composition becomes this average one of 78c + 22v. The average surplus-value of 22 (110 / 5) accrues to each of these capitals of 100, so the average rate of profit is 22 percent, and the price of each fifth of the total product is 122 (100 cost price + 22 average profit). This is the birth of the general rate of profit: 22 percent.

The Cost Price Complication and its Non-Importance for the Rate of Profit

Here Marx introduces an important technical refinement. He warns: "in order not to arrive at totally incorrect conclusions, we must not take all the cost prices as 100." The problem is that the assumption that the entire constant capital enters the annual product (so that the value of commodity I is 80c + 20v + 20s = 120) "may well be the case in some spheres of production, in certain conditions, but hardly with a ratio between c and v of 4:1." The fixed and circulating components of c differ across capitals, and fixed components depreciate at different rates, adding unequal quantities of value to the product.

But — and this is the key point — "this is immaterial, however, as far as the profit rate is concerned." Marx demonstrates this with a beautiful modus tollens: whether the 80c gives up its value of 80, or 50, or 5 to the annual product, and whether the annual product is accordingly 80c + 20v + 20s = 120, or 50c + 20v + 20s = 90, or 5c + 20v + 20s = 45 — in all these cases the excess of the value of the product over its cost price is 20, and in all these cases this 20 must be calculated on a basis of 100 to arrive at the rate of profit. Hence the profit rate of capital I is always 20 percent. The cost price changes; the profit rate does not. This is because the rate of profit measures surplus-value against the total capital advanced (100), not against the cost price (the capital consumed).

The Full Table with Different c Used Up

To make this "still clearer," Marx presents a second table (pages 3), in which different parts of the constant capital enter the value of the product. Let me reproduce and analyze it carefully:

Capitals Rate of Surplus-Value Surplus-Value Rate of Profit c Used Up Value of Commodities Cost Price
I. 80c + 20v 100% 20 20% 50 90 70
II. 70c + 30v 100% 30 30% 51 111 81
III. 60c + 40v 100% 40 40% 51 131 91
IV. 85c + 15v 100% 15 15% 40 70 55
V. 95c + 5v 100% 5 5% 10 20 15
Total 390c + 110v — 110 110% — — —
Average 78c + 22v — 22 22% — — —

The crucial insight here: the value of commodities is now c (used up) + v + s. For capital I: 50 + 20 + 20 = 90; cost price = c used up + v = 50 + 20 = 70. The surplus-value is still 20, the rate of profit still 20% (20/100). The value of the commodities is lower (90 rather than 120) because less constant capital is transferred, but the profit rate is unchanged. This confirms the earlier point. The total sum of the five capitals remains 500 = 390c + 110v, so the average composition is still 78c + 22v, the average surplus-value still 22.

The Even Distribution of Surplus-Value and the Divergences

Now Marx presents the climactic table (pages 3–4), where the average profit of 22 is evenly distributed across capitals I–V:

Capitals Surplus-Value Value of Commodities Cost Price Price of Commodities Rate of Profit Divergence of Price from Value
I. 80c + 20v 20 90 70 92 22% +2
II. 70c + 30v 30 111 81 103 22% −8
III. 60c + 40v 40 131 91 113 22% −18
IV. 85c + 15v 15 70 55 77 22% +7
V. 95c + 5v 5 20 15 37 22% +17

The mechanics of the "price of commodities" column: price = cost price + 22 (the average profit). For I: 70 + 22 = 92. For II: 81 + 22 = 103. For III: 91 + 22 = 113. For IV: 55 + 22 = 77. For V: 15 + 22 = 37. The "divergence of price from value" is price minus value: 92 − 90 = +2; 103 − 111 = −8; 113 − 131 = −18; 77 − 70 = +7; 37 − 20 = +17.

The profound result: commodities are sold at 2 + 7 + 17 = 26 above their value, and 8 + 18 = 26 below their value, so the divergences cancel each other out. This is the heart of the chapter's resolution. The high-composition capitals (IV, V — more constant capital, less living labor) sell above value because their own surplus-value is too small to give them the 22% average; the low-composition capitals (II, III — more variable capital, more living labor) sell below value because they produce more surplus-value than the average rate requires. The total is preserved: the sum of divergences is zero, so the sum of prices equals the sum of values. And it is only because commodities are sold at these prices that the rates of profit for capitals I–V are equal at 22%, "irrespective of their different organic compositions."

The Definition of Prices of Production

Marx now formally defines his new category: "The prices that arise when the average of the different rates of profit is drawn from the different spheres of production, and this average is added to the cost prices of these different spheres of production, are the prices of production." Their prerequisite is "the existence of a general rate of profit," which "presupposes in turn that the profit rates in each particular sphere of production, taken by itself, are already reduced to their average rates." These particular (sphere-specific) rates are to be developed from the value of the commodity "as shown in the first Part of this volume." Without this value-theoretic derivation, "the general rate of profit (and hence also the production price of the commodity) remains a meaningless and irrational conception." Thus: price of production = cost price + percentage profit added in accordance with the general rate of profit = cost price + average profit.

This passage contains a crucial methodological point against any notion that Marx's prices of production are mere market prices or arbitrary markups. The price of production depends on value; it is a transformed form of value. To abandon the value-theoretic derivation is to render the general rate of profit "meaningless and irrational." Marx is here explicitly defending — in advance — against the charge (later leveled by Böhm-Bawerk) that Volume 3 contradicts Volume 1. There is no contradiction: the price of production is derived from value and presupposes it.

General Rate of Profit from Competition and the Definition of Average Profit

Marx then restates the dynamic: because of the differing organic composition of capitals, "very different amounts of labour are set in motion by capitals of equal size," so "very different amounts of surplus labour are appropriated." The rates of profit "are accordingly originally very different." These different rates "are balanced out by competition to give a general rate of profit which is the average of all these different rates." The profit that falls to a capital of given size according to this general rate — whatever its organic composition — is average profit. And the price of a commodity equal to its cost price plus the part of the annual average profit on the capital applied in its production (not simply capital consumed) that falls to its share "according to its conditions of turnover" is its price of production. Note the care: the average profit is calculated on the capital advanced (used), allocated "according to its conditions of turnover."

The Worked Example with 500 Capital

Marx gives a concrete numerical example. A capital of 500, of which 100 is fixed capital, with 10 percent depreciation of this fixed capital, plus a circulating capital of 400 during one turnover period. Let the average profit for this turnover period be 10 percent. The cost price of the product produced during this turnover = 10c (depreciation) + 400 (c + v circulating) = 410. The price of production = 410 cost price + 50 (10% profit on the capital advanced of 500) = 460. This example elegantly demonstrates that the profit is calculated on the entire capital advanced (500), not merely the capital consumed (410), and that depreciation enters as a distinct component. The distinction between capital consumed and capital advanced is essential.

The Joint-Stock Company Analogy

Marx now deploys one of his most memorable and theoretically fertile analogies. Although capitalists "get back on the sale of their commodities the capital values consumed," they "do not secure the surplus-value and hence profit that is produced in their own sphere." What they secure is only the surplus-value "that falls to the share of each aliquot part of the total social capital, when evenly distributed, from the total social surplus-value or profit produced in a given time by the social capital in all spheres." Every 100 units of capital advanced "draws... the profit that accrues to 100 units... as an nth part of the total capital."

The analogy: "The various different capitals here are in the position of shareholders in a joint-stock company, in which the dividends are evenly distributed for each 100 units, and hence are distinguished, as far as the individual capitalists are concerned, only according to the size of the capital that each of them has put into the common enterprise, according to his relative participation in this common enterprise, according to the number of his shares." This is a profound claim: all social capital functions as a single collective enterprise, and each individual capital is treated as a shareholder entitled to a pro-rata dividend of the total social surplus-value. The capitalist class as a whole is a collective appropriator, and each capitalist's profit depends not on his own exploitation of his workers but on the total exploitation of the working class by capital as a whole. This is the economic basis of class solidarity among capitalists (and of their collective interest in the rate of exploitation as such).

Marx then contrasts: while the cost price is completely governed by the outlay within each sphere, the profit added to it "is governed not by the mass of profit that is produced by this specific capital in its specific sphere of production, but by the mass of profit that falls on average to each capital invested, as an aliquot part of the total social capital." He footnotes this with Cherbuliez (Antoine-Élisée Cherbuliez, 1797–1869, a Swiss economist combining elements of Sismondi and Ricardo, author of Richesse ou pauvreté, Paris, 1841, pp. 70–72, and treated in Theories of Surplus-Value, Part III, Chapter XXIII).

The Reconciliation: Sum of Prices = Sum of Values

Marx immediately restates the individual capitalist's position in light of the analogy: "If a capitalist sells his commodities at their price of production, he withdraws money according to the value of the capital that he consumed in their production and adds a profit to this in proportion to the capital he advanced as a mere aliquot part of the total social capital. His cost prices are specific [to his sphere]. But the profit on top of this cost price is independent of his particular sphere of production, it is a simple average per 100 units of capital advanced."

The grand reconciliation follows. Imagine the five investments I–V "belong to one and the same person." The variable and constant capital consumed in each would form a distinct portion of each commodity's price (the cost price). These cost prices "would thus be different for each kind of commodity I–V." But the capitalist "might very well count them all as profit on the total capital he advanced, so that a definite aliquot part would fall to each capital of 100." So cost prices differ per commodity, but the profit per 100 units "would be the same." The "total price of commodities I–V would thus be the same as their total value" — the sum of cost prices plus the sum of surplus-value/profit — "in point of fact, therefore, the monetary expression for the total quantity of labour, both past and newly added, contained in commodities I–V. And in the same manner, the sum of prices of production for the commodities produced in society as a whole... is equal to the sum of their values." This is the first fundamental equality of the transformation.

The Apparent Contradiction from Inter-branch Purchases

Marx raises an obvious objection: the elements of productive capital are generally bought on the market, "so that their prices include an already realized profit," so that "the profit in one branch of industry goes into the cost price of another." Does this not break the equality?

His answer is subtle and important. If we put the sum of cost prices of all commodities on one side and the sum of profits/surplus-values on the other, "the calculation comes out right." Take commodity A: its cost price may contain the profits of B, C, D, just as the profits of A may in turn go into B, C, D, etc. Now, "the profit of A will be absent from its own cost price, and the profits of B, C, D, etc. will be absent from theirs. None of them includes his own profit in his cost price." If there are n spheres of production, each making a profit of p (and the symbol for the cost price of a single commodity is k), "then the cost price in all together is k − np." The reasoning: each sphere's cost price includes the profits of other spheres but not its own; so summing, the total "cost prices" overstate the true cost by exactly the sum of all profits (np), which have been double-counted. Profits cannot appear twice — once as part of the price of the linen and once as the profit of the flax producers. The profits of one sphere appear on the profit side "only because the commodity in question was itself an end-product, so that its price of production does not go into the cost price of another commodity."

Marx formalizes: if a certain sum p₀ goes into the cost price of a commodity as the profit of the producers of the means of production, and on this cost price a profit of p₁ is added, the total profit P = p₀ + p₁. The total cost price of the commodity, "discounting all portions of the price that count towards profit, is then its own cost price minus P." Using k for this (adjusted) cost price, k + P = k + p₀ + p₁. He cross-references Capital Volume 1, Chapter 9, Section 2, pp. 331–32, where he had shown that a product can be treated as if one part simply replaces capital while the other only represents surplus-value, and he notes that to apply this to the total social product, rectifications are needed because "the profit contained in the price of flax... cannot figure twice."

The Refutation of Double-Counting

Marx deepens the point: "There is no distinction between profit and surplus-value when the surplus-value of A, for instance, goes into the constant capital of B. As far as the value of commodities is concerned, it is completely immaterial whether the labour contained in them is paid or unpaid. This shows only that B pays the surplus-value of A. In the total account, A's surplus-value cannot figure twice." The distinction between profit and surplus-value disappears at the aggregate level (they are equal in total), and what matters is only that no surplus-value is counted twice.

However, there is a real distinction at the level of individual commodities: apart from the divergence of B's price from its value, the same situation holds for the commodities forming the constant part of B (and indirectly its variable capital as means of subsistence for workers). The constant portion of capital is itself "equal to cost price plus surplus-value, i.e. now equal to cost price plus profit," and this profit "can again be greater or less than the surplus-value whose place it has taken." As for variable capital: the average daily wage is equal to the value product of the hours needed to produce the worker's necessary means of subsistence, but this number of hours "is itself distorted by the fact that the production prices of the necessary means of subsistence diverge from their values." This is a critical second-order effect: even the value of labor-power (which determines v) is affected by prices of production, since the worker's subsistence basket is sold at prices of production, not values. Yet the whole thing "is always reducible to the situation that whenever too much surplus-value goes into one commodity, too little goes into another, and that the divergences from value that obtain in the production prices of commodities therefore cancel each other out."

The "Dominant Tendency" and the Average of Perpetual Fluctuations

Marx's conclusion from this reconciliation is epistemologically crucial: "With the whole of capitalist production, it is always only in a very intricate and approximate way, as an average of perpetual fluctuations which can never be firmly fixed, that the general law prevails as the dominant tendency." The law of value is not a directly observable, exact empirical regularity; it operates as a tendency, realized only through the never-ceasing oscillation of market prices and the cancelling out of divergences. This is a mature statement of Marx's method: the law is a real, causal force that asserts itself as the outcome of chaotic fluctuations, not a frictionless equilibrium condition.

Turnover Times: Bracketed and then Restored

Marx now explicitly restores what he had earlier bracketed. Since the general rate of profit is formed by the average of the various rates of profit on each 100 units "over a definite period of time, say a year," the distinction between capitals "by the distinction in turnover times is also obliterated." But — and this is a crucial qualification — "this distinction plays a decisive role for the various different rates of profit in the various spheres of production, by means of whose average the general rate of profit is formed." In other words: before the averaging, turnover time is a determinant of the sphere-specific profit rate; after the averaging, it is washed out. So two sources of divergence — organic composition and turnover time — both feed into the different rates whose average is the general rate. Marx had said he would "deal with this later"; here he acknowledges it without yet fully elaborating.

The Relative Magnitudes of Capitals in Different Spheres

Marx next corrects a possible misunderstanding. In the earlier illustration every capital was taken as 100, "in order to make clear the percentage differences in the rates of profit." But the actual masses of surplus-value produced in each sphere depend on the magnitude of the capital applied. The particular rate of profit of a sphere is not affected by whether 100, m × 100, or xm × 100 is applied — "the profit rate remains 10 per cent, whether the total profit is 10 on 100 or 1,000 on 10,000." However, since the rates of profit differ across spheres, and since they differ because different masses of surplus-value are produced according to the proportion of variable capital, "it is evident that the average profit per 100 units of social capital, and hence the average or general rate of profit, will vary greatly according to the respective magnitudes of the capitals invested in the various spheres."

The Four-Capital Example (A, B, C, D)

Marx illustrates with a four-capital example. Let the rate of surplus-value be 100% for all. Let the variable capital per 100 units of total capital be 25 for A, 40 for B, 15 for C, 10 for D. Then each 100 units yields surplus-value/profit of 25, 40, 15, 10 — total 90. If the four capitals are equal in size, the average rate of profit is 90/4 = 22½ percent.

But now suppose the total capitals are A = 200, B = 300, C = 1,000, D = 4,000. The profits produced would be 50, 120, 150, 400 respectively (each being the per-100 profit times the number of hundreds: A: 25 × 2 = 50; B: 40 × 3 = 120; C: 15 × 10 = 150; D: 10 × 40 = 400). Total profit = 720 on a total capital of 5,500, giving an average rate of profit of 720/5,500 = 13⅑ percent. This dramatically demonstrates that the general rate of profit depends not only on the rates in each sphere but on the relative weights — the amounts of capital invested in each sphere. The simple average (22½%) is very different from the weighted average (13⅑%).

The Two Factors Determining the General Rate of Profit

Marx now states the conclusion formally and definitively. "The general rate of profit is determined therefore by two factors:"

  1. The organic composition of the capitals in the various spheres of production, i.e., the different rates of profit in the particular spheres;
  2. The distribution of the total social capital between these different spheres, i.e., the relative magnitudes of the capitals invested in each particular sphere, and hence the relative share of the total social capital swallowed up by each sphere.

He adds a homely analogy: "It is the same as in the case of the average rate of interest that a money-lender makes if he lends different capitals at different rates of interest, e.g. at 4, 5, 6, 7 per cent, etc. The average rate is completely dependent on how much of his capital he had lent out at each of these different interest rates." This analogy is theoretically significant: the general rate of profit is a weighted average, a purely statistical resultant, not a mysterious substance.

The Emergence of Cost Price and Production Price as Transformed Forms

Marx then marks the historical-theoretical transition: "In Volumes 1 and 2 we were only concerned with the values of commodities. Now a part of this value has split away as the cost price, on the one hand, while on the other, the production price of the commodity has also developed, as a transformed form of value." This sentence announces the central category of the whole Volume: price of production as the transformed form of value. The whole subsequent analysis of Volume 3 operates on this new terrain.

The General Formula:

Marx now develops the general algebraic expression. Take the average social capital as 80c + 20v with annual rate of surplus-value s' = 100%, so the average annual profit for a capital of 100 is 20 and the average annual rate of profit is 20 percent. For any cost price k of the commodities annually produced by a capital of 100, their price of production = k + 20.

In spheres where composition is (80 − x)c + (20 + x)v, the surplus-value actually created is 20 + x, i.e., more than 20, so the commodity value is k + 20 + x, which is more than the price of production (k + 20). In spheres where composition is (80 + x)c + (20 − x)v, the surplus-value created is 20 − x, and the commodity value is k + 20 − x, less than the price of production (k + 20). Thus: production prices equal values only where composition by chance happens to be precisely 80c + 20v. This is the general theorem in the most compressed form: high-composition capitals have values below prices of production; low-composition capitals have values above prices of production; only the average composition has value equal to price of production.

Higher, Lower, and Average Composition Defined

Marx now systematically defines the classification. The degree of development of social productivity of labor differs across spheres, "being higher or lower according to the quantity of means of production set in motion by a certain specific amount of labour." Hence:

  • Capitals of higher composition: those containing a greater percentage of constant capital than the social average, and thus a lesser percentage of variable capital.
  • Capitals of lower composition: those with a relatively smaller share of constant capital and a relatively greater share of variable capital.
  • Capitals of average composition: those whose composition coincides with that of the average social capital.

If the average social capital is 80c + 20v, then 90c + 10v is above the social average, and 70c + 30v is below. In general, for an average social capital of mc + nv (where m + n = 100, m and n constant), (m + x)c + (n − x)v represents a capital of higher composition, and (m − x)c + (n + x)v one of lower composition. This formalization makes the concept rigorous and general.

The Three-Capital Table (I, II, III)

Marx then presents a compact table illustrating how these capitals function once the average rate of profit is established (assuming one turnover per year), where capital I is the average composition:

  • I. 80c + 20v + 20s. Rate of profit = 20%. Price of product = 120. Value = 120.
  • II. 90c + 10v + 10s. Rate of profit = 20%. Price of product = 120. Value = 110.
  • III. 10c + 30v + 30s. Rate of profit = 20%. Price of product = 120. Value = 130.

For capital II (higher composition), value is 110 < price 120. For capital III (lower composition), value is 130 > price 120. Only for capital I is value equal to price. This crystallizes the whole argument: "Commodities produced by capital II thus have a value less than their price of production, and those produced by capital III have a price of production less than their value." Marx cautions, however, that in applying these terms to specific cases, "the ratio between c and v may depart from the general average not just as a result of a difference in the technical composition, but also simply because of a change in value of the elements of constant capital." This is the dual determination of organic composition with which the chapter began: it is the value composition that matters for the profit rate, and this can shift even without a change in the technical composition, purely from a change in the prices/values of constant capital elements.

The Modification of Cost Price

Marx now returns to the cost price, arguing that the development above "involves a modification in the determination of a commodity's cost price." He had originally assumed the cost price of a commodity equaled the value of the commodities consumed in its production. But for the buyer, "it is the price of production that constitutes its cost price and can thus enter into forming the price of another commodity." Since the price of production of a commodity can diverge from its value, the cost price of a commodity (in which the price of production of other commodities is involved) can stand above or below the portion of its total value formed by the value of the means of production going into it. This is the crucial "second-order" effect: once prices of production are established, they feed back into cost prices, propagating the divergence.

Nevertheless, Marx insists, "it still remains correct that the cost price of commodities is always smaller than their value." Why does the potential error of equating cost price with the value of the means of production consumed not invalidate the analysis? Because "this error in the past is a matter of indifference to the capitalist. The cost price of the commodity is a given precondition, independent of his production, while the result of his production is a commodity that contains surplus-value, and therefore an excess value over and above its cost price." The cost price is given to the capitalist from outside; what interests him is the surplus-value (profit) over it. For the total social capital, where price of production equals value, the assertion that cost price < value is identical to the earlier formulation. And so: "the cost price of a commodity simply depends on the quantity of paid labour it contains, while the value depends on the total quantity of labour it contains, whether paid or unpaid; the price of production depends on the sum of paid labour plus a certain quantity of unpaid labour that is independent of its own particular sphere of production." This threefold distinction — cost price = paid labor; value = paid + unpaid labor; price of production = paid labor + socially average unpaid labor — is one of the sharpest formulations in the chapter.

The Formula k + kp'

Marx now refines the formula. Since p = kp′ (where p′ is the general rate of profit), the price of production is k + kp′. His example: if k = 300 and p′ = 15 percent, the price of production = k + kp′ = 300 + 300 × 15/100 = 345. This formula — price of production = cost price × (1 + p′) — is the algebraic culmination of the chapter's constructive work.

The Three Causes of Changes in Production Prices

Marx enumerates the circumstances in which the price of production of commodities in a particular sphere can change in magnitude:

  1. While the value of the commodities remains the same (same quantity of dead and living labour as before), as a result of a change in the general rate of profit independent of the particular sphere;
  2. While the general rate of profit remains the same, by a change in value in the particular sphere itself, either from a technical change or from a change in value of the commodities entering as formative elements into its constant capital;
  3. Finally, by the common action of these two circumstances.

He adds an important temporal/methodological observation: for all the great changes in actual rates of profit in particular spheres, "a genuine change in the general rate of profit, one not simply brought about by exceptional economic events, is the final outcome of a whole series of protracted oscillations, which require a good deal of time before they are consolidated and balanced out to produce a change in the general rate." Hence, in all shorter periods (even leaving aside market-price fluctuations), "a change in prices of production is always to be explained prima facie by an actual change in commodity values, i.e. by a change in the total sum of labour-time needed to produce the commodities." He is careful to exclude "a mere change in the monetary expression of these values," citing Corbet (An Inquiry into the Causes and Modes of the Wealth of Individuals, London, 1841, p. 174).

Changes in the General Rate of Profit: Three Cases

Marx then analyzes how the general rate of profit can change. Taking the total social capital as a whole: sum of values of commodities = value of constant capital + value of variable capital + surplus-value. Assuming a constant level of exploitation, the profit rate can change (with the mass of surplus-value constant) in three cases: (1) if the value of constant capital changes; (2) if the value of variable capital changes; (3) if both change. All result in a change in C (total capital), thereby changing g (the general rate of profit). Hence "a change in the general rate of profit assumes a change in the value of the commodities which enter as formative elements into the constant capital, the variable capital, or both simultaneously."

Alternatively, with the value of commodities constant, the general rate of profit can change if the level of exploitation of labour changes. Or, with the level of exploitation constant, if the sum of labour applied changes in relation to the constant capital (technical changes), though such technical changes "must always show themselves in, and thus be accompanied by, a change in value of the commodities."

The Qualitative vs Quantitative Distinction between Profit and Surplus-Value

Marx now makes his pivotal conceptual distinction. In the first Part, "surplus-value and profit were identical, seen from the point of view of their mass." The rate of profit is from the beginning different from the rate of surplus-value, but "this appears simply as a different way of calculating the same thing." However, given that the rate of profit can rise or fall while the rate of surplus-value stays the same, and given that "all that interests the capitalist in practice is his rate of profit," this circumstance "completely obscures and mystifies the real origin of surplus-value from the very beginning."

Why? Because the rate of profit measures surplus-value against the total capital, surplus-value "appears... as having arisen from the total capital, and uniformly from all parts of it at that, so that the organic distinction between constant and variable capital is obliterated in the concept of profit." Surplus-value "denies its own origin in this, its transformed form, which is profit; it loses its character and becomes unrecognizable." Yet at this first stage, the distinction between profit and surplus-value "simply involved a qualitative change, a change of form," while any difference in magnitude lay between the rates, not between profit and surplus-value themselves (they were still equal in mass).

The Genuine Difference in Magnitudes Once a General Rate Exists

This changes decisively with the establishment of a general rate of profit and an average profit. "It is now purely accidental if the surplus-value actually produced in a particular sphere of production, and therefore the profit, coincides with the profit contained in the commodity's sale price." Now "profit and surplus-value themselves, and not just their rates, will as a rule be genuinely different magnitudes."

At a given level of exploitation, the mass of surplus-value created in a particular sphere is now more important for the overall average profit of the social capital — and thus for the capitalist class in general — than it is directly for the capitalist within each particular branch. It matters to him "only in so far as the quantity of surplus-value created in his own branch intervenes as a co-determinant in regulating the average profit." But "this process takes place behind his back. He does not see it, he does not understand it, and it does not in fact interest him."

The Complete Concealment of the True Nature and Origin of Profit

The consequence is profound. "The actual difference in magnitude between profit and surplus-value in the various spheres of production (and not merely between rate of profit and rate of surplus-value) now completely conceals the true nature and origin of profit, not only for the capitalist, who has here a particular interest in deceiving himself, but also for the worker." With the transformation of values into prices of production, "the very basis for determining value is now removed from view."

Marx traces the two stages of concealment:

  • In the simple transformation from surplus-value into profit, the profit portion of commodity value confronts the other portion as cost price, and "the concept of value thus already goes by the board as far as the capitalist is concerned," because he deals not with the total labour the commodity cost but only the part he paid for (in the form of means of production, living or dead). Profit appears to him as "something standing outside the immanent value of the commodity."
  • With the establishment of a general rate of profit, this idea is "completely confirmed, reinforced and hardened" by the fact that the profit added to the cost price "is not actually determined, if the particular spheres of production are taken separately, by the value formation that proceeds within these branches, but on the contrary established quite externally to them."

"This inner connection is here revealed for the first time." Marx then delivers his sweeping indictment: "all economics up till now has either violently made abstraction from the distinctions between surplus-value and profit, between rate of surplus-value and rate of profit, so that it could retain the determination of value as its basis, or else it has abandoned, along with this determination of value, any kind of solid foundation for a scientific approach, so as to be able to retain those distinctions which obtrude themselves on the phenomenal level." This "confusion on the part of the theorists shows better than anything else how the practical capitalist, imprisoned in the competitive struggle and in no way penetrating the phenomena it exhibits, cannot but be completely incapable of recognizing, behind the semblance, the inner essence and the inner form of this process." (This is the anticipatory statement of the method of Theories of Surplus-Value / Volume 4.)

The Double Significance of the Laws of the Profit Rate

Marx now reinterprets the laws of the profit rate developed in the first Part. They have a double significance:

(1) As laws of the general rate of profit. Given the many causes leading profit rates to rise or fall, "one might believe that the general rate of profit would have to change every single day. But as the movement of one sphere of production will cancel out the movement of another, the forces mutually counteract and paralyse each other." The process is slow; the "suddenness, multilateral character and differential duration of fluctuations in the particular spheres" lead them to "partly compensate for one another in their temporal succession, so that a fall in price succeeds a rise, and vice versa," and thus they "remain local." The various local fluctuations "reciprocally neutralize one another." Since the general rate is determined not only by the average rate in each sphere but also by the distribution of total capital across spheres — a distribution "constantly changing" — there is "a constant source of change in the general rate of profit — but a source of change that also becomes paralysed, for the most part, given the uninterrupted and all-round character of this movement."

(2) Within each sphere there is room for shorter or longer periods in which the profit rate in this sphere fluctuates, "before this fluctuation, a rise or a fall, is consolidated for a sufficient time to affect the general rate of profit and thus to have more than a local significance." Within these spatial and temporal limits, "the laws of the profit rate developed in the first Part of this volume similarly continue to apply." This is a sophisticated methodological point: the laws developed at the level of the individual capital remain operative within each sphere, while the general rate is the resultant of their interaction.

The Practical State of Affairs and the Individual Capitalist's Perspective

Marx ties the theory back to practice. "The theoretical opinion regarding the first transformation of surplus-value into profit, i.e. that each portion of capital yields profit in a uniform way" (footnote: Malthus, Principles of Political Economy, 2nd edn, London, 1836, p. 268) "expresses a practical state of affairs." However an industrial capital may be composed — whether a quarter is dead labour and three-quarters living labour, or three-quarters dead labour and only a quarter sets living labour in motion, "so that in the one case three times as much surplus labour is sucked out, or surplus-value produced, as in the other" — with the same level of exploitation and "ignoring individual differences, which disappear anyway, since in both cases we are concerned only with the average composition of the sphere of production as a whole — in both cases it yields the same profit."

"The individual capitalist (or alternatively the sum total of capitalists in a particular sphere of production), whose vision is a restricted one, is right in believing that his profit does not derive just from the labour employed by him or employed in his own branch. This is quite correct as far as his average profit goes." Marx here concedes the phenomenal correctness of the capitalist's view. "How much this profit is mediated by the overall exploitation of labour by capital as a whole, i.e. by all his fellow-capitalists, this interconnection is a complete mystery to him, and the more so in that even the bourgeois theorists, the political economists, have not yet revealed it."

This leads to the apparent refutation of the labor theory of profit: "Saving of labour — not only the labour necessary to produce a specific product, but also the number of workers employed — and a greater use of dead labour (constant capital), appears a quite correct economic operation, and seems from the very beginning not to affect the general rate of profit and the average profit in any manner. How therefore can living labour be the exclusive source of profit, since a reduction in the quantity of labour needed for production not only seems not to affect the profit, but rather to be the immediate source of increasing profit, in certain circumstances, at least for the individual capitalist?"

This question is the ideological crux of the chapter. The appearance is that capital-saving (mechanization, reducing workers) directly increases profit, which seems to refute the labor theory of value. Marx's whole analysis in this chapter is the answer: the mechanizing capitalist does get the same rate of profit as everyone else, and this is precisely because profit is redistributed from the spheres that use more living labor. The appearance is real but the essence (living labor as source) is concealed by the redistribution. This sets up the later analyses (especially of the falling rate of profit and crises).

The Variable Capital and the Most Important Mystification

Marx now turns to the special case of variable capital, "the most important thing, since it is the source of surplus-value and since everything that conceals its position in the capitalist's enrichment mystifies the entire system." Here "the situation looks cruder, or at least this is the way it appears to the capitalist."

He gives a numerical example. A variable capital of £100 represents the wages of 100 workers. If these 100 workers, with a given working day, produce a weekly product of 200 items of a commodity (200C), then 1C — ignoring the constant capital portion — costs £100/200 = 10 shillings, since £100 = 200C. (Note the unit confusion in the text, which shifts between £ and shillings; the substance is clear.)

Now assume a change in the productivity of labour. If it doubles, the same number of workers produce twice the 200C (i.e., 400C) in the same time. In this case, insofar as the cost price consists simply of labour, £100 now equals 400C, and so 1C = £100/400 = 5 shillings. If productivity is reduced by half, the same labour produces only (200/2)C = 100C, and since 100C = £100, 1C = £1. (The text's notation "2 02°c" and "2 02 oc" is an OCR corruption; the arithmetic is that halving productivity halves the output to 100C, making each C worth £1.)

The significance: the changes in the labour-time required for production (and therefore in value) now appear "in connection with the cost price, and therefore also with the price of production, as a different distribution of the same wages over more or fewer commodities, according to whether more or fewer commodities are produced in the same labour-time for the same wages." What the capitalist (and the political economist) sees is that "the part of the paid labour that falls to each item of the commodity changes with the productivity of labour, and so too therefore does the value of each individual article." What he does not see is "that this is also the case with the unpaid labour contained in each article, and the less so, as the average profit is in fact only accidentally determined by the unpaid labour absorbed in his own sphere."

The chapter's final sentence: "The fact that the value of commodities is determined by the labour they contain now continues to percolate through only in this crudified and naive form." This is the conclusion of the entire movement: the labor theory of value, once transformed into prices of production and obscured by the equalization of profit rates, survives in consciousness only as the crude, naive perception that "wages determine prices" or that "cost of production determines price" — a degraded vestige of the real law.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Fundamental Principles Revealed

First, the law of value operates as a tendency, not a frictionless empirical regularity. The chapter's most important methodological lesson is that value determination asserts itself only "as an average of perpetual fluctuations which can never be firmly fixed," as "the dominant tendency." The exact equalities (sum of prices = sum of values; sum of profits = sum of surplus-value) hold at the level of the total social capital, but the individual divergences are systematic, permanent, and mutually cancelling. This dissolves the naive reading of the labor theory of value as a claim about every individual price.

Second, the rate of profit is a social or class phenomenon, not an individual one. The joint-stock company analogy is not mere ornament. It expresses a structural truth: profit arises from the exploitation of the working class as a whole, and each capitalist's profit is his pro-rata share of the total social surplus-value. The rate of profit is thus determined by the aggregate organic composition and the distribution of total capital — two factors, both social. This is the economic foundation of the capitalist class as a collective appropriator, and it explains why individual capitalists can be indifferent to (even hostile toward) the source of their own profit.

Third, appearance and essence are systematically inverted under capitalism. The transformation of values into prices of production does not merely obscure the origin of profit — it inverts it. High-composition (mechanized) capitals appear to be more profitable per unit of living labour saved, since they yield the same average profit while employing less labor. The mechanizing capitalist seems to prove that dead labor (constant capital) is the source of profit, precisely because the redistribution conceals the transfer from labor-intensive spheres. "How therefore can living labour be the exclusive source of profit?" is a question the phenomenal forms seem to answer against Marx — but the analysis of the chapter shows the opposite.

Fourth, scientific categories must be derived, not presupposed. Marx's insistence that the general rate of profit "remains a meaningless and irrational conception" without the value-theoretic derivation is a defense of systematic method. The price of production is a transformed form of value, not an independent datum. Any economics that begins from prices of production (as vulgar economics does) has abandoned the scientific foundation.

Broader Theoretical Implications

The resolution of the "great contradiction." The chapter definitively resolves the apparent contradiction between Volume 1's labor theory of value and the empirical fact of equalized profit rates. The resolution is not that values are irrelevant, but that they are transformed. Total value = total price of production; total surplus-value = total profit. This is the "invariance condition" that later economists (Bortkiewicz, Sraffa, and the "transformation problem" literature) would contest — but the chapter itself is Marx's own statement of the resolution, and it is rich, formal, and internally coherent.

The theory of the falling rate of profit. The two-factor determination of the general rate — organic composition and distribution of capital — lays the groundwork for the later analysis of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall as the organic composition rises over time. The "double significance" of the laws of the profit rate (general and sphere-specific) is the methodological apparatus for that analysis.

The theory of class and ideology. The chapter is a model of ideology critique through economic analysis. It shows why the phenomenal forms of capitalism systematically generate a false consciousness — not merely among capitalists but among workers and theorists — and it shows that this false consciousness is not a simple error but a necessary reflection of real appearances. "The practical capitalist, imprisoned in the competitive struggle... cannot but be completely incapable of recognizing, behind the semblance, the inner essence." This is Marx's mature theory of the relationship between appearance, essence, and ideology.

The critique of political economy. The closing indictment — that all prior economics either abstracted violently from the distinctions or abandoned the value foundation — frames Volume 4 (Theories of Surplus-Value). It identifies the central theoretical failure of classical economics (Smith, Ricardo, and their successors): the inability to hold together the determination of value by labor and the equalization of profit rates. Marx claims to have solved it; the history of subsequent economics (marginalism, the transformation debate) is, in part, a series of responses to whether he did.

Practical and Philosophical Implications

The chapter's practical implication for the working class is stark: the exploitation of any particular worker by his particular employer is a part of a total social exploitation, and the wage-bargain in any one sphere is mediated by the total social capital. The struggle over wages and hours is thus a struggle over the rate of exploitation of the whole class, not merely a local matter. Conversely, the chapter shows why the defense of profit ("capital deserves its return") has an apparent plausibility: the average profit does seem to arise from capital as such, and the redistribution that produces it is invisible.

Philosophically, the chapter is a case study in the dialectic of essence and appearance. The real (value) is not the immediate (price); the immediate is a necessary form of manifestation of the real, which simultaneously reveals and conceals it. This is the methodological core of Marx's mature work — the "concrete" is "the unity of diverse determinations," and the movement from abstract value to prices of production is the movement from essence to existence, in which the essence persists in and through its own self-concealment. The chapter's final image — value determination "percolating through only in this crudified and naive form" — is a precise statement of how a real law can survive in consciousness only in a degraded, fetishized shape, which is exactly the task of critique to decode.

Summary of the Chapter's Constructive Results

  1. The general rate of profit is a weighted average of the sphere-specific rates, determined by (a) the organic compositions of capitals in different spheres and (b) the distribution of total social capital across those spheres.
  2. Average profit = the general rate of profit applied to the capital advanced.
  3. Price of production = k + kp′ (cost price plus the average profit), a transformed form of value.
  4. Sum of prices of production = sum of values; sum of profits = sum of surplus-value, at the level of total social capital, because the divergences cancel (26 above, 26 below, in the illustrative example).
  5. Profit and surplus-value diverge in magnitude (not merely in rate) once the general rate exists; this divergence is systematic and conceals the origin of profit.
  6. Turnover time is a co-determinant of sphere-specific rates, washed out in the average.
  7. Cost price itself is modified by prices of production and is always less than value (and generally less than price of production).
  8. The organic composition of capital is determined by the technical composition and the values of the elements; capitals are classified as higher, average, or lower composition relative to the social average.

Marx thus completes, in Chapter 9, the movement from the hidden essence of capitalist production (value and surplus-value, developed in Volumes 1 and 2 and the first Part of Volume 3) to its visible surface (prices of production and average profit). He shows that the surface is not arbitrary but law-governed — a transformed form — and that its law-governed character is what conceals the essence. The chapter thereby both solves a technical problem in value theory and diagnoses a structure of illusion that is intrinsic to capitalism itself. It is, in this sense, the pivot on which the entirety of Capital turns.
