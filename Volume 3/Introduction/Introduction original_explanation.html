Volume 3 • Introduction

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Chapter's Core Problem, Thesis, and Intellectual Objective

This ~33,000-word Introduction, written by the Belgian Marxist economist Ernest Mandel for the Penguin (Pelican Marx Library) edition of Karl Marx's Capital, Volume 3, is not a conventional editor's preface. It is a sustained, polemical, and theoretically dense defense of Volume 3's analytical architecture from the standpoint of what Mandel calls "mature dialectical method." Its central thesis can be stated bluntly: Volume 3 of Capital is the most controversial of the three volumes because it is the volume that answers the question "Whither capitalism?" — and Marx's answer (the system is immanently crisis-ridden, unable to overcome its contradictions without abolishing private property, competition, profit and the market economy) is intellectually intolerable both to capitalists and their apologists and to "neutral" academic economists and reformists. The controversy, therefore, is not primarily a logical problem about mathematics; it is a socially and politically motivated resistance to an unpalatable conclusion.

Mandel's intellectual objective is threefold. First, he wants to reconstruct the "fundamental logic of Capital" by integrating Volume 3 into a unified structure with Volumes 1 and 2, arguing (via a schematic diagram, pp. 14–15) that the whole work forms one coherent architecture moving from the simple value-form up to the tendency toward collapse of the capitalist system. Second, he wants to defend Marx's specific doctrines — especially the equalization of the rate of profit, the transformation of values into prices of production, the tendency of the average rate of profit to decline (TRPF), and the theory of surplus profits/ground rent — against three main waves of criticism: (a) the von Bortkiewicz/Sraffa "transformation problem" critique (which Mandel sorts into the "feedback controversy" and the "monetary confusion"), (b) the Okishio/neo-Ricardian charge that TRPF is theoretically or empirically false, and (c) the various "mono-causal" theories of crisis (disproportionality, under-consumption, over-accumulation) which Mandel thinks are each partially right but must be integrated into a multi-causal theory. Third, and most ambitiously, Mandel uses the Introduction as a vehicle to articulate his own theoretical synthesis — incorporating long waves, the concept of "technological rent," and a reformulated collapse theory based on automation and the absolute decline of value-production — all while insisting that these extensions remain faithful to Marx's method and conclusions.

How this Chapter Fits into the Broader Subject and Theoretical Framework

Capital Volume 3 occupies a specific place in Marx's six-book plan: it is the volume that deals with "the process of capitalist production as a whole", i.e., the unity of production (Volume 1) and circulation (Volume 2). Volume 1 analyzes the factory and the origin of surplus-value; Volume 2 analyzes the market place and realization, laying out the reproduction schemas and demonstrating that capitalism cannot achieve steady "enlarged reproduction" — equilibrium is a product of constantly reappearing disequilibria, growth necessarily takes the form of the industrial cycle, and periodic crises of over-production are inevitable. Neither volume, however, works out "the precise way in which these contradictions… are interrelated" so that the system's laws of motion lead to explosive crises and collapse. That is precisely the task of Volume 3 — even though, as Mandel stresses, Marx left the volume unfinished (most gravely, Part Seven with only a barely-started Chapter 52 on social classes) and he never completed the projected volumes on the world market, competition, the industrial cycle, and the state, which he had explicitly postponed.

This incompleteness is, for Mandel, the key reason the controversies have been so persistent. But the basic reason is social and political: Volume 3 tells capitalists, their "hangers-on", "neutral" economists who tacitly presuppose the permanence of commodity production, and philanthropic social reformers that the system breeds mass poverty and destitution in a way that cannot be cured without abolishing the system itself. The Introduction therefore situates itself simultaneously inside the history of economic theory (against Bohm-Bawerk, Bortkiewicz, Sweezy, Sraffa, Steedman, Okishio) and inside the history of the international labour movement (from the Second International "orthodox" Marxism of Kautsky and Bernstein's revisionism, through Luxemburg, Bukharin, Varga, Grossmann, to "state monopoly capitalism" theory and Euro-communism). Mandel's structural position is that of a revolutionary Trotskyist defending Marx's classical conclusions against both bourgeois critics and "econimist" or reformist distortions inside the workers' movement — and willing, where he thinks Marx was silent or insufficiently concrete, to extend the theory himself.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Architecture of Volume 3: Revenues, Classes, and the "Intermediate Steps"

Mandel opens by anchoring Volume 3 in a concrete analytical problem, not in an abstract principle. Volume 1 solved the problem of the origin of the two basic categories of revenue, wages and profits: surplus-value is produced only by living labour, purchased with the variable portion of capital, not the constant portion (machinery, raw materials, buildings, energy). Volume 3 does not re-open that question. What it explains is distribution: how specific sectors of the ruling class participate in the division of the total mass of surplus-value, and how these specific economic categories are regulated.

Mandel enumerates four ruling-class beneficiary groups — industrial capitalists; commercial capitalists; bankers; capitalist landowners — yielding five categories of revenue: wages; industrial profits; commercial (and banking) profits; interest; land rent. These are then re-grouped by Marx into the three classical categories wages, profits, land rent. This tri-partite division matters because it is the analytical ground on which Marx will construct his demonstration that the class structure reproduces itself through distribution — an argument Mandel returns to in the very last substantive section.

But to get there, Mandel insists, "a whole series of intermediate steps have to be taken," and these steps are the spine of the volume:

  1. The rate of profit must be distinguished as a separate analytical category from the rate of surplus-value (r = s/v distinguished from s/(c+v)).
  2. The tendency toward equalization of the rate of profit between all capitals must be discovered — a discovery whose "discovery" aspect is critical because it is what breaks the naive, direct correspondence between surplus-value produced and profit realized.
  3. From these two conceptual innovations Marx deduces the centre-piece of the volume: the tendency of the average rate of profit to decline, absent countervailing tendencies.
  4. Profit itself then subdivides into entrepreneurial profit (industry, transport, trade) and interest — that portion accruing to owners of money-capital who merely lend.
  5. Finally, surplus profit is introduced — surplus-value excluded from the general equalization — and its most fully studied special case is land rent.

Here Mandel makes one of his characteristic interpretive moves: he argues that Part Six on ground rent is really about "the more general problem of monopoly giving rise to surplus profit", and that Marx in Chapters 10 and 14 explicitly treats land rent as but a special case. The claim, then, is that Marx "anticipates the whole contemporary theory of monopoly prices and profits," and does so with "greater clarity as to their origins than" the twentieth-century academic economists (Joe Bain, Sylos-Labini, Dorfman) who have tried to solve the same puzzle. This is not a minor flourish; it is the connective tissue that allows Mandel, later in the chapter, to extend "differential land rent" into mineral rents and technological rents as expressions of the same underlying logic.

Mandel draws attention to a striking absence — the unfinished Part Seven and particularly the barely-initiated Chapter 52 on social classes. He hypothesizes that it would have supplied "a vital link between the economic content of the class struggle between capital and labour… and its overall economic outcome," partly sketched in Volume 3's Chapters 11 and 15. The subtitle "The Process of Capitalist Production in its Totality" is therefore, he suggests, aspirational: the "totality" actually includes circulation (per Volume 2) plus the world market, competition, the industrial cycle, and the state — all of which were in Marx's plan, all of which there is no indication he abandoned, and all of which were "alas unwritten." This incompleteness is not used by Mandel as an excuse; it is used as the diagnostic key explaining why the controversies (especially about crisis, which Marx never treated in one place) have been so durable.

The Equalization of the Rate of Profit: Mechanism, Labour Theory of Value, and the "Three Series of Problems"

Mandel next turns to the analytical mechanism that underlies everything else: the equalization of the rate of profit. He begins by reconstructing the Volume 1 result. Surplus-value is produced only by living labour — i.e., by the fraction of capital spent on labour-power, which Marx calls variable capital (v); the fraction spent on buildings, machinery, raw materials, energy is constant capital (c). From this, one might naively conclude that "the greater the proportion of capital which each industrial branch spends on wages, the higher its rate of profit" — since the rate of profit is the relation between surplus-value produced and total capital invested (c+v).

This naive conclusion is rejected on two grounds, both of which recur throughout the Introduction. First, it "would contradict the basic logic of the capitalist mode of production, which consists of expansion, growth, enlarged reproduction, through a substitution of living by dead labour" — that is, through a rising organic composition of capital (c/v, or more precisely the value-composition of capital in so far as it reflects the technical composition). This rising organic composition is itself driven by two forces: (a) capitalist competition, in which cost-price reduction depends on ever more efficient, labour-saving machinery, and (b) the class struggle — because the only long-run way accumulation can avoid labour shortage and thus a rising real wage (which would sharply reduce the rate of surplus-value) is by piling up fixed constant capital, i.e., substituting machinery for living labour. Second, empirical evidence "overwhelmingly confirms" that labour-intensive branches do not normally earn higher rates of profit.

From these premises Marx draws his conclusion: in a fully developed and normally functioning capitalist economy, each industrial branch does not directly receive the surplus-value produced by the wage-labour it employs. It receives only a fraction of the total surplus-value produced by productive labour, proportional to the fraction it represents of all capital expended. Concretely:

  • Branches with below-average organic composition (more labour, more v relative to total capital) do not realize all the surplus-value their own workers produce; part of it is transferred to branches with above-average organic composition.
  • Branches with above-average organic composition receive more surplus-value than is directly produced by "their" wage-labourers.
  • Only branches whose individual organic composition exactly equals the social average receive exactly their own produced surplus-value, with no transfers in or out.

This is the material basis of the common interest of all capital-owners in the exploitation of labour, which thereby takes the form of a collective class exploitation — with inter-capitalist competition merely deciding how the total mass is redistributed. This formulation is crucial for the later section on capitalist class solidarity.

Mandel then identifies "three series of problems" that this process of equalization raises:

  1. What is its relation to the labour theory of value in general?
  2. What are the concrete mechanisms by which equalization occurs in real life?
  3. What is the "technical" solution to the transformation of values into prices of production — where "price of production" = production costs (capital outlays) + average profit multiplied by those outlays?

He observes that the first two have provoked relatively less controversy than the third, "probably because of their more 'abstract' character," yet they are "of the highest importance for the inner cohesion of Marxist economic theory," and Marx's treatment shows his "dialectical method at its most mature."

Problem 1: Relation to the Labour Theory of Value

Marx's argument, as Mandel reconstructs it, is that because value is in the last analysis a social, not an individual, category, the below-average-organic-composition branches objectively waste social labour from the standpoint of capitalist society as a whole (i.e., from the standpoint of "equality" of commodity-owners). Therefore the market does not return to their owners the full value created. Inversely, above-average-organic-composition branches (with above-average social productivity of labour) objectively economize socially necessary labour, and are rewarded by receiving a higher proportion of all surplus-value than their workers directly produce. Value realization and value production are being carefully separated.

Counter-argument 1 (productivity comparability): "Is productivity of labour comparable in different branches… inasmuch as these do not produce goods that are interchangeable?" Mandel's answer is dynamic: compare the rates of increase of productivity of labour across branches over time. More generally, the specific organic composition of capital in each branch, constantly shifting as productivity changes, "can be considered as a general index, a means of measurement, of social productivity of labour." He quotes Marx (p. 318 below) to the effect that the progressive decline of variable capital relative to constant capital is "identical with the progressively rising organic composition… of the social capital as a whole… just another expression for the progressive development of the social productivity of labour." In a market economy of constant technical revolution, demand shifts, and capital flux, Mandel says, this assumption is "both theoretically tenable and empirically verifiable."

Counter-argument 2 (the alleged contradiction between value-production and value-transfer): "But is there not a basic contradiction between considering all labour effectively expended… as value-producing, and at the same time explaining the transfers of value (surplus-value)… as a function of objective waste or economy of social labour?" Mandel, appealing to Rubin's Essays on Marx's Theory of Value, insists no. Value as the embodiment of abstract human labour is "not a problem of measurement, of numeraire, but a problem of essence." Each community has a total labour potential — the number of producers × socially accepted average annual work-days × work-hours. This potential is an objective social fact in a given country and time-frame (year). From it flows the total value produced during the year. Socially necessary labour, which treats some labour-hours as "wasted" and hence unaccounted for socially, "only implies redistribution of value inside each branch of production, except in cases of monopoly." Extending this to the economy as a whole changes nothing: all labour actually expended in production has been value-producing; it cannot be made larger or smaller by anything occurring outside the sphere of production. The problem of compensation on the market is a problem of distribution, not of production.

Mandel's dialectical formulation: it is "perfectly possible that actually expended private labour in a given branch, at the average rate of productivity of that branch, is socially necessary labour and has really produced value, while at the same time the owners of the commodities in which it is embodied do not receive full compensation on the market for all that embodied value, or receive a counter-value higher than the amount of value embodied in their commodities." The mediating concept is that total value equals total prices of production — an equal sum of labour-hours/weeks/years, an equal total labour potential. What is modified on the market, at the inter-branch level, is exclusively "a problem of (re)distribution of value, not one of production of value." He cites the crucial passage (p. 774 below) on the law of value as it makes itself felt "not in relation to the individual commodities… but rather to the total products at a given time of particular spheres of social production."

Mandel notes an internal Marxist debate here: he sides with Rubin against Catherine Colliot-Thélène, who in her Afterword to Rubin's A History of Economic Thought asserts that Marx's double definition of "socially necessary labour" (by average branch productivity and by the relation of branch output to socially recognized needs) involves a contradiction. Mandel retorts: "Where Colliot-Thélène sees a contradiction, there is in fact a difference — between value production, which is strictly limited to the sphere of production, and value realization, which occurs in the sphere of circulation and depends inter alia upon relations between the structure of production and the structure of demand." The law of value adapts the distribution of the labour force to social needs post festum, because under commodity production this cannot be done a priori. This does not imply that labour expended was not value-producing; it means "value produced has been redistributed: that the equivalent of some of it is not received by those who own the commodities thus produced." He also gently rebukes Maurice Dobb for treating labour as a numeraire.

Problem 2: The Concrete Mechanism of Equalization

Here Mandel is at his most economically concrete and most insistent on dialectical method. Equalization is always a tendency, never a permanent reality. His exposition proceeds from a simplifying assumption, then relaxes it.

Step 1 (fixed demand structure): Starting from actual realization of the total surplus-value produced in each branch, branches with lower organic composition (more wages relative to total outlays) will show a higher rate of profit than branches with higher organic composition. All else equal (above all: assuming no change in the distribution of total demand for the different use-values), this above-average rate of profit attracts additional capital. Additional capital raises supply above social demand → prices fall → the rate of profit falls. Conversely, in above-average-organic-composition branches, the initially below-average rate of profit causes capital withdrawal → production declines below social demand → prices rise → profits rise to the social average. It is the flux and reflux of capital between branches that is the driving force. Moreover, this flux and reflux "is indeed the main way in which capital accumulation (growth) occurs in actual life," i.e., as an uneven process — branches never grow at the same rhythm or over the same time spans. Hence equalization "presupposes their relative inequality": it "is a process which constantly realizes itself by negating itself." Empirical confirmation, Mandel says, is available to anyone studying the real history of capitalist branches of industry, mining and transport.

Step 2 (weakening the assumption of initial great unevenness): Mandel concedes that the process does not require great initial unevenness in profit rates, nor that higher rates of profit coincide with greater labour intensity. "It would be sufficient to assume a single initial situation of that kind to make the process perfectly logical and coherent." In fact, very early in industrial capitalism the average rate of profit becomes a known entity (bank credit and the stock exchange playing a "not unimportant role" here; cf. p. 311 below). The real process is therefore less one of capital flowing from below-average to above-average branches, and more one of firms seeking "surplus profits" over and above the known average rate of profit, essentially through revolutionary innovations (possibly creating entirely new branches). The average rate of profit is "constantly shaken and re-established by the reactions which this constant revolution in the technique of production and the organization of labour provokes." Each firm trying to maximize its own rate of profit contributes, "independently of its wishes and designs," to the tendential equalization of the rate of profit. This is a cornerstone of Mandel's anti-Okishio argument later. He adds a footnote (n. 14) giving a concrete historical example: canal-building and mining firms in the early industrial revolution may have had higher rates of profit than the first textile mills due to lower organic composition.

Step 3 (abandoning stable demand): If we drop the simplifying assumption of stable demand in a given time-span, "we only have to introduce additional mediations; the result remains substantially the same." Two sub-cases: (a) If a below-average-organic-composition branch also benefits from above-average growth of social demand, prices decline more slowly despite the influx of capital — "but this will only attract even more additional capital, until equalization finally occurs." (b) If such a branch is relatively 'older' and suffering decline of total demand, the influx of capital + rising output leads more rapidly to falling prices/profits and final equalization. For branches losing capital due to initially lower profits, the reasoning is the obvious counterpart. Mandel also records a subtle point of Marx's (n. 16): the relative weight of firms operating at above-/average-/below-average productivity within a branch can temporarily mean that it is not the average level of productivity which determines the commodity's value — but "competition will rapidly do away with such situations, in the absence of structural scarcity or monopoly."

Problem 3: The Transformation of Values into Prices of Production — The Feedback Controversy

The third series of problems — the "'technical' problem of the transformation of values into prices of production for each specific commodity" — is by far the most contentious, and Mandel divides the critical literature into two bodies: the feedback controversy and the monetary confusion.

The feedback controversy originated with the Prussian statistician Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz. His claim: in Marx's Chapter 9 solution of the transformation, only the values of currently produced commodities (outputs) are transformed, not the values of input-commodities — so Marx is accused of inconsistency, computing inputs in values and outputs in prices of production. A stream of authors — "some claiming to be Marxists, others obviously adhering to other economic doctrines" — has repeated the charge. Mandel cites the "obvious" textual evidence often quoted from Chapter 9: the passage in which Marx writes that "the development given above also involves a modification in the determination of a commodity's cost price… if the cost price of a commodity is equated with the value of the means of production used up in producing it, it is always possible to go wrong."

Mandel, however, insists this quotation "should not be made to say more than it does." It says only that mixing value-calculations in inputs and price-of-production-calculations in outputs is "likely to arrive at numerically erroneous conclusions" — which is "rather obvious, since the whole analysis precisely concerns the deviation of prices of production from values." Crucially, the extract does not imply that prices of production of inputs should be calculated within the same time-span as prices of production of outputs. The passage immediately following the one Bortkiewicz quotes explicitly rejects this interpretation: "Our present investigation does not require us to go into further detail on this point. It still remains correct that the cost price of commodities is always smaller than their value… The cost price of the commodity is a given precondition, independent of his, the capitalist's, production…" (Mandel's italics). And even more clearly, from Volume 3 p. 266: in all periods shorter than that required for a change in the general rate of profit to consolidate, "a change in prices of production is always to be explained prima facie by an actual change in commodity values, i.e. by a change in the total sum of labour-time needed to produce the commodities."

Mandel draws the logical conclusion: inputs in current cycles are data, given at the start of the cycle, and do not have a feedback effect on the equalization of the rates of profit in various branches during that cycle. It suffices to assume such inputs are also calculated in prices of production — but these prices of production result from the equalization of rates of profit during the previous cycle. "For any inconsistency to disappear." This dissolves the von Bortkiewicz charge.

But Mandel immediately anticipates the obvious objection: could one not argue that raw-material prices fluctuate constantly, changing many times during a year, so that feedback effects do occur and part of the inputs participate in the current (annual) redistribution of surplus-value? His reply is emphatic: No. Prices of production of raw materials, like all other inputs bought by capitalists currently occupied in production, are "unchangeable data" — they "cannot vary through ups or downs of current production of surplus-value, or current changes in the organic composition of capital occurring during a given year." Capitalists pay a given price, which does not change a posteriori as a function of what is occurring during a year in final surplus-value redistribution. And the argument holds even if capitalists buy raw materials currently rather than at year's beginning, and even if one assumes away all pre-existing stocks.

Why? Because the formation of prices of production — i.e., the calculation of the average rate of profit — is not a constantly moving process. It is linked to the overall realization of surplus-value of all (most) currently produced commodities. There must be a minimum time-span before one can speak of a new average rate of profit replacing a previous one. Mandel even says an assumption of annual change "is probably an exaggeration rather than an underestimate." Thus currently purchased raw materials, even on a quarterly or monthly basis, "do not fundamentally change the prices of production (average rate of profit), as resulting from the capital movements which had occurred during the previous year." One must not confuse formation of prices of production (a redistribution of the total surplus-value produced for society as a whole) with current fluctuations of market prices, which Marx explicitly excludes.

The reason for this relative rigidity (rigidity of prices of production = rigidity of average rates of profit in a country) is grounded in the very nature of the processes producing equalization: determination of the total mass of surplus-value (surplus labour), and the fluxes and refluxes of capital — large-scale capital movements between branches, determining changes and differences in the organic composition both of productive sectors as a whole and of each sector separately. "Such overall social movements cannot vary from quarter to quarter, let alone from month to month." The relative indivisibility of fixed capital is itself a "formidable obstacle to such broad movements under advanced capitalist conditions, except in the case of radical devalorization of capital under conditions of severe crisis."

Mandel's meta-conclusion: "Not only is Marx theoretically consistent when he assumes prices of production of inputs resulting from equalization movements in different time-spans… This also corresponds much more closely to the real, empirically verifiable operation of the capitalist system as we know it than does the opposite assumption of von Bortkiewicz and his followers."

Against Alternative "Solutions" and Mathematical Models

Mandel briefly surveys attempts to solve or "correct" the transformation problem: J. Winternitz (who sought a solution preserving total prices of production = total value), and more recently Anwar Shaikh (who uses an "iterative method" rather than simultaneous equations). Mandel does not endorse any mathematical model as such; he argues that "mathematical models cannot, in and of themselves, 'solve' theoretical problems. They can only formalize interrelations previously understood as such." Many authors of such models, he charges, "silently assume correlations which have not been previously proved or empirically tested." Their equations may be mathematically consistent but theoretically wrong if they don't correspond to a meaningful representation of the problem supposedly being solved.

Two examples are given:

  1. The Okishio theorem (N. Okishio, 1961, 1963): the author puts fixed capital "between brackets altogether" to draw conclusions about the trend of the rate of profit. But if the growth of fixed capital is one of the main determinants of TRPF, the theorem proves nothing.

  2. The von Bortkiewicz "solution" of the transformation problem (accepted by Sweezy, Sraffa, Seton, and many others): besides assuming uniform profits for all products — not all branches or firms, "which is quite another story" — it assumes that only those equations involving commodities entering into production of other commodities are needed. It is then logical that the organic composition of department III (whose commodities do not enter the reproduction process) does not influence the average rate of profit. But this tells us nothing about department III in Marx's analysis, where such a distinction is explicitly excluded, and "especially about what happens in the really functioning capitalist economy, i.e., in real life." Mandel underlines with an example: to say that the organic composition of the armaments industry, including its size, is "immaterial to the real rate of profit of a real capitalist economy" is "quite untenable — especially if one takes a look at the size of that department in, say, 1943 in Germany or 1944 in the U.S.A." (The insight about department III he credits to Emmanuel Farjoun of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.)

The Monetary Confusion

The second line of attack on Marx's transformation solution involves confusing prices of production with market prices, and more generally importing questions about the expression of values as prices, i.e., money. Mandel singles out Paul Sweezy (in The Theory of Capitalist Development, 1942) for having perpetuated this confusion in his take-over of von Bortkiewicz's critique, and Ian Steedman (in Marx after Sraffa, 1977) for following in his footsteps.

Marx himself, Mandel insists, is crystal clear that prices of production do not concern market prices. The very title of Chapter 9 specifies the transformation of values of commodities into prices of production. Values are quantities of labour, and have nothing to do with money prices as such. Equalization of the rate of profit occurs through the transfer of quantities of surplus-value from one branch to another — and surplus-value too is a quantity of labour (surplus labour), not money. Mandel notes that he deliberately omitted a crucial sentence in the earlier citation and will now use it to eliminate all doubt: "We are not referring here, of course, to a mere change in the monetary expression of these values" (p. 266). If the problem does not concern monetary expression of values, it ipso facto does not concern monetary expression of prices of production.

Yes, Marx does introduce market prices and the influence of competition — but in Chapter 10, immediately following his transformation solution in Chapter 9 — and there he clearly and explicitly distinguishes fluctuations of market prices and of monetary expressions of value from the fluctuations in the average rate of profit which determine changes in prices of production. Engels, Mandel adds (n. 28), explicitly envisages the case where the total sum of money profits resulting from market prices is lower than total surplus-value produced, because value has declined due to rising productivity (letter to Conrad Schmidt, 12 March 1895). This corroborates that monetary and value-level accounts are on different planes.

Behind the confusion lies an insufficient understanding of Marx's theory of money. Money (gold) is treated by Marx as a special commodity with its own "intrinsic" value; only for this reason can it serve as general equivalent for the exchange-value of all other commodities. It follows immediately that fluctuations of market (monetary) prices may always be the result of a dual movement: changes in the value of a commodity and changes in the value of the money-commodity (gold). But changes in the intrinsic value of the money-commodity have identical effects on the market prices of all other commodities — hence they cannot change mutual exchange relations (relative prices). Paper money does not alter this. Inflation of paper money means only that an increasing amount of paper dollars, pounds, etc. represents the same quantity (e.g., one ounce) of gold. What is true for the money expression of value is likewise true for the money expression of prices of production, which concern only a redistribution of quantities of surplus-value between branches.

Mandel now draws out the "real" empirical problem that he thinks would arise if one insisted on inputs being expressed in market prices (which is what capitalists actually face): "The 'inputs' in the reproduction tables could only be treated as inputs in really occurring capitalist production… if they were expressed in market prices, and not in prices of production." The real issue would be: how to "transform" values not into prices of production, but first into prices of production and then into market prices — where the second stage would have to involve real monetary problems, specifically "the interrelationship between the average value of commodities and the average value of gold."

The theoretical stakes, Mandel concludes, are: does the "transformation problem" concern an immediate move from essence to appearance (i.e., to day-to-day production and circulation)? Or — as Mandel "would strongly maintain" — is it only a mediating link in the process of cognition, which does not yet deal with immediately verifiable empirical data, i.e., market prices?

To make the conceptual relations clear, Mandel inserts a diagram (p. 30), which can be reconstructed in prose:

  • For a specific commodity: individual value (quantity of labour contained in that unit) → market value (socially average socially necessary labour contained in it) → price of production (socially necessary labour modified by equalization of rates of profit between branches = average costs of production in each branch + average profit of all branches).
  • For the money-commodity (gold): individual value (quantity of labour contained in the product of each specific gold mine) → market value (quantity of labour contained in gold actually produced under the lowest conditions of productivity) → price of production (total costs of production in all gold mines + average profit, divided by total output). "There is obviously no 'market price' of gold, as this would mean the value of gold expressed in gold."
  • Then: 'intrinsic' market prices of commodities (prices of production expressed in quantities of gold, of money) → 'fluctuating' market prices of commodities (prices of production expressed in quantities of gold and modified under very short-term fluctuations of supply and demand, i.e., fluctuating around the 'axis' of intrinsic market prices = prices of production = values).
  • Finally, operation of the law of value under capitalism: fluctuations of 'intrinsic' market prices, other than very short-term ones, are determined by relative value movements (relative increases or decreases of productivity of labour in the output of a given commodity and in the output of gold), mediated through deviations from the average rate of profit.

Mandel closes this section with the Sraffa-von-Bortkiewicz critique as systematized by Pierre Salama (Sur la valeur, 1975). Salama, Mandel says, reveals "a series of underlying theoretical assumptions of which the authors themselves are not always aware." Specifically: further disaggregation of the von Bortkiewicz system — applying to von Bortkiewicz some of the criticisms he himself directs at Marx, e.g., that within department I, means of production used exclusively for producing department III commodities have a different status — "leads unavoidably to the elimination of all value calculations and, therefore, of exploitation itself from the system." Mandel is careful to say this does not imply that Salama, Farjoun, and others have "definitively resolved all the difficulties"; there is "still room for further discussion and research." But — and this is the polemical thrust — "neither have von Bortkiewicz, Seton and Sraffa 'definitively' proved Marx wrong."

The Declining Rate of Profit Controversy

Mandel's next major section takes up the "centre-piece" of Volume 3: the tendency of the average rate of profit to decline (TRPF). Marx derives it from his definition of the average rate of profit as total surplus-value produced ÷ total capital invested. Since the part of capital which alone produces surplus-value (variable capital, buying labour-power) tends to become a smaller and smaller part of total capital — because of the fundamentally labour-saving tendency of technical progress (substituting machinery for living labour) and because of the rise in the value of raw materials in total output — the organic composition of capital (in its value expression) tends to rise, and hence there is an inbuilt tendency for the average rate of profit to decline.

Marx's own caveat, stressed by Mandel: this is a tendency, not an uninterrupted linear development. Marx stresses "powerful countervailing forces at work" which may neutralize or even reverse the tendency, and other forces which slow it down.

The Countervailing Forces

Mandel begins with the most important countervailing force: the possibility of increasing the rate of surplus-value (s′ = s/v). Technically, it might seem this could indefinitely compensate for a rising organic composition. To see this, Mandel rewrites the rate of profit:

Starting from pr′ = s / (c + v), divide numerator and denominator by v:

pr′ = (s/v) / (c/v + 1) = s′ / (q + 1)

where: - s′ = the rate of surplus-value (s/v), - q = the organic composition of capital (c/v), - pr′ = the rate of profit.

So the rate of profit is directly proportional to the rate of surplus-value and inversely proportional to the organic composition of capital (plus the additive constant 1). If the rate of surplus-value increased in the same proportion as the organic composition, the rate of profit would cease to decline.

Mandel argues this proportional increase is impossible in the long run. The organic composition can rise to infinity (fully automated production from which living labour is totally excluded — cf. Levinson, Capital, Inflation and the Multinationals, 1971, and the note that labour costs in some petro-chemical works have fallen below 0.1% of total production costs). But the rate of surplus-value cannot rise to infinity. As long as living wage-labour is employed, no level of productivity — even fully automated factories — is imaginable in which workers could reproduce the equivalent of all the consumer goods they need in "a couple of minutes' or even a couple of seconds' work." The higher the existing level of productivity and the higher the socially recognized real wage, the harder it becomes to raise s′ substantially without seriously lowering real wages — which would (a) provoke a sharp social and political crisis and (b) create a tremendous problem of overproduction, since the mass of use-values (including wage-goods) increases even more rapidly than productivity and accumulation.

Furthermore, once we approach complete automation, s — which is not a proportion but an absolute mass — starts to decline rapidly along with v, as the number of wage-earners and labour-hours diminish steeply. In a fully automated economy, surplus-value would disappear altogether, as living-labour inputs disappear. Hence it would be "absurd to consider formally a 'rate of surplus-value' s/v when surplus-value itself no longer exists."

Other countervailing forces enumerated by Marx, per Mandel:

  • Cheapening of elements of constant capital (raw materials and machinery) — by slowing the growth of q, this slows the decline of the rate of profit.
  • Quickened turnover of capital — since the annual mass of profit is a function of the number of production cycles an identical circulating money capital can perform. Turnover itself depends on a quickened circulation process (faster transport and sale) and a shortened production process.
  • Foreign trade — with capital outflow towards countries with lower organic composition.
  • Extension of capital investment into hitherto non-capitalistically organized branches (e.g., many "service industries" under late capitalism), where the organic composition is initially lower.
  • Lowering of real wages — by raising s′ above and beyond the increase which normally results from productivity growth in the wage-goods industry.

Finally, Mandel notes a point Marx stressed in Chapter 14 but not in Chapter 15: the decline in the rate of surplus-value can be (and normally is) accompanied by a rise in the mass of surplus-value — and hence in the mass of profit. Though this is not itself a countervailing factor against TRPF, it is a countervailing factor against some of TRPF's economic consequences. The capitalist class does not panic and shut up shop when profits rise from $100 billion to $200 billion "just because these $200 billion now represent 'only' a 5 instead of an 11 per cent return on total capital."

Time-spans: Cycle, Long Waves, Secular Trend

Mandel then distinguishes three time-spans over which the law has been analyzed:

  1. The industrial (business) cycle — here there is "wide consensus today between Marxists and academic economists specializing in business-cycle studies" (e.g., W. C. Mitchell, Business Cycles and Their Causes, 1941) that ups and downs of the rate of profit correlate closely with ups and downs of production.
  2. The 'long waves' — the successive periods of quicker and slower growth of the capitalist economy as a whole — a middle span to which Mandel says "hitherto too little attention has been paid." He cites "overwhelming evidence" of three significant increases in the average growth rate: after the revolutions of 1848; around 1893; and at the start of the Second World War in the U.S. and at the end of the forties in Western Europe and Japan. Since rising rate of growth = stepped-up accumulation = requires an upsurge (not decline) in the average rate of profit, Mandel asks what conditions prevailed immediately prior to these turning points. His answer, iterated as the principal thesis of his own work (Late Capitalism, 1975, ch. 4; The Long Waves of Capitalist Development, 1980), is that the "counteracting factors" enumerated by Marx combined in a particular way to neutralize or reverse the tendency for a longer period than normally occurs at a certain stage of the industrial cycle. Crucially, the temporary neutralization "in no way contradicts its general validity": expansionary long waves are regularly followed by depressive long waves in which the tendency manifests itself more strongly and durably than during the normal cycle. "Its actions can be delayed by countervailing factors, but only for it to reassert itself with a vengeance."
  3. The 'secular' time-span — the lifespan of the capitalist mode of production as such, for whose capacity for indefinite survival the law is "a vital question." This introduces the theory of collapse (Zusammenbruchstheorie), to which Mandel returns in the final section.

Against the Critics: Gillman, the Neo-Ricardians, the Okishio Theorem, and the Empirical Argument

Mandel notes that "the very operation of the law (its truth content)" has been increasingly challenged. In a footnote (n. 37), he ridicules the idea that the law "manifests itself exclusively, or mainly, through its negation" — "the position adopted by several Soviet authors" such as S. L. Wygodski, who, before the "second slump," saw the law as confirmed by a tendency towards a rising rate of profit.

Some challenges stem from the appearance of long-term stepped-up postwar growth, which "seemed somehow incompatible — in Marxist terms themselves — with a declining rate of profit." Hence Gillman's (The Falling Rate of Profit, 1957) efforts to discover categories like "realization expenses" (presumably deducted from surplus-value, which is reduced to "surplus-value appropriated by productive capital") or "surplus" — whose growth is supposed to explain why the rate of profit as Marx conceived it stops falling. Mandel observes dryly that "events since 1974–5 have caught up with this type of argument."

More systematic is the neo-Ricardian challenge, whose main theoretical argument is the Okishio theorem: "As every capitalist will introduce machinery only if this increases his rate of profit, how can increased profits for every capitalist lead to a decrease in the rate of profit for capitalists taken together?" Mandel identifies two flaws in this reasoning:

First flaw — the assumption about capitalist motivation. It is not true that every capitalist introduces new machinery only if it increases his rate of profit. As Marx himself points out, this is his voluntary inclination, but he may be forced to introduce new machinery to keep his market share or save his firm from bankruptcy — i.e., to cut his cost price under the pressure of competition, in spite of the effect on his rate of profit. More correct to say: capitalists hesitate to introduce machinery which cuts the amount of profit — but the amount (mass) of profit and the rate of profit are two quite different categories. The former may rise while the latter falls. Mandel cites Georgios Stamatis (n. 40): the decisive difference between unit profit margins (the difference between cost price and sale price per unit produced) and the Marxist concept of the rate of profit, which must take into account the total value of fixed capital used. Stamatis's "tragedy for capital" is that the same capitalist methods of mechanization which lead to lower unit costs and rising unit profit margins "in the end result in an above-average increase in total fixed capital investment — which is one of the forces triggering off a rise in the organic composition of capital in a higher proportion than the rise in the rate of surplus-value, thereby causing the rate of profit to decline." Mandel adds a barbed remark: Stamatis's book is "amazingly schizophrenic" — the entire first part extols the law in painstaking detail, then by "a breathtaking salto mortale" the author concludes that the law no longer applies because capitalism no longer applies "specific capitalist methods of production."

Second flaw — misunderstanding of "laws of motion." These laws "operate independently from, and in spite of, conscious decisions by individual capitalist firms." In fact, they are "the objective and unforeseen effects of conscious decisions by these firms." No capitalist knows in advance what the real result of buying new machinery will be. "Only when the commodities produced with the help of this new machinery have been sold, and several successive annual balance-sheets have been drawn up, will these results become known." Therefore it is perfectly possible — indeed inevitable — that every capitalist's purchase of more machinery is intended to increase his mass and rate of profit, but the final end-result of all these decisions is a situation where the average rate of profit of all is actually reduced. Mandel footnotes Anwar Shaikh's study of Dobb's crisis theory.

The main empirical neo-Ricardian argument: the organic composition of capital is not rising at all over time but remains more or less even — technical progress in the long run is neither essentially labour-saving nor essentially capital-saving, but "neutral" (a notion Mandel traces to Roy Harrod). The alleged index of this stability is the capital/output ratio over time. Mandel responds on two fronts:

  1. The capital/output ratio is "definitely not identical (or parallel) to the organic composition of capital." It erroneously identifies constant capital with fixed capital: the weight of the value of raw materials, which tends to become a growing part of constant (and total) capital, is "completely eliminated from the reasoning."
  2. The "wage bill" in national income conflates variable capital (payment of productive labour) with payment of unproductive labour, which "comes at least partially out of surplus-value." Given the steady growth of unproductive labour under late capitalism, "the distinction is statistically decisive."

Additionally, Shaikh has demonstrated (in "An Introduction to the History of Crisis Theories," 1977) that the "stable capital/output ratio" itself should be seriously challenged from a statistical point of view, and corresponds "to a large extent to an imprecise or wrong use of statistical categories by bourgeois statisticians" — confirmed by initial detailed studies (e.g., R. J. Gordon's work in Survey of Current Business, 1971; and the study of Granou, Baron, and Billandot, whose own calculation, Mandel critically notes, incorporates the rate of surplus-value and thus becomes "to some extent the reciprocal of the rate of profit," rising strongly in the post-war period).

Mandel concedes: an empirical demonstration of rising organic composition on a macro-economic basis (from national income and GNP statistics) is "not easy to provide" due to the lack of transparency of real-value relations when measured in current market prices. But — and here's the strategic move — a close corollary is the share of labour costs in total annual production costs. Here "we are on much more solid statistical ground," since numerous monographs examine this relation for separate branches over time. Mandel's rhetorical conclusion: "One would have a hard time discovering a single branch of production in which labour costs constitute a larger part of total current (annual) production costs today than they did on the eve of the Second World War, or at the beginning of the twentieth century — let alone a century or a century-and-a-half ago." Despite tendencies to cheapen machinery and raw materials, "the basic trend of long-term capitalist growth and technical progress has indeed been a labour-saving one." And this is precisely what the terms "mechanization" and "growing automation" express. Marx's theoretical achievement was to stress this trend at a time when it was "scarcely recognized as historically decisive for the capitalist mode of production."

Marxist Theories of Crisis: Against Mono-Causality

Mandel now turns to the most fragmented area: crisis theory. Marx never left a completed, fully worked-out theory of crisis; his observations on the industrial cycle and crises of overproduction are scattered across several books, articles, and letters. It is "tempting" — and several contemporary Marxists (David Yaffe, Paul Mattick) have indeed done so — to see TRPF as Marx's main contribution to explaining crises of overproduction. Mandel's verdict on this is yes and no.

Within the industrial cycle, "the ups and downs of the rate of profit are closely correlated with the ups and downs of production." But this statement alone is not sufficient to provide a causal explanation of the crisis. It can be — and has been — misunderstood mechanically as: crises are "caused" by insufficient surplus-value production → capital cannot be sufficiently valorized → cut-down of current investment → reduced employment → cumulative reduction of income, sales, investment, employment, etc. → until the fall in employment and devalorization of capital have raised the rate of surplus-value and reduced the mass of capital, allowing the rate of profit to rise again → cumulative regrowth.

Mandel's critique of this "vulgar sense" of crisis-by-TRPF-decline:

  1. It is wrong because it confuses the impossibility of valorizing additionally accumulated capital with the impossibility of valorizing all previously invested capital (cf. below, pp. 360–61). It also identifies fluctuations in investment decisions of capitalist firms with fluctuations of current surplus-value production — but the former may continue to grow while the latter declines, and vice versa.
  2. The main weakness is its concentration on the sphere of production alone, founded on "a confusion about the very nature of the commodity and of commodity production." Like Say's loi des débouchés, it tacitly assumes there is no specific value realization problem, only one of surplus-value production. This in turn assumes production for barter, not production for sale, and that somehow, "at least at a macro-economic level, all value produced is automatically realized."

Marx himself explicitly refuted this (Mandel quotes from Chapter 15 at length, "my italics" following Marx): "The conditions for immediate exploitation and for the realization of that exploitation are not identical. Not only are they separate in time and space, they are also separate in theory. The former is restricted only by the society's productive forces, the latter by the proportionality between the different branches of production, and by the society's power of consumption. And this is determined neither by the absolute power of production nor by the absolute power of consumption but rather by the power of consumption within a given framework of antagonistic conditions of distribution… It is further restricted by the drive for accumulation…"

  1. It is dangerous from the standpoint of defending the working class, because the conclusion might be drawn that the crisis could be overcome and employment rise "if only real wages were to be cut and surplus-value (profits) thereby automatically increased." This confronts the working class and trade unions with an agonizing choice between defending real wages and fighting unemployment — making labor responsible for the loss of jobs. Reformist proponents of class collaboration "are only too ready to come forward with arguments of this kind, calling upon the workers to make the necessary sacrifices to 'save jobs'…" Empirical experience has shown repeatedly that this "is not borne out empirically." It represents "an ideological weapon designed to impose the burden of the crisis on the working class and assist an increase in the rate of surplus-value." 'Profit squeeze' theories (e.g., Glyn and Sutcliffe, 1972) involve "a similar danger of misuse." (Mandel also footnotes Maurice Dobb's Political Economy and Capitalism — Dobb's assumption that capitalists introduce new machinery only when wages rise is "not the same as the 'profit squeeze' theory, but it is not far from it," and Shaikh has correctly criticized these assumptions.)

Even defenders of the falling-rate explanation who reply indignantly that "the decline in the rate of profit is a function of the rising organic composition of capital, not of a decline in the rate of surplus-value," and who insist that the rate of surplus-value rises until the very eve of the crisis but cannot offset q — even they "forget" that the rate of profit is a function of both q and s′. Except at starvation wages (below the physiological minimum, which "no longer exists in any industrialized country"), a cut in real wages always implies a rise in surplus-value produced, hence a higher rate of profit than before the cut. So we are "back at square one: to argue that the crisis is exclusively caused by insufficient surplus-value production is to assist the employers' argument that it can, at least partially, be overcome by a cut in real wages."

Mandel's general principle: critique of any mono-causal explanation of crises. In Marxist theory, crises of overproduction are simultaneously crises of over-accumulation of capital and crises of overproduction of commodities. "The former cannot be explained without pointing to the latter; the latter cannot be understood without referring to the former." The crisis can only be overcome if there occurs simultaneously a rise in the rate of profit and an expansion of the market — which "disarms both the employers' and the reformists' arguments."

He then catalogues the three main variants of mono-causal crisis theory:

Variant 1: The pure disproportionality theory. Basic cause of the cycle and ensuing crisis is capitalist anarchy of production — the fact that capitalist investment decisions cannot spontaneously lead to equilibrium conditions (the correct proportion of value fractions produced and money flows generated in departments I and II, which Marx defined in Volume 2). Hence the unavoidable breakdown of equilibrium. Proponents: Mikhail Tugan-Baranovsky, Rudolf Hilferding, Nikolai Bukharin (with Bukharin "a bit more cautious," taking into account the restricting force of limited mass consumption). The conclusions are obvious and dangerous: if monopolies could 'organize' investment — a "general cartel" (Hilferding) — there would be no crises of overproduction; "capitalism without crises." (Mandel cites Tony Cliff as sharing this conviction.) As Roman Rosdolsky (1977) has pointed out, however, these theorists overlook the fact that the disproportion between production and consumption — the tendency of capitalism to develop productive forces in an unrestricted way while it imposes strict limits upon mass consumption — is inherent to capitalism, independent of the disproportional development of departments I and II due to competition and anarchy of production. The "grotesque consequences" are best exemplified by Tugan-Baranovsky himself, who "seriously argued — and demonstrated 'mathematically' — that department I could develop completely independently from department II, to the point where the output of consumer goods would tend to fall towards zero, without such a development causing any crisis whatsoever."

Variant 2: The pure under-consumption by the masses theory. Basic cause: the gap between output (or productive capacity) and mass consumption (workers' real wages or purchasing power); crises essentially take the form of overproduction of commodities in department II. Over-accumulation and overproduction in department I appear as results of this overproduction/over-capacity in consumer goods. Non-Marxist ancestors: Thomas Malthus, Sismondi, the Russian Narodniks. Marxist proponents: Karl Kautsky, Rosa Luxemburg, Nathalia Moszkowska, Fritz Sternberg, Paul Sweezy. Weakness: the basic assumption — "not always clearly understood, but at least clearly expressed, by Sweezy" — that "somehow there is a fixed proportion between the development of department I and the development of the productive capacity of department II." Since the growth of organic composition and of s′ both increase purchasing power for means of production more strongly than for consumer goods, the conclusion is obvious: an unsaleable residue of consumer goods. But this assumption is logically unproven and contrary to the very nature of capitalist growth, characterized by growing mechanization or (borrowing the term from von Böhm-Bawerk) greater 'roundaboutness' of production. Capitalist growth does imply a larger proportion of total output taking the form of means of production — although not accompanied by an absolute decline in the production of consumer goods or stagnation of department II capacity. Once this is understood, neither the growth of c/v nor the growth of s/v need automatically lead to overproduction of consumer goods. They will do so only if the fraction output I / output II grows more slowly than the fraction demand for means of production / demand for consumer goods. But nothing in the capitalist mode of production necessitates this — it cannot be mathematically or logically demonstrated.

The political danger in under-consumption theories (from which Mandel says Luxemburg "completely avoided" it) is that they can lead to reformist conclusions not unlike the "harmonicist" implications of disproportionality theories: "The former tend to think that capitalism could avoid crisis if real wages were larger, or if the government distributed additional 'purchasing power' in the form of social security and unemployment disbursements — i.e., 'redistributed' national income in favour of the workers, 're-transformed' a part of surplus-value into additional indirect wages." What these "solutions" overlook: capitalist production is production for profit. Any sizable redistribution of national income in favour of workers on the eve or in early stages of a crisis, when the rate of profit is already declining, means a further decline in that rate of profit through a reduction of s′. Under these conditions, capitalists will not increase investment, "even if sales of previously produced stocks of consumer goods go up." The depression continues. (Mandel cites neo-Keynesians in Britain, France, West Germany as the holders of such views; cf. Alternative Wirtschaftspolitik, 1979.)

Variant 3: The pure over-accumulation theory. Main reason for crisis: insufficient mass of surplus-value produced compared to the total amount of accumulated capital. Mandel has dealt with the weakness above (confusion of dimensions) and its dangerous implications for the proletarian class struggle.

The demographic sub-variant (associated with Makatoh Itoh; historically with Otto Bauer): after long periods of prosperity, the reserve army of labour tends to disappear, causing real wages to rise to a point where they abruptly reduce s′ and hence the rate of profit. This is the border case Marx calls, in Chapter 15, 'absolute over-accumulation of capital.' Mandel's reply: while this cannot be excluded from a general theoretical point of view, in the real history of capitalism — under conditions of extensive international labour mobility and even vaster potential migrations from underdeveloped countries (he cites ~1 million illegal immigrants a year from Mexico and Central America to the US, with about 15 million unemployed in those two regions alone — plus fifty million housewives not gainfully employed) — "any such 'population pressure' on capitalism seems centuries removed from us." It also "greatly underestimates capitalism's capacity rapidly to reconstruct a reserve army of labour, by concentrating on rationalization investments which are macro-economically employment-reducing." Confirmation: throughout the 1970s, the total unemployed in OECD countries doubled from ten million in 1970 to twenty million in 1980, while the number of jobs destroyed by technical progress was far larger — millions of immigrant workers had to return home, millions of women and young people dropped out of the labour market, and many productive workers were transformed into unproductive ones. A more sophisticated version by Ferenc Jánossy (1966) sees in the inability of capitalism to develop enough skilled (especially highly skilled) workers a bottleneck which pushes up real wages at the end of 'prosperity'; here again, the flexibility of capital — in speeding up skill formation (including at factory level) and in reducing the need for highly skilled labour via technology — is "greatly underestimated."

Mandel's own synthetic critique of the over-accumulation theory as self-sufficient: Its proponents argue that as long as accumulation proceeds smoothly, consumption by final consumers automatically grows (more wage-labour employed, generally at increasing wages; unproductive consumption out of surplus-value also grows); hence no glut of consumer goods can appear so long as TRPF has not significantly slowed accumulation. The first part is true, as far as it goes. But the conclusion does not follow. The analysis proves only that consumption grows as long as accumulation grows. It does not prove that consumption grows in the same proportion as the productive capacity of department II. Indeed, the combined operation of rising organic composition in department II and rising s′ makes it probable that (at least periodically) consumption, while growing, grows less than department II productive capacity. In which case a glut of consumer goods can occur before accumulation has slowed down in the economy as a whole.

Similarly, the assumption that a slow-down in investment triggers the crisis before any overproduction of commodities manifests itself is "in the best of cases only one possible variant of the crisis scenario." Current investment decisions depend on two variables: past profit realizations (available surplus-value for accumulation) and future profit expectations. About the current rate of profit — a macro-economic end-result — capitalist firms have "no way of knowing anything precise, as long as their own and other capitalists' annual balance-sheets have not been drawn up." It is possible that past profit realization does not yet reflect a decline in the rate of profit, but investment is still cut because of growing signs of glut or over-capacity. Conversely, past profit realization may already reflect the beginning of a decline, but investment will still be expanding because the firm believes it can expand sales. Profit expectations include estimates about expected market conditions and market shares — hence a tendency for investment to 'overshoot' even after the rate of profit has started to decline. Many firms may believe they can increase their market share or profit from technological advantages by expanding. "All these decisions cannot stop the rate of profit from declining. But they can produce growing overproduction of commodities before accumulation of capital actually slows down."

Mandel's Positive Synthesis: A Multi-Causal Model of the Cycle

Mandel asserts that "elements of a correct theory of capitalist crisis are, of course, present in all three mono-causal explanations," and they have to be integrated. His integration proceeds by distinguishing successive forms taken over time by the accumulation of capital:

Phase 1 — Boom: Business is brisk, current output is easily sold (demand seems stronger than supply), profits are high. Investment boom → runs rapidly into bottlenecks in both sub-sections of department I: machinery and equipment and raw materials. Both sub-sections of department I are "by their very nature, less flexible in adapting rapidly to demand than is department II." Hence additional investment, capital accumulation, occurs on a larger and larger scale in department I. More means of production have to be produced to produce additional means of production for producing additional consumer goods. Good profit expectations plus high profit realizations motivate the boom. There is a shift of investment toward department I. An uneven development (disproportion) between departments I and II is set in motion. (Mandel notes in footnote 71 that Marx saw in the massively bunched introduction of fixed capital at 7–10 year intervals both a main reason for the periodicity of the cycle and the determining factor for its average duration; on "overshooting" he cites Hicks, Harrod, Domar.)

Phase 2 — Simultaneous phenomena at the point of crisis: On the one hand, the additional means of production produced come into the production process only after a time-lag. When they enter, they increase productive capacity in both departments "by leaps and bounds." But the relatively high rates of profit and investment imply that real wages and consumer-goods demand (from capitalists/connoisseurs and workers) could not have developed in the same proportion as this sudden increase in productive capacity — hence a tendency to increasing overproduction (or over-capacity), in the first place in department II. On the other hand, the massive introduction of new means of production occurs with new up-dated techniques characterized by a basically labour-saving bias — i.e., by an increased organic composition of capital. This presses down the rate of profit, especially since under boom conditions the rate of surplus-value cannot increase in the same proportion, or even does not increase at all. Hence a tendency to over-accumulation: part of newly accumulated capital can no longer be invested at the average rate of profit, or is not invested at all, "pushed towards speculation, etc." Credit expansion, for a certain time, "covers the gap. But it can only postpone the crash, not avoid it." Overproduction now tends to spread from department II to department I. Growing overproduction of commodities (over-capacity in a growing number of branches), combined with growing over-accumulation, "must of necessity lead to sharp cut-backs in productive investment." Disproportionality between the two departments now jumps from an 'over-extension' of department I into an 'under-development' of that department. Investment falls more quickly than current output. (Note in n. 74: this is not an absolute rule — overproduction could start in certain sub-sectors of department I; the two latest crises — 1974–5 and 1979–80 — both started in automobiles and housing, i.e., durable consumer goods, sub-sectors of department II.)

Phase 3 — The Crash. It can (but does not necessarily) take the initial form of a credit and banking crash. There ensues a general collapse of commodity prices (expressed in gold), together with a decline in output and employment. There is a general devalorization of capital, resulting simultaneously from (a) the collapse of prices (commodity capital), (b) a large number of bankruptcies, and (c) a decline in the value of fixed capital and raw-material stocks of surviving firms. Mandel emphasizes the theoretical point: this general collapse of prices is nothing but the adaptation of market prices and prices of production (through a lower average rate of profit) to the general lowering in the value of the average commodity — which is the unavoidable outcome of the general increase of investment, organic composition of capital, and average productivity of labour during the previous period. Capitalists try to postpone "this hour of reckoning as long as possible — whence the over-extension of credit, speculation, over-trading, etc. on the eve of the crash. But they cannot postpone it indefinitely."

Phase 4 — Recuperation. For the system as a whole, the crash's effects are "healthy, however nasty they may be for individual capitalists." General devalorization of capital is not accompanied by a proportional reduction in the mass of surplus-value produced. Equivalently: an identical mass of surplus-value can now valorize a smaller total capital. Hence the decline in the rate of profit can be stopped and even reversed. Three mechanisms:

  1. Large-scale reconstitution of the reserve army of labour during crisis/depression → vigorous increase in s′, not only via speed-ups but even via a cut in real wages → higher rate of profit.
  2. Raw material prices fall more than the prices of finished goods — part of constant capital becomes cheaper, slowing the rise of q, again pushing up the average rate of profit on industrial capital.
  3. Once stocks have been sufficiently depleted and current production sufficiently cut for demand again to outstrip supply (especially in department II), a new cycle of stepped-up accumulation and productive investment can begin.

The conclusion Mandel draws: TRPF "is less a direct explanation for crises of overproduction properly speaking, than a revelation of the basic mechanism of the industrial cycle as such" — "an uncovering of the specifically capitalist, i.e., uneven, disharmonious, mode of economic growth, which unavoidably leads to successive phases of declining rates of profit, and recuperation of the rate of profit as a result, precisely, of the consequences of the previous decline."

The Textual Basis for Multi-Causality

Mandel argues that "there can be little doubt that this multi-causal explanation… corresponds to Marx's own conviction, at least as expressed here in Volume 3." He cites three further passages, alongside the one already quoted (p. 614–15):

Passage 1 (p. 614–15): Considering a simplified society of only industrial capitalists and wage-labourers, and leaving aside changes in price preventing large portions of total capital from being replaced in average proportions (which, "in the overall context of the reproduction process… particularly as developed by credit, must recurrently bring about a situation of general stagnation") and leaving aside "fraudulent businesses and speculative dealings that the credit system fosters" — even then, "a crisis would be explicable only in terms of a disproportion in production between different branches and a disproportion between the consumption of the capitalists themselves and their accumulation." But as things actually are, the replacement of the capitals invested depends on the consumption capacity of the non-productive classes; and the consumption capacity of the workers is restricted partly by the laws governing wages and partly by the fact that they are employed only as long as they can be employed at a profit. "The ultimate reason for all real crises always remains the poverty and restricted consumption of the masses, in the face of the drive of capitalist production to develop the productive forces as if only the absolute consumption capacity of society set a limit to them."

Passage 2 (p. 367): "Periodically, however, too much is produced in the way of means of labour and means of subsistence, too much to function as means for exploiting the workers at a given rate of profit. Too many commodities are produced for the value contained in them, and the surplus-value included in this value, to be realized under the conditions of distribution given by capitalist production, and to be transformed back into new capital, i.e. it is impossible to accomplish this process without ever recurrent explosions."

Passage 3 (p. 419–20): A concrete account of how crisis emerges through the circuit of credit and circulation: "The manufacturer may actually sell to the exporter, and the exporter to his foreign customer; the importer may sell his raw materials to the manufacturer, and the manufacturer sell his products to the wholesaler, etc. But at some particular imperceptible point the commodity lies unsold; or else the total stocks of producers and middlemen gradually become too high. It is precisely then that consumption is generally at flood tide, partly because one industrial capitalist sets a series of others in motion, partly because the workers these employ, being fully occupied, have more than usual to spend… And besides this, there is also… a constant circulation between one constant capital and another (even leaving aside the accelerated accumulation) which is initially independent of individual consumption… for production of constant capital never takes place for its own sake, but simply because more of it is needed in those spheres of production whose products do go into individual consumption. This can continue quite happily for a good while, stimulated by prospective demand… The crisis occurs as soon as the returns of those merchants who sell far afield (or who have accumulated stocks at home) become so slow and sparse that the banks press for payment for commodities bought, or bills fall due before any resale takes place."

Credit and the Rate of Interest

Mandel next moves to the role of credit, connecting it to the doctrine of Volume 2. Volume 2 stressed the importance of previous accumulation (and presence) of money-capital, its periodic injection into and outflow from the operations of productive capital, for making expanded reproduction possible for "capital in general." Volume 3 stresses the key importance of credit for "many capitals," i.e., for the fluctuations of the industrial cycle under competition.

Doctrinal sequence: the appearance of a generally known average rate of profit unavoidably leads to an equalization of the rate of interest too. Surplus-value is first split between profit for entrepreneurial capital (industrial, commercial, banking, and agricultural entrepreneur profit as distinct from passive landowners) and interest. Through the capitalist banking system, all available money reserves — savings, non-invested surplus-value, and idle money capital — are transformed into functioning capital, i.e., lent to capitalist firms actually operating (employing wage-labour) in production or circulation. In this way, capitalists operate with much more capital than they personally own. Capital accumulation can proceed at a much quicker pace than if each firm could only enlarge reproduction on the basis of its own realized profits.

A first-sight illusion: This expansion of credit seems to accentuate TRPF. The total amount of profit distributed among all capitalist firms is now lower than the total surplus-value produced — the difference being exactly the total amount of interest paid to passive owners of money-capital (not to be confused with profits of banks, i.e., the average profits on their own capital, not on their deposits). But Mandel says this is a false impression. The average rate of profit is the division of the total amount of surplus-value produced by the total amount of social capital. If, as a result of division of labour among capitalists or over-accumulation, part of that capital is not itself directly productive — not engaged in the direct production of surplus-value — this does not change its nature as capital, i.e., "value constantly on the look-out for an accretion of value."

According to Marx here, the effects of credit (like those of trade) on TRPF are opposite to what at first appears. They tend to put a brake on, or even reverse the tendency, through three simultaneous mechanisms:

  1. Faster rotation of capital. Trade and credit allow capital to rotate more rapidly — increasing the number of productive cycles through which a single sum of money capital can pass in, say, one year — thereby increasing the mass of surplus-value and the annual rate of profit (same surplus-value produced during each cycle, other things equal). This is why industrialists are ready to allow commercial and banking capital to share in the general distribution of entrepreneurial profit, "although neither commercial nor banking capital produces surplus-value." Such capital "does not produce surplus-value itself, but it helps industrial capital and agricultural capital produce additional surplus-value." (Mandel explains: industrial capital rotates faster if wholesale and retail merchants buy produced commodities immediately from industrial capitalists and keep them in stock until the 'last customer' appears — this division of labour inside the capitalist class explains why industrialists abandon part of surplus-value to the merchants as commercial profit.)

  2. Faster concentration of capital. By enlarging the scope and tempo of accumulation in the productive sphere (over and above profits directly owned by industrialists and capitalist farmers), commerce and trade accelerate the concentration of capital, thereby stimulating technical progress and the production of relative surplus-value — which again counteracts TRPF.

  3. Joint-stock companies (corporations). By this device, credit creates a situation in which a large part of capital, owned by stockholders, is not expected to receive the average rate of profit at all, but is content with the average rate of interest only. Hence the average rate of entrepreneurial profit is much higher than it would be if all (or the largest part) of capital were directly entrepreneurial capital, i.e., had to receive the average rate of profit.

Beyond these three, credit has structural functions: (a) The greater flexibility of money capital not tied to any specific firm or branch is one of the main reasons the equalization of the rate of profit can so easily occur and be recognized under capitalism — i.e., why social capital remains relatively mobile in spite of growing investment in fixed, relatively immobile capital. (b) Parallel to the reserve army of labour, these huge reserves of money capital are preconditions for sudden, rapid phases of feverish expansion characteristic of the industrial cycle and of capitalist growth as uneven and disharmonious. (c) The banking system plays in part the role of a social clearing-house, constantly transferring capital from branches with stagnating or declining demand to branches with growing demand not satisfied by current production/capacity; deviations of distinct rates of profit from the average are the guiding mechanism. (d) Credit both expands accumulation "to its utmost limits" and functions as "the main lever for over-speculation, over-trading and overproduction."

The Desynchronization of the Credit Cycle

Mandel then lays out a precise chronology of the credit cycle and the rate of interest relative to the industrial cycle:

  • Recovery / initial upsurge: Money capital relatively abundant; self-financing high; rate of interest relatively low; entrepreneurial profit above average. (Footnote 81 clarifies: under permanent inflation of paper money this applies to the real, not nominal, rate of interest. Real rate = nominal rate minus rate of inflation. The extent of credit inflation under late capitalism is indicated by "several lengthy periods of negative 'real' rates of interest in key capitalist countries.")
  • Boom peak, overheating, crash: Money capital scarcer and scarcer; self-financing declines precipitately; demand for money capital grows constantly; the rate of interest grows "by leaps and bounds, not in spite of but as a function of the decline in the average rate of profit." Firms now borrow not to expand business but to escape bankruptcy — not to gain additional entrepreneurial profits but to save their capital. At this precise moment, the rate of interest can actually be above the rate of entrepreneurial profit (which "cannot, of course, 'normally' be the case").
  • Crisis / depression proper: Investment declines steeply; demand for credit collapses; rate of interest slides rapidly, which helps entrepreneurial profit slowly to pick up again.

Marx's Theory of Surplus Profits

Mandel now argues that "the fact that Marx's theory of differential land rent in reality represents a special case of a more general theory of surplus profits has not hitherto been sufficiently appreciated." And it is all the more strange, since Marx makes the point explicitly in Volume 3, in several passages of Parts One and Two, and returns to it in Parts Six and Seven.

The basic approach is a straightforward application of the labour theory of value. Whether labour expended in producing a given commodity is recognized as average socially necessary labour is not a simple physical matter of an actual number of labour-hours expended — of a given fraction of society's total labour potential being used for a given commodity. It is a function of: - the total amount of labour expended in all the units producing that commodity, compared to the total amount of labour society wishes to devote to it; - the relation between the productivity of labour in the given productive unit and the average productivity of the branch as a whole.

Mandel footnotes a key internal debate here. Rubin correctly denied a purely physiological (reified) definition of "abstract labour" but contended it is quantifiable, based on labour-time and intensity. Mandel agrees with Rubin against Colliot-Thélène (n. 82): the alleged contradiction between defining "socially necessary labour" both by average productivity and by the relation between branch output and socially recognized needs is not a contradiction but a difference between value production (sphere of production) and value realization (sphere of circulation). The law of value adapts distribution of the labour force to social needs post festum — but labour expended in the production process is value-producing. It is just that value produced has been redistributed: the equivalent of some of it is not received by those who own the commodities thus produced. He cites Marx (p. 774 below) on the law of value applied to the total products of particular spheres rather than individual commodities — "so that not only is no more labour-time devoted to each individual commodity than necessary, but out of the total social labour-time only the proportionate quantity needed is devoted to the various types of commodity."

Three cases of current production distinguished by Marx (as Mandel reconstructs):

Case 1 — Normal capital mobility in a branch of output. Inflows and outflows of capital, regulated by price oscillations inducing profit-rate oscillations, normally balance social supply and demand. Then equalization of the rate of profit applies to the branch. - Firms at average productivity of the branch → average rate of profit. - Firms below average productivity → less than average profit; risk being crowded out in crisis/depression. - Firms above average productivity (technological advances) → temporary surplus profit, i.e., profit over and above the average, resulting from the difference between individual costs of production and average costs of production in the branch. But this surplus profit will generally disappear in crisis/depression, when the new technology becomes generalized and the average productivity of labour (value of the commodity) is adapted to the initially higher productivity.

Case 2 — Branches with structurally stagnant or declining demand (i.e., 'outmoded' branches, structural overproduction). - Only firms at above-average productivity receive the average rate of profit. - Firms at average productivity receive less than average. - Firms at below-average productivity sell at a loss and go out of business. - In general, when capital mobility is normal, such branches become 'normalized' (revert to Case 1) even before a general crisis of overproduction occurs, through massive closures.

Case 3 — Structurally (or institutionally) determined scarcity. Capital influx is hampered or prevented by natural or artificial monopolies. There is a long-term preponderance of demand over supply. - Firms at the lowest productivity still receive the average rate of profit — they determine the price of production (or value) of the commodity. - Firms at higher productivity (average or above) receive a long-term surplus profit protected by the monopoly — by the powerful obstacle hindering influx of additional capital. This surplus profit does not disappear even in crisis/depression, though it is lowered in absolute terms by the fall in the average rate of profit.

These monopoly surplus profits are called differential rents. In Volume 3, three instances are distinguished: land rent; mineral rents; technological rents. (Mandel explains he uses "technological rent" as an extension of Marx's land rent, for conditions of 'artificial monopoly' due to technological monopolies.) Land rent divides into agricultural land rent and urban land rent.

Definitions:

  • Natural monopolies are determined by the fact that access to natural resources necessary for production (from a use-value standpoint) is limited, and these are not reproducible at will by capital — applies to land as such (especially a given use-value: desired relative fertility, desired location); to mineral sources; to climatological preconditions for certain use-values (cotton, natural rubber, tropical fruits, etc.).

  • Artificial monopolies are determined by limits in capital mobility related not to natural conditions but to conditions arising from the results of specific stages (forms) of accumulation: (a) concentration of capital (if starting a new firm with minimum profitability requires £500 million or $1,000 million, that's an evident obstacle to entry for most capitalists); (b) monopoly rights in patents, inventions, or research in certain new fields (or qualitative advantages in applying them); (c) organized practices by a small number of firms to keep out potential competitors; and so on.

As clearly follows, natural and artificial monopolies are always relative, never absolute. Land is not reproducible — but possibilities for capital investment on existing land can be vastly expanded; internationally, tremendous areas of potentially agricultural land are not yet exploited; capitalist technology can be pushed to production without land. Mineral resources are finite — but synthetic production of originally natural raw materials (fibres, rubber, oil) is not finite to anything like the same degree.

The dynamics of monopolies: (a) The bigger the initial capital outlays for profitable production, the smaller the number of potential new competitors. (b) The higher the surplus profits, the stronger the inducement for 'many capitals' to band together and risk huge initial investments to get a slice of the cake. (c) The more decisive advances in technology lead to stable surplus profits over longer periods, the stronger the pressure for potential competitors to leap ahead by a new technological revolution. Examples: the challenge to IBM's quasi-monopoly by micro-processors and Japanese trusts in fifth-generation computers.

Mandel's provisional conclusion: "All monopoly surplus profits are always limited in time and, in the long run, tend to disappear, and… commodities produced in initially monopolized branches tend to be exchanged at their prices of production." Whether this "long run," at least for industrial products under monopoly capitalism (since ~1890), is the 'long wave' — as Mandel hypothesized in Late Capitalism — or not, remains a subject for further investigation. In footnote 89, Mandel defends the idea — advanced in his Marxist Economic Theory and defended in Late Capitalism — of an equalization of surplus profits side by side with the equalization of average profit, implying the co-existence during a certain time-span of two average rates of profit, one in the monopolized and one in the non-monopolized sectors. Marx himself, Mandel says, explicitly proposes it in Volume 3 (p. 1001).

The Relative Character of Monopoly and the Role of Structural Scarcity

To grasp the relative (never absolute) nature of any monopoly, and hence the limited nature in time of any surplus profit, "it is necessary to reintroduce into our analysis the phenomenon of structural scarcity which was its starting-point." Marx (p. 279): "If the demand is so strong, however, that it does not contract when price is determined by the value of commodities produced in the worst conditions, then it is these that determine the market value." Only if obstructions to capital mobility (obstacles to increases in output) create conditions under which social demand for the goods is for long periods higher than or equal to the total amount of commodities produced (including those produced under the lowest conditions of productivity/soil fertility) will units of production with lower costs realize surplus profits in the form of differential rents.

Once social demand for the goods of the monopolized branch recedes, stagnates, or grows more slowly than production even under relative monopoly, differential rent will tend to be reduced and surplus profits to decline. (This does not mean they disappear completely, where the monopoly is natural, as long as differences in fertility subsist.)

The historical example of agriculture: The huge increases in average productivity of agricultural labour — "which have been one of the main characteristics of the development of capitalism in the twentieth century, and have indeed exceeded the rate of growth of industrial productivity of labour" — have completely altered the demand/supply relation for basic foodstuffs in advanced capitalist countries. The situation of structural scarcity has been transformed into a situation of structural overproduction, co-determined by the decreasing place of food expenditure in total consumers' expenditure when real incomes rise (Engel's Law). Not only has differential rent therefore been strongly contracting, but large tracts of farm land have been reconverted into pastures, and pasture into forests or waste land. Massive closures of coal pits in the fifties, sixties, and early seventies, when oil was much cheaper than coal, are a parallel development in mining, with a correlated decline of differential coal-mining rents.

But the process can be reversed. (a) When social demand — mediated through market prices — suddenly surges beyond output for ten or twenty years, structural scarcity reappears and differential rents massively reappear. Example: gold production since the collapse of Bretton Woods, when imperialist Central Banks could not maintain gold at $35 or $42 (35 SDR) an ounce (footnote 92 explains SDRs as based on a common basket of currencies, constantly re-appreciated against the dollar since 1971, so the "official" IMF gold price rose from $35 to $42). The upsurge of the free-market gold price — to $100, then $200, finally more than $600 an ounce — made many 'marginal' mines profitable again and led to a feverish development of gold-mining investment. Concretely (Dreyfus study, Le Monde, 29 Jan 1980): the more productive among the twenty main South African gold mines were producing at ~$95/oz at end-1979 (the single most productive at $64/oz); the less productive of these twenty at ~$200/oz (highest single figure $265/oz). This gives a differential rent of more than $100 an ounce for the former category as against the latter, once gold sells at more than $200 + average profit — say, above $240–250/oz.

The deeper reason for the coexistence of monopoly tendencies and their periodic decline: surplus profits are deducted from the total amount of profit distributed among all capitalists who participate in the equalization of the rate of profit. They tend to reduce the general cake distributed among all bourgeois except the monopolists. As there is a tendency for the average rate of profit to decline, monopolies of all kinds — including monopoly property in land — tend to accentuate that decline. Hence the pressure of capital to overcome natural or artificial barriers to mobility, to reduce or eliminate monopolistic situations. The outcome of this tug-of-war is "a function of the relative strength of different layers of the ruling class." In the twentieth century, the pressure has been more successful with regard to absentee capitalist landlords (separate from capitalist agricultural entrepreneurs) than with regard to industrial, transport, or mining monopolies — though not a few cases of collapse of monopolistic surplus profits can be cited in these realms too.

This pressure operates on both possible hypotheses about the source of monopoly surplus profits — whether they are actually produced inside the monopolized branches, or are (in several cases) transfers of value from non-monopolized to monopolized sectors. For in both hypotheses, the mass of surplus-value to be shared among all capitalists who do not enjoy rents is substantially lower than it would have been with 'perfect' mobility of capital into all branches — i.e., their average rate of profit has been lowered. And when this accentuates a tendency already operating for deeper reasons, "the counter-pressure will be all the more powerful."

The Specificity of Capitalist Agriculture

Mandel next examines Volume 3's treatment of agriculture. Marx extends a notion stressed at the end of Volume 1: the key importance of private appropriation of land — its transformation into the private property of a limited class — for the very birth, consolidation, and expansion of the capitalist mode of production. This mode presupposes the modern proletariat, which "has no access to means of production and subsistence and is, therefore, under the economic compulsion to sell its labour-power." Means of subsistence = primarily food, which can be produced with minimal means wherever access to land is free. Hence the creation of the modern proletariat hinges, to a large extent, on barring free access to land to people possessing no capital.

Historical process: Private appropriation of land in Western Europe mainly took place between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries and culminated in the sale of village 'free' land reserves (communal lands) unleashed by the French Revolution (cf. Otto Bauer, Der Kampf um Wald und Weide, 1925). It was repeated throughout the last part of the nineteenth and the whole of the twentieth century in Eastern Europe, the Americas, the Middle East, Africa, Japan, and South-East Asia. The most repulsive form of forcible separation of the original population from its fertile land reserves occurred in Eastern and Southern Africa; it continues today in countries like Brazil, Iran, the Philippines, and Mexico (despite partial achievements of the 1910–17 Revolution).

But the interrelation between consolidation of capitalism, accumulation, and struggle against TRPF is much more complex than this compulsion to transform all land into private property.

The specific historical form in much of Europe and Japan: generalization of private property in land initially took the form of ownership by a social class separate and apart from functioning capitalists (capitalist farmers, entrepreneurs). These capitalist landowners (distinct from semi-feudal or feudal landlords) barred entry to their land by the capitalist class unless they received a special 'unearned' income in the form of absolute land rent (same rule applies to rentier-proprietors of urban land vis-à-vis building-industry capitalists). Elsewhere, different layers of the ruling class appropriated the 'surplus' land: foreign settlers (Robin Murray's concept of 'founder's rent' — Mandel thinks Murray is right, at least with regard to overseas countries with above-average fertile land compared to Western Europe, but he thinks Murray gives excessive weight to such 'rent' in explaining international migrations, capitalist expansionism, and the origins of imperialism); or local landowners, merchants, usurers. There are some — rather rare — cases of combinations of both processes.

In all cases where actual ownership of land became separated from capitalist farming, absolute land rent appeared. Like differential land rent, absolute rent is a fraction of total surplus-value, deducted from the residue to be divided among all capitalist entrepreneurs and owners of money capital. This deduction is "all the more onerous in that, contrary to differential rent, it is not open to erosion or equalization through the laws of motion of the capitalist mode of production properly speaking" (competition, technical progress, increase in organic composition, concentration and centralization). It thus "puts a brake upon capital accumulation in agriculture." Hence the organic drive of capital to eliminate the separation of landownership and capitalist farming: gradually transforming landowners into entrepreneurs, and land-renting farmers into (a) a majority of wage-earners and (b) a minority of landowning farmers. The transformation of a situation of structural scarcity of food into structural plenty (latent overproduction) in most industrialized countries powerfully assists this process. (Footnote 96 gives the evidence: OECD note, Feb 1980 — total wheat stocks in imperialist countries averaged more than 50 million tonnes in every single year between 1970/71 and 1979/80; total end-year stocks of butter and skimmed milk in imperialist countries rose from 289,000 tonnes in 1970 to 1.4 million tonnes in 1979.) This represents "a tendential disappearance of absolute rent in the imperialist countries."

Deeper law of value at work: Source of absolute land rent is the lower organic composition of capital in agriculture as compared with industry — i.e., the higher mass of surplus-value produced by agricultural labourers as compared with industrial labourers employed by the same amount of total capital. The barrier of landownership separated from capitalist enterprise makes it possible for landowners to prevent this supplementary surplus-value from being sucked into the general process of equalization of profit. Thus rent is indeed an obstacle to the full flowering of capitalist agriculture — a source of relative backwardness of agriculture compared to industry (productivity of labour in agriculture vs. industry). But Marx, who himself stressed this relative backwardness, noted it was not a fixed and final characteristic of the mode of production but could sooner or later be overcome. As agriculture becomes more industrialized, as the substitution of human labour by dead labour (machinery, fertilizers) is applied on an ever-increasing scale, as contemporary agro-business arises, the difference in organic composition of agricultural as against industrial capital tends to disappear. Consequently the material basis of absolute land rent disappears. Robin Murray's apt expression: just as the formal subordination of labour to capital is transformed into real subordination in agriculture, so formal subordination of land under capitalist agriculture is transformed into real subordination of land as a material element in capitalist agricultural production.

The empirical scale of agricultural industrialization in the U.S. (Mandel's data): - Between 1915–19 and 1973–7, productivity of labour in wheat and soybean production increased tenfold, when measured by the labour-hours needed to produce 100 bushels. For maize, the increase was thirtyfold. - Production assets (including livestock and raw materials stocked on farms, thus roughly comparable to constant capital) per farm worker increased fivefold in current dollars between 1963 and 1978. Per capita disposable income per farm worker only increased less than threefold, half of which originated from sources outside farming. Wages for hired labour barely doubled during the same period. Mandel comments: "A good index of the increase in the organic composition of capital, if there ever was one!" - Simultaneously, the 'emancipation' of capitalist agriculture from the use of land has made giant strides in animal husbandry — hog-raising, cattle-raising, and the aptly named 'broiler industry.' By 1972, 75 per cent of U.S. beef was raised on feedlots, the largest accommodating as many as 125,000 cattle at a time.

Two important qualifications:

  1. The reappearance of absolute rent in modified form. While absolute land rent originating in the separation of landownership from capitalist farmers (differential land rent does not originate in ownership — ownership only determines who appropriates it) tends to disappear under industrialization, it reappears in modified form as the generalized mortgaging of land owned by small and medium-sized capitalist farmers — i.e., as the transfer of a significant part of surplus-value produced in agriculture to banks and finance capital (Kautsky, La Question agraire). Mandel adds: the growing role of big food-transforming firms (increasingly multinationals) and big cooperative societies controlled by rich farmers increasingly tend to cut farmers off from direct access to the market. According to French economist Bernard Kayser (in Economie et Statistiques, No. 102, July–August 1978), barely 25% of France's agricultural production is sold by farmers themselves to final consumers or self-consumed; all the rest passes through large capitalist intermediaries, which take their own toll, "similar to — and often parallel with — mortgage interest."

  2. The contradictory dynamic in semi-colonial countries. Real capital movements are guided not by the average rate of profit but by deviations from it. So while capital tends to eliminate absolute rent in older capitalist countries, it also constantly tends to reproduce it, essentially (but not exclusively) in countries where capitalism has penetrated belatedly. There thus operates, at the level of the world economy, "a kind of process of internationalization of land appropriation and creation of absolute land rent." Brazil offers outstanding examples.

The dual relation of capitalism to food production: Because agricultural production is food production, and food is an essential element in the reproduction of labour-power (quantitatively its main element, at least in the earlier phases of capitalist development), there is a contradictory element in capitalism's relation to agriculture. For (real or potential) agricultural capitalists, the main problem is eliminating the dual structure of landownership and farming enterprise. For (national) capital as a whole, the main short-term problem is to ensure access to food on the cheapest possible conditions, be it through capitalist, semi-capitalist, or pre-capitalist modes of production.

This means capital as a whole has a vested interest, at least during early phases of capitalist development (which are being reproduced today in most semi-colonial countries, even those semi-industrialized), in maintaining a substantial part of the peasantry under conditions where it still has access to some land — not enough to provide a minimum basis of livelihood, but sufficient to provide part of the annual food intake of the peasant family, forcing these peasants to look for employment during part of the year. Rising capitalism, therefore, "both ruthlessly suppresses free access to land through generalization of private ownership of land, and skilfully defends minifundia, i.e., small-scale parcellized subsistence farms, which enable wages to be pushed below the subsistence level since this semi-proletarian sub-section of the wage-earning class produces part of its own food." Mandel mentions the political and social function of such policies by bourgeois governments: they slow down the concentration and permanent urban settlement of the proletariat; they maintain an easily manipulated electoral base that is less easy to unionize or organize in workers' parties. But the economic function must also be clearly acknowledged, especially in semi-colonial countries. And the direct exploitation of these miserable 'private owners' by capital takes the form not of extortion of land rent but of extortion of usury interest, the parcel owners being permanently burdened by debt. (Footnote 102 references the extensive literature on migrant labour in South Africa and Rhodesia — Wolpe, Bell, Wilson, Arrighi.)

The overall evolution of agriculture under capitalism: a resultant of the interaction of five often contradictory tendencies just outlined — an index of the maturity of capitalist development in a national economy as a whole. On a world scale, this culminates in a tragic end-result: the internationalization of absolute land rent means a growing gap between the average productivity of labour in food production in the imperialist countries and in semi-colonial countries. Both the growing penetration of capitalism into semi-colonial agriculture (with the accompanying phenomenon of a shift from food to commercial crops) and the attempts of bourgeois governments to 'stabilize' parcellized subsistence farming tend to increase that gap further. The consequence is that food surpluses on a world scale tend to become increasingly concentrated in fewer and fewer countries — most of them imperialist ones. In other words, differential land rent on the world market is accessible only to a smaller and smaller number of capitalist large-scale farmers (agro-businesses).

The empirical data Mandel gives (pp. 69–70): - Wheat yield per hectare in 1977: 0.89 metric tons in Africa, 1.17 in South America, 1.36 in Asia, 1.45 in the USSR, versus 3.86 metric tons in EEC countries, over 4 in the richest US Mid-West states. - In 1976, 90% of world exports of wheat and flour came from five countries: US, Canada, Australia, France, Argentina. - In the US, fewer than 150,000 farms out of 1.7 million — those with sales over $100,000 — accounted for more than 50% of the total value of grain sold; the ratio of concentration is substantially higher in grain exports.

Capitalism as a System and the Bourgeoisie as a Class

Mandel next highlights an outstanding feature of Volume 3: the way Marx ties together economic analysis and social analysis at the level of the system in its totality — at a level higher than he did in Volume 1 (inside the factory, the production process proper). In Chapters 48 and 51, Marx shows how the reproduction of a specific form of division of the 'national income' (annually produced new value) — between wages on the one hand and profits, interest, rents on the other — automatically reproduces capitalist relations of production, i.e., the basic class relations and class inequality which define the system.

Theoretical weakness of reformism, says Mandel: Failure to understand this basic truth. Whether wages are high or low, whether 'indirect' wages (social security) are inexistent or extensive, they cannot upset the basic class relations and class inequality. Wages cannot rise to the point of substantially lowering surplus-value without triggering: - a massive 'investment strike' by capital (steep decline of accumulation), coupled with - a frantic attempt to step up the replacement of living labour by machinery; - both processes acting to halt and reverse the rise in wages through massive unemployment (and cuts in public 'social' expenditure). "The one thing it is impossible to do with capitalists is to force them to invest or produce at a loss!"

Mandel inserts a footnote (107) on 'indirect' wages. Today, indirect or 'socialized' wages (social security, etc.) are a substantial part of the total reproduction costs of labour-power — according to certain authors, up to 50%, at least in Britain and France (Ian Gough, The Political Economy of the Welfare State, 1979, p. 109; A. Capian, 1979). But this does not represent any 'vertical' redistribution in favour of wages at the expense of profits — because it is compensated by huge deductions from gross wages in the form of taxes and social-security contributions, which also amount to roughly 50%. What is occurring is a 'horizontal' redistribution, in favour of certain sectors of the wage-earning class and at the expense of others. Capian's example: France — higher salary-earning categories have only 18.2% of gross money incomes deducted for social-security contributions, whereas lower wage-earning categories' deductions rise to 31.5%.

Further structural argument: The very trend towards increased organic composition, towards increased concentration of capital, towards a strong rise in the minimum requirements for founding new productive units in all branches, constantly consolidates monopoly ownership of the means of production by the bourgeoisie as a class, making it "physically impossible for even the best-paid workers to save enough out of their wages to embark seriously upon an industrial enterprise of their own." While this is less true in small retail trade and small service business (or in small-scale farming during times of acute unemployment; see n. 109 on workers returning to subsistence farming), the overall trend is very clear. Wages tend to be spent over the whole life-span of the wage-earner. They cannot lead to any serious accumulation of capital. (n. 110: with the growth of mass production, workers' 'induced needs' — and the number of goods and services the average social wage is supposed to buy — tend to increase as a by-product of capital accumulation itself.)

So wages do not just reproduce labour-power: they also reproduce a special class under permanent economic compulsion to sell its labour-power. Likewise, private appropriation of surplus-value does not just lead to accumulation of capital: it also reproduces a social class which can monopolize the means of production and thus oblige the wage-earners continuously to sell their labour-power; continuously to produce surplus labour, surplus-value, and profits for the exclusive benefit of the latter.

Asymmetry between the two processes: The two are not symmetrical. Even when real wages have a tendency to secular increase and 'workers' savings' become a large-scale phenomenon, these do not free the individual wage-earner from his proletarian condition; they just represent 'deferred consumption' — an additional insurance fund over and above socialized 'indirect wages' (social security) to complement his reduced income in times of sickness, unemployment, or retirement, or to defray such extra expenditures as better education or weddings of his children, etc. Under late capitalism, there exists a powerful incentive for the capitalist class to deprive workers of the right to dispose of these savings freely, or even to expropriate them tout court — inflation being only the mildest of the various forms of partial or total expropriation (example: pension funds 'owned' by U.S. labour unions but completely managed by large banks — de facto expropriation already far advanced; completed in Nazi Germany).

On the other side: The fact that all sectors of the bourgeois class have access to a fraction of the sum-total of socially produced surplus-value, even if their own capital is not directly used by themselves in surplus-value-producing endeavours, does not imply that this access is equal for every capitalist. Not only does the appearance of monopolies operate in the opposite direction. The law of concentration and centralization of capital acts even more powerfully. Stepped-up competition eliminates many more middle and large-scale capitalists (not to speak of petty ones) than upper layers of the wage-earning class succeed in breaking through the barrier to becoming small independent entrepreneurs in service industry, retail trade, or agriculture.

The sum-total of the entire social evolution is a constant increase in the part of the population composed of wage-earners and a constant decline in the part composed of independent businessmen. Mandel asserts: "Not one of Marx's predictions has been more thoroughly confirmed by empirical evidence (repeated claims to the contrary notwithstanding) than that which identified a long-term trend to class polarization under capitalism." He gives the empirical support: in the U.S., wage-earners as part of the total active population increased from 62% in 1880, to 71% in 1910, to 78.2% in 1940, to 89.9% in 1970. Marx was able to make that sweeping historical forecast, "so strongly denied by almost all his contemporaries, because… he understood that the division of 'net value' (value added) into wages and surplus-value had to lead, under the pressure of capitalist competition, to more and more wage-earners being unable to become capitalists and fewer and fewer capitalists being able to remain capitalists."

Capitalist relations of distribution, rooted in capitalist relations of production but by no means identical with them (n. 114 cites Marx's Critique of the Gotha Programme and Trotsky's The Revolution Betrayed on the persistence of bourgeois norms of distribution in the transition period), constantly reproduce these relations of production. But they also reproduce the basic material preconditions of class struggle and class solidarity, both in distribution (the market) and in production (the factory). Four specific structural conditions:

(1) The individual worker has no economic resources on which he can fall back, cannot 'wait' till its market price (the offered wage) goes up before selling his labour-power. This makes collective organization of such sales — unionization and collective bargaining — a powerful inbuilt tendency under capitalism, reproducing itself universally wherever wage-labour appears.

(2) The fluctuations of the reserve army of labour, in the last analysis, regulate the fluctuations of real wages. This creates a strong inbuilt interest for the mass of wage-earners to ensure high levels of employment — to demand elementary economic policies at the level of the economy as a whole that tend to limit unemployment. (n. 115 concedes: this is at least the long-term interest of all wage-earners; since labour markets are partially fragmented nationally and sectorally — labour mobility not unlimited — short-term interests of relatively privileged parts of the working class might conflict with long-term ones.)

(3) The fact that surplus labour is the very essence of surplus-value and profit (more exactly of RIP: Rents, Interests, and Profits) creates an equally strong inbuilt tendency in the working class to challenge speed-ups, reorganizations, and forms of control of the labour process which tend to increase the mass of surplus labour and its degrading, de-humanizing effects upon the individual worker as well as upon whole sections of the working class. (n. 116 cites Braverman, Labor and Monopoly Capital.)

(4) The fact that capital can and must periodically challenge all the partial conquests of the workers — both in distribution (increases in wages and social-security payments; free collective bargaining, trade-union rights, the unrestricted right to strike) and in production (reduction of the working week and working day; forms of control over the rhythm of work and the organization of the labour process; union rights inside the work-place in general), especially through ruthless revolutions in technology — "at least periodically teaches the most intelligent, energetic and militant parts of the working class that (to paraphrase Marx) it is not enough to fight for higher wages, it is also necessary to fight for the abolition of the wage system." (n. 117: the printers' union example, undercut by electronic composition; n. 118 cites Marx, 'Wages, Price and Profit'.)

Conversely: the fact that, under capitalism, ownership of any substantial quantity of money (the starting level differing by period and country) automatically transforms that money into money capital — which not only automatically partakes in the general distribution of total socially produced surplus-value (through acquiring the average rate of interest) but is also thus transformed potentially into additional productive capital (money capital put at the disposal of 'functioning' capitalists) — creates a powerful class solidarity among all owners of capital in the common exploitation of all wage-earners as a class; in other words, "creates the material basis of bourgeois class solidarity and class consciousness." In this sense, all capitalists have a common interest in opposing 'excessive' wage increases; in supporting measures which increase the mass of profits; in supporting speed-up practices and 'rationalization investments'; and in generalizing these throughout industry (n. 120 notes this is true not only for productive labour but also for wage-labour employed by commercial and banking capital — such labour does not directly produce surplus-value but enables capital invested in these spheres to appropriate part of surplus-value produced in the productive sectors; industrialists accept this deduction because it enables them to economize their own capital and increases the production of surplus-value via more rapid rotation; at the same time they want to reduce 'circulation costs' to the utmost, since these are deductions from their own profits). All capitalists have a common interest in trying to prevent the rise of militant unionism; or, when that becomes impossible, in trying to limit or curtail trade-union rights, to establish various forms of state control over trade unions, etc. — whatever their differences as to tactics, forms, tempo, or extent.

Likewise, the very nature of private ownership of capital and capitalist competition, through the mediation of each firm searching to maximize its own profit (striving for surplus profits over and above the average), creates the mechanisms through which the general laws of motion of the system impose themselves. By this very fact, through elimination of the weakest capitalist firms, it ensures a temporary successful reversal of TRPF. Each capitalist working for his own individual interest thus "ensures the long-term reproduction, consolidation and expansion of the capitalist system as a whole."

In the same way: Capitalists' attempts to increase the surplus labour extracted from their own labour-force — by constantly striving to increase the productivity of labour, to organize mass production of an increasing number of commodities, and thereby to lower the value (expressed in gold prices) of all commodities — tend to create a collective interest of the bourgeois class in not limiting mass consumption (except in the initial stages of capitalist industrialization). This helps to counteract realization difficulties which inevitably accompany enlarged reproduction and accumulation, despite the accompanying tendency towards increasing exploitation (towards a historically rising rate of surplus-value). This creates "a basic class interest of the bourgeoisie in 'normal' rather than 'abnormal' conditions of exploitation, including whenever possible rising real wages and elementary social legislation, in order to defuse the explosive character of the class struggle. Direct repression designed to discipline the working class is used only under exceptional circumstances, in grave structural crises (whether economic, political, or a combination of both)."

The asymmetry of solidarity: Mandel stresses that the two processes just outlined — whereby a self-conscious working class and a self-conscious bourgeois class are constituted — are not symmetrical. In spite of all the inherent segmentations of the working class (craft, national, sex, generational, etc., divisions), "there are no inbuilt structural obstacles to the overall class solidarity of workers under capitalism. There are only different levels of consciousness, which make the conquest of that overall class solidarity more or less difficult, more or less uneven in time and space."

The same is not true of bourgeois class solidarity. In periods of prosperity, when struggles are essentially for larger or smaller shares of an increasing mass of profits, class solidarity easily asserts itself among capitalists. In periods of crisis, however, competition has to take "a much more savage form" — since for each individual capitalist it is no longer a question of getting more or less profit, but one of his survival as a capitalist. So there are instances of acute crisis of the system in which no economic or political solidarity can assert itself among the capitalist class; in which, even in the face of the gravest collective danger for the system, sectional or individual interests will prevail over collective, class ones. (n. 122: this is true internationally even more than nationally — imperialist wars are the extreme expression of this trend.) Mandel points out this applies to inter-capitalist competition, not to the class struggle between Capital and Labour as such — in that struggle, by contrast, "the graver the socio-political crisis, the more sharply ruling-class solidarity will assert itself."

The fundamental asymmetry, then, is structurally connected with the basically different relations of capitalists and wage-earners towards private property and competition. Private property and competition are built into the very nature of the capitalist class. Competition among wage-earners, however, is "imposed upon them from outside, not structurally inherent in the very nature of the class." On the contrary, wage-earners normally and instinctively strive towards collective cooperation and solidarity (n. 123 roots this in the very process of production under large-scale industry, based upon cooperative labour organization). Hence, to whatever extent competition among themselves is periodically reproduced — especially in times of economic crisis or after major social or political defeats — it can always be overcome by subsequent efforts to organize and raise class consciousness, assisted by the very advances of capital accumulation itself.

The Mystification of 'Factors of Production' and Scientific Labour

In Part Seven of Volume 3, Marx pays great attention to the mystifying appearance of revenues 'produced' by different 'factors of production': land, labour, and capital. Mandel observes that this mystification has, in our day, been extended through the quest for growth rates or income accretions 'produced' by scientific progress or even by higher education (n. 124 cites Blaug's Economics of Education, including titles like 'Investment in Human Capital', 'Rate of Return on Investment in Education', 'The Productivity of Universities', etc.).

Mandel's critique of this mystification: "In and of itself, 'science' produces neither value nor income. The results of scientific research, incorporated into new forms of machinery and new forms of labour organization, increase productivity of labour and thus undoubtedly contribute to the increase of material wealth. But this is something quite different from the production of value or income." What these formulas mystify is the fact that, under capitalism, private ownership of the means of production, and the transformation of manual and intellectual labour (including scientifically creative labour), "enable the capitalist (the capitalist firm) to incorporate into the total value produced in the course of the commodity-producing process the results of the cooperation, inventiveness and skill of all manpower employed. And this occurs essentially in the form of surplus-value, since the results in question do not directly change the reproduction costs of labour-power, which alone represent necessary labour." Qualities of labour thus appear as qualities separate and apart from labour: "as either qualities of 'capital' (which is represented as a mass of things, instruments, machinery and other means of production) or qualities of 'science' (which is again separated from labour as some pure product of the brain)."

For Marx, scientific labour is the very essence of 'general labour', i.e., creative labour developing new discoveries and inventions. But like collective (socialized) labour, it is indissociably related to the process of cooperation of many manual and intellectual workers working together. Mandel quotes a long passage (Volume 3, pp. 198–9) on the savings in the use of fixed capital as a result of large-scale, directly social, socialized labour, of direct cooperation within the production process; on large-scale production as the only condition for the economy arising from productive consumption in common; and on how "it is only the experience of the combined worker that discovers and demonstrates how inventions already made can most simply be developed, how to overcome the practical frictions that arise in putting the theory into practice." Marx distinguishes universal labour (all scientific work, all discovery and invention — brought about partly by cooperation of men now living, partly by building on earlier work) from communal labour (which simply involves the direct cooperation of individuals).

The Destiny of Capitalism: The Collapse Controversy

The final and longest section asks: "Does Capital contain a theory of the final and inevitable downfall of the capitalist mode of production? Is the answer to be found in Volume 3, and specifically in Marx's determination of TRPF? Do the laws of motion of the capitalist mode of production imply that the system cannot forever survive its inner contradictions?" These questions — the "collapse controversy" — have been asked ever since Capital first appeared, and have played a crucial role in the history of Marxist theory after Marx and in the international labour movement.

The Orthodox Initial Position

The initial position of "orthodox" Marxists inside the Second International was cautious but clear: the system would collapse in the end through a general sharpening of all its internal contradictions. Engels by and large supported this view (n. 126: Erfurt Programme of the German SPD, supervised by Engels; August Bebel's famous Reichstag speech of 3 February 1893, highly praised by Engels, presenting collapse as resulting from interaction of the decline of the middle classes, growing concentration/centralization of capital, growing class polarization, growing class contradictions, successive grave economic crises, growing dangers of war, growing threats against political democracy, and growing class consciousness of the proletariat). The view could base itself on a number of passages from Capital — though admittedly more from Volume 1 than Volume 3 (n. 127 cites Capital Vol. 1, pp. 929–30; and adds: "Lucio Colletti is wrong to reduce Marx's 'collapse theory' simply to the theory of the TRPF"). Its main merit was to integrate the class struggle, the growth of the labour movement and of working-class consciousness, into overall perspectives regarding the final destiny of the capitalist system.

A crucial conceptual distinction: Whether capitalism can survive indefinitely or is doomed to collapse is not to be confused with the notion of its inevitable replacement by a higher form of social organization (socialism). It is quite possible to postulate the inevitable collapse of capitalism without postulating the inevitable victory of socialism. Indeed, rather early in revolutionary Marxism, the two were conceptually separated in a radical fashion, with the destiny of capitalism formulated as a dilemma: the system cannot survive, but may give way either to socialism or to barbarism. (n. 128 cites Rosa Luxemburg, 'What Does the Spartakusbund Want?', 1972.)

While both Marx and Engels — especially the older Engels, faced with the tremendous and apparently irresistible rise of the modern labour movement — exhibited a robust optimism as to the future of socialism, "they were always careful, when the question was posed at its most general, abstract, historical level, to reject any idea of historical inevitable sequences of social organization (modes of production)." On a number of occasions they pointed out that the passage from one mode of production to another depended upon the outcome of concrete class struggles, which might end either with the victory of the more progressive, revolutionary class, or in the mutual destruction of both the old ruling class and its revolutionary adversary and in a protracted decadence of society.

Bernstein's Revisionism

The initial position was challenged by the so-called revisionists around the German Eduard Bernstein, who denied that there was any inherent tendency for the inner contradictions of capitalism to sharpen. They postulated, on the contrary, that these contradictions would decrease. They did not, however, conclude that capitalism would survive forever, but rather believed it would fade away gradually, so there was no need to overthrow it by revolutionary means (n. 129 cites Bernstein, Evolutionary Socialism). Most later variants of gradualism and reformism (including Euro-communism in recent years) have common roots in Bernstein's writings, which are "remarkable for the clear and consistent way in which they pose the problem — the only trouble being that their predictions proved to be wrong."

Mandel's historical rebuttal: "Far from leading to permanent peace, capitalism has led to two world wars and risks a third one, suicidal for the whole of mankind. Far from its leading to an ever-smoother functioning of the international capitalist economy, we have witnessed the catastrophic crises of 1920–21, 1929–32 and 1938, followed, after the post-Second World War boom, by a new long slump starting in the late sixties or early seventies. And far from ever-increasing freedom and democracy, the twentieth century has seen much greater repression and far bloodier dictatorships than anything Marx, Engels or other nineteenth-century socialists ever witnessed or could have imagined in their day." (n. 130: Crosland, The Future of Socialism, as a typical example.)

Rosa Luxemburg's Collapse Theory

It is in this context that followers of Marx attempted to formulate more rigorously the probable destiny of capitalism. Rosa Luxemburg was the first to try to elaborate, on a strictly scientific basis, a theory of inevitable collapse, in The Accumulation of Capital (1913; English trans. 1963). Her argument: enlarged reproduction, with full realization of surplus-value produced during the process of production, was impossible under 'pure' capitalism. That mode of production, therefore, had an inherent tendency to expand into a non-capitalist milieu — gobbling up surviving petty commodity production inside the capitalist metropolis and expanding continuously towards the non-capitalist periphery (colonial and semi-colonial countries). This expansion — including its most radical forms: contemporary colonialism and murderous colonial wars, imperialism, and imperialist wars — was indispensable for the survival of the system. If and when that non-capitalist milieu disappeared, the system would collapse, since it would be unable to fully realize surplus-value. But Luxemburg made it clear that, long before that final moment, the simple consequences of these increasingly violent forms of expansion, as well as the consequences of the gradual shrinking of the non-capitalist milieu, would sharpen the inner contradictions of the system to the point of explosion, thereby preparing its revolutionary overthrow.

The methodological objection (raised by Bukharin in Imperialism and the Accumulation of Capital; by Henryk Grossmann, Das Akkumulations- und Zusammenbruchsgesetz des kapitalistischen Systems, 1967, p. 22; by Claudio Napoleoni; and echoed elsewhere): that by basing the perspective of inevitable collapse exclusively on the system's laws of motion, its inner economic mechanism, Luxemburg was moving back towards 'economism' — regressing from the way Marx, Engels, and their first disciples always integrated economic laws and movements with the class struggle.

Mandel's reply: "This objection, however, is unjustified." While it is true that the contemporary history of capitalism — indeed of any mode of production in any epoch — cannot be satisfactorily explained if the class struggle (especially its outcome after certain decisive battles) is not treated as a partially autonomous factor, it is likewise true that "the whole meaning of Marxism disappears if this partial autonomy is transformed into an absolute one." It is precisely the merit of Luxemburg, as well as of several of her subsequent antagonists in the collapse controversy, "to have related the ups and downs of the class struggle to the inner laws of motion of the system." If one were to assume that the infinite adaptability of the capitalist system, the political astuteness of the bourgeoisie, or the inability of the proletariat to raise its consciousness, or the alleged 'integration' of the working class into bourgeois society, "could, in the long run and for an undefined length of time, neutralize or reverse that system's inner laws of motion and intrinsic contradictions, i.e., prevent them from asserting themselves, then the only scientifically correct conclusion would be that these laws of motion do not correspond to the system's essence: in other words, that Marx was basically mistaken when he thought he had discovered that essence." This is different from the possibility of temporary ups and downs in the sharpening of contradictions — which are not only possible but even inevitable, as Marx himself pointed out in his treatment of TRPF.

Bukharin and Varga

A second attempt to produce a scientifically rigorous collapse theory (though less rigorous, Mandel says, than Luxemburg's) was made during and immediately after WW I by certain leading radical Marxist economists who greatly influenced Lenin in drafting Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism: the Russian Nikolai Bukharin and the Hungarian Eugen Varga. While avoiding any mono-causal reduction, these authors formulated the hypothesis that capitalism had entered an irreversible period of historical decline, resulting from a combined manifestation of all its sharpened contradictions: reduction of markets; decline of world trade; decline of the international division of labour; decline of money economy and even partial reversion to barter and pre-capitalist forms of production in capitalist countries; decline of material production; collapse of the credit system; absolute decline in workers' standard of living; recurrent wars and civil wars; recurrent revolutionary explosions and victorious socialist revolutions.

Mandel's assessment: While this analysis may offer a relatively convincing description and explanation of what actually occurred in 1914 (or even 1912)–1921 and again in 1930–40 (or even in certain parts of the world in 1945–8), "it gets into serious trouble once confronted with post-Second World War developments in the international capitalist economy. Tending to theoretical eclecticism, it lacks the deeper rigour needed to tie all these various developments to the basic laws of motion of the system. In particular, it avoids any discussion of the reasons why the countervailing factors, enumerated by Marx as able temporarily to neutralize TRPF, would definitely cease to be effective in the epoch of capitalist decline; why the huge devalorization and destruction of capital which occurred in the 1929–32 crisis and the Second World War, coupled with a huge upsurge in s′ (as a result both of catastrophic working-class defeats and of a powerful increase in the productivity of labour in department II, as a result of a new technological revolution), could not lead to a new upsurge in the productive forces — inevitably ending in a new reassertion of sharpened contradictions of the system." (n. 135: Varga took a more cautious attitude after WW II, representing a 'bridge' position on the way to the harmonicist conceptions of the theoreticians of 'state monopoly capitalism'.)

The offshoot: the concept of the 'general crisis of capitalism' — in which the emphasis has become progressively shifted from the inner laws of motion of the system towards the outside challenges it increasingly meets as the result of a chain of victorious socialist revolutions (which have shrunk the geographical area in which it can operate). Initially (arising from the October Revolution), the concept still established an interrelation between that outside challenge and the ensuing sharpening of the system's inner contradictions. But this has become less and less the case in later variants, especially the 'state monopoly capitalism' theory fully developed after WW II.

The paradox pursued to its ultimate: Here the 'basic' contradiction is clearly defined as that between the 'socialist camp' and the 'capitalist camp', and no longer as the increasingly explosive inner contradictions of the capitalist system itself. The paradox is pushed to the point where Soviet authors (e.g., N. Inosemzev, Der heutige Kapitalismus, 1973) seriously assert that, as a result of the 'competition between the two systems', capitalism is 'condemned' to continuous growth! In this way the theory of collapse is " 'dialectically' turned into its very opposite: the possibility for capitalism to survive forever." The system's capacity to eliminate for an indefinite period the most serious effects of its inner contradictions is postulated — until such time as the economic, social, and cultural superiority of the socialist camp finally asserts itself.

Mandel's political diagnosis: This intellectual contortion is "structurally related to the specific interests of the Soviet bureaucracy — both its attempts to maintain conditions of peaceful coexistence with international capitalism, and its concern to maintain the subordination of a large section of the international labour movement to its own diplomatic manoeuvres — and, as such, represents a typical phenomenon of ideological mystification." (n. 137 additionally cites Mandel's Late Capitalism, pp. 513–22, and Jacques Valier, Le PCF et le capitalisme monopoliste d'état, 1976, for a more general critique of 'state monopoly capitalism' theory.)

Henryk Grossmann

A third — once again, more rigorous — attempt was offered in the late twenties by the Polish Marxist Henryk Grossmann. This was essentially a generalization (an extreme extrapolation) of Marx's law of TRPF. Grossmann tried to prove that, in the long run, countervailing forces cannot prevent the law from asserting itself with increasing strength — up to the point where all accumulated capital tends to be unable to become valorized, i.e., to the point where the total mass of surplus-value cannot ensure sufficient accumulation, even if the subsistence of the capitalist class itself falls to zero.

Mandel's assessment: "There are many weaknesses in this theory" (n. 139: the most systematic critiques are by Fritz Sternberg, 1930, and Nathalia Moszkowska, 1935). The main one is that Grossmann does not really prove that all the countervailing forces gradually lose their capacity to neutralize the declining rate of profit. He especially underestimates the effects of massive devalorization (and destruction) of capital, which has historically proven much larger in scope than he visualized (his book was finished before the 1929–32 crisis unfolded fully and before WW II's destruction). Therefore Grossmann's somewhat arbitrary numerical starting point — the reproduction schemas Otto Bauer worked out in reply to Luxemburg's Accumulation of Capital — leads to results that ignore the effects of devalorization cycles of capital. Such a hypothesis is untenable in light of real capitalism's history (which has witnessed twenty-one crises of overproduction since the establishment of the world market for industrial goods). Marx explicitly points out the devalorization-of-capital function of crises in Chapter 15 of Volume 3.

Hence, says Mandel, one can only consider Grossmann's successive figures as representing not annual totals but averages for seven/ten-year cycles. Thus the final collapse of the system is postponed to the twenty-second century (after thirty-seven seven/ten-year cycles). If the initial proportions between departments I and II were more realistic — and they should have been, since in the 1920s capitalism had nowhere even approached a situation where two-thirds of current production occurred in department I — the postponement would be even more pronounced: only after fifty or sixty cycles, i.e., after 400 or 500 years. Inadvertently, Grossmann, obsessed by his mono-causal explanation, was led to demonstrate precisely the opposite of what he intended: the extreme longevity rather than the final collapse of the system, as a function of its inner laws of motion.

Baran and Sweezy — Not Really a Collapse Theory

Mandel distinguishes the Baran/Sweezy theory of the growing difficulty of 'surplus realization' by monopoly capitalism from a collapse theory. It is not a variant of Luxemburg's, nor a fourth distinct collapse theory of its own, because Baran and Sweezy, while underlining the growing difficulties for 'surplus realization', at the same time stress the system's capacity to integrate the working class socially and thereby ensure its perpetuity — albeit under conditions of permanent quasi-stagnation — rather than its inevitable collapse. Like the more extreme proponents of 'state monopoly capitalism' theory, these authors "have to project the system's real enemies outside the system itself: third-world peasants; marginalized super-exploited layers; and so on. But they are nowhere able to demonstrate that these social forces anywhere have a potential social and economic strength comparable to that of the modern proletariat. Since such forces are not vital to the system's basic productive relations, they can be variously ignored, or integrated, or crushed, without making the system incapable of functioning." So this is not really a 'collapse of capitalism' theory at all. (n. 141: filiation between Baran/Sweezy and Kalecki, Steindl, Joan Robinson; n. 142: it is no accident that most 'third-worldist' Marxists exaggerate capitalism's ability to 'restructure' itself on a world scale by purely economic processes.)

Mandel's Own Synthesis — Automation and the Absolute Decline of Value-Production

As with the mono-causal crisis theories, "there are obviously correct elements in each of the three versions of collapse theory outlined above. These have to be tied together in order to furnish a coherent theory of the inevitable collapse of capitalism, consistent with all the inner laws of motion and contradictions of that mode of production, as unfolded by Marx's analysis in Capital."

One element in Grossmann's analysis is important, if not decisive, as the starting-point: the point in time when, in addition to the tendency of the rate of surplus-value to decline, the mass of surplus-value ceases to grow and begins to decline — first gradually, then permanently. This would obviously be "the most serious blow to a continuous process of capitalist accumulation." Grossmann, however, fails to point out the concrete content of such an incipient decline in surplus-value production, "which I have tried to specify in Late Capitalism: a level of mechanization, of semi-automation — let us say, of spreading full automation — of a growing number of branches of output, in which the total input of productive labour-hours starts to decline, hence in which total value-production declines."

This does not automatically imply an immediate decline in the absolute mass of surplus-value, since the big increase in productivity of labour inherent in 'robotism' can reduce necessary labour-time proportionally to the reduction of absolute value production. In the long run, however, this is impossible without more and more severe reductions even in real wages. After a certain point, moreover, "it becomes physically impossible." So the extension of automation beyond a given ceiling leads, inevitably, first to a reduction in the total volume of value produced, then to a reduction in the total volume of surplus-value produced. This in turn unleashes a fourfold combined 'collapse crisis':

  1. A huge crisis of decline in the rate of profit;
  2. A huge crisis of realization (the increase in productivity implied by robotism expands the mass of use-values produced in an even higher ratio than it reduces real wages, and a growing proportion of these use-values becomes unsaleable);
  3. A huge social crisis (n. 143 cites Marx, p. 372: "A development in the productive forces that would reduce the absolute number of workers, and actually enable the country to accomplish its entire production in a shorter period of time, would produce a revolution, since it would put the majority of the population out of action.");
  4. A huge crisis of 'reconversion' (capitalism's capacity to adapt) through devalorization — the specific forms of capital destruction threatening "not only the survival of human civilization but even the physical survival of mankind or of life on our planet."

Mandel's caveat regarding 'limits of growth' (n. 144): He notes he cannot here deal with the problem of 'limits of growth' argued by some to be inherent not in the capitalist mode of production but in large-scale industrial production itself, seen as inevitably depleting natural resources. Marx was very much aware of the problem (cf. Volume 3, pp. 949–50; Capital Vol. 1, pp. 636–8) but saw it as "a by-product of the specific (and distorted) forms of technological development characteristic of capitalism, not as an inevitable product of the application of the natural sciences to production. This implies that the problem is soluble in a different social framework, without mankind having to forgo the advantages of freeing itself from uncreative mechanical labour." Mandel cites ecological critics Barry Commoner (The Closing Circle, 1972) and Harry Rothman (Murderous Providence, 1972) as similar conclusions from a non-Marxist ecological standpoint.

A possible way out — but only postponing: there is one way out via the massive transformation of 'services' into commodity-producing branches (which add to total value production). Indeed, it is already starting in key services: health, education, banking, public administration. "This indicates how wrong it is to speak of late capitalism as a post-industrial society." (n. 145 cites Daniel Bell, The Coming of Post-Industrial Society, 1973.) On the contrary, "we are only now entering the age of full industrialization of a whole series of branches which have escaped that process up to now." But this only postpones the time of reckoning. For the industrialization of service sectors reproduces there, after a certain transition period, the very same processes of massive mechanization, semi-automation, and full automation for which micro-processors have already provided the necessary technical tools. (The same applies to the industrialization of underdeveloped countries as a way out of structural crisis.) "So it is impossible to see how capitalism can escape its final fate: economic collapse."

The Subjective Dimension of Collapse

Mandel adds a fundamentally important subjective dimension. With the development of semi-automation and automation, a new significant reversal occurs of the revolution constantly produced by capitalism in labour organization and the actual labour process. A massive reintroduction of intellectual labour into the process of production is inevitable, alongside at least a relative decline in the extreme parcellization of labour characteristic of Taylorism. The more wage-labour is employed for supervising functions and maintenance of delicate and costly equipment, the more its own skill, level of culture, and degree of involvement in the production process becomes an indispensable element of reproduction of capital. Hence: - Not only are the cooperative qualities of objectively socialized labour inside the factory developed to a higher degree; - But "the consciousness of the workers that they are able to run factories instead of capitalists or capitalist managers takes a giant leap forward." Thus the growing crisis of capitalist relations of production — both objectively and subjectively, i.e., in terms of their legitimacy in the eyes of the working class and of larger and larger sectors of the population as a whole — and the challenge which workers' struggles pose for these, become "an integral part of the system's tendency towards collapse."

The necessary dialectical negation: Such a trend towards upgrading labour in productive sectors with the highest technological development must "of necessity be accompanied by its very negation": a rise in mass unemployment, in the extent of marginalized sectors of the population, in the number of those who 'drop out' and of all those whom the 'final' development of capitalist technology expels from the process of production. This means "only that the growing challenges to capitalist relations of production inside the factory are accompanied by growing challenges to all basic bourgeois relations and values in society as a whole, and these too constitute an important and periodically explosive element of the tendency of capitalism to final collapse."

The Final Thesis: Barbarism or Socialism

Mandel closes with his characteristic dual-formulation: as he said earlier, not necessarily of collapse in favour of a higher form of social organization or civilization. Precisely as a function of capitalism's very degeneration, phenomena of cultural decay, of retrogression in the fields of ideology and respect for human rights, multiply alongside the uninterrupted succession of multiform crises with which that degeneration "will face us (has already faced us)." Barbarism, as one possible result of the collapse of the system, is "a much more concrete and precise perspective today than it was in the twenties and thirties." Even the horrors of Auschwitz and Hiroshima "will appear mild compared to the horrors with which a continuous decay of the system will confront mankind."

Under these circumstances, "the struggle for a socialist outcome takes on the significance of a struggle for the very survival of human civilization and the human race." The proletariat, as Marx has shown, "unites all the objective prerequisites for successfully conducting that struggle; today, that remains truer than ever. And it has at least the potential for acquiring the subjective prerequisites too, for a victory of world socialism. Whether that potential will actually be realized will depend, in the last analysis, upon the conscious efforts of organized revolutionary Marxists, integrating themselves with the spontaneous periodic striving of the proletariat to reorganize society along socialist lines, and leading it to precise goals: the conquest of state power and radical social revolution."

The final line, characteristic of Mandel's revolutionary optimism: "I see no more reason to be pessimistic today as to the outcome of that endeavour than Marx was at the time he wrote Capital."

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles Revealed

Mandel's Introduction reveals several deep structural principles that operate as the unifying architecture of the chapter:

Principle 1: The "essence/appearance" distinction as epistemic, not merely terminological. The most theoretically consequential move Mandel makes is the insistence that the transformation of values into prices of production is "only a mediating link in the process of cognition, which does not yet deal with immediately verifiable, empirical data, i.e., market prices." This is more than a defense of Marx against von Bortkiewicz. It is a claim about what kind of theory Marxist economics is. If value relations are essence and prices of production are a first mediation still on the plane of essence, then market prices (which are what capitalists empirically encounter) are appearance. This means: (a) the value category is not falsifiable directly by price data; (b) any "solution" to the transformation problem which treats the problem as one of numerical computation between empirically observable magnitudes is category-confused; (c) the empirical verification of the labour theory of value must occur at the level of tendencies over time (e.g., labour costs falling as a share of total production costs across all branches over long periods), not at the level of short-run cross-sectional price fitting.

Principle 2: Tendencies, not states. Nearly every important concept in the chapter is a tendency: the equalization of the rate of profit, the fall of the average rate of profit, the rise of the organic composition of capital, the monopoly surplus profit, the formation of the reserve army. Mandel repeatedly stresses that these are "always a tendency, never a permanent reality" — a process which "constantly realizes itself by negating itself." This is dialectical in the strict sense: it is not a matter of approximation to an equilibrium, but of a law which operates via its own negation. The fall of the profit rate operates via the periodic recuperation of the profit rate; equalization operates via persistent inequality; the reproduction of class relations operates via struggle. Collapse (for Mandel) operates via devalorization cycles that both postpone and deepen it.

Principle 3: The intrinsic relation between economic and social analysis. The chapter culminates in the claim that capitalist relations of distribution automatically reproduce capitalist relations of production, which in turn automatically reproduce both working-class and capitalist class consciousness and solidarity. But the two class-solidarities are structurally asymmetric. Capitalists are constitutively competitive (private property and competition are "built into the very nature of the capitalist class"), and so their class solidarity is unstable in crisis. Workers are constitutively cooperative (their production process is cooperative), and so their class competition is "imposed upon them from outside, not structurally inherent in the very nature of the class." This is a strong, provocative thesis about the structural asymmetry of the two classes under capitalism — the material basis for believing that working-class solidarity is more robust in the long run than capitalist solidarity. It is also the reason Mandel insists that Marx's prediction of class polarization has been amply confirmed empirically (US data 1880 → 1970).

Principle 4: Mono-causality is always wrong. Mandel's methodological commitment to multi-causality runs through three areas: crisis theory (disproportionality / under-consumption / over-accumulation must be integrated), collapse theory (Luxemburg / Bukharin-Varga / Grossmann must be integrated), and the theory of the profit rate (the rate of profit as a function of both the organic composition and the rate of surplus-value). The corollary is a warning against every attempt to reduce a complex social process to a single determining factor — a warning which turns out to have political consequences too: mono-causal crisis theories typically end by arming reformists or employers with arguments against the working class.

Principle 5: The economics of class struggle. Mandel repeatedly draws out the class-political implications of what appear to be purely theoretical debates. Under-consumption theories and pure over-accumulation theories are "dangerous" because either they imply that the crisis could be solved by raising workers' purchasing power (reformist temptation) or that it could be solved by cutting wages (employer argument). The correct theory — crises are simultaneously over-accumulation and overproduction — can only be overcome by simultaneous rise in profit rate and expansion of the market. This is a theory which "disarms both the employers' and the reformists' arguments" and makes the working class the historical agent of a solution requiring the abolition of the wage system, not its negotiation.

Broader Theoretical Implications

1. The opening up of the "time-span" problem. One of Mandel's most original contributions here — and one that goes beyond Marx's own explicit formulations — is the systematic distinction between (a) the industrial-cycle time-span, (b) the long-wave time-span (forty to fifty years), and (c) the secular "collapse" time-span. This has since become a major research program in Marxist political economy, and Mandel's Late Capitalism and Long Waves of Capitalist Development made the long-wave dimension a central contested topic. The Introduction integrates this into Marx's framework by arguing that Marx's countervailing forces are precisely the ones that temporarily neutralize the tendency, with their effects lasting longer than one cycle, thereby producing the long-wave shape of capitalist growth.

2. The externalization and internalization of class enemies. Mandel's critique of Baran/Sweezy and of 'state monopoly capitalism' theory turns on a single penetrating insight: both theories end up locating capitalism's real enemy outside the system — third-world peasants, marginalized super-exploited layers, the Soviet bloc. Mandel's rebuttal — that such forces are not vital to the system's basic productive relations and hence can be variously ignored, integrated, or crushed without making the system incapable of functioning — is decisive. The modern proletariat is vital to capitalist production: it produces surplus-value, and its struggle directly constrains capital's valorization. Any "collapse theory" that substitutes some other agent is, Mandel concludes, "not really a 'collapse of capitalism' theory at all."

3. Mass devalorization as the missing variable. Grossmann's failure — and by extension the failure of "pure" extrapolations of TRPF — is the failure to assign sufficient weight to massive devalorization and destruction of capital (financial crises, wars, bankruptcies), which historically has been more expansive than any theoretical model assumed. Devalorization is not a marginal correction; it is the mechanism by which the falling rate of profit is periodically arrested. Its historical forms — the 1929–32 crash and WW II — were massive enough to permit a long-wave upswing. This both undermines pure extrapolations to "collapse in N cycles" and shows why the collapse must be understood as a multi-dimensional crisis, not a mathematical limit.

4. The semiotics of scientific labour. Mandel's discussion of 'general labour' and 'communal labour' (drawing on Marx, Vol. 3, pp. 198–9) is a compressed but powerful argument about the ideological mis-representation of intellectual production under capitalism. The individual scientific worker's contribution appears as a property either of "capital" or of "science"-as-detached-from-labour. This "mystification" — parallel to Marx's treatment of the trinity formula (land, labour, capital) in Part Seven — is, for Mandel, one of the most important ideological functions of capitalist political economy. Educated workers are supposed to accept that their contribution is recognized in "human capital" schemes; in fact, the entire intellectual product of their labour is incorporated into capital's total value as surplus-value, since it does not change the reproduction costs of labour-power.

5. The contingent, class-struggle-mediated nature of the passage beyond capitalism. Perhaps the most important conceptual distinction in the entire chapter — and the one most at odds with mechanical readings of Marx — is that collapse is not the same as socialist revolution. The outcome is a bifurcation: socialism or barbarism. Marx and Engels themselves, Mandel notes, "were always careful… to reject any idea of historical inevitable sequences of social organization." The passage from one mode of production depends on "concrete class struggles," which might end in the victory of the progressive class, or in "the mutual destruction of both the old ruling class and its revolutionary adversary," or in protracted decay. This is not "economism"; it is rigorous Marxism, and it is the necessary frame within which all the purely economic tendencies Mandel has defended become politically meaningful.

Broader Practical Implications

1. For revolutionary strategy. Mandel's political conclusion is that the objective prerequisites for socialist transformation are more fully present than ever, and the subjective prerequisites depend on "organized revolutionary Marxists" integrating with "the spontaneous periodic striving of the proletariat." The Introduction thus ends by framing theory as preparation for practice — a stance which is also behind its insistence that theoretical errors (mono-causal crisis theory) can produce political errors (reformism, wage-cut arguments).

2. For the critique of reformism. Mandel's demonstration that wages cannot be raised sufficiently to abolish exploitation, that social-security systems only redistribute horizontally among wage-earners, and that any serious redistribution of income in favour of wages would trigger an investment strike and rapid mechanization, is the theoretical core of his critique of all gradualism — from Bernstein to Euro-communism. The Introduction explicitly links theoretical reformulations of capitalist survivability to the bureaucratic interests of the Soviet state and to reformist trade-union leaderships in Western countries.

3. For the study of imperialist political economy. The sections on capitalist agriculture and world food markets offer a compressed but formidable theory of the internationalization of absolute land rent, the role of agro-business, the concentration of food surpluses in a small number of imperialist countries, and the strategies of bourgeois governments in maintaining minifundia while suppressing free access to land. These are foundational for the later literature on the political economy of hunger, agrarian change, and dependency.

4. For philosophy of science. Mandel's critique of mathematical modeling — "mathematical models cannot, in and of themselves, 'solve' theoretical problems; they can only formalize interrelations previously understood as such" — is a methodological claim with consequences far beyond economics. It warns against the substitution of formal elegance for theoretical adequacy, and it identifies a particular trap: models can be internally consistent while their premises silently assume away the very phenomenon under study (as in the Okishio theorem's bracketing of fixed capital; as in the von Bortkiewicz solution's exclusion of department III). The Chapter thus doubles as a philosophical defense of the priority of conceptual comprehension over formalization in the social sciences.

The Closing Vision

The chapter ends not in a technical theorem but in a vision: capitalism is not unstable because it is imperfectly managed, but because its very internal mechanisms — the rising organic composition of capital, the tendency of the profit rate to fall, the asymmetries of realization, the reproduction of class relations, the inherent drive to substitute dead labour for living labour — cannot be stabilized without abolishing private property, competition, profit, and commodity production themselves. Whether this inner logic culminates in socialist revolution or in social and even civilizational barbarism is, for Mandel, not determined in advance: it depends on the outcome of concrete class struggles. The objective preconditions are in place, and the system's own development — automation, socialization of labour, mass education of workers — produces them ever more fully. But the transition itself is a matter of revolutionary political practice. Mandel's Introduction is, in the end, an argument for reading Capital as one book — production, circulation, and their unity in the process as a whole — and for reading it as a theory both of the necessity and of the indeterminacy of capitalism's fate.
