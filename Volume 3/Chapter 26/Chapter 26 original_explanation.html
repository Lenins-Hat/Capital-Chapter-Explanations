Volume 3 • Chapter 26: Accumulation of Money Capital; Its Influence on the Interest Rate

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 26 of Capital, Volume III, titled "Accumulation of Money Capital, and its Influence on the Rate of Interest," is a densely forensic chapter that belongs to the broader Part V of the work, "The Division of Profit into Interest and Profit of Enterprise." Its central problem is deceptively narrow: how is the rate of interest actually determined, and what is the relationship between the accumulation of money capital and that rate? But the chapter's true intellectual objective is polemical and theoretical in equal measure. Marx sets out to demolish the theoretical pretensions of the Currency School — represented above all by Samuel Jones Loyd (Lord Overstone), a banker and the intellectual architect of the 1844 Bank Act, and by William Norman, a director of the Bank of England — by demonstrating that their account of the rate of interest is either a tautology, a confusion of money with capital, or a self-serving apology for the banker's parasitic gains during crises.

The chapter proceeds by an unusual method. Rather than constructing a deductive argument in the abstract, Marx arranges a parade of primary evidence: lengthy extracts from the 1847-48 parliamentary report on Commercial Distress, from the 1857 Select Committee on Bank Acts, from contemporary pamphlets such as The Currency Theory Reviewed (1845) and J. G. Hubbard's The Currency and the Country (1843), and from the testimony of bankers, merchants, and witnesses. Into this evidential stream Marx inserts a running, often sarcastic, commentary, and Engels interpolates a long editorial clarification distinguishing an advance of capital from an advance of money. The chapter is thus both a work of economic theory and an exercise in ideological unmasking: it treats parliamentary testimony as a text to be decoded, exposing the class interests that hide behind the technical vocabulary of "value of capital," "supply and demand of capital," and "the quantity of money."

The core thesis that emerges is threefold. First, the rate of interest is determined by the demand and supply of money capital — that is, of loanable money funds — as distinct from capital in its material or commodity form. Second, the "value of capital" invoked by Overstone is either simply another name for the rate of interest itself (a tautology) or a covert appeal to the rate of profit, which, while it forms a general upper limit to interest, does not explain how interest is fixed within that limit. Third, and most corrosive, the bankers' claim that they "advance capital" to industry is in most ordinary cases false: discounting a bill of exchange is merely the conversion of a commercial claim into money, a sale and purchase, not a one-sided transfer of capital. The banker's habit of seeing every payment as an advance is a professional deformation that, when imported into political economy, generates the whole confused controversy over whether the banker lends capital or mere "currency."

The chapter fits into Volume III's larger architecture as the monetary and credit-theoretic complement to the analysis of the division of profit. Where earlier chapters establish that profit splits into interest and profit of enterprise, this chapter asks what determines the size of the interest portion, and it locates the answer in the sphere of money capital — credit, bank reserves, gold flows, and the state of the reproduction process. It also sets up the later, more systematic treatments of the credit system (Chapters 32 and 33) and the Currency Principle (Chapter 34), explicitly cross-referencing them. The chapter thus functions as a hinge: it moves from the abstract division of surplus value into its concrete institutional forms, and it prepares the ground for the critique of the banking and currency legislation that Marx regards as the legal codification of the Currency School's illusions.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Framing Testimony: Surplus Wealth, Its Money Form, and the Search for Employment

The chapter opens not with Marx's own voice but with a long quotation from The Currency Theory Reviewed (London, 1845, pp. 32-4), a pamphlet whose author Marx uses as a foil and an informant. The passage lays out the problem that the chapter will spend its length dissolving. In England, it says, there takes place "a steady accumulation of additional wealth, which has a tendency ultimately to assume the form of money." Here we encounter the first key concept of the chapter: money capital, the form that surplus wealth takes when it seeks investment. The point is immediately sharpened by an axiom of great theoretical weight: "money itself, as money, yields neither." Money held as money is barren; it must be parted with for "some species of investment that shall yield either interest or profit." This observation already contains the germ of the distinction the chapter will enforce — money as such is not capital that produces a return; it becomes interest-bearing capital only when it is lent or invested.

From this, the author derives a dynamic law of recurring glut: unless "concurrently with this ceaseless influx of surplus capital, there is a gradual and sufficient extension of the field for its employment," England must suffer "periodical accumulations of money seeking investment." The image is hydraulic — surplus wealth flows in ceaselessly, and if the channels of investment do not widen correspondingly, it pools up. The pamphlet then provides a crucial historical illustration: for a long series of years, "the grand absorbent of the surplus wealth of England was our public debt." When in 1816 the debt reached its maximum and "operated no longer as an absorbent," at least twenty-seven million pounds per annum "was necessarily driven to seek other channels of investment," alongside "various return payments of capital." The argument culminates in a claim Marx clearly regards as revealing: enterprises entailing large capital and creating openings for surplus capital "are absolutely necessary... to take care of the periodical accumulations of the superfluous wealth of society, which is unable to find room in the usual fields of application." Thus the construction of railways, canals, and grand infrastructure is presented not as a response to genuine productive need but as a sink for idle money capital — a theme Marx will later validate when he lists "over-investment in railways" among the causes of the 1847 crisis.

The same author's 1845 observations complete the picture of an economy awash in unemployed wealth: prices "sprung upwards from the lowest point of depression," Consols touching par, bullion in the vaults of the Bank of England exceeding "the treasure held by that establishment since its institution," shares ranging at "wholly unprecedented" prices, and interest "declined to rates which are all but nominal." These are read as evidence that "another heavy accumulation of unemployed wealth exists at this hour in England, that another period of speculative excitement is at hand." The theoretical payload here is the inverse correlation between the abundance of loanable money and the rate of interest: glut depresses interest to near zero, and speculation is the outlet.

Hubbard's Mechanism: Gold Flows, Idle Money-Capital, and the Rate of Interest

Marx next introduces J. G. Hubbard, The Currency and the Country (London, 1843), whose testimony supplies a stylised mechanism of gold flows and interest rates. Hubbard first concedes a methodological caveat: "the import of bullion is no sure sign of gain upon the foreign trade," though "in the absence of any explanatory cause" it does "prima facie represent a portion" of such gain. The substantive example is a deficient harvest requiring an import of corn and an export of gold to the value of five million pounds. Here Marx inserts a crucial parenthetical gloss: the "circulation" that Hubbard says is reduced means "idle money-capital rather than means of circulation." This distinction — between money functioning as idle loanable capital and money functioning as a medium of circulation — is the conceptual pivot of the entire chapter, and Marx flags it immediately so that the reader will not be misled by the currency theorists' habitual conflation.

Hubbard's mechanism runs as follows. The circulation "would of course be reduced by the same amount." An equal quantity of circulation might still be held by individuals, but the deposits of merchants at their bankers, the balances of bankers with their money-broker, and the reserve in their till "will all be diminished," and "the immediate result of this reduction in the amount of unemployed capital will be a rise in the rate of interest," assumed to move from four per cent to six. Trade being sound, "confidence will not be shaken, but credit will be more highly valued." The reverse case is symmetrical: if all prices fall, "the superfluous currency returns to the bankers in increased deposits — the abundance of unemployed capital lowers the rate of interest to a minimum," until higher prices or more active trade "call the dormant currency into service," or until it is absorbed by foreign investments. The mechanism is thus a crude quantity relation: more idle money capital, lower interest; less idle money capital, higher interest. Marx presents it as a useful description, but his later critique will show that the bankers mistake this superficial money-market relation for the causal determination of interest by real capital.

The 1847-48 Crisis: The Harvest Failure, Suspension of the Bank Act, and the Anatomy of Panic

The chapter now turns to the Report on Commercial Distress 1847-8, which becomes the central historical case study. The harvest failure and famine of 1846-7 forced a major import of foodstuffs. The report's narrative of contagion is quoted at length: imports "very largely in excess over... exports," a "considerable drain upon the banks," and "an increased application to the discount brokers... for the discount of bills." The brokers "began to scrutinize the bills" and the "facilities of houses then began to be very seriously curtailed, and the weak houses began to fail." Houses that "relied upon their credit... went down," which "increased the alarm"; bankers, finding they could no longer rely with the same confidence on "turning their bills and other money securities into bank-notes," curtailed facilities and "in many cases refused them altogether." They "locked up their bank-notes... to meet their own engagements; they were afraid of parting with them." Alarm and confusion increased daily, and "unless Lord John Russell... had issued the letter to the Bank... universal bankruptcy would have been the issue."

This passage dramatises a phenomenon Marx will later formalise: in crisis, the demand is not for capital but for means of payment and for liquidity. Russell's letter suspended the Bank Act (of 1844), the legislative embodiment of the Currency Principle that Overstone would spend the rest of the chapter defending. The suspension is, in effect, an admission by the authorities that the Act was strangling commerce by rigidly tying the note issue to the gold reserve.

Charles Turner's testimony sharpens the point about the form of wealth: "Some houses had large means, but not available. The whole of their capital was locked up in estates in the Mauritius, or indigo factories, or sugar factories." Having liabilities of five or six hundred thousand pounds and "no available assets to pay their bills," they were "entirely dependent upon their credit." This is the distinction between capital and liquidity in its starkest form: capital in the form of colonial estates is real, but in a crisis it cannot be converted into money at anything but a ruinous discount. Samuel Gurney reinforces the diagnosis: "At present (1848) there is a limitation of transaction and a great super-abundance of money" (i.e., abundance of loanable money alongside depressed trade), and — in a line Marx will return to — "I do not think it was owing to the want of capital; it was owing to the alarm that existed that the rate of interest got so high."

The scale of the drain is quantified: in 1847 England paid at least nine million pounds abroad in gold for food imports, £7½ million from the Bank of England and £1½ million from other sources. Morris, Governor of the Bank, testified that by 23 October 1847, public stocks and canal and railway shares had depreciated by £114,752,225. Under Bentinck's questioning, Morris conceded that all property invested in stocks and produce was depreciated; raw cotton, silk, and wool were sent to the continent at depreciated prices; sugar, coffee, and tea "were sacrificed as at forced sales." Morris declared it "inevitable that the country should make a considerable sacrifice for the purpose of meeting the efflux of bullion." When asked whether it would not have been better "to trench upon the £8,000,000 lying in the coffers of the Bank" than to get gold back "at such a sacrifice," Morris replied flatly: "No, I do not." Marx labels this exchange, with heavy irony, the "commentary on this heroism" — meaning the defence of the gold reserve at the expense of the productive economy.

The Distributive Fallout: Dividends, Hoarding, and the Money-Dealer's Harvest

Marx then turns to the distributional question: who benefited from the crisis and the Bank Act? Disraeli's examination of Mr W. Cotton, a Bank director and former Governor, establishes that the dividend to Bank proprietors was 7 per cent in 1844 and 9 per cent in 1847, and that the Bank now paid the income tax for its proprietors, which it had not done in 1844. Disraeli's summary — "since the passing of the Act, the dividend to the proprietors has been raised from 7 per cent to 9 per cent, and the income tax... is now paid by the Bank" — is confirmed by Cotton. Engels's footnote 83 explains the technical point: before 1844 the Bank fixed the dividend and then deducted income tax as it was paid to individual shareholders; after 1844 the Bank first paid income tax on its total profit, and the dividend was then paid "free of income tax," so the same nominal percentages are higher by the amount of the tax. The Bank Act, in short, worked "very well for the proprietors."

The theme of deliberate hoarding is then documented through Mr Pease, a country banker: as the Bank raised its rate, "every one seemed apprehensive; country bankers increased the amount of bullion in their hands, and increased their reserve of notes," and many who had kept "a few hundred pounds of gold and bank-notes, immediately laid up thousands in our desks and drawers." Uncertainty about discounts and about bills being current in the market produced a "general hoarding." A committee member drew the conclusion in class terms: "whatever may have been the cause during the last 12 years, the result has been rather in favour of the Jew and money-dealer, than the productive classes generally." Marx's inclusion of this remark — with its contemporary antisemitic idiom, which Marx does not soften — shows his interest in the class incidence of monetary policy. Tooke adds the decisive detail that in the hardware districts of Warwickshire and Staffordshire, "a great many orders for goods were declined to be accepted in 1847, because the rate of interest which the manufacturer had to pay for discounting his bills more than absorbed all his profit." Here the rate of interest is shown to have devoured the profit of enterprise, a phenomenon that will become central to Marx's critique of Overstone's claim that high interest cannot destroy profit.

Norman and the Currency Principle: The "Commodities Used in Production" Theory of Interest

The chapter's theoretical core begins with the testimony of Mr Norman, a Bank of England director and "a leading spokesman for the Currency Principle," before the 1857 Select Committee on Bank Acts (B. A. 1857). Norman states (3635) that the rate of interest depends "not upon the amount of notes, but upon the supply and demand of capital," and defines capital as "commodities or services used in production" (3636). Pressed on whether he includes all commodities in "capital" when speaking of the rate of interest, he answers yes. In 3637 he offers a naturalistic illustration: a cotton manufacturer borrows notes from his banker, goes to Liverpool, buys cotton; "what he really wants is the cotton; he does not want the notes or the gold, except as a means of getting the cotton"; or he borrows notes to pay wages, and the workers use money to buy food and lodging. In 3638, when asked "But interest is paid for the money?", Norman concedes "it is, in the first instance," but then pivots: if the manufacturer buys cotton on credit, "the difference between the ready-money price and the credit price at the time at which he is to pay for it is the measure of the interest. Interest would exist if there was no money at all."

Marx's critique of this passage is a model of his method. He dismisses it as "complacent rubbish... entirely worthy of this pillar of the Currency Principle," and identifies its logical structure. Norman's "discovery" is the trivial observation that banknotes and gold are means of buying things and that people do not borrow them for their own sake. From this he infers that the interest rate is governed by "the demand and supply of commodities, which is what we have always been told governs the market price of commodities." Marx's objection is decisive: "But quite different rates of interest are compatible with the same market prices." The demand-and-supply of cotton explains the price of cotton; it does not explain why the rate of interest should be three per cent at one time and seven per cent at another when commodity prices are unchanged. Norman's theory fails on its own terms because it cannot produce a determinate interest rate.

Marx's Inversion: The Rate of Interest as the Measure of the Cash-Credit Price Gap

Faced with the correct remark "But interest is paid for the money" — which implies the question of what the banker's interest, earned without any dealing in commodities, has to do with commodities — Norman answers that the difference between the ready-money price and the credit price of cotton is the measure of interest. Marx seizes on this as an inversion. "Quite the opposite," he writes. "The prevailing rate of interest, the regulation of which it is the task of our genius Norman to explain, is the measure of the difference between the cash price and the price on credit."

Marx then works out the mechanics in a fully specified example that functions as the chapter's central proof. Cotton is for sale at its cash price, determined by the market price, itself governed by demand and supply. "Say that the price is £1,000." This concludes the sale-and-purchase transaction between manufacturer and cotton broker. "But now there is a second transaction as well. This is one between lender and borrower." The value of £1,000 is advanced to the manufacturer in cotton, and he must repay it in money "say in three months' time." The interest on £1,000 for three months, "as determined by the market rate of interest," forms "the extra charge over and above the cash price." Marx summarises the separability: "The price of cotton is determined by supply and demand. But the price for the advance of the cotton's value for three months, for the £1,000, is determined by the rate of interest." The transformation of cotton into money capital in this credit relation, Marx notes, is what "proves to Mr Norman that interest would exist even if money did not." Marx's rebuttal is categorical: "If there was no money at all, there would certainly not be a general rate of interest."

This passage quietly advances a substantive theory: interest is the price of advancing value over time, and it is determined on the money market independently of the prices of the commodities whose value is advanced. Norman's confusion is to read the temporal price margin backwards as the effect of commodity supply and demand rather than as the independent variable set by the money market.

The Profit Rate as Limit and the Demand for Money Capital

Marx next extracts the kernel of truth from the banker's vocabulary while rejecting its causal claims. The "vulgar conception of capital as 'commodities used in production'" contains an ambiguity: in so far as commodities figure as capital, they express their value as capital — as distinct from their value as commodities — "in the profit that is made from their productive or commercial use." The rate of profit "necessarily has always something to do with the market price of the commodities bought and the demand and supply for them, even if it is determined by quite different factors." Here Marx makes a concession that is also a boundary: "There is no doubt at all that the rate of profit forms a general limit to the rate of interest." But, he insists, what Norman was supposed to explain is "just how this limit is determined" — and it is determined "by the demand and supply for money capital as distinct from other forms of capital."

Marx then poses the next-order question: how is the demand and supply for money capital itself determined? He answers with two linked propositions. There is "beyond doubt a tacit connection between the supply of material capital and the supply of money capital," and "it is equally clear that the industrial capitalists' demand for money is determined by the circumstances of actual production." In other words, the money market is not autonomous in the last instance; it is conditioned by the real reproduction process. Norman, "instead of enlightening us on this subject," merely repeats the wisdom that the demand for money capital is not identical with the demand for money as such. Marx reads this evasion psychologically: it exists "only because Overstone, he, and the other currency prophets always have at the back of their minds a bad conscience about the way they are seeking by way of artificial legislative intervention to make the means of circulation into capital as such, and to raise the rate of interest." Here the theoretical critique and the political critique fuse: the Currency School's confusion is not innocent error but the intellectual cover for legislation designed to enrich bankers.

Overstone's First Foray: The "Value of Capital" Tautology

Lord Overstone (Samuel Jones Loyd) now takes the stage, and Marx quotes his testimony at 3653: fluctuations in the rate of interest arise from "one of two causes: an alteration in the value of capital" or "an alteration in the amount of money in the country." Overstone claims all great fluctuations, whether in duration or extent, "may be distinctly traced to alterations in the value of capital," citing the rise of 1847 and of 1855-6, while the minor fluctuations arising from the quantity of money "are small both in extent and in duration." Marx's comment is sarcastic: "Superb! The value of capital, generally speaking is precisely the rate of interest!" A change in the rate of interest is thus derived from a change in the rate of interest — a pure tautology. Marx then offers the alternative reading: if Overstone means by "value of capital" the rate of profit, "then this penetrating thinker comes back to the fact that the interest rate is governed by the profit rate" — which, while true as a limiting relation, provides no account of the actual determination. The rest of the chapter is, in effect, an extended elaboration of this dilemma: Overstone is caught between tautology and an unexplained appeal to profit.

Gurney's Naive Confession

Before pressing the theoretical case, Marx interposes Samuel Gurney's testimony before the House of Lords committee. Asked whether great fluctuations in the rate of interest are advantageous to bankers or dealers in money, Gurney answers: "I think they are advantageous to dealers in money. All fluctuations in trade are advantageous to the knowing man." Asked whether the banker might ultimately suffer by impoverishing his best customers, he says: "No; I do not think it has that effect perceptibly." Marx's single line of French — "Voila ce que parler veut dire" ("That's what I call talking") — signals that Gurney has unwittingly admitted the class truth of the matter: the money-dealer profits from volatility, including volatility that ruins his clients. This is the "naive" counterpart to Overstone's evasive sophistication.

The 1847 Crisis Dissected: The Quid Pro Quo and the Dearth of Money Capital

Marx now promises to return later to the question of how the rate of interest is influenced by the sum of money available, but states that Overstone is already "guilty here again of a quid pro quo." He constructs the true causal account of 1847. The demand for money capital decreased for various reasons before October (there was "no worry about monetary tightness" before that point). Then a heterogeneous list of causes converged: "Dearer corn, rising cotton prices, the unsaleability of sugar on account of overproduction, railway speculation and crash, the flooding of foreign markets with cotton goods, the forcible export and import trade with India... for the purpose of speculation in bills of exchange." Marx's analytical summary is precise: "All these things, overproduction in industry as well as underproduction in agriculture, i.e. quite different reasons, led to a rise in the demand for money capital, i.e. for credit and money." And crucially: "The increased demand for money capital had its origins in the course of the production process itself."

This is the theoretical heart of Marx's positive account. The demand for money capital is not an autonomous monetary phenomenon; it is generated by disturbances in the real reproduction process — harvest failure, overproduction, speculative misallocation. Once generated, however, it operates through the money market: "whatever the cause, it was the demand for money capital that made the rate of interest, the value of money capital, rise." Marx then dismantles Overstone's explanation as a tautology: "If Overstone is trying to say that the value of money capital rose because it rose, this is a tautology. But if by 'value of capital' what he means here is a rise in the profit rate as a cause of the rise in the rate of interest, this immediately proves to be false." The proof is a simple but powerful observation: "The demand for money capital, and thus the 'value of capital', can rise even though profit is falling; as soon as the relative supply of money capital falls, its 'value' rises."

Marx then exposes Overstone's political motive: to prove that the 1847 crisis and its high interest had "nothing to do with the 'quantity of money' present, i.e. with the provisions of the 1844 Bank Act which he had inspired." Marx concedes a nuance — the Act was not the sole cause — but insists it was a cause: "as soon as fear of exhaustion of the Bank's reserve (and this was a creation of Overstone's) added monetary panic to the 1847-8 crisis." Then he returns to the substantive cause: "There was a dearth of money capital brought about by the excessive size of operations, in comparison with the means available, and brought to a head by a disturbance in the reproduction process..." He illustrates with the corn dealers: what those who had bought corn at 120 shillings per quarter lacked, when the price fell to 60 shillings, "was the 60 shillings too much which they had paid, and the corresponding credit for this in loans with the corn as security." Here is a crucial analytical point: "It was in no way a lack of banknotes that prevented them from converting their corn into money at the former price of 120 shillings." The same applies to the over-importers of sugar and to those who had tied up floating capital in railways and replaced it by conducting their business on credit.

Marx then formulates the relationship between money capital and real capital that is perhaps his most important theoretical contribution in the chapter: "But this enhanced value of money capital corresponded directly to the fallen monetary value of real capital (commodity capital and productive capital). The value of capital in the one form rose, because the value of capital in the other form fell." Overstone's error is to "identify these two values of two different kinds of capital in a single unique value of capital," and to oppose both to "a lack of means of circulation, of ready money." The final observation is lapidary: "The same amount of money capital, however, can be loaned with very different quantities of the circulation medium." Value of money capital and quantity of currency are independent variables; Overstone conflates them.

The Bank Rate Table of 1847: Interest Rises as Profits Fall

Marx now deploys Overstone's own example as empirical refutation. The official Bank rate in 1847 ran as follows:

  • January: 3–3½ per cent
  • February: 4–4½ per cent
  • March: generally 4 per cent
  • April (panic): 4–7½ per cent
  • May: 5–5½ per cent
  • June: mostly 5½ per cent
  • July: 5 per cent
  • August: 5–5½ per cent
  • September: 5 per cent, with minor variations of 5¼, 5½, and 6 per cent
  • October: 5, 5½, 7 per cent
  • November: 7–10 per cent
  • December: 7–5 per cent

The trajectory — from 3 per cent to a peak of 10 per cent, then a partial retreat — is read by Marx as the empirical signature of a crisis of reproduction: "In this case interest rose because profits declined and the money values of commodities fell enormously." This directly falsifies Overstone's claim that the rise was due to a rise in the value of capital (if that means real capital). "So if Overstone says on this that the rate of interest rose in 1847 because the value of capital rose, he can only mean by the value of capital the value of money capital, and the value of money capital is precisely the rate of interest and nothing else. But later on he gives the game away and identifies the value of capital with the rate of profit." The table thus converts a theoretical point into an evidential one: interest and profit moved in opposite directions, so interest cannot have been driven by a rising rate of profit.

The 1856 Credit-Jobbers and the Coming 1857 Crisis

Marx notes that, as far as the high interest rate paid in 1856 is concerned, Overstone "was in fact unaware that this was in part a symptom that the kind of credit-jobbers were coming to the fore who paid interest not out of their profits, but out of other people's capital." Overstone "contended only a few months before the crisis of 1857 that 'business is perfectly sound'." This anticipates the great crisis of 1857, whose proximity Marx himself was observing. The reference to credit-jobbers paying interest out of capital (a kind of Ponzi dynamic) also introduces a phenomenon that will figure in the later chapters on credit: the emergence of financial operators whose returns are not grounded in productive profit.

The "Logical Absurdity": A Thing Destroying Its Own Cause

Overstone's testimony at 3722 states: "That idea of the profits of trade being destroyed by a rise in the rate of interest is most erroneous." His reasons: a rise in interest is "seldom of any long duration"; and if it is long and large, "it is really a rise in the value of capital, and why does value of capital rise? Because the rate of profit is increased." Marx extracts the admission: "Here, then, we finally learn what the 'value of capital' means." He then adds a crucial correction to Overstone's implicit model: "Besides, the rate of profit can remain high for a long period, even though profit of enterprise falls and the interest rate rises, so that interest comes to absorb the greater part of profits." This is the analytical distinction — between the total rate of profit or surplus value (which can remain high) and the profit of enterprise (which is what bears the subtraction of interest) — that Overstone collapses in his own argument.

At 3724 Overstone declares that complaining that a rise in interest destroys its own cause (high profit) "is a sort of logical absurdity, which one does not know how to deal with." Marx retorts with a syllogism: it is about as logical as saying that "the increased profit rate has been in consequence of a rise in commodity prices brought about by speculation, and to complain that the rise in prices destroys its own cause, i.e. speculation, is a logical absurdity." The rhetorical page then turns into a broadside: "Only for a usurer enamoured of his high rate of interest is it a logical absurdity that a thing can ultimately destroy its own cause. The greatness of the Romans was the cause of their conquests, and it was their conquests that destroyed their greatness. Wealth is the cause of luxury, and luxury has a destructive effect on wealth. The artful dodger!" Marx calls Overstone "this millionaire, this 'dung-hill aristocrat'." The theoretical point stands alongside the invective: "if a high rate of profit and the expansion of business can be the cause of a high interest rate, this in no way means that a high interest rate is the cause of high profits." Sexa conclusively, "the question is precisely whether this high interest persisted (as was actually discovered in the crisis) or even reached its climax after the high rate of profit had gone the way of all flesh" — that is, whether interest outlives the profit that is supposed to sustain it. The evidence of the crisis shows that it does.

The £45 to £120 Million Expansion and the Myth of Consumed Savings

At 3718 Overstone offers his most substantive piece of reasoning: the great rise in the discount rate "arises entirely from the increased value of capital." Over thirteen years of the Act, the trade of the country has increased "from £45,000,000 to £120,000,000." This enormous demand upon capital should normally be supplied out of "the annual savings of this country," but recently those savings have been "consumed in the unprofitable expenditure of war" (the Crimean War). "I confess that my surprise is, that the rate of interest is not much higher than it is."

Marx's rebuttal is multi-pronged. First, the conceptual objection: "He seems to imagine that on the one hand there was this enormous expansion of the reproduction process, i.e. an accumulation of real capital, and that on the other hand there was a 'capital' for which an 'enormous demand' developed, in order to bring about this gigantic increase of trade!" But "wasn't this gigantic increase in production itself the increase in capital, and if it created a demand, did it not create at the same time the supply, and at the same time also an increased supply of money capital?" This is a genuinely important theoretical point: real accumulation and monetary accumulation are not two separable processes; the expansion of production is itself the expansion of capital and tends to generate the corresponding money capital. Second, the empirical objection: "If the rate of interest rose to a very high level, this was simply because the demand for money capital grew still more quickly than the supply, which means that, as industrial production expanded, it was conducted to a greater extent on the basis of credit." This introduces the crucial concept of accommodation: "the real industrial expansion gave rise to an increased demand for 'accommodation', and this latter demand is evidently what our banker understands by the 'enormous demand upon capital'." Accommodation here has the technical sense of credit extended without the equivalent of actual commodity transactions — credit to keep the machinery going, not to finance new real investment.

Third, the historical objection: "how then did England accumulate in 1792-1815, which was a war of a quite different order from the little Crimean war?" If war-consumed savings dry up the "natural source" of capital, "from what source did capital flow?" Since England took out no loans from abroad, "if there was an artificial source as well as this natural one, it would certainly be the method most favoured by a nation to use the natural source in war and the artificial source in business." Fourth, the fundamental objection about money capital: "if there was only the old money capital, could its effectiveness be doubled by a high rate of interest?" Overstone "evidently believes that the country's annual savings... are simply transformed into money capital. But if there was no real accumulation, i.e. a rise in production and an increase in the means of production, what would be the good of an accumulation of claims on this production in the money form?" This is a crucial anti-mercantilist and anti-financialization point: claims in money form are not real wealth, and multiplying them cannot substitute for real accumulation.

Marx concludes the section by naming Overstone's central equivocation: "The rise in the 'value of capital' which follows from the high rate of profit is lumped together by Overstone with the rise that follows from an increased demand for money capital. This demand may arise from causes completely independent of the rate of profit. He himself adduces as an example that in 1847 it rose as a result of the devaluation of real capital. According to whether it suits him, he relates the value of capital either to real capital or to money capital." The equivocation is not accidental but systematic — it permits Overstone to slide between incompatible explanations as the argument requires.

Discounting Misunderstood: Anticipation of Money vs. Borrowing Additional Capital

Marx then turns to Overstone's account of discounting as "a further sign of our banking lord's dishonesty, as well as his restricted banker's point of view." At 3728 Overstone declares it "impossible" to give an answer to what the ordinary rate of profit is. At 3729 the questioner asks whether a variation of 2 to 7 or 8 per cent in the discount rate must materially affect a profit rate of 7 to 10 per cent. Marx inserts a critical gloss: "The question itself confuses the rate of profit of enterprise with the rate of profit, and overlooks the fact that the profit rate is the common source of both interest and profit of enterprise. The interest rate can leave the rate of profit unaffected, but not profit of enterprise." Overstone's reply is that parties will not pay a discount rate that seriously interrupts their profits; "they will discontinue their business rather than do that." Marx comments: "Certainly, if they could do so without being ruined. As long as their profits are high, they pay the discount rate because they wish to, and when it is low, they pay it because they have to."

Overstone's account of discounting now unfolds: "What is the meaning of discount? Why does a person discount a bill?... Because he wants to obtain the command of a greater quantity of capital." Marx interrupts with "Halte-la!" and offers the correct account: the businessman discounts "because he wants to anticipate the return in money of his tied-up capital, and prevent his business coming to a standstill. Because he has to meet payments that are due. He requires more capital only if the business is going well, or if he is speculating with someone else's capital, even when business is bad. Discounting is in no way simply a means for expanding his business." Overstone then explains the motive for wanting more capital as profit-seeking: "it would not be profitable to him to do so if the discount destroyed his profit." Marx's rejoinder: the assumption "that bills are discounted only in order to expand a business, and that the business is expanded because it is profitable" is false in its first clause. "The ordinary businessman discounts his bills to anticipate the money form of his capital and in this way keep the reproduction process going; not to expand his business or spend additional capital, but rather to balance the credit he gives with the credit he takes." If he does want to expand on credit, "it is little use to him to get bills of exchange discounted, as this simply converts money capital that he already has from one form to another; he would rather take out a fixed loan for a longer period." The credit swindler, by contrast, gets accommodation bills discounted "to expand his business and to cover one squalid deal with another; not to make profit, but to get his hands on other people's capital." Marx thus distinguishes three cases: the ordinary discount of a genuine commercial bill (transformation of form), genuine long-term borrowing for expansion, and accommodation finance (pure credit swindling).

Bullion and the Rate of Interest: Cayley's Trap

Overstone had identified discounting with borrowing extra capital; now he retracts as "the screws are applied." At 3730 he retreats: "There is no doubt that in any particular transaction, if a person can get his command of capital at a low rate of interest rather than at a high rate of interest, taken in that limited view of the matter, that is convenient to him." Marx observes that it is "not at all a limited view of the matter when Lord Overstone suddenly comes to understand by 'capital' simply his banking capital, and to see the man who discounts a bill of exchange with him as a man without capital, since his capital exists in the commodity form, or the money form of his capital is a bill which Lord Overstone converts into another money form."

At 3732 Cayley attempts to trap Overstone into admitting the bullion-reserve theory: is it a fact that when bullion is about £9-10 million, the rate of interest is 6 or 7 per cent, and when £16 million, 3 to 4 per cent? Overstone's reply tries humour: "I do not apprehend that that is so... but if it is, then I think we must take still more stringent measures than those adopted by the Act of 1844, because if it be true that the greater the store of bullion, the lower the rate of interest, we ought to set to work... to increase the store of bullion to an indefinite amount, and then we should get the interest down to nothing." Cayley persists at 3733: if £5 million of bullion is restored and interest falls to 3 or 4 per cent, how could that fall be attributed to a great decrease in trade? Overstone replies that he said the recent rise, not the fall, was connected with the great increase in trade. Marx notes the logical structure of the trap: "If a rise in the rate of interest, together with a contraction in the gold reserve, is a sign of an expansion of business, then a fall in the rate of interest, together with an expansion in the gold reserve, must be a sign of a contraction of business. Overstone has no answer to this."

"Money Is the Instrument for Obtaining Capital": Who Are the Capitalists?

At 3736 Overstone makes a theoretical statement Marx singles out: "money was the instrument for obtaining capital." Marx's gloss: "This is precisely the confusion, to see it as an instrument; it is a form of capital." Overstone elaborates: under a drain of bullion it is "not the capitalists, it is those who are not capitalists, who want to obtain money... because through the money they obtain the command of the capital of the capitalist to carry on the business of the persons who are not capitalists." Marx notes that Overstone "now explains in so many words that manufacturers and merchants are not capitalists, and that the capitalist's only capital is simply his money capital." At 3737, asked whether the parties who draw bills of exchange are capitalists, Overstone retreats: "The parties who draw bills of exchange may be, and may not be, capitalists." Marx: "Now he is stuck."

The next exchanges concern whether merchants' bills of exchange represent commodities sold or shipped. Overstone denies that such bills represent the value of commodities in the same way banknotes represent gold (3740, 3741). Marx calls this "a little insolent." At 3742, asked whether it is not the merchant's object to get money, Overstone answers: "No; getting money is not the object in drawing the bill; getting money is the object in discounting the bill." Marx uses this to distinguish bill drawing from bill discounting: "Drawing bills of exchange is transforming commodities into a form of credit money, just as discounting bills is transforming this credit money into a different money, i.e. banknotes." Overstone at least concedes that the purpose of discounting is to receive money, having previously claimed that discounting obtains additional capital.

At 3743, asked whether the mercantile community under panic (1825, 1837, 1839) wants capital or the legal tender, Overstone says: "Their object is to get the command of capital to support their business." Marx's correct account: "Their object is to obtain means of payment for bills on themselves that fall due, on account of the shortage of credit that has set in, and not to have to unload their commodities below their proper price." If they have no capital at all, "of course they obtain capital with these means of payment, since they obtain value without an equivalent" — a striking early formulation of what would later be called seigniorage or pure credit creation. The general theoretical statement follows: "The demand for money as such always consists simply in the desire to convert value from the form of commodities or creditor's claims into the form of money. Hence, even aside from crises, the great distinction between borrowing capital and discounting, the latter being simply the transformation of monetary claims from one form into another, or into actual money itself." This distinction — borrowing capital vs. discounting — is the conceptual spine of the remainder of the chapter.

Engels's Interpolation: The Decisive Distinction Between Advances of Capital and Advances of Money

At this point Engels, as editor, interpolates a long clarification (marked off in the text). He begins by noting that for both Norman and Loyd-Overstone "the banker is always someone who 'advances capital', and his client the person who demands 'capital' from him." He lists Overstone's locutions: someone has a bill discounted "because he wants to obtain capital" (3729); it is convenient if he "can get his command over capital at a low rate of interest" (3730); "Money is the instrument for obtaining capital" (3736); in panic the great desire is "to get the command of capital" (3742). Engels's diagnosis: "For all Loyd-Overstone's confusion as to what capital is, it emerges clearly enough that what he describes as capital is what the banker gives his client, a capital that the client did not possess previously and which is advanced to him, being in addition to what the client previously disposed of."

Engels then offers the psychological explanation of the banker's worldview: "The banker has grown so accustomed to figuring as distributor of the available social capital in the money form (distributing it in loans) that any function in which he hands out money appears to him as a loan. All money that he pays out appears to him as an advance." If the money is given directly as a loan, this is literally correct; if it is used to discount a bill, "it is in fact an advance for him until the bill falls due." The idea is thus reinforced that "he can make no payments that are not advances." Engels carefully distinguishes two senses of "advance": the general economic sense in which any investment of money with the object of making interest or profit is an advance the owner makes to himself as entrepreneur; and the specific sense "that the banker transfers a sum to his client as a loan, which increases by that much the capital at the latter's disposal." It is the transference of the banker's specific sense into political economy that "has led to the confusing controversy as to whether what the banker makes available to his clients in cash is capital or mere money, means of circulation, 'currency'."

Engels then resolves the controversy by adopting the standpoint of the bank's client — "what he requires and obtains." Three cases are distinguished:

  1. Loan on personal credit, without security. "He receives without condition an advance of a certain value in addition to the capital that he previously applied. He receives it in the money form; not just money, but money capital."

  2. Advance against securities deposited with the bank. This "is an advance in the sense that money is paid to him under condition of its repayment, but it is not an advance of capital." For the securities also represent capital, "and moreover a higher amount than the advance." The recipient "receives less capital value than he deposits; and this is in no way an acquisition of extra capital for him. He does not undertake the transaction because he needs capital, but rather because he needs money. Thus there is an advance of money here, but not an advance of capital."

  3. Advance by discounting bills of exchange. "The form of an advance also disappears. There is just a simple sale and purchase." The bill becomes by endorsement the property of the bank, the money the property of the client. "There is no question now of repayment." Buying cash with a bill "is no more an advance than if he had bought the cash with some commodity or other, cotton, iron or corn." Capital advance requires a one-sided transfer over time; but "every purchase and sale between one dealer and another is a transfer of capital." The only capital advance in discounting would be the discount of an accommodation bill "not representing any commodities sold, and this no banker will accept if he recognizes it for what it is." Conclusion: "In the regular discount business, therefore, the bank's client does not receive any advance, either in capital or in money, but he receives money for the commodity he has sold."

Engels draws the practical inference: since Loyd-Overstone "is accustomed only in the rarest of cases to advance his funds without collateral (he was my firm's banker in Manchester), it is equally clear that his pretty description of the masses of capital that the generous bankers advance to manufacturers in need is sheer invention." This is a personal, empirical refutation grounded in Engels's own experience as a Manchester manufacturer.

Engels then anchors the distinction in Marx's later text. From Chapter 32: "The demand for means of payment is simply a demand for convertibility into money, in so far as the merchants and producers are able to offer good security; it is a demand for money capital, in so far as this is not the case, i.e. in so far as an advance of means of payment gives them not only the money form, but also the equivalent that they lack for payments, in whatever form this might be." From Chapter 33: "When the credit system is developed, so that money is concentrated in the hands of the banks, it is they who advance it, at least nominally. This advance is only related to the money in circulation. It is an advance of circulation, not an advance of the capitals it circulates." And Mr Chapman's confirmation from the 1857 evidence: "The banker has the bill, the banker has bought the bill" (Question 5139). Engels closes: "We shall come back to this theme again in Chapter 28."

This interpolation is one of the chapter's most important theoretical contributions, because it disentangles the concept of money capital from the concept of real capital at the point of the credit transaction. It also demonstrates Engels's editorial role in systematising and extending Marx's argument.

Overstone's Own Definition of Capital

At 3744, asked to describe what he actually means by "capital," Overstone answers: "Capital consists of various commodities, by means of which trade is carried on; there is fixed capital and there is circulating capital. Your ships, your docks, your wharves... are fixed capital; your provisions, your clothes, etc., are circulating capital." This is the classical-physiocratic taxonomy with a vulgar twist; it makes capital a heterogeneous collection of things rather than a social relation or a value that yields surplus value. Marx's earlier point — that commodities express their value as capital only in the profit they yield — is the implicit rebuttal.

The Drain of Bullion, "Loss of Capital," and the Two-Stage Difficulty

Overstone then recites the Ricardian theory of money (3745): "In the natural state of things the money of the world is distributed amongst the different countries of the world in certain proportions, those proportions being such that... the intercourse between any one country and all the other countries of the world jointly will be an intercourse of barter; but disturbing circumstances will arise to affect that distribution, and when those arise, a certain portion of the money of any given country passes to other countries." This is the automatic distribution-of-specie doctrine in its canonical form.

At 3746-47 Overstone equivocates on whether the export of bullion is a "loss of capital": "If you treat bullion as capital, no doubt it is a loss of capital; it is parting with a certain proportion of those precious metals which constitute the money of the world." At 3748-49 he confirms that a change in the discount rate is "a mere sign of an alteration in the value of capital," but adds that fluctuations arising from an alteration in "the quantity of money" (meaning actual gold) are "very small." At 3750 he says that in a period of high discount there is "less capital, in one sense of the word. The proportion between capital and the demand for it is altered; it may be by an increased demand, not by a diminution of the quantity of capital." Marx's marginal comment notes the inconsistency: "it was this very capital that was just now money or gold, and a little earlier on the rise in the rate of interest was explained by a high rate of profit that arose from the expansion of the business or capital, not from its contraction."

At 3751 Overstone defines the capital under discussion tautologically as "the capital which the country has at its command for conducting its business," and when business doubles "there must be a great increase in the demand for the capital with which it is to be carried on." Marx mocks the circularity: "This artful banker first doubles business activity, and then the demand for capital with which this is to be doubled. All he ever sees is his client, who asks Mr Loyd for a bigger capital to double his business with." Overstone then states: "Capital is like any other commodity, it will vary in its price... according to the supply and demand." Marx's gloss: "according to Mr Loyd capital is nothing but the totality of commodities," so "commodities change their price twice, once as commodities and the other time as capital." This is the reductio of the commodity-capital theory: if capital is simply commodities, and the price of capital is the interest rate, then commodities have two prices — a commodity price and an interest price — which is absurd. At 3752 Overstone denies that the rate of discount is connected with the gold in the Bank's coffers; at 3753 he says the Bank of England "is not a place for the deposit of capital, it is a place for the deposit of money." At 3754 he concedes ("It is very probable") that accumulation of bullion may coincide with low interest, because a period of diminished demand for capital (money-capital; 1844-5 was a period of prosperity) is a period in which "the means or instrument through which you command capital may accumulate." At 3755 he concedes a possible "coincidence of time" but denies a "connection of principle" — though Marx notes that his own Bank Act of 1844 "made it a principle of the Bank of England to regulate the interest rate by the quantity of bullion in its possession."

At 3758 Overstone collapses money and capital into a single difficulty presented in stages: "You are putting two things together which I do not join in that form; their difficulty is in getting capital, and their difficulty also is in getting money... The difficulty of getting money and the difficulty of getting capital is the same difficulty taken in two successive stages of its progress." Marx pounces: "Now the fish is caught again. The first difficulty is to discount a bill or obtain an advance by depositing commodities as security. It is the difficulty of transforming capital or a commercial token of value for capital into money." This is the difficulty of converting commodities or credits into cash, expressed in a high rate of interest. "But once the money is received, where then is the second difficulty? If it is only a question of paying, does anyone ever have difficulty in getting rid of his money? And if it is a question of buying, when has anyone ever found difficulty in buying in times of crisis?" Even granting a rise in the price of corn or cotton, "the difficulty could still not present itself in the value of the money capital, i.e. the rate of interest, but would rather do so in the price of the commodity; and this difficulty is already solved in that our man now has the money that he needs to buy it with." The "two-stage difficulty" is thus a verbal illusion.

At 3760 Overstone says a higher discount rate is "an increased difficulty of getting money, but it is not because you want to have the money; it is only the form" — and Marx adds parenthetically, "(and this form brings profit into the banker's pocket)" — "in which the increased difficulty of getting capital presents itself according to the complicated relations of a civilized state." The phrase "complicated relations of a civilized state" is Overstone's euphemism for the financial system that extracts tolls on every conversion.

The Banker as "Go-Between": "Entrusting" as Lending at Interest

At 3763 Overstone defines the banker as "the go-between who receives deposits on the one side, and on the other applies those deposits, entrusting them, in the form of capital, to the hands of persons who, etc." Marx seizes on the word "entrusting": "Here we finally have what he means by capital. He transforms money into capital by 'entrusting' it, as he puts it rather euphemistically, i.e. lending it out at interest." The banker's magic consists in calling the act of lending at interest an act of "entrusting capital." The money is capital only because it is lent at interest; the terminology presupposes what it is meant to prove.

The Great Concession and the "High Profit Rate" Smoke Screen

Overstone repeats at 3805: "When the money in the country is diminished by a drain, its value increases, and the Bank of England must conform to that alteration in the value of money" — Marx glosses: "i.e. in the value of money as capital, or in other words, in the rate of interest, for the value of money as money, compared with commodities, remains the same" — "which is meant by the technical term of raising the rate of interest." The distinction Marx enforces here is important: the value of money as money (its purchasing power over commodities) is invariant; what changes is the value of money as capital, i.e. the rate of interest. So Overstone, having denied any essential connection between the quantity of money and the rate of interest (3755), now explains the rate of interest by the quantity of money.

At 3819 Overstone says "I never confound those two" — meaning money and capital; Marx's wry gloss: "for the simple reason that he never distinguished between them." At 3834 he describes the large sum paid for corn in 1847 as "in point of fact capital, for the supply of the necessary provisions of the country." At 3841 he asserts "an intimate connection" between the rate of discount and the state of the reserve, because "the state of the reserve is the indicator of the increase or the decrease of the quantity of money in the country; and in proportion as the money in the country increases or decreases, the value of that money will increase or decrease, and the bank-rate of discount will conform to that change." Marx: "Here, therefore, he concedes what he denied categorically in no. 3755." At 3842 Overstone asserts an "intimate connection" between the amount of gold in the Issue Department and the reserve of notes in the Banking Department. Marx's technical correction: "Here he explains the changes in the rate of interest in terms of the changes in the quantity of money. What he says about this is false. The reserve can decline because the money in circulation in the country increases. This is the case if the public accept more notes and there is no decline in the metal reserve. But then the interest rate rises, because the banking capital of the Bank of England is limited by the Act of 1844. Overstone, however, cannot speak of this, since according to this Act the two departments of the Bank have nothing to do with one another." This is a precise legal-institutional point: the 1844 Act split the Bank into an Issue Department and a Banking Department, ensuring that the Banking Department's lending capacity is limited by the reserve, so that a rise in the public's demand for notes (not a fall in gold) can raise interest — a cause that Overstone's own theory forbids him to acknowledge.

At 3859 Overstone formulates the general proposition: "A high rate of profit will always create a great demand for capital; a great demand for capital will raise the value of it." Marx: "Here at last we have the connection between the high profit rate and the demand for capital, as Overstone conceives it. Now in 1844-5, for example, there was a high rate of profit in the cotton industry, since raw cotton was cheap and remained so despite a strong demand for cotton goods. The value of capital (and from an earlier passage, Overstone means by capital that which every person needs in his business), i.e. in this case the value of the raw cotton, was not increased for the manufacturers. Now the high rate of profit may have caused many cotton manufacturers to expand their businesses. In this way their demand for money capital would rise, but not for anything else." In other words, high profit generates demand for money capital, not demand for "capital" in the sense of physical commodities; the cotton was cheap, and the physical input was not scarce — only the loanable funds were. This is the empirical illustration that dissects Overstone's identification of capital with commodities.

The Reserve, the Two Departments, and the Abandonment of the 1840 Position

At 3889 Overstone says: "Bullion may or may not be money, just as paper may or may not be a bank-note." At 3896 he abandons his 1840 argument that the fluctuations in notes out of the Bank ought to conform to fluctuations in bullion, conceding only that "now with the means of information which we possess, the notes out of the Bank of England must have added to them the notes which are in the banking reserve of the Bank of England." Marx's commentary: "This is superlative." The 1844 Act stipulates that the Bank prints as many paper notes as it has gold in its reserve, plus £14 million more, so its note issue naturally fluctuates with the gold reserve. But since the present means of information show that the mass of notes the Bank may manufacture (and the Issue Department transfers to the Banking Department) — this internal circulation, fluctuating with the money reserve — "does not determine the fluctuations in the circulation of banknotes outside the walls of the Bank of England," it follows that "the latter, the real circulation, now becomes completely immaterial for the Bank's management, and the circulation between the two departments of the Bank, whose difference from the real circulation is shown by the reserve, becomes the sole decisive factor." For the outside world, this internal reserve is important only "in so far as the reserve indicates how close the Bank is to the legal maximum of its note issue, and how much its clients can still obtain from the Banking Department." This is a devastating internal critique of the operation of the 1844 Act: the Act produces a rigid internal accounting relation between the two departments that has no necessary relation to the real circulation of notes in the economy, yet that internal relation becomes the controlling variable for credit conditions.

The Closing Malafides: The French Loan and the Retreat to the "Student's Chamber"

The chapter's final exhibit is a "brilliant example of Overstone's malafides." At 4243, asked whether the quantity of capital oscillates monthly so as to alter its value in the way shown by recent discount-rate oscillations, Overstone concedes that "the relation between the demand and the supply of capital may undoubtedly fluctuate, even within short periods," and illustrates: "If France tomorrow put out a notice that she wishes to borrow a very large loan, there is no doubt that it would immediately cause a great alteration in the value of money, that is to say, in the value of capital, in this country." At 4245 he pushes the example further: if France wants suddenly thirty millions' worth of commodities, "there will be a great demand for capital, to use the more scientific and the simpler term." At 4246 the questioner asks whether the capital France wishes to buy with her loan is one thing and the money with which she buys it another, and whether it is the money that alters in value. Overstone retreats: "We seem to be reviving the old question, which I think is more fit for the chamber of a student than for this committee room." Marx's closing line: "And with this he retires, though not into the chamber of a student." Footnote 84 directs the reader to the end of Chapter 32 for more on Overstone's confusion of concepts where capital is concerned.

The French-loan example is instructive in itself: a sudden foreign loan converts money capital into a commodity demand, altering "the value of money, that is to say, the value of capital" in England — i.e., raising the interest rate. This is a real money-market phenomenon, but Overstone cannot distinguish the money market from the commodity market, and so he cannot answer the questioner's simple distinction between the capital France wants to buy and the money she uses to buy it. The retreat to "the chamber of a student" is, therefore, an admission that the theoretical question cannot be answered on Overstone's own terms.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Chapter 26 yields several fundamental theoretical principles. First, and most centrally, the rate of interest is determined by the demand and supply of loanable money capital, not by the supply and demand of commodities and not by the quantity of the circulating medium. The rate of profit, while it forms a general upper limit to the rate of interest, no more explains the actual level of interest than the level of the sea explains the height of each wave. Marx's own positive account locates the ultimate causes of movements in money capital in the real reproduction process: overproduction in industry, underproduction in agriculture, speculative misallocation of floating capital, and the disturbances these generate in the demand for credit. The money market is thus not autochthonous; it is a particular refraction of the process of capital accumulation.

Second, the chapter establishes a sharp conceptual distinction between money capital and real capital. These are two forms of the same value, and their values can move in opposite directions: in a crisis, "the value of capital in the one form rose, because the value of capital in the other form fell." Commodity capital depreciates precisely as loanable money capital appreciates (its rate of interest rises). Overstone's intellectual sin is to fuse these two distinct values into a single, equivocal "value of capital," which allows him to slide between incompatible explanations — now it means the rate of profit, now it means the rate of interest, now it means the money held in bank coffers.

Third, the chapter dismantles the banker's self-serving concept of "advancing capital." Engels's interpolation demonstrates that in the ordinary discount business there is no advance at all: there is a sale and purchase, the conversion of a bill into money. An advance implies a one-sided transfer of value over time; the discount of a genuine commercial bill is reciprocal and instantaneous. The banker's professional habit of seeing every payment as an advance is a false universalisation of his own role. This dissolution of the banker's claim to be the benefactor who "advances capital" to industry is one of the chapter's most practically consequential moves: it denies the financial sector the ideological alibi of productive contribution.

Fourth, the chapter exposes the class content of monetary policy. The Bank Act of 1844 raised the proprietors' dividend from seven to nine per cent and shifted the income tax burden from proprietors to the Bank; the crisis of 1847 transferred wealth from the productive classes to "the Jew and money-dealer"; the manufacturers of the hardware districts declined orders because the discount rate swallowed their profit of enterprise; the Bank hoarded notes while commerce starved; the Great Concession of the Act's suspension was extracted only under imminent threat of universal bankruptcy. The theoretical confusion of the Currency School is thus not innocent: it provides the intellectual cover for a legislative framework that enriches the financial sector at the expense of production.

Fifth, the chapter offers a model of immanent critique. Marx does not refute Overstone primarily by appeals to external evidence; he refutes him by internal inconsistency — by catching him conceding at 3841 what he denied at 3755, by showing that his own 1847 data refute his theory, by demonstrating that his own explanations oscillate between tautology and contradiction. This method embodies a broader epistemological lesson: the ideology of the dominant class, when pressed, yields up its own incoherence.

The broader implications are considerable. For a theory of crises, the chapter shows that financial crisis is not, in the first instance, a purely monetary phenomenon — a shortage of banknotes — but a disturbance in the reproduction process expressed through the money market. The remedy for crisis cannot therefore be the mechanical adjustment of the note issue to the gold reserve; indeed, the 1844 Act made matters worse by adding monetary panic to a crisis of production. For a theory of the state, the chapter demonstrates that ostensibly technical monetary legislation is a class instrument, and that the parliamentary interview is a genre in which the trained evasion of a banker can be read against the grain to reveal the reality being concealed. For a theory of knowledge, the chapter is a masterclass in exposing how the vocabulary of a profession — the banker's "capital," "advance," "entrusting" — constructs a self-flattering picture of the world that collapses under interrogation.

The chapter's closing gesture — Overstone retiring "though not into the chamber of a student" — is more than a witticism. It names the central fault line of the chapter: the banker treats the technical question of the rate of interest as too abstract, too "fit for the chamber of a student," to be answered in a committee room, precisely because he cannot answer it without exposing the emptiness of his own claims. Marx's chapter answers it instead, and in doing so, converts the chamber of the student into the courtroom of the analyst.
