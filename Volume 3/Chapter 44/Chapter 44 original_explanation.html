Volume 3 • Chapter 44: Differential Rent Also on the Poorest Land Cultivated

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 44 of Capital, Volume 3, is the hinge on which Marx's entire theory of ground-rent turns. It belongs to Part VI, "The Transformation of Surplus Profit into Ground-Rent," and it follows directly from the two preceding constructions of differential rent in its two forms: differential rent I (DR I), which arises from differences in the fertility and situation of different plots of land receiving equal capital outlays, and differential rent II (DR II), which arises from differences in the productivity of successive capital outlays on the same plot. The chapter's problem is elegantly narrow and analytically explosive: can the worst land under cultivation — the land that determines the governing price of production, the land that in Ricardo's system is by definition rent-free — nevertheless yield a rent?

Marx's answer is yes, and he demonstrates it three times over. In the Ricardian tradition, rent exists only because soils differ, and the margin of cultivation is by construction a no-rent margin: if the value (price of production) of corn is set by the least fertile soil in use, that soil sells its output at exactly its cost price and yields nothing to the landlord. Everything hinges on the identity between the individual price of production of the worst soil and the general, market-governing price of production. This chapter exists to break that identity — and not by the crude route of bringing still worse land (A₋₁) into cultivation, which would merely redefine the margin and shuffle land A into the rent-bearing series (and thus lie outside DR II altogether). Instead, the identity is broken from within DR II: successive capital investments with deficient productivity on the better, rent-bearing soils can themselves become the price-governing investments, pulling the market price above A's individual price of production, so that A begins to yield rent. Marx's summary is the chapter's thesis statement: "As soon as differential rent II comes into play, by way of successive capital investments, the limits to the rising production price can be governed by better land, and the worst land, the basis for differential rent I, can then also bear rent. In this case, then, all cultivated land would bear rent in the sense of simple differential rent."

The intellectual objectives are threefold. First, to dissolve the zero-rent margin — to show that rent on the poorest land can be generated by purely differential mechanisms, without appealing to monopoly or landed property as such. Second, to map every route by which this can occur, distinguishing (i) rent on A produced by a rise in the governing price generated by deficient marginal investments on B, (ii) rent on A produced with a constant or even falling price when additional capital on A itself exhibits surplus productivity, and (iii) rent on A produced when successive investments on A show declining productivity and the resulting surplus profit is fixed as rent by the landowner. Third, and decisively, to expose the point at which landed property as a social relation — the landlord's power to hold excess profit back from the equalizing tendency of competition — intrudes to raise the price of production rather than merely registering the outcome of differential fertility. This last move prepares the terrain for the theory of absolute rent that follows.

The chapter's second, appended half — "Differential rent and rent as simply interest on the capital incorporated into the soil" — attacks the "truly amusing theory" that rent on land improved by capital is interest, while rent on naturally superior land is rent. Marx collapses the distinction: all so-called permanent improvements merely supply artificially what neighbouring land possesses naturally, and once the invested capital is amortized, the residue is pure differential rent. The closing footnote ties the whole chapter back to the labour theory of value: nature never determines value; it determines only the quantity of product that a given quantity of labour yields.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Opening Set-Up: A Demand Shock and the Choice of the Marginal Supplier

The chapter opens with a stipulated premise, the classic Marxian device of a "let us assume" that isolates a mechanism. The demand for corn is rising, and the supply can only be met in one of three technically distinct ways:

  1. by successive capital investments with deficient productivity on the rent-bearing lands (i.e., additional doses on B, C, D that yield less per dose than the earlier ones);
  2. by additional capital investment on land A, the worst land previously in cultivation, also with declining productivity;
  3. by capital investment on new lands of inferior quality to A — the land designated A₋₁.

Land B is taken as the representative rent-bearing land, and the previously governing production price is £3 per quarter. Marx appends a scale note that will matter for the whole chapter's quantitative structure: "This 1 qr may represent 1 million qrs, and each acre 1 million acres" — a reminder that these are not toy quantities but the social aggregates of a whole agricultural sector, and that the "1 qr" of extra product is the material form of a whole increment of social capital.

The first analytic move is a comparison of alternative sources of the marginal quarter. If the extra quarter can be produced more cheaply by extra capital on B than by the same extra capital on A, or than by descending to A₋₁ (which can only produce at £4 per quarter), then the extra capital on B would govern the market price. Marx illustrates with a numerical fork: suppose an extra outlay of £4 (production costs including profit) were needed on B to produce a further quarter, while on A that quarter could be produced at £3½ — then it would "obviously be produced on A and not on B." Since the marginal supplier determines the governing price, the cheaper alternative would displace B as price-governor. He therefore assumes, for the sake of the argument, that the extra quarter on B costs £3½. That £3½ then "would be the governing price for the total production."

2.2 The Core Theorems Stated in the First Pass

With the governing price at £3½, Marx runs the arithmetic of B: B sells its product, now 4½ quarters, for £15¾. Its production costs were £6 for the first 3½ quarters plus £3½ for the final quarter, a total of £9½. The surplus profit remaining for rent is therefore £6¼, "against only £4½ before." This is the first crucial result: a capital investment of declining productivity on the better land increases that land's rent both absolutely and as a share of output.

The second crucial result follows immediately. Because the market price has risen to £3½ while A still produces a quarter at a cost of £3, "the acre of A would also yield a rent of £½." And — pivotally — "it would not be the worst land A, but the better land B, that governed the production price of £3½." This is the inversion of the Ricardian sequence: the price-governing unit is no longer the worst land; it is the marginal dose of capital on better land. The worst land is thereby converted into a rent-yielding soil simply because someone else has become the price-setter.

Marx then nails down the assumption that makes this analysis specifically about DR II rather than DR I: "It is assumed here of course that there is no new accessible land of quality A and as well situated as that already cultivated, but that either a second capital investment would be needed on the stretch of A already cultivated, albeit at a still higher cost of production, or else it would be necessary to bring in still worse land A₋₁." The qualifier "as well situated" is not decorative. If untilled land of equal quality and equal location existed, competition among tenants would push its use forward at the old price and the price rise could not be consolidated into rent. Location, hence differential rent, is partly a transport-cost phenomenon — a point Marx will return to at the end of the chapter.

2.3 The Two Tables and the £2½-Capital / 20-Per-Cent-Profit Norm

Before proceeding, Marx specifies the accounting convention on which all figures rest: "price of production refers to the sum of the capital advanced plus 20 per cent profit, i.e. £½ profit on each £2½ capital, making a total of £3." In other words, price of production = capital advanced + average profit (in the classical formula, k + p̄), and for one acre of A the outlay is £2½ producing £3 of value. This is the price below which a capitalist farmer will not voluntarily invest and above which he earns surplus profit. The 20 per cent rate is conventional and, as will become important, is assumed constant across the chapter's counterfactual scenarios.

Four analytic features of these tables deserve close attention. First, money rent exceeds corn rent in significance: corn rent rises from 10½ to 11½ quarters, only a 9.5 per cent increase, while money rent rises from £31½ to £40¼, a 28 per cent increase, because every quarter of corn rent is now valued at the higher price of £3½. Rising corn prices therefore inflate the value of an almost unchanged physical surplus — the landlord's windfall is a price phenomenon as much as a physical one.

Second, rent on A (½, or 1/7 of a quarter) arises without any change in A's own technique. A produces exactly what it produced before; only the price at which it sells has changed. This is the purest form of the chapter's thesis.

Third, the distribution of rents among B, C, D widens absolutely (B +£1¾, C +£2¾, D +£3¾) but the proportionate relation narrows, because each estate's rent is now inflated by the same higher price.

Fourth, note the striking mechanical detail that the same acre of B now carries a price of production of £9½ rather than £6 — that is, the "price of production" column measures the total capital sunk into the acre, so that a rising entry in this column is a record of intensified cultivation, not of inflation.

2.4 Engels's Editorial Correction: Rent as a Cost for the Tenant

Marx's table does not stand uncorrected. The editor's footnote in the text (marked "F. E.") intervenes: "The calculation here is again not completely correct." Engels recomputes. For the farmer of B, the 4½ quarters cost first £9½ in production costs and second £4½ in rent — altogether £14, "an average of £3⅑ per qr." This average price then becomes the governing market price. Rent on A would accordingly come to £⅑ rather than the table's £½, while that of B would remain £4½ as before: 4½ quarters at £3⅑ = £14, which, minus £9½ production costs, leaves exactly £4½ of surplus profit.

Two things are happening here, and both are worth flagging as intellectual caveats rather than dismissing as pedantry. On one level Engels is performing an arithmetic audit: the table's marginal (£3½) and average figures do not reconcile cleanly once B's rent is treated as a contractually fixed charge that the tenant must recover from the sale price. On another level, Engels is implicitly invoking a real institutional feature of capitalist agriculture that Marx himself stresses later in the chapter — once rent has been fixed, it functions for the tenant as a component of his costs, and therefore cannot simply be assumed away by the equalizing movement of competition. Engels's conclusion, though, is that "despite the need to alter the figures, the example shows how differential rent II enables the better land that already bears rent to govern the price, and how in this way all land, even that which was previously devoid of rent, may be turned into rent-bearing land." The theorem survives the correction; only the decimals change.

2.5 The General Law of Corn Rent and the Problem of the Profit Rate

Having established the schema, Marx states its most general corollary: "The corn rent must increase once the governing production price of corn rises, i.e. once an increase takes place in the price of a quarter of corn from the price-governing land or in the level of the price-governing capital investment on one of the land types." The mechanism is equivalent to a general decline in natural fertility: it is "the same as if all types had become less fertile," producing less per unit of the standard £2½ outlay instead of the previous yield.

But this immediately raises a question that a competent reader must press: if corn becomes dearer, doesn't the farmer's real profit fall? Marx blocks this objection pre-emptively by listing the conditions under which the profit rate can remain unchanged even as the corn price rises. The farmer, he notes, can buy less corn with his profit; but the rate of profit — which is what governs whether capital flows into agriculture — can nevertheless stay constant if:

  • wages do not rise, because they are "pressed down to the physical minimum, i.e. below the normal value of labour-power" — a blunt acknowledgment that the normal value of labour-power is itself a variable that capital can violate;
  • because other objects of working-class consumption, "those provided by manufacture, become relatively cheaper" — cheapening the wage-goods bundle offsets the dearer bread;
  • because the working day is prolonged or intensified, raising surplus value in the non-agricultural branches, "which however is what governs agricultural profit";
  • or because the same capital invested in agriculture "includes more of the constant and less of the variable variety" — a higher organic composition, hence a larger output per worker with the same wage bill.

This passage is a compact restatement of the whole architecture of Volume 3: the agricultural profit rate is regulated by the general profit rate, which is regulated by the rate of surplus value and the organic composition of capital. The point is that a rising corn price does not mechanically depress agricultural profitability, because the same forces that raise the price of the wage bundle may simultaneously be cheapening the reproduction of labour-power.

2.6 The First Route to Rent on A — Deficient Extra Investment on Better Soil

Marx now formally names the first route: rent arises on the formerly poorest land "from the difference between its individual price of production, which was formerly the governing one, and the new, higher price of production at which the last bit of extra capital supplies the extra product needed on better soil but with deficient productivity." The two halves of the definition must not be confused. A's own price of production has not changed; it is still £3. What has changed is the market-governing price, which has been lifted to the level set by the marginal dose on B. The surplus profit on A is thus a differential surplus: the difference between a given individual price and a newly higher general price.

He immediately mark off the boundary of the case. If the extra product had to be supplied by A₋₁ at £4 per quarter, then A's rent would rise to £1 per acre — larger than the 10/- style figures of the previous case — but "in this case, however, A₋₁ would take the place of A as the worst cultivated land, and A would come into the series of rent-bearing types as its lowest member." That is a change in the scale of DR I, not a demonstration of DR II, and "this case therefore lies outside a treatment of differential rent II, which arises from the varying productivity of successive capital investments on the same stretch of land." This is a methodological discipline characteristic of Marx: the mechanism must be traced to the correct law, not merely to a numerically similar outcome.

2.7 Two Further Routes and the Central Difficulty of Land A

Marx announces the remaining two routes: (a) with a constant price — "any given price, even one lower than previously prevailing" — if the additional capital investment on A leads to surplus productivity, "which must prima facie always be the case up to a certain point"; and (b) if the productivity of successive capital investments on land A declines. Both presuppose that the increased production is required by the state of demand — the demand assumption is never dropped, since without it extra output simply depresses price and generates no surplus profit at all.

At this point Marx identifies the genuine theoretical knot. The law previously established is that "it is always the individual average price of production of a quarter for the total production (or the total capital outlay) that is decisive." For better lands, the general price is given from outside and acts as a barrier; the individual average price can be compared against it and the difference capitalized as rent. But on A, "unlike the better types of land, there is no production price given outside itself, such as would restrict the equalization between the individual production price and the general one. For the individual production price of A is precisely the general production price that governs the market." A is a self-referential case: its individual price is the general price. How then can a difference arise within A itself?

2.8 Route Two, Case (1): Rising Productivity of Successive Investments on A

Marx answers with a worked case. Suppose £5 (capital) is advanced on one acre of A, with a cost of production of £6 (the £5 plus the 20 per cent profit). Where the first £2½ yielded 1 quarter, the second yields 2 quarters, so that the improved acre produces 3 quarters for £6 of production cost — an average of £2 per quarter. If those 3 quarters sell at £2 each, "A continues to bear no rent" — but the whole basis of DR II has shifted: £2 has become the governing production price instead of £3. A capital of £2½ now yields an average of 1½ quarters on the poorest land, and "this is now the official yield for all superior types of land when £2½ is invested. A part of their former surplus product goes from now on into forming their necessary product, just as a part of their surplus profit goes into the formation of the average profit." The parallel is instructive: just as a general productivity gain converts surplus profit into ordinary average profit, so a general fertility gain converts surplus product into necessary product. Improvement on the worst land, generalized, destroys rent everywhere by lowering the governing price.

But Marx then distinguishes the case where the general price does not follow suit. For the better lands "the general production price is a given barrier to capital investment," and their individual costs remain what they were. If they are still valued at the old price, the arithmetic changes: the 1 quarter from the first investment on A costs £3, but each of the 2 quarters from the second costs only £1½. A corn rent of 1 quarter and a money rent of £3 therefore arises on A "even though the 3 qrs are still sold at their old price of £9 altogether." Here is the surplus-product rent in pure form: it appears when the average productivity on A rises but the market price remains anchored to the old, less productive level.

Now Marx adds a third investment of £2½ with the same yield as the second, giving 5 quarters for £9 of production cost. If A's individual average price remains governing, each quarter must now sell at £1⅘ (£9 ÷ 5 = £1.8), and "the average price would have fallen again, not because of a new rise in the yield of the third capital investment, but rather because of the addition of a new capital investment with the same extra yield as the second." This is the chapter's most counter-intuitive result: repeated investments of higher but constant yield on the worst land cause a proportionate fall in the price of production, and with it in the differential rent of all other land types. What would increase rent on B or C decreases rent when it happens on A, because on A the individual price authorizes the general price.

If instead the first dose's £3 per quarter remains the regulator, then the 5 quarters must sell at £15, and the differential rent for the later investments on A must amount to £6 (30 shillings currency units aside: £15 minus £9 = £6).

2.9 The "Nonsense" of Marginal Attribution and the Question of Whether Rent Actually Arises

Marx here inserts a methodological warning of exceptional importance for the later history of economics: "It would be nonsense to say that a third of the capital had produced 1 qr, and the remaining two-thirds had produced 4 qrs. £9 per acre would always produce 5 qrs, while £3 would produce only 1 qr." This is a direct rejection of the practice of assigning to each "dose" of capital its own discrete marginal product and then treating rents as payments to the more productive doses. Capital is a joint input on the acre: only the total outlay and the total product have determinate meaning in the aggregate accounting. This anticipates the later marginalist construction of a downward-sloping demand curve for capital-doses and denies its legitimacy at the level of the individual holding.

Marx then returns to the question of whether a rent actually arises at all: "Whether or not a rent arises here — a surplus profit — would depend entirely on the circumstances. Normally, the governing price of production would have to fall." The condition under which the price falls is a general revolution in agriculture: if improvements on A are undertaken precisely because they have become general on the better soils, then "when we speak of the natural fertility of land A, we assume that it is obtained with £6 or £9 instead of with £3." The "natural" fertility of A is itself historically relative — it depends on the prevailing technique — a point of enormous consequence for the critique of any notion of an intrinsic, technical natural fertility.

But if the improvement affects only a small portion of A's acreage at the outset, then the improved part yields a surplus profit that "the landowner would quickly reach out to turn completely or in part into rent, and fix it as such." As demand keeps pace with the growing supply and the whole of A is gradually shifted to the new method, "rent could gradually form on all land of quality A and the surplus profit would be completely or partially confiscated, according to the market conditions."

2.10 Landed Property as a Price-Raising Power — and the Free-Trade Variant

This yields the chapter's most politically charged formulation. "The establishment of equality between A's production price and the average price of its product in conditions of increased capital outlay might ... meet an obstacle in the fixation in the form of rent of the surplus profit of this increased capital outlay." The consequence is that "it would again be the transformation of the surplus profit into ground-rent, i.e. the intervention of landed property, that raised the production price, instead of the differential rent being simply the result of differences between the individual production price and the general one." On land A, this intervention "would prevent the two prices from coinciding because it would prevent the production price from being governed by A's average production price; a higher production price than necessary would be maintained, and rent created accordingly."

Concealed in that dense sentence is the seed of the next chapter's theory of absolute rent: rent here is no longer a passive residue of differential productivity, but an active cause of a higher price of production. Landed property functions as a barrier to the equalization of individual and general prices.

Marx drives the point home by extending it to the case of free imports: "Even with the free import of corn from abroad, the same result could be obtained or maintained, if the farmer were compelled to turn to other uses, e.g. pasture, such land as was capable of competing in grain cultivation without yielding rent, at the price of production governed by foreign conditions, with the result that only rent-bearing land — i.e. only land whose individual average price of production per quarter was less than that determined by conditions abroad — would be used for the cultivation of grain." Free trade, on this analysis, does not necessarily abolish rent by exposing domestic producers to cheap foreign corn; it can instead shrink the margin of cultivation until only rent-yielding land is still in grain, with the land that would have been the rent-free margin withdrawn to pasture. The general conclusion is a compromise between the pure competition model and the landlord-determined one: the production price "would fall ..., though not to the average price. It would stand higher than this, but below the production price of the worst cultivated land A, so that competition from new land would be restricted."

2.11 Route Three, Case (2): Declining Productivity of Additional Capital on A

The final substantive case reverses the direction of the productivity assumption. Land A₋₁ can produce each additional quarter at £4; land A can do it at £3½ — cheaper than A₋₁, but £½ dearer than the quarter produced by A's first investment. The total price of the 2 quarters produced on A would then be £6½, an average price of £3¼. "The production price would rise, but only by £¼, whereas if the additional capital was applied to new land which produced at [£3¼–£3½], it would rise by a further £¼ ... and would thereby cause a proportionate rise in all other differential rents."

The structural consequence is that the same extra quarter can be had by two routes with two different effects on the rent structure. If it comes from additional capital on A, the general price rises only to A's new average (£3¼) — and at that price A yields no rent, since its average price of production and the governing price coincide. If it comes from new land, the price rises further, and all other rents rise proportionately. Marx again underscores the pivotal point: "It depends once more on how far the second capital investment on the available land A has become general, whether the production price is equalized to the average price, or whether the individual production price of the second capital investment, £3½, becomes the governing one."

And once again the landlord intervenes. If the second investment's individual price of £3½ becomes governing, then A yields a rent — and it does so "even on acres of A on which no extra capital investment had been made, and which therefore still continued to produce at £3 per qr." Untouched acres benefit from their neighbour's investment. But this outcome requires a condition: "As long as there are still untilled stretches of A, the price could rise only temporarily to £3½. The competition of new stretches of A would keep the price of production down to £3 until all land A able by its favourable situation to produce at less than £3½ per qr was exhausted." Marx adds the crucial caveat that closes the door on the neat Ricardian story: "This is what we would assume, even though when one acre of a certain land bears rent, the landowner will not lease out another acre of the same land rent-free." Once rent exists anywhere on A, no landlord will contract out a comparable acre for nothing; the mere existence of rent on one acre re-prices all comparable land. This is the micro-institutional mechanism by which a temporary differential price becomes a permanent rent.

2.12 Liebig, Declining Soil Productivity, and the Classic Result Restated

Marx closes the analytic core with a bibliographic gesture: "Liebig should be consulted on the declining productivity of the soil when successive capital investments are made." The editor's footnote supplies the identification — Freiherr Justus von Liebig (1803–73), the organic chemist and pioneer of agricultural chemistry, author of Die Chemie in ihrer Anwendung auf Agricultur und Physiologie (7th edn, 1862). The footnote adds that Marx borrowed from Liebig the concept of metabolism (Stoffwechsel), which he transformed into the analysis of the labour process in Volume 1, Chapter 7. That is a striking piece of intellectual genealogy: the concept of nature's material cycles entering human labour as a metabolic exchange comes directly out of the agricultural-chemistry debate over soil exhaustion.

Marx then restates the classical DR II result in its most general form: "We have seen how the successive decline in surplus productivity of capital investments always increases the rent per acre when the price of production is constant, and how it can even do this when the price is falling." The chapter has thus connected the declining returns literature with the rent literature in a single theorem.

2.13 The Free Gifts of Nature and the "Relative Increase in Price"

The general note that closes the notebook entry is among the most philosophically loaded passages in Volume 3. Marx begins: "From the standpoint of the capitalist mode of production, there is always a relative increase in the price of products if, in order to obtain the same product, an outlay must be made that was previously unnecessary." The reasoning is then unpacked: the replacement of capital consumed in production is not simply the replacement of values embodied in means of production. Natural elements that enter production as agents without costing anything — "whatever role they might play in production — do not go in as components of capital, but rather as a free natural power of capital; in fact a free natural productive power of labour, but one which on the basis of the capitalist mode of production presents itself as a productive power of capital, like every other productive power."

This is a double claim. First, an economic claim: natural powers that cost nothing do not enter into price determination "as long as the product supplied with its aid is sufficient to meet the demand." Only when a larger product is required than the free natural power can yield — so that the additional product must be produced "without the aid of this natural power or with human assistance" — does a new element, human labour, enter the capital advanced. "A relatively greater capital investment is thus needed to obtain the same product. All other circumstances remaining the same, production becomes more expensive."

Second, a critical-naturalist claim: the free gift of nature appears under capitalism as a productive power of capital rather than of labour or of nature, exactly as every other social productive power does. This is not incidental; it is the mechanism by which fertility differences become the basis of a revenue, not merely of a physical abundance. The passage effectively explains why scarcity of natural powers has a price consequence: the price is not paid for the power itself, but for the labour required to replace what the power previously supplied free of charge. Value still comes from labour; the disappearance of the free natural subsidy merely increases the labour required to secure the same product.

2.14 The Appended Section: "Differential Rent and Rent as Simply Interest on the Capital Incorporated into the Soil"

(The text is marked "(From a notebook 'Begun mid-February 1876': F. E.)," indicating that Engels lifted this section from a later notebook and inserted it here.)

Marx opens the second half with a catalogue of what "so-called permanent improvements" actually are. They change the physical characteristics of the soil, and partly its chemical properties, by operations requiring capital outlay, and thus "can be considered as an incorporation of capital into the soil." But — and this is his crucial reduction — they "almost all boil down to giving a particular piece of land, the soil in a particular and restricted place, characteristics that other land somewhere else, and often quite close by, possesses by nature." He then gives five concrete pairs: one piece of land is naturally level, another must be levelled; one is naturally drained, another requires artificial draining; one has a naturally deep top-soil, another must be artificially deepened; one clay soil is naturally mixed with the requisite sand, another must be artificially corrected; one meadow is naturally irrigated or silt-covered, another must be made so "by labour, or, in the language of bourgeois economics, by capital." That sardonic closing clause sets the analytic target: bourgeois economics systematically substitutes capital for labour in describing what was actually done.

Against this backdrop Marx attacks the target theory: "Now it is a truly amusing theory which asserts that on the land whose comparative advantages are acquired, rent is interest, while on the other land, which has these advantages by nature, it is not." He grants that in practice the question is "confused" because "rent really does coincide with interest in the one case, so it is also called interest and has to be misnamed as such in the others, where this is positively not the case." But the confusion is a category error, and Marx dissolves it with a two-step argument:

First, causation: "after the capital investment has been made, the land bears the rent not because capital has been invested in it but rather because the capital investment has made the land a more productive field of investment than before." The investment is a condition of the rent, not its cause; the cause is the differential in productivity relative to the still-unimproved land.

Second, the equalization test: "If we assume that all the land in a country requires this capital investment, each piece of land which has not yet passed through this state has to do so, and the rent borne by the land already provided with this capital investment (the interest that it yields in the given case) is just as much a differential rent as if it possessed this advantage by nature and the other land had to obtain it artificially." In other words, imagine the counterfactual in which every plot requires the same improvement. Then the improved plot holds no differential advantage, and its rent vanishes. The fact that some land can be improved while other land has not yet been improved is a transitory differential — no different in kind from the natural differential between good and bad soil.

Third, amortization: "This rent which can be resolved into interest also becomes pure differential rent as soon as the capital laid out is amortized. Otherwise the same capital would have to lead a double existence as capital." The point is an accounting theorem of some elegance. Suppose a farmer invests £1,000 in drainage and receives, in the form of higher rent, both the ordinary interest on that £1,000 and repayment of the capital itself. If the "rent" continued to include the amortization element after the capital had been recovered, the same capital would have been repaid to its owner while still being counted as productive capital — existing twice over, once in the owner's pocket and once in the soil. Once amortization is properly completed, the only thing that remains is the surplus over the new general level of productivity, i.e. pure differential rent.

2.15 The Closing Footnote: Nature, Labour, and the Value Controversy

The final footnote (editorially appended) connects the chapter to the theoretical controversy that frames all of Part VI: "It is a most curious phenomenon that all those opponents of Ricardo who struggled against the determination of value exclusively in terms of labour, when faced with the fact that differential rent arose from differences in land, maintained that in this case nature determined value instead of labour, though at the same time they allowed this determination in the case of the land's position, or even, and still more, for interest on capital put into the soil for the purpose of cultivation."

The polemical structure is telling. The anti-Ricardians wanted to use differential rent as a counter-example to the labour theory of value: different prices for corn from different soils must mean that nature, not labour, determines value. But they simultaneously accepted that the location of land (a socially determined, transport-cost differential) and the interest on capital invested in the soil play the same role — both of which are plainly labour- and capital-determined. Marx's riposte is that the objection confuses two distinct quantities: "The same labour produces the same value for the product created in a given time; but the size or amount of this product, and thus the portion of value which falls to a particular aliquot part, depends for a given quantity of labour solely on the amount of the product, and this in turn on the productivity of the given amount of labour, not on its absolute amount." That is, the magnitude of value is fixed by labour time; what fertility determines is the quantity of use-values that a given quantity of labour yields, and hence the proportion of total value that attaches to each "aliquot part" of output. A bushel of corn from rich soil embodies the same socially necessary labour as a bushel from poor soil — the valueless differential of nature does not put a second, independent source of value into the commodity.

The footnote's closing sentence is a precise statement of the position: "Whether this productivity is due to nature or society is quite immaterial. But in the case where it itself costs labour, i.e. capital, it increases the costs of production by a new component, which is not the case when nature alone is involved." Nature alone contributes only use-value abundance; only labour (or labour embodied in capital) contributes value and hence a price-affecting cost component. This is why "free gifts" must be replaced by paid labour before they can raise the price of production.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Three fundamental principles emerge from this chapter with unusual clarity.

First, the identity of the marginal producer and the price-governor is historical, not logical. The entire Ricardian apparatus depends on the assumption that the worst land in use sets the price. Marx demonstrates that once DR II is in play, the marginal dose of capital — which may lie on the best land — can become the price-governor. The worst land then earns rent, and every cultivated acre becomes rent-bearing. The "no-rent margin" is thus a limit case that holds only under the special and transient assumption of equal capital intensity across all soils.

Second, the economic category of rent is a social relation, not a physical fact. Throughout, the productivity differential is a necessary but insufficient condition for rent. The decisive further condition is the "intervention of landed property" — the landlord's power to fix a surplus profit as rent before the competitive equalization of individual and general production prices has time to do its work, and the landlord's refusal to lease comparable acres rent-free once rent exists on one. The same physical differential, in the absence of this social relation, would either fall to the consumer as a lower price or be competed away as average profit.

Third, the distinction between value and use-value is the load-bearing wall of the whole theory. Nature contributes abundance; labour contributes value. The differential rents that seem to arise "from the land itself" are always traceable back to differing quantities of product per unit of labour — a productivity effect, not a nature effect. When the natural power is itself replaced by labour (drainage, levelling, irrigation, deepening), production costs rise by a new component, and only then does the free gift of nature leave a trace in price.

The practical and theoretical implications ramify in several directions. For the historical analysis of capitalist agriculture, the chapter explains why rising corn prices benefit landlords disproportionately: money rents rise faster than corn rents, and rents on the poorest land appear precisely in periods of intensification. For the theory of free trade, it shows that cheap foreign corn need not abolish rent if domestic capital is instead pushed to abandon marginal grain land for pasture; the production price falls "not to the average price" but only to a level constrained by the rent-bearing acreage that remains. For the later development of economics, the "nonsense" passage is a decisive rejection of marginal-product attribution in agriculture, and the analysis of rent as a barrier to price equalization is one of the earliest statements of the modern theory of imperfect competition in land markets. For ecological and resource economics, the closing note on free natural powers anticipates the contemporary "depletion cost" literature: a natural input's price impact appears only when its free availability ceases and it must be replaced by labour. For Marxist theory itself, the chapter bridges DR I/DR II and absolute rent, showing that the theory of differential rent can generate rent on the worst land without monopoly, and then showing how monopoly (landed property) pushes even that result further, above the level warranted by differential productivity alone.
