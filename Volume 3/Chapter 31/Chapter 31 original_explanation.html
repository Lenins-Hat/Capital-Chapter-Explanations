Volume 3 • Chapter 31: Money Capital and Real Capital: II

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

This chapter is the second half of a two-part investigation Marx titles "Money Capital and Real Capital," and it opens with an explicit confession of incompleteness: "We have still not finished with the question of how far the accumulation of capital in the form of money capital for loan coincides with genuine accumulation, the expansion of the reproduction process." That sentence states the chapter's central problem with surgical precision. The question is not whether money-capital grows, but whether its growth indexes real growth. Marx's answer, developed across two numbered sections, is a systematic and emphatic no — or rather, a qualified and diagnostic only sometimes, and then only indirectly.

The chapter's core thesis is that the accumulation of loanable money capital is, for long stretches of the industrial cycle and through several distinct mechanisms, independent of — and occasionally inversely related to — the accumulation of real, productive capital. Marx isolates four analytically separable sources of loan-capital growth: (1) counter-cyclical surpluses that reflect the contraction of industrial capital after a crisis, or the temporary self-sufficiency of commercial credit during early recovery; (2) purely technical and institutional economies — the expansion and concentration of the banking system, savings on reserve funds and means of payment — which manufacture loan capital out of the same monetary substance "by purely technical means"; (3) exceptional external shocks, paradigmatically the gold discoveries of Australia and California in 1852–53; and (4) the routine, ongoing transformation of capital or revenue into money that is then deposited and thereby converted into loan capital. Only the fourth of these is an expression and result of genuine accumulation — and even then, as Marx shows with his yarn example at the chapter's close, it enters loan capital before and independently of its reconversion into productive capital, which moreover requires "a certain minimum limit."

The intellectual objective is therefore twofold. Polemically, the chapter wages war on what Marx calls the "credit gibberish of the money market" — the conceptual chaos in which, as he caustically observes, "money is capital, and bullion is capital, and banknotes are circulation, and capital is a commodity, and debts are commodities, and fixed capital is money invested in paper that is hard to sell!" Analytically, the chapter supplies the mechanisms by which the money form of capital acquires a real autonomy from the material reproduction process — an autonomy that is not merely a cognitive error but an objective feature of the credit system, and the material precondition of speculative crisis.

Within the broader architecture of Capital Volume 3, Part V, this chapter belongs to the analysis of how surplus-value is divided into interest and profit of enterprise, and to the emergence of a distinct class of money-lending capitalists — bankers, billbrokers, "discount houses" — whose profit is explicitly identified here as "simply a deduction from the surplus-value that the reproductive agents extract" and as an appropriation "at the expense of both the industrial and commercial capitalists." The chapter thus connects the theory of interest-bearing capital to a class analysis of finance and, through the trade-cycle material at its centre, to the theory of crisis. Its method is characteristic of Marx's late manuscript style: abstract dialectical categories are constantly tested against, and illustrated by, verbatim extracts from the parliamentary Bank Committee reports of 1857 and 1858 and from The Economist of 1847 — evidence that supplies not merely illustration but the raw institutional texture of the argument.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Distinction That Governs Everything

Marx begins by splitting a single phenomenon into two that are constantly confused: "(1) the mere transformation of money into loan capital; (2) the transformation of capital or revenue into money that is transformed into loan capital." The distinction is between a formal operation and a substantive one. In case (1), money that already exists simply changes hands and changes legal form — it becomes a loan, an interest-bearing claim. Nothing has been produced; no new value has entered the world. In case (2), something real has happened upstream: capital or revenue has been generated in the reproduction process and then assumes the money form, from which it can be deposited and lent. Only the latter "is related to the genuine accumulation of industrial capital, and only this can involve a positive accumulation of capital for loan." The word positive is doing heavy lifting here: it signals that there are forms of loan-capital growth that are negative or neutral with respect to real accumulation. The two numbered sections of the chapter are organized precisely around this split — Section 1 examines the many ways loan capital can grow without positive accumulation; Section 2 examines the genuine case.

Section 1 — The Two Counter-Cyclical Surpluses of Loan Capital

Marx returns to a result established earlier: an "over-abundance of loan capital, which is related to productive accumulation only by standing in inverse proportion to it," arises in two specific phases of the industrial cycle. He is precise about the phases. The first is "the time when industrial capital in the two forms of productive capital and commodity capital has contracted, i.e. at the beginning of the cycle after the crisis." The second is "the time when improvement sets in but commercial credit still has little need for bank credit."

The mechanism of the first case is a release of money capital. During the boom, money was locked into productive capital (plant, materials, labour-power purchased) and commodity capital (finished goods awaiting sale or in transit). The crisis devalues and liquidates these; capital is "set free" in money form because there is nothing productive to do with it. So it "appears as unoccupied loan capital." The surplus of loan capital therefore "expresses the stagnation of industrial capital" — it is a symptom of real contraction, a mirror image of the destruction of real capital, and thus "the exact opposite of genuine accumulation."

The mechanism of the second case is different and subtler. Here business is reviving, but it is financed out of commercial credit — the credit that reproductive agents extend to one another directly, in commodity form. Marx's gloss: this independence "rest[s] on the fluidity of returns, short terms of credit and operations predominantly conducted with one's own capital." Three conditions, each of which keeps the billbroker's door unopened. And note the sociological observation: "The speculators who rely on the credit capital of others have not yet appeared on the scene; while people who operate with their own capital are still far removed from anything like pure credit operations." What Marx is tracking is a maturity gradient of the credit system: early recovery is a world of owner-operators; late boom is a world of leveraged speculators. Between them lies the transition that will make the money market the master of the industrial cycle rather than its servant.

In the second phase, the surplus of loan capital "coincides with the renewed expansion of the reproduction process: it accompanies it without causing it." This formula — accompanying without causing — is one of the chapter's most important conceptual instruments. It marks a relation of correlation without causation, which will recur. And notably, the surplus is "already on the decline and is still only relative to the demand": the over-abundance is diminishing; what remains is merely the gap between supply and a still-rising demand. Even here, loan capital does not lead; it lags.

Why Cheap Money Promotes Real Accumulation: The Profit-of-Enterprise Channel

Marx then makes a claim that appears to soften the antithesis: in both cases, "the expansion of the accumulation process proper is promoted, because the low rate of interest ... increases the portion of the profit that is transformed into profit of enterprise." This is the inverse of the chapter's central theme, and it deserves careful unpacking. Profit of enterprise is the portion of total profit (surplus-value) that remains to the functioning capitalist after interest has been deducted. It is the residual claim. Therefore, when the interest rate is low, the borrowing capitalist retains more of the surplus-value he extracts, which raises his incentive and capacity to accumulate. Marx adds that low interest coincides with low prices in the first phase (post-crisis, when everything is cheap) and with slowly rising prices in the second (early recovery, when demand is gathering). Both price environments favour accumulation by lowering replacement costs or by promising capital gains on inventory.

He also pre-empts an apparent counter-example: interest does rise during the height of prosperity to its average level, "although it has risen, it has not done so in relation to profit." The decisive variable is the ratio of interest to profit, not the absolute rate. This is an important methodological point: the money-market rate of interest is not, in itself, an index of the burden on capital. The relation between interest and profit is a distributive relation, and only that relation tells us how much surplus-value the functioning capitalist keeps.

The Technical Manufacture of Loan Capital: "Floating Capital"

The chapter then pivots to its second, and more radical, source of non-genuine accumulation. "We have seen on the other hand how an accumulation of loan capital may take place without any genuine accumulation, by purely technical means such as the expansion and concentration of the banking system, saving on the circulation reserve or even on private individuals' reserve funds or means of payment, which are in this way transformed into loan capital for short periods." The phrase purely technical means is crucial: no production, no surplus-value, no new wealth — only institutional reorganization and economization of idle balances. Marx calls the resulting fund floating capital, a term he takes from his witnesses and subjects to withering criticism.

Two properties of this floating capital are emphasized. First, its duration mismatch: it "only ever receives the form of loan capital for short periods (and thus should only be used for short-term discounting)." It is inherently unsuitable for fixed or long-term investment. Second, its liquidity illusion: "it is constantly flowing back and forth. If one person withdraws it, someone else puts it in." This is a rotating fund, and its aggregate size can grow "quite independently of genuine accumulation."

Marx's source is the Bank Committee of 1857, Question 501, where Mr Weguelin, Governor of the Bank of England, is asked what "floating capital" means. Weguelin's answer bundles together: "capital applicable to loans of money for short periods"; "The Bank of England notes ... the country banks circulation, and the amount of coin which is in the country"; and, on further questioning, "the reserves of the bankers, in which there is a considerable fluctuation." When asked whether the active circulation in fact varies much, Marx inserts his own parenthetical: "(Though it is a very major distinction as to who it is that advances the active circulation, the money-lender or the reproductive capitalist himself)." This parenthetical is the analytical hinge of the whole passage. The quantity of circulating media is one thing; the question of who advances it is another and more important thing. Weguelin's answer conflates the two, and in doing so conceals the growth of money-lenders' power behind a statistical constant.

Marx then glosses Weguelin's mention of bankers' reserves: a large fluctuation occurs in the portion of deposits bankers have not lent out, which functions as their reserve, "though a lot of it also figures as the reserve of the Bank of England, with which their reserves are deposited." Already here we see a hierarchy of reserves — private banks deposit with the central bank, which becomes the reserve of reserves. And Weguelin concludes that floating capital "may be bullion, including metal money" (Q. 503).

This elicits Marx's most rhetorically savage passage, which is also analytically substantive: "It is truly amazing how all the categories of political economy take on a new meaning and form in this credit gibberish of the money market. Floating capital here is the expression for circulating capital, which is of course something completely different, and money is capital, and bullion is capital, and banknotes are circulation, and capital is a commodity, and debts are commodities, and fixed capital is money invested in paper that is hard to sell!" Each clause names a genuine category error. Floating capital has been equated with circulating capital — but "floating" describes a term/duration property of loans, while "circulating" describes a turnover property of productive capital. They are orthogonal. Money has been equated with capital: but money is a means of purchase and payment, whereas capital is a value that self-expands — the identification is the foundational fetish of the money market. Bullion is called capital: but bullion is a hoard or a world-money reserve. Debts are called commodities: but a debt is a legal claim on future value, and calling it a commodity obscures that its "price" is inversely related to the rate of interest. And most strikingly, fixed capital is described as "money invested in paper that is hard to sell" — an almost oxymoronic formulation, since illiquidity is precisely what fixed capital is not supposed to be, and paper is precisely what fixed capital is not made of. Marx's point is that the practitioners of the money market systematically invert the very categories that would allow them to understand their own system.

The Deposit Revolution: From Ipswich Farmers to Lombard Street

The chapter now supplies the quantitative and institutional substance behind this "technical" expansion. The joint-stock banks of London increased deposits from £8,850,774 in 1847 to £43,100,724 in 1857 — roughly a five-fold increase in a decade. Marx cites the Committee's inference that "a large part has been derived from sources not heretofore made available for this purpose," namely that "the practice of opening accounts and depositing money with bankers has extended to numerous classes who did not formerly employ their capital (!) in that way." The exclamation mark after "capital" is Marx flagging the euphemism: what has actually happened is that money which previously circulated outside the banking system — hoards, household balances, working cash — is now being drawn into it. Marx's key analytical point is that this is not new capital; it is existing money reclassified. The very act of deposit transforms it into loanable capital.

The testimony of Mr Rodwell, Chairman of the Association of the Private Country Bankers, is cited to make this concrete and to give it a social-geographic dimension: "in the neighbourhood of Ipswich this practice has lately increased four-fold among the farmers and shopkeepers of that district; that almost every farmer, even those paying only £50 per annum rent, now keeps deposits with bankers." Here we have the penetration of the credit system into the countryside — the mobilization of agricultural surplus into urban money markets. The path traced by the Committee is a chain: rural deposits → aggregate finds its way "to the employments of trade" → "especially gravitates to London, the centre of commercial activity" → employed "first in the discount of bills, or in other advances to the customers of the London bankers" → and what remains unused by the bankers "passes into the hands of the billbrokers, who give to the banker in return commercial bills already discounted by them."

The Billbroker Machine: Rediscounting and Fictitious Credit

Marx then performs a decisive analytical operation on this chain, following the legal form of the transaction to its real content. "Since the banker makes advances to the billbroker on the bill that the broker has already once discounted, he actually rediscounts it." But this is only the first layer. "In actual fact very many of these bills have already been rediscounted by the billbroker, and with the same money that the banker uses to rediscount the bills presented by the billbroker, the broker rediscounts new bills." Two things follow. First, the same money is doing repeated rediscount work — a velocity phenomenon that makes the volume of loan capital exceed any underlying quantity of money. Second, because the broker is lending on bills he has created or re-endorsed, the chain of claims lengthens faster than the chain of real transactions.

The Bank Committee's own verdict is quoted: "Extensive fictitious credits have been created by means of accommodation bills, and open credits, great facilities for which have been afforded by the practice of joint-stock country banks discounting such bills, and rediscounting them with the billbrokers in the London market, upon the credit of the bank alone, without reference to the quality of the bills otherwise." The final clause is the crux. In a sound bill market, the discounter evaluates the underlying commercial transaction — was goods actually sold, is the acceptor good for it? Here, the discount is made on the bank's name alone. Accommodation bills are bills drawn and accepted without any underlying commercial transaction, purely to raise money; open credits are unsecured credit lines. The presence of both in the rediscount chain means the system can expand claims on nothing but confidence.

The Economist on Lombard Street: A System of Interregional and Imperial Redistribution

The long extract from The Economist (1847, p. 1334) provides the systemic picture, and Marx uses it because it is a bourgeois source stating the facts with unintended candour. The passage begins from a regional imbalance: "capital ... has accumulated in some districts of the country more rapidly than it could be used, while, in others, the means of employing capital have increased more rapidly than the capital itself." Agricultural districts have surplus deposits they cannot profitably use at home; mercantile, manufacturing and mining districts have a demand for capital exceeding their own means. The resolution is the emergence of "a new class of houses in the distribution of capital, who, though usually called billbrokers, are in reality bankers upon an immense scale." Marx's emphasis falls on this naming problem: a new function (wholesale interbank lending) is hiding under an old name (billbroker). This is a crucial institutional innovation concealed by terminological inertia.

The billbrokers "receive, for such periods, and at such rates of interest as were agreed upon, the surplus-capital of bankers ... as well as the temporary unemployed moneys of public companies and extensive mercantile establishments, and advance them at higher rates of interest to bankers in those districts where capital was more in demand, generally by rediscounting the bills taken from their customers." So the broker is a spread-earner on interbank interest differentials. "In this way Lombard Street has become the great centre in which the transfer of spare capital has been made from one part of the country, where it could not be profitably employed, to another, where a demand existed for it."

Then comes the fateful expansion of collateral: at first the transactions were "confined almost exclusively to borrowing and lending on banking securities." But as capital accumulated and was economized by banks, the discount houses' funds "became so large that they were induced to make advances first on dock warrants of merchandise" (warehouse receipts for goods sitting in docks), "and next on bills of lading, representing produce not even arrived in this country, though sometimes, if not generally, secured by bills drawn by the merchant upon his broker." This is a decisive escalation: lending secured by claims on goods still at sea, sometimes secured only by further bills. Each step removes the lender further from any verifiable real asset.

The consequences are narrated as a cascade. "This practice rapidly changed the whole character of English commerce." The facilities in Lombard Street empowered "the brokers in Mincing Lane," who passed them to the importing merchant; the merchant took them up "and thus the practice has become so common of late years that it may be said to be now the general rule, and not the rare exception, as it was twenty-five years ago." Then the final escalation: "large sums have been raised in Lombard Street on bills drawn against the forthcoming crops of distant colonies" — lending against a harvest not yet planted or reaped. And the destination of the liberated capital: "the importing merchants ... invest their floating capital with which their business has hitherto been conducted, in the most objectionable of all fixed securities — foreign plantations — over which they could exercise little or no control."

This is a complete causal chain of credit-induced misallocation. Rural deposits, mobilized by country banks, centralized in Lombard Street, rediscounted to provincial banks, extended against dock warrants and bills of lading, freeing mercantile capital, which is then locked into illiquid, distant, uncontrollable colonial assets. Marx's summary is devastating: "That is the 'nice' way to devour credits." He then describes the epistemic condition of the original depositor, which is the chapter's deepest point about consciousness and credit: "The rural depositor imagines he is simply depositing with his banker, and also imagines that when the banker makes loans it is to private individuals whom he knows. He does not have the remotest suspicion that the banker puts his deposit at the disposal of a London billbroker, over whose operations neither of them have the slightest control." The chain of intermediation has broken the correspondence between ownership and risk-perception. The depositor thinks he holds a safe, local, personal claim; he actually holds an undifferentiated position in a national and imperial speculative network.

Railway Construction: A Second Temporary Source of Loan Capital

Marx adds a brief but significant supplementary case: "major public undertakings, such as railway construction, can temporarily increase loan capital, in that the sums paid up always remain for a certain while in the hands of the banks and are at their disposal until they are actually spent." This is a construction-fund or sinking-fund phenomenon — call it the time-lag between share subscription and capital expenditure. Money paid in for shares sits in banks as deposits before being spent on rails, land and wages. During that interval it is loan capital. This is a genuinely "technical" source of loan capital created by the pace of investment itself, and it shows that even genuinely productive investment generates a transient pool of loan capital that is not itself productive. It will become a major theme in Marx's later account of the 1840s railway mania.

Loan Capital vs. Quantity of Circulation: The Definitive Separation

The next move is theoretical and crucial: "The volume of loan capital, moreover, is completely different from the quantity of circulation." Marx defines quantity of circulation as "the sum of all banknotes in circulation in a particular country, together with all metal money, including precious metal in the form of bullion" — and notes that "a part of this quantity forms the banks' reserve and is constantly fluctuating in size."

The empirical proof of the separation is startling. On 12 November 1857 — the very date on which the Bank Act of 1844 was suspended, i.e. at the height of a panic — "the entire reserve of the Bank of England was only £580,751 (including London and all its branches); their deposits at the same time amounting to £22,500,000; of which near six and a half million belonged to London bankers" (Bank Acts, 1858, p. lvii). The ratio is roughly 39:1 in deposits to reserve. A colossal mass of loan capital rested on a minuscule metallic base. Marx's theoretical claim — that loan capital is not the same as circulating money — is thereby demonstrated by the most extreme data point available.

He then specifies what determines the rate of interest, carefully bracketing two other kinds of variation: "setting aside those taking place over longer periods, or the differences between interest rates in different countries; the first kind being conditioned by variations in the general rate of profit, and the second by differences in profit rates and in the development of credit." What remains is short-run variation, which "depend[s] on the supply of loan capital (all other factors, the state of confidence, etc., taken as equal), i.e. of capital lent in the form of money, in metal or notes; as distinct from industrial capital that is lent as such, in the commodity form, by commercial credit among the reproductive agents themselves." Here Marx establishes a clean analytical partition: money-loan capital (bank credit) versus commodity-loan capital (commercial credit). The interest rate is the price of the former specifically.

The £20 Hypothesis: Velocity versus Capital

Marx then lays out what is essentially a small formal argument to show that loan capital is not the same as money in circulation. "If £20 is lent five times in the course of a day, a money capital of £100 is lent altogether, and this would equally mean that this £20 had functioned at least four times as means of purchase or payment; for if it were without the mediation of purchase and payment, so that it had not represented the transformed form of capital (commodities, including labour-power) at least four times, it would not constitute a capital of £100 but simply five claims of £20 each."

The argument has an explicit minimum condition: the same note cannot be lent five times in a day and constitute £100 of capital unless it has successively mediated four real purchases or payments, i.e. unless the loan is repeatedly grounded in a real transaction that transforms commodities into money and back. If it is not so grounded — if the £20 is simply passed from hand to hand as a loan, or re-discounted as a fictitious bill — then the five acts of lending do not constitute a capital of £100; they constitute "simply five claims of £20 each." This is Marx's most rigorous formulation in the chapter of the difference between nominal claims and real capital. Velocity counts as capital only when it is the velocity of money mediating real metamorphoses of capital (commodity → money → commodity, including labour-power). Otherwise it is just multiplied paper.

Deposits, Return Migrations and the £100 Retailer

Marx then restates the conditions under which "large sums will be deposited while the amount of means of circulation is relatively small." There are exactly two: "(1) the number of purchases and payments that the same piece of money performs; (2) the number of return migrations in which it comes back to the banks as a deposit, so that its repeated function as means of purchase and payment is mediated by its renewed transformation into a deposit."

Return migration is the key concept: money that circulates does not merely circulate; it comes home to the banking system, is re-deposited, and is lent out again. The illustrative circuit is elegant and fully worked: a retail trader deposits £100 a week with his banker in money. The banker uses this to pay out part of a manufacturer's deposit. The manufacturer pays it to his workers. The workers use it to pay the retailer — who deposits it again with the bank. "The £100 deposited by the retailer has thus to serve firstly to pay out a deposit of the manufacturer's, secondly to pay the workers, thirdly to pay the retailer himself and fourthly to deposit a further part of the same retailer's money capital; for at the end of twenty weeks, if he did not have to draw on this money himself, he would have deposited £2,000 in the bank, using the same £100."

Note the arithmetic: 20 weeks × £100 = £2,000 of deposits from a single £100 note. Note also that every link in the circuit is a real economic act: payment of wages, purchase of consumption goods, extraction of surplus-value (the manufacturer's deposit represents realized value). Marx is at pains to show that the multiplication of deposits is not fictitious here — it is the money form of a genuinely recurring reproduction circuit. The astonishing growth of deposits is therefore compatible with a small note circulation, precisely because deposits measure turnover, not stock.

This also explains why the only visible sign of how much money capital is idle is the reserve: "The extent to which this money capital is unoccupied is shown only in the ebb and flow of the banks' reserve funds." Hence Weguelin's testimony that "the gold in the Bank of England is the 'only' reserve capital." Marx quotes him at length: "1258. Practically, I think, the rate of discount is governed by the amount of unemployed capital which there is in the country. The amount of unemployed capital is represented by the reserve of the Bank of England, which is practically a reserve of bullion. When, therefore, the bullion is drawn upon, it diminishes the amount of unemployed capital in the country, and consequently raises the value of that which remains." And: "1364. The reserve of bullion in the Bank of England is, in truth, the central reserve, or hoard of treasure, upon which the whole trade of the country is carried on ... And it is upon that hoard or reservoir that the action of the foreign exchanges always falls."

Marx lets this stand, but its implications are subversive. If a £580,751 reserve governs a £22.5 million deposit mass, then the interest rate — the price of the use of capital — is determined by a marginally small and externally vulnerable quantity. The credit system's apparent massive autonomy rests on a hair-trigger base. This is precisely the point at which the chapter's theme — the separation of money capital from real capital — becomes a theory of fragility: the more the system's loan capital is disconnected from real accumulation, the more its behaviour is determined by a reserve that has nothing to do with production.

The Empirical Proof of Genuine Accumulation: Export Statistics and the Ratchet

Marx then supplies the measure of real accumulation against which all this monetary froth must be judged: "One measure for the accumulation of genuine capital, i.e. productive and commodity capital, is provided by the statistics of exports and imports." Note he does not say the measure, but one measure — and the one he chooses is physical*, cross-border movement of goods.

The claim he establishes is that the English industrial cycle (1815–70) exhibits a ratchet pattern: "the maximum for the final period of prosperity before the crisis reappeared as the minimum for the period of prosperity that followed next, only to rise then to a new and much higher maximum." In other words, each cyclical trough bottoms out above the previous peak.

The data is laid out in sequence. In the prosperous year 1824, the real or declared value of exports from Great Britain and Ireland was £40,396,300. With the crisis of 1825, exports fell below this level, fluctuating between £35m and £39m annually. With the return of prosperity in 1834, the total rose above the earlier peak to £41,649,191, and in 1836 reached a new maximum of £53,368,571. In 1837 it fell again to £42m — and here is the ratchet: "the new minimum was already higher than the maximum of the previous cycle" (£40.4m). It then fluctuated between £50m and £53m. Recovery raised the 1844 total to £58[½]m, "already exceeding the 1836 maximum by far." 1845 reached £60,111,082; then fell back to somewhat over £57m in 1846, almost £59m in 1847, almost £53m in 1848; rising to £65[¼]m in 1849, almost £99m in 1853, £97m in 1854, £94[½]m in 1855, almost £116m in 1856, with a maximum in 1857 of £122m. In 1858 it fell to £116m, but had already risen in 1859 to £130m, in 1860 to almost £136m, in 1861 to only £125m — "here again the new minimum is higher than the previous cycle's maximum" — and in 1863 to £146[½]m.* Marx notes that the same pattern holds for imports, "which indicate the expansion of the market," but insists: "Here we are concerned only with the scale of production."

The purpose of this long statistical detour is dialectical. It shows that underneath the credit cycle — with its fictitious bills, its £580,751 reserves, its rediscount chains — there is a real, secular, ratcheting expansion of production. The money-capital phenomena of Section 1 are superstructural; the export figures are the base. And crucially, the export series also displays cyclical crashes (1825, 1837, 1846–48, 1857, 1861) that align with the credit catastrophes. So the autonomy of loan capital is not absolute: it is a real autonomy that is periodically and violently reabsorbed into material reality.

Engels' editorial note (marked "F. E.") adds a qualification: the pattern is "true for England only for the period of its effective industrial monopoly; but it is true in general for the totality of countries with modern large-scale industry, as long as the world market is still expanding." This is a significant theoretical claim: the ratchet is not a national peculiarity but a property of capital in its expansive phase, conditional on an expanding world market. If the world market ceases to expand, the ratchet breaks — with consequences Engels does not spell out but which the reader can infer.

Section 2 — Transformation of Capital or Revenue into Money That Becomes Loan Capital

Having exhausted the non-genuine sources, Marx turns to the genuine case. He defines the terrain negatively first: "Here we are considering the accumulation of money capital in so far as this does not express a stagnation in the flow of commercial credit, or saving either on the actual circulating medium or on the reserve capital of the agents engaged in reproduction." Having excluded the two "technical" cases from Section 1, he identifies the residual sources.

The first is external and accidental: "accumulation of money capital may arise through an exceptional influx of gold, as happened in 1852 and 1853 as a result of the new gold mines in Australia and California." The mechanism is precise: "This gold was deposited in the Bank of England. The depositors accepted notes in return, which they did not directly deposit again with bankers. In this way, the circulating medium underwent an extraordinary increase. (Evidence of Weguelin, B.A. 1857, no. 1329.) The Bank tried to valorize these deposits by reducing its discount rate to 2 per cent. In the course of six months in 1853, the quantity of gold piled up in the Bank grew to some £22–£23 million." Note that this is not genuine accumulation either — it is an influx of the money commodity that raises the quantity of circulation directly. The Bank's response — cutting the discount rate to 2% — is an attempt to make the deposits yield, i.e. to convert dead metal into valorized capital. This is an early, vivid instance of what Marx elsewhere calls the effect of gold discoveries in lowering the value of money and stimulating credit. The gold is real wealth in one sense, but it is not the product of the reproduction process; it is an addition to the money material that immediately swells loan capital.

The Money-Lending Class and the Redistribution of Surplus-Value

Marx then makes his central class-theoretic statement of the chapter. "All money-lending capitalists obviously accumulate directly in the money form, while we have seen that real accumulation by industrial capitalists occurs as a rule through an increase in the elements of reproductive capital itself." This is a structural asymmetry: the industrial capitalist accumulates by expanding plant, stock and labour-power (i.e. by adding to productive capital, which must then be monetized to be realized); the money-lending capitalist accumulates by adding coupons, which is immediate and requires no production.

From this asymmetry follows a dynamic: "The development of the credit system and the tremendous concentration of the money-lending business in the hands of big banks must therefore already accelerate in and of itself the accumulation of loanable capital, as a form separate from genuine accumulation." Note the causal structure: the credit system's own development is a cause, not merely an effect, of the divergence. Then Marx closes the loop on value theory: "This rapid development of loan capital is therefore a result of the genuine accumulation, as an effect of the development of the reproduction process, and the profit that forms the source of accumulation for these money capitalists is simply a deduction from the surplus-value that the reproductive agents extract (as well as an appropriation of part of the interest on the savings of others). Loan capital accumulates at the expense of both the industrial and commercial capitalists."

This is the chapter's most important theoretical assertion about class and value. Banks create nothing. Their accumulation is a transfer — a redistributive claim on the surplus-value of others, plus a cut of the interest on the savings of the non-capitalist classes. This has an immediate implication: the growth of finance capital is not the growth of wealth but a change in its distribution, and it can proceed independently of, and at the expense of, industrial accumulation.

Crisis Speculation in Devalued Securities

Marx then shows a second, more aggressive form of money-capitalist accumulation: buying distressed assets. "We have seen how, in the bad phases of the industrial cycle, the rate of interest may rise so high that it temporarily swallows up profits entirely for some branches of business, particularly those unfavourably located." Simultaneously, "the prices of government paper and other securities fall. This is the moment when money capitalists buy up this devalued paper on a massive scale, as it will soon go up again in the later phases, and even rise above its normal level. They will then sell it off, thereby appropriating a part of the public's money capital."

Three profits are realized here. First, a capital gain on the resale. Second, for "those securities that are not sold off," a higher yield relative to purchase price — "they yield a higher interest, since they were bought below their price." Third, the general counter-cyclical accumulation — "all the profit which the money capitalists make, and which they turn back into capital, they transform first of all into money capital for loan." Hence: "we already have an accumulation of this money capital as distinct from the genuine accumulation — even if the accumulation of its off-shoot — when we simply consider the money capitalists, bankers, etc. themselves, as the accumulation of this particular class of capitalists." The phrase "even if the accumulation of its off-shoot" concedes that this is derivative of real accumulation, but insists on its formal autonomy. And the law of motion: "this must grow with each extension of the credit system, as it accompanies the genuine expansion of the reproduction process." Again, accompanies — not causes.

Who Bears the Loss: Depositors Versus Banks

Marx adds a distributive refinement that anticipates modern analysis of financial losses. "If the rate of interest is low, this devaluation of money capital falls principally on the depositors and not on the banks." When asset values fall, it is the ultimate savers who absorb the loss, because banks have intermediated and matched positions; the depositor's claim is nominally fixed but its purchasing power over capital assets falls. Historical evidence is supplied: "Before the development of joint-stock banking, three-quarters of all bank deposits in England did not receive any interest. And where interest is now paid, this is at least 1 per cent less than the market rate." The banks thus enjoy a structural spread — a margin on intermediation that is pure deduction from the depositing public.

Revenue Itself Becomes Loan Capital

The final movement of the chapter generalizes the argument. Marx sets aside interest-bearing paper ("we ignore here the part that is invested in interest-bearing paper and accumulates in this form") and examines "the portion that is placed on the market as money capital for loan."

First, the undistributed profit of industrial capital: "the section of profit that is not spent as revenue, being rather designed for accumulation, but which the industrial capitalists concerned do not have any immediate employment for in their own businesses." This profit "exists first of all in commodity capital, making up a portion of its value, and is realized in money together with it." If it is not immediately reconverted into productive elements, "it must persist for a certain period in the money form." And here is the key dynamic: "Its amount rises with the volume of the capital itself, even given a declining rate of profit." Even in stagnation, the absolute mass of investible surplus grows — because the capital stock on which it is calculated grows.

Second, and more strikingly, revenue itself: "The part to be spent as revenue is gradually consumed, but in the meantime it constitutes loan capital as a deposit with the banker. And so even the growth of the part of the profit spent as revenue is expressed in a gradual and constantly repeated accumulation of loan capital." The logic is the time-lag of consumption: the rich do not spend their income instantly; between receipt and expenditure, it sits in a bank and is lent out. Consumption itself therefore generates loan capital at the moment of receipt and destroys it at the moment of spending.

Third, Marx generalizes to all revenues: "this holds good of all revenues, in so far as they are only gradually consumed — i.e. ground-rent, the higher forms of salary, the incomes of the unproductive classes, etc. All of these assume for a time the form of money revenue and can hence be converted into deposits and thereby into loan capital." This is a decisive expansion: it shows that the pool of loanable capital is fed not only by capitalist profit and saving but by every monetary receipt in the economy, including rent, salaries and the incomes of the unproductive classes. The credit system is a gigantic machine for skimming the time between income receipt and income expenditure, and for turning that time into someone else's interest-bearing capital.

The Yarn Illustration: Money as the "Value Existence" of Surplus-Value, and the Minimum Limit

The chapter closes with a compressed theoretical statement, illustrated by the spinner. Consider a spinner who has sold his yarn for cotton; the part of the yarn that forms revenue and is exchanged for money aside, the actual existence of his industrial capital is the yarn — which has passed into the possession of the weaver, or even the private consumer. This yarn, "whether it serves for reproduction or for consumption, is the existence of both capital value and of the surplus-value contained in it." The amount of surplus-value transformed into money depends on how much surplus-value the yarn contained — a fact about the production process, not about the money market.

But then the decisive step: "as soon as it is transformed into money, this money is simply the value existence of that surplus-value. And as such it becomes an element of loan capital." The concept of value existence (Wertdasein) expresses the ontological shift: surplus-value existed as a physical, tangible, productive thing (yarn) within an industrial capital; upon sale it becomes a pure value magnitude — money — and in that form it is instantly eligible to become loan capital. "No more is needed for this than that it should be transformed into a deposit, if it has not already been lent out by its owner."

The final sentence is a materialist caveat that closes the section and, effectively, the chapter: "To be transformed back into productive capital, on the other hand, it must already have reached a certain minimum limit." This is the counterweight to everything that has gone before. Money can become loan capital freely, instantly, with no minimum. But money can become productive capital only in discrete, lumpy amounts — a new machine, a new plant, a new workgang — "a certain minimum limit." Hence the structurally asymmetric ease of the transformation into loan capital versus the difficulty of the transformation into productive capital. This asymmetry is, in the end, the material root of the entire divergence the chapter has traced. Money capital's autonomy from real accumulation is not an illusion of the money market's rhetoric; it is grounded in the money form's infinite divisibility, perfect liquidity and instantaneous transferability as against the lumpiness, illiquidity and slow turnover of the things that actually produce.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter yields several fundamental principles that far exceed its immediate textual occasion.

First, the autonomy–dependence paradox of finance. Loan capital is systematically autonomous in form from real accumulation — it can grow through technical banking economies, gold influxes, deposit mobilization, counter-cyclical recycling and the mere time-lag of consumption — yet it remains materially dependent on real accumulation for its ultimate value source. This is not a contradiction but the precise structure of the credit system: the form floats free while the substance remains anchored. Marx's insistence that money capitalists' profit is "simply a deduction from the surplus-value that the reproductive agents extract" supplies the anchoring; his catalogue of autonomous growth mechanisms supplies the floating.

Second, the systematic dissociation of risk from perception. The rural depositor of the Ipswich passage "does not have the remotest suspicion" that his deposit underwrites operations in London and the colonies "over whose operations neither of them have the slightest control." Marx identifies, a century and a half before the vocabulary existed, the mechanism of opacity in securitized intermediation: lengthening chains of claims sever the correspondence between the risk borne and the risk understood, with the consequence that the pool of loan capital expands beyond the capacity of any participant to monitor.

Third, the class theory of finance. Bankers, billbrokers and discount houses do not create value; they appropriate portions of surplus-value already extracted in production and portions of the interest on others' savings. The growth of finance capital is therefore a redistributive and potentially parasitic phenomenon, capable of "devouring credits" and of diverting capital into "the most objectionable of all fixed securities." Marx simultaneously notes where losses ultimately land — on depositors, not banks — which is a strikingly modern insight into asymmetric absorption of financial devaluation.

Fourth, the statistical-methodological lesson. Marx's use of the £580,751 / £22,500,000 ratio makes a permanent point: monetary magnitudes (deposits, loan volume, "money capital") are not measures of real wealth. Deposits measure turnover and circulation, not stock of capital; the £20 lent five times is £100 only if it has mediated real transactions — otherwise it is five paper claims. When monetary aggregates are read as proxies for real accumulation, the analysis becomes precisely the "credit gibberish" Marx mocks. The correct measure of genuine accumulation, he insists, is physical: the export and import series with its ten-year cycles and its ratchet.

Fifth, the theory of crisis and its ratchet. The export data establish a secular expansion beneath the credit cycle: each cycle's minimum exceeds the previous maximum. Credit autonomy is therefore periodically and violently reabsorbed — the panic of 1825, 1837, 1847, 1857 each shows loan capital collapsing back toward its metallic base (a £580,751 reserve). Engels' editorial qualification — that the ratchet holds "as long as the world market is still expanding" — is an implicit warning about what happens when external expansion ceases.

The broader implications are considerable. Marx here provides one of the earliest and most rigorous accounts of what contemporary analysis calls shadow banking: the billbrokers who are "in reality bankers upon an immense scale," operating outside formal banking regulation, transforming short-term deposits into longer and riskier claims, and expanding credit "upon the credit of the bank alone, without reference to the quality of the bills." He also sketches a theory of interbank and core-periphery capital flows — the gravitational pull of Lombard Street, the mechanized transfer of rural surplus to metropolitan centres, and the export of that surplus into colonial fixed assets — anticipating modern analyses of global imbalances and capital account dynamics. And he articulates the elementary asymmetry between the instantaneous liquidity of money and the irreducible lumpiness of productive investment ("a certain minimum limit"), which explains why credit systems persistently generate pools of loan capital that have no immediate productive employment, and why financial booms must find speculative outlets for money that cannot become capital.

The chapter's philosophical core is its insistence on value existence: surplus-value is one thing as a mass of yarn and another as a sum of money, and the transition between them is not merely formal but ontologically consequential. The same surplus-value that was a determinate physical magnitude embedded in a production process becomes, as money, a fungible, mobile, impersonal claim — and, as such, an element of loan capital able to travel the world. The credit system is thus revealed not as a collection of errors but as the objective social machinery through which the money form of value separates itself from the productive body of capital, generating a real autonomy with real speculative consequences, and ultimately reasserting — in crisis — the material reality it appeared to have escaped.
