Volume 3 • Chapter 8: Different Compositions of Capital in Different Branches of Production

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 8 opens the second Part of Volume 3 — "The Transformation of Profit into Average Profit" — and it is a chapter of deliberate narrowness whose narrowness turns out to be its most dangerous feature. Its immediate objective is modest and technical: to demonstrate, on a strictly controlled set of assumptions, that capitals of equal size invested in different spheres of production must yield unequal rates of profit, because the organic composition of capital (and, secondarily, the length of turnover) differs from sphere to sphere. The chapter therefore does not yet explain the formation of a general rate of profit; it explains the obstacle that such a rate must overcome. It is the negative prelude to the positive theory that follows.

Marx constructs his argument in three moves. First, he narrows the field with a set of idealizing assumptions: the rate of surplus-value, the length of the working day, and therefore the wage level are held constant across all spheres, so that everything he has demonstrated in Part One about the effect of changing values and turnover times on a single capital can be transposed from a diachronic to a synchronic register — what was previously a succession of states of one capital now becomes a set of simultaneous differences among many capitals. Second, he executes the analytic core of the chapter: a series of arithmetical illustrations in which capitals of identical magnitude (100 units) split into wildly different proportions of constant and variable capital yield correspondingly different masses of surplus-value, hence radically unequal rates of profit. This is the chapter's decisive theorem, and it rests on a single bedrock proposition — living labour is the sole source of surplus-value — reinforced by a reductio: if a 90c + 10v capital produced the same profit as a 10c + 90v capital, "surplus-value and hence value in general had a completely different source from labour, and in this way any rational basis for political economy would fall away." Third, Marx confronts the consequence, which is explosive: if his theory of value implies permanently divergent profit rates, then the theory seems flatly contradicted by the observable world, where no such divergence exists and, he insists, none could exist without abolishing the entire capitalist system.

That last admission is the pivot of the chapter. Its closing sentences do not resolve the contradiction; they set it up and leave it hanging — "The theory of value thus appears incompatible with the actual movement, incompatible with the actual phenomena of production, and it might seem that we must abandon all hope of understanding these phenomena." The final paragraph offers the escape hatch: because the cost price is identical for equally-sized capitals regardless of composition (the capitalist cannot tell whether his £100 is 90c + 10v or 10c + 90v), competition between capitals is waged on the terrain of equal cost prices, and it is on that terrain that an average profit is generated. Chapter 8 thus closes at the threshold of the transformation problem. Theoretically, it belongs to Marx's larger project of demonstrating that the concrete forms of capitalist economic life (prices of production, average profit) are not anomalies standing beside the labour theory of value but necessary appearances generated by it. Intellectually, the chapter functions as a stress test of the value theory itself — it shows the theory producing a result that falsifies the phenomenal world, and thereby forces the introduction of a mediating mechanism (the average rate of profit formed through competition) without which the theory would collapse.

The chapter is therefore indispensable: it is where Marx deliberately makes his own theory look wrong in order to show why things are not as they seem.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Methodological Premise: Idealization and the "Concept" of a Sphere of Production

Marx begins by recalling the achievement of Part One ("In the previous Part we showed, among other things, how the rate of profit may vary, either rising or falling, even with the same rate of surplus-value") and then immediately erects the scaffolding of this chapter's argument: "we now assume that the degree of exploitation of labour, i.e. the rate of surplus-value, and the length of the working day, is the same in all the spheres of production."

This is more than a simplifying hypothesis; it is a methodological confession. Marx knows that in reality the rate of surplus-value differs enormously from industry to industry, and that wages vary widely. His justification for ignoring these differences is drawn from Adam Smith, whom he credits in a footnote and in the text with having shown that inter-sphere variations in wages and profits "cancel one another out through all kinds of compensations, either real or accepted by prejudice." The footnote on Smith is unusually rich in editorial judgment (probably Engels's): Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776) "gave bourgeois political economy its developed form," and was "not just scientifically important, but also a major ideological weapon for the developing industrial capitalist class." Marx's relationship to Smith is thus doubly coded — Smith is the man who had already done the preliminary empirical spadework of showing that such variations wash out, yet he is also the man whose fundamental theoretical conceptions Marx subjects, elsewhere (Theories of Surplus-Value), to his fullest critique.

Marx then extends the idealization to the wage differentials that correspond to the distinction between simple and complex labour (the topic of the first chapter of Volume 1, p. 135 in the Pelican edition). His point here is analytically sharp: the higher pay of a goldsmith relative to a day-labourer does not alter the degree of exploitation, because the goldsmith's skilled labour also produces a proportionately greater surplus-value. "In a general analysis of the present kind, it is assumed throughout that actual conditions correspond to their concept, or, and this amounts to the same thing, actual conditions are depicted only in so far as they express their own general type."

This sentence is the theoretical charter of the whole chapter and deserves close attention. "Actual conditions correspond to their concept" (Begriff) is a Hegelian formulation meaning that Marx abstracts from contingent frictions in order to expose the essential logic of the capitalist mode of production. Frictions in wage equalization — local obstacles to the equalization of wages and working hours — are "accidental and inessential" for the general investigation, however important for specialist work on wages. Marx is deliberately declaring what he is not explaining, so that the phenomenon he is explaining can be seen in pure form.

Two boundary conditions are also stated. First, differences in the rate of surplus-value between countries are "completely outside the scope of our present investigation," whose object "is simply to present the way in which a general rate of profit is arrived at within one particular country." Second, Marx sketches how the international case would eventually be handled — first vary the national rates of surplus-value, then compare national profit rates on that given basis; whatever residual variation remains must be due to circumstances in which, "as in this chapter, surplus-value is assumed to be everywhere the same."

From Diachronic Succession to Synchronic Coexistence

The next move is one of the most elegant methodological operations in the entire volume. In Part One, Marx had shown that a single capital's rate of profit could rise or fall under a constant rate of surplus-value if (a) the value of some portion of its constant capital changed (altering the c/v ratio), or (b) its turnover time lengthened or shortened. The amount of profit (= amount of surplus-value) was unaffected by these value fluctuations; only the rate, i.e. profit relative to the capital advanced, was modified — except indirectly, where such fluctuations tied up or released capital.

Now Marx performs the transposition: "What we previously viewed as changes that the same capital underwent in succession, we now consider as simultaneous distinctions between capital investments that exist alongside one another in different spheres of production." The law governing a succession of states of one capital becomes the law governing a coexistence of different capitals. Hence his two announced investigative tasks: (1) differences in the organic composition of capitals, and (2) differences in their turnover time.

A crucial qualifier is appended: whenever the chapter speaks of the composition or turnover of capital "in a specific branch of production," it means "the normal, average situation for capital invested in this branch," not "chance differences between individual capitals invested there." The unit of analysis is the sphere or branch, represented by its average capital — which is why the chapter's conclusions apply to industrial averages, not to individual firms.

The Two-Faced Nature of Variable Capital

Before introducing the composition concept formally, Marx pauses to establish that with s' and the working day constant, variable capital functions as an index of the mass of labour-power set in motion. If £100 is the weekly wage of 100 workers, then n × £100 is the wage of n × 100 workers, and if £100 represents 6000 hours of labour (100 workers × 60 hours), then £200 represents 12,000 hours and £50 only 3000. The variable capital serves "as an index of the mass of labour set in motion by a certain total capital."

This seemingly innocuous convention sets up the chapter's deepest dependency: everything hinges on the fact that variable capital stands proxy for living labour, not merely for the wage bill.

The Three Compositions: Technical, Value, Organic

Marx now defines, with terminological care, the triad on which the whole chapter turns. "By the composition of capital we mean... the ratio between its active and its passive component, between variable and constant capital." But two distinct relationships hide within this single expression.

The first is the technical composition of capital: the proportion, determined by technical conditions at a given stage of productivity, between a definite mass of labour-power (a number of workers) and a definite mass of means of production (machines, raw materials) that those workers set in motion and productively consume. "A definite number of workers corresponds to a definite quantity of means of production, and thus a definite amount of living labour to a definite amount of labour already objectified in means of production." This proportion may be the same in branches of industry that are very far apart, and may differ between branches of the same industry. Marx calls technical composition "the actual basis of its organic composition."

The second is the value composition of capital: the ratio of the values of the variable and constant portions. It follows the technical composition only when values happen to line up. The decisive illustration: certain operations in copper or iron might involve the same technical proportion between labour-power and means of production, but "because copper is dearer than iron, the value relationship between variable and constant capital will be different in each case." The distinction reveals itself in two ways: the value ratio can change while the technical composition stays constant (price changes), and the value ratio can stay the same while the technical composition changes (if the change in quantities is cancelled by an opposite change in their values).

The organic composition of capital — the chapter's master concept — is then defined precisely: "the name we give to its value composition, in so far as this is determined by its technical composition and reflects it." The distinction is essential. A mere value composition (say, a temporarily inflated price of copper) is not an organic composition; only a value ratio that expresses and reflects an underlying technical ratio qualifies. This guards Marx against conflating price perturbations with structural composition.

He then re-specifies the assumptions in the light of this: variable capital is taken as an index of a definite mass of living labour (not, as in Part One, of a possibly-changed price for the same labour); a difference in the constant capital may index either a different volume of means of production set in motion, or a different value of those means of production — and "here, therefore, these two aspects both come into consideration."

The Wage as Index of More Labour Than It Contains

There follows one of the chapter's most philosophically loaded passages, the "fundamental point" Marx insists must be noted. Assume £100 is the weekly wage of 100 workers; the working week is 60 hours; the rate of surplus-value is 100 per cent. The workers work 30 hours for themselves and 30 gratis. The £100 wage embodies only 3000 hours (the necessary labour of 100 workers × 30 hours), yet the same workers perform 6000 hours, the other 3000 being embodied in the £100 of surplus-value the capitalist pockets.

There is therefore a fundamental distinction within variable capital itself: (1) as the value laid out in wages — a definite quantity of objectified labour (50 worker-weeks = £100); and (2) as an index of the mass of living labour it sets in motion (100 worker-weeks = 6000 hours). "This last is always greater than the labour contained in the variable capital," and is expressed in a higher value determined by the number of workers set in motion and the quantity of surplus labour they perform. The wage of £1, "the expression of half a week's labour, sets a whole week's labour in motion."

This passage is doing double duty. It explains why £100 of variable capital can be the index of £200 of value product; and it pre-empts a potential confusion — namely that £100 "contains" only 50 weeks of labour, so that the operative magnitude must be defined by what it commands, not what it is.

First Numerical Excursus: Unequal Organic Compositions at Equal Capital Size

With the conceptual apparatus in place, Marx delivers the chapter's central demonstration. Consider two capitals of equal size, £700 each:

  • Sphere A: 600c + 100v = 700
  • Sphere B: 100c + 600v = 700

Under s' = 100 per cent, A's 100v yields 100 of surplus-value; B's 600v yields 600. The surplus labour appropriated is 50 working weeks (3000 hours) in A and 300 working weeks (18,000 hours) in B — six times as much. Consequently:

  • Rate of profit in A = 100/700 = 14 2/7 per cent
  • Rate of profit in B = 600/700 = 85 5/7 per cent

The rate in B is six times that in A, and — crucially — the mass of profit is also six times greater (600 versus 100), because six times as much living labour has been set in motion with the same capital.

The general formula being instantiated here is worth writing out explicitly, since Marx's arithmetic presupposes it throughout:

p' = s/(c + v) = s'·v / (c + v)

where p' is the rate of profit, s is surplus-value, s' is the rate of surplus-value, and C = c + v is total capital. Equivalently, p' = s'·(v/C). With s' constant, the rate of profit is strictly proportional to the share of variable capital in the total capital — that is, to v/C. In A, v/C = 100/700 = 1/7, giving p' = 1 × 1/7 = 14 2/7 per cent. In B, v/C = 600/700 = 6/7, giving p' = 85 5/7 per cent. The entire chapter is a commentary on this formula.

The Percentage Reduction: Why Absolute Scale Is Irrelevant

Marx immediately generalizes by scaling A up. Suppose A invests not £700 but £7,000, while B remains at £700. Keeping A's organic composition (600c : 100v = 6:1), A now uses £1,000 as variable capital, employing 1000 workers for a week = 60,000 hours of living labour, of which 30,000 are surplus. A's profit grows to £1,000, but its rate of profit is unchanged:

p'_A = 1000/7000 = 100/700 = 14 2/7 per cent

against B's 85 5/7 per cent. "With equal amounts of capital, the rates of profit here are different, since at equal rates of surplus-value the masses of surplus-value and therefore profit that are produced differ as a result of the different masses of living labour set in motion."

The conceptual payoff is that "differing organic compositions of capitals are thus independent of their absolute magnitudes. The only question is always how much of each 100 units is variable capital and how much is constant." This licenses the reduction of all capitals to percentage terms, which is exactly what the rest of the chapter does.

Second Numerical Excursus: Equal Technical Basis, Unequal Constant Capital Values

Marx now sharply distinguishes a second case from the first. Suppose the technical conditions are the same in A and B, but the value of the constant capital element differs — say, machinery and raw materials are simply dearer in B.

  • Case A: 200c + 100v = 300
  • Case B: 400c + 100v = 500

At s' = 100 per cent, both produce £100 of surplus-value, hence £100 of profit. But:

  • p'_A = 100/300 = 33⅓ per cent
  • p'_B = 100/500 = 20 per cent

Expressed per £100 of capital, in B only £20 (a fifth) forms variable capital, while in A £33⅓ (a third) is variable. "B produces less profit for each £100 than does A, because it sets less living labour in motion [for each £100]. The difference in the rate of profit is thus reduced here again to a difference in the mass of profit — because mass of surplus-value — produced for each 100 units of capital invested."

The distinction between this case and the first is then stated with precision: equalizing A and B in the second case would require only a change in the value of the constant capital with the technical basis unchanged; in the first case, "the technical composition itself differs between the two spheres of production and would have to be transformed in order for such an equalization to occur." This is precisely the difference between the mere value composition and the organic composition defined earlier — the second case is a difference in value composition without a difference in organic composition properly understood, yet it produces the same effect on the profit rate. The upshot is that the quantitative variable doing the work is always the same: the proportion of each 100 units that is variable.

The Reductio: The Impossible Alternative

Marx then deploys the chapter's strongest arm — a reductio ad absurdum aimed at any attempt to derive profit from anything other than living labour.

Capitals of equal size, operating with the same working day and degree of exploitation, produce very different amounts of surplus-value and therefore profit, and this is because their variable portions differ according to their differing organic composition. Hence "equal-sized portions of the total capital in different spheres of production include sources of surplus-value of unequal size, and the only source of surplus-value is living labour."

The reductio is stated bluntly: "If a capital whose percentage composition is 90c + 10v were to produce just as much surplus-value or profit, at the same level of exploitation of labour, as a capital of 10c + 90v, it would be as clear as day that surplus-value and hence value in general had a completely different source from labour, and in this way any rational basis for political economy would fall away."

Marx then puts numbers on the absurdity. With £1 as the weekly wage for 60 hours' work and s' = 100 per cent, a single worker's total value product in a week is £2.

  • 10 workers (the 90c + 10v capital): total product £20, wages £10, so surplus-value = £10 → rate of profit 10 per cent.
  • 90 workers (the 10c + 90v capital): total product £180, wages £90, so surplus-value = £90 → rate of profit 90 per cent.

"If it should be otherwise, value and surplus-value would have to be something other than objectified labour."

This is the theoretical nerve-center of the chapter: the divergence of profit rates is not an unfortunate by-product of the analysis but a requirement of any theory in which labour is the sole source of value. A theory that produced equal profit rates for 90c + 10v and 10c + 90v could not be a labour theory of value at all.

The Non-Proportionality of Profit to Capital Size

An important corollary follows immediately and is stated with some emphasis: because capitals of equal size in different spheres — and therefore capitals of different sizes considered by percentage — produce unequal profits, "the profits of unequal capitals in different spheres of production cannot stand in proportion to their respective sizes." If profits did increase in proportion to capital size, that would itself imply equal profit rates for equal capitals regardless of composition — which has just been shown impossible. "It is only within the same sphere of production, where the organic composition of capital is therefore given, or between different spheres of production with the same organic composition of capital, that the mass of profit stands in exact proportion to the mass of capital employed."

This is Marx isolating the restricted domain in which the naive "profit is proportional to capital" assumption actually holds — the same domain in which what Marx elsewhere calls the "law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall" can be stated cleanly, namely the domain of a single composition.

Value and Surplus-Value Before Averaging: The 110 and the 190

Marx then restates the argument in terms of commodity values, under the assumption that commodities are sold at their values. "The value of a commodity is equal to the value of the constant capital contained in it, plus the value of the variable capital reproduced in it, plus the increment on this variable capital, the surplus-value produced." Given a certain rate of surplus-value, the mass of that surplus-value depends on the mass of variable capital. Hence:

  • A capital of 100 composed as 90c + 10v + 10s = 110
  • A capital of 100 composed as 10c + 90v + 90s = 190

If commodities sell at their values, the first product sells at 110, of which 10 is surplus-value (unpaid labour); the second sells at 190, of which 90 is surplus-value. This restatement matters because it shows that the divergence in profit rates is not an artefact of applying profit to cost price, but is already present at the level of values and surplus-values. The divergence Marx identifies is one that exists before any question of price-of-production arises — which is exactly why it constitutes a problem for the theory.

The International Comparison: Europe and Asia

Marx then offers his most striking illustration — a comparison of national rates of profit. In a European country, s' might be 100 per cent (the worker works half the day for himself, half for his employer); in an Asian country it might be 25 per cent (four-fifths for himself, one-fifth for his employer). Assume further that the European composition of national capital is 84c + 16v, while the Asian, "where little machinery, etc. is used and relatively little raw material productively consumed in a given period of time," is 16c + 84v. The calculation runs:

  • Europe: value of product = 84c + 16v + 16s = 116; rate of profit = 16/100 = 16 per cent.
  • Asia: value of product = 16c + 84v + 21s = 121; rate of profit = 21/100 = 21 per cent (84 × 0.25 = 21).

Thus the rate of profit would be higher in the Asian country despite the rate of surplus-value being only a fourth as great. Marx notes with relish that "Carey, Bastiat and their like would draw precisely the opposite conclusion" — a reference to the apologetic "harmony of interests" economists. The footnote on Bastiat is characteristically acidic: Marx regarded him as "the most superficial and thus successful representative of apologetic vulgar economics," citing the Postface to the Second German Edition of Volume 1.

Two caveats must be noted honestly here. First, as Marx himself immediately remarks, "different national rates of profit generally depend on different national rates of surplus-value; but in this chapter we are comparing unequal rates of profit that spring from one and the same rate of surplus-value." This illustration is therefore a counterfactual application of the chapter's method to a case that is outside the chapter's stated scope — its purpose is purely to display the elegance of the composition effect. Second, an arithmetic nuance deserves flagging: the text as printed says the Asian rate would be "some 25 per cent higher" than the European, whereas 21 per cent is in fact about 31 per cent higher than 16 per cent (the absolute difference being 5 percentage points). The figure is either loose or reflects numerical rounding in the manuscript; the direction and magnitude of the result are unaffected, and the substantive point — that a high-composition, high-exploitation economy can have a lower national profit rate than a low-composition, low-exploitation economy — stands.

Turnover Time as the Second Source of Disparity

Marx now turns to his second announced variable: "Besides the differing organic composition of capital... there is a further source of inequality between rates of profit: the variation in the length of capital turnover in the different spheres of production." Drawing on Chapter 4, he recalls that with the same composition of capital, rates of profit vary inversely to turnover time, and that the same variable capital turning over in different periods brings in unequal masses of surplus-value over the course of a year. Turnover variation is therefore a second cause of why equal capitals need not produce equally large profits in equal periods of time.

This is the point at which the synchronic transposition is most fully at work: the temporal dimension (how long a capital takes to return) becomes one of the axes along which different spheres differ, no longer a process a single capital undergoes but a structural characteristic distinguishing one sphere from another.

Fixed and Circulating Capital: A Distinction Without Independent Force

Marx then disposes of a possible objection with considerable surgical care. Does the proportion in which capital is composed of fixed and circulating elements affect the profit rate? His answer: not in itself. "It can only affect it either if this differing composition coincides with a differing ratio between the variable and constant portions, in which case the variation in the rate of profit is due to this difference and not to the different ratio between circulating and fixed; or alternatively if the varying ratio between fixed and circulating components involves a variation in the turnover time that it takes to realize a certain profit."

He elaborates: capitals with different proportions of fixed and circulating capital do indeed have different turnover times, and this does influence the profit rate where it materially affects the mass of surplus-value a capital can appropriate in a given time. But turnover variations are significant "only in so far as it affects the mass of surplus-value that the same capital can appropriate and realize in a given time." Where unequal circulating/fixed compositions do not entail unequal turnover times, the unequal composition yields nothing: the two capitals "realize equal profits in equal times, even though they take different times to turn over."

The Engels footnote 21 pertinently illustrates and qualifies the argument, warning that Marx's claim about fixed/circulating capital and equal rates of profit "is correct only when capitals A and B have a different value composition, but nevertheless their variable components in percentage terms are directly proportionate to their turnover times, or in inverse proportion to their number of turnovers." Engels then supplies a numerical proof:

  • Capital A = 20c fixed + 70c circulating = 90c + 10v = 100. With s' = 100 per cent, the 10v produces 10s in one turnover; rate of profit for the turnover = 10 per cent. If A turns over twice a year, its annual rate is 20 per cent.
  • Capital B = 60c fixed + 20c circulating = 80c + 20v = 100. The 20v produces 20s per turnover; rate per turnover = 20 per cent. If B turns over once, its annual rate is also 20 per cent.

The two capitals realize equal annual rates despite differing composition (fixed/circulating) and differing turnover counts. The general conclusion Marx draws is that the differing proportions of circulating and fixed capital "do not have any bearing in themselves on the rate of profit; what is decisive is the ratio between the variable capital and the constant." He adds a warning against an epidemiological error of inference: where fixed capital is strongly developed, this is merely an expression of the fact that production is large-scale and constant capital predominates over variable — it is the predominance, not the fixedness, that lowers the profit rate. Anyone who saw the vast fixed capital of modern industry and concluded that fixed capital as such depresses the profit rate would have "incorrect conclusions" foisted upon them.

Interim Summary and the Antinomy of the Theory of Value

Marx now consolidates: in different branches of industry unequal profit rates prevail, corresponding to different organic compositions and — within the stated limits — different turnover times. So, at a given rate of surplus-value, only for capitals of the same organic composition (with equal turnover times) does the law hold, as a general tendency, that profits stand in direct proportion to the amount of capital, and that capitals of equal size yield equal profits in the same period of time.

And then comes the fracture. "The above argument is true on the same basis as our whole investigation so far: that commodities are sold at their values. There is no doubt, however, that in actual fact, ignoring inessential, accidental circumstances that cancel each other out, no such variation in the average rate of profit exists between different branches of industry, and it could not exist without abolishing the entire system of capitalist production. The theory of value thus appears incompatible with the actual movement, incompatible with the actual phenomena of production, and it might seem that we must abandon all hope of understanding these phenomena."

This is the chapter's climax and its most self-critical moment. Marx juxtaposes two propositions: (a) the theory of value, on his premises, entails permanently divergent profit rates across spheres; (b) empirically and by the logic of capitalist competition, no such divergence exists, and none could exist, since capital mobility would immediately level any differential. The opposition is not merely apparent but real at this stage of the analysis. If (a) is true, the theory seems falsified by (b) — the very phenomena the theory is meant to explain appear to contradict it. Marx does not pretend the contradiction away with a hand-wave; he states it in its most damaging form and defers the resolution.

The Bridge to Resolution: The Equality of Cost Prices

The final paragraph supplies the first plank of that resolution. From Part One it emerged that cost prices are the same for the products of different spheres of production if equal portions of capital are advanced in their production, no matter how different the organic composition of those capitals. In the cost price, "the distinction between variable and constant capital is abolished, as far as the capitalist is concerned." A commodity that costs the capitalist £100 to produce costs him the same whether he lays out 90c + 10v or 10c + 90v. "In each case it costs him £100, neither more nor less. Cost prices are the same for equal capital investments in different spheres, however much the values and surplus-values produced may differ."

This equality of cost prices is the basis for the competition between capital investments by means of which an average profit is produced. The chapter thus ends on a promissory note: the very equality of cost prices that obscures the underlying divergence of surplus-value masses is also the concrete mechanism through which capitals compete and thereby level the profit rate. The apparent antinomy will be dissolved not by abandoning the value theory, but by showing that the average rate of profit is itself a form generated by the value relations the theory describes — the subject of the chapters that follow.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

1. The whole chapter rests on a single identification: constant rate of surplus-value + varying composition = varying rate of profit. In algebraic form, p' = s' · (v/C). Everything else is elaboration. The theorem is not merely a curiosity; it is the direct expression of the proposition that living labour is the sole source of surplus-value. Any theory that generated equal profit rates for 90c + 10v and 10c + 90v would have already abandoned the labour theory of value. The chapter is therefore, in effect, a proof by reductio of the labour theory of value's internal consistency — a demonstration that the theory's apparent embarrassments flow from its own logic, not from its failures.

2. The synchronic transposition is the methodological key. Marx's procedure — reading a succession of states of one capital as a coexistence of many differently-composed capitals — is what allows the analytic apparatus of Part One to be repurposed to explain structural differences between spheres. This is a general feature of Marx's method: dynamics investigated at one level of abstraction become statics at a higher level. What looks like a temporal process (a capital's turnover, a change in the value of its constant elements) becomes a systematic differentiation (between branches, between national economies).

3. The chapter distinguishes sharply between three uses of "composition." Technical composition (a physical ratio of labour-power to means of production), value composition (a ratio of their values), and organic composition (value composition insofar as it reflects technical composition). This triad is not pedantry. It allows Marx to show that equalizing capitals in the second numerical case (differing values of the same technical quantity) requires only a change of values, whereas equalizing them in the first (differing technical ratios) requires a transformation of the technical basis itself. It also guards against the error of treating every price-driven shift in c/v as a change in organic composition.

4. Variable capital is theorized as an index of living labour, not simply as a wage bill. The wage of £1 that expresses half a week's labour sets a whole week's labour in motion. This is why £100 of variable capital, which contains only 50 worker-weeks of objectified labour, can be the index of 100 worker-weeks of living labour and of a value product of £200. The subtlety pre-empts a cruder reading in which the profit rate could be read off simply from wage costs.

5. Profit is not proportional to capital size in general — and the restricted domain where it is must be carefully specified. The law of proportionality holds only within a sphere (where composition is given) or between spheres of identical composition. This negative result is exactly why a general rate of profit must be formed through competition rather than presupposed — and it is why Marx calls the divergence between the theory of value and the uniform observable profit rate an "antinomy."

6. Turnover time is a second, independent axis of disparity. Combined with composition, it accounts for all divergences at a constant rate of surplus-value. But its effect is mediated entirely through its impact on the mass of surplus-value a capital can appropriate in a given period; where it does not have this effect, it is irrelevant. Similarly, the fixed/circulating distinction has no independent force — it acts only through its effect on c/v or on turnover.

7. The chapter deliberately makes the labour theory of value look falsified — and leaves it hanging. The most intellectually striking feature of Chapter 8 is its refusal to smooth over the contradiction. Marx asserts in the strongest terms that no variation in the average rate of profit can exist between branches, and that this result — paradoxically — flows from the very analysis that seems to require variation. He does not retreat into the "prove it empirically" evasion; he states that theory and reality are, at this stage, flatly opposed. The resolution is deferred to the mechanism of competition operating on the terrain of equal cost prices — cost prices being the one magnitude on which composition has no effect and through which capitals can therefore be compared and levelled.

8. The broader implication is philosophical and political. Philosophically, the chapter demonstrates the necessity of the movement from essence (value and surplus-value) to appearance (price of production, average profit) in Marx's system. The apparent contradiction between the theory and the phenomena is not a refutation but a task: to show how the uniform rate of profit is a necessary form of appearance generated by the value relations themselves. Politically, the demonstration that a low-exploitation, low-composition economy (Marx's Asia) can have a higher profit rate than a high-exploitation, high-composition economy (Marx's Europe) demolishes at a stroke the apologetic "harmony" arguments of Carey and Bastiat — whose conclusions, as Marx writes, are "precisely the opposite" of his own. The rate of profit is not a moral reward for capital's productivity; it is a quotient determined by the fraction of capital laid out in living labour. The higher the exploitation, the more machinery, the richer the country — and, paradoxically, the lower the rate of profit. That inversion, established here in the abstract, is the seed of the whole subsequent analysis of capitalist dynamics in Volume 3.
