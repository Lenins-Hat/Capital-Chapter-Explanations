Volume 3 • Chapter 3: The Relation of the Rate of Profit to the Rate of Surplus-Value

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem

Chapter 3 of the third volume of Capital opens the volume's Part One, whose title — "The Transformation of Surplus-Value into Profit" — names the entire intellectual operation that the volume must perform. That operation begins here, with a deliberately narrow, almost austere question: given that the profit accruing to a capital is quantitatively identical with the surplus-value that capital produces, what is the precise mathematical relationship between the rate of profit and the rate of surplus-value? The chapter is, as Marx announces on its first page, "firstly, therefore, a purely mathematical one."

This is not a rhetorical flourish. It is a methodological declaration. Marx is about to do something that neither the classical political economists nor his own earlier volumes had done with full rigour: he will hold the mass of profit equal to the mass of surplus-value, strip away every complicating circumstance he can legitimately abstract from, and then trace — algebraically, case by case, without remainder — how the ratio p' = s/C behaves when each of its constituent magnitudes is varied in turn. The result is not a narrative but a taxonomy: a complete enumeration of the possible relations between the two rates, culminating in a set of summary propositions that read like the theorems of a formal system.

The thesis that emerges is deceptively simple and enormously consequential: the rate of profit is a function of two and only two major factors — the rate of surplus-value (s') and the value composition of the capital (v/C). Everything else in the chapter is the unfolding of that claim. Because the rate of profit is the product of these two factors (p' = s' · v/C), the two underlying rates are systematically decoupled: the rate of surplus-value can rise while the rate of profit falls, fall while the rate of profit rises, and either can move in perfect indifference to the other. The "rate of profit" is therefore not a transparent index of exploitation intensity. It is a compound, and the chapter's whole purpose is to decompose the compound and catalogue its permutations.

The Intellectual Objective

The objective is threefold.

First, to correct the naive identification. Volume 1 had established s' = s/v, the rate of surplus-value. The capitalist, however, does not experience his return as a ratio to the variable capital he advances; he experiences it as a ratio to the whole capital he has sunk into the enterprise, machinery and raw materials included. That re-description — from s/v to s/(c+v) — is not a mere change of notation. It is, Marx argues throughout the volume, the point at which the origin of profit is obscured. The chapter establishes rigorously how much the obscuring operation costs in numerical terms, and under exactly what conditions the obscuring operation leaves the magnitude untouched.

Second, to build the analytical machinery for everything that follows. The chapter explicitly foreshadows: the effect of turnover on the annual rate of profit (deferred to a later chapter, with Vol. 2, Ch. 16 supplying s'n); the effect of a saving in constant capital and of changes in the prices of raw materials (deferred to Chapters 5 and 6); the necessity of the falling rate of profit in "Part Three of this volume" under conditions of rising productivity; the equalization of profit rates and the divergence of surplus-value from profit proper — interest, ground-rent, taxes — (deferred to Part Two); and the comparison of profit rates between countries. The chapter is thus a keystone: it articulates the framework on which the law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, the formation of prices of production, and the theory of ground-rent will all be hung.

Third, to discipline a rival theoretical tradition. The chapter contains a single, sharply aimed polemical incision. In Section II.1, Marx notes that the case of a constant percentage composition of capital, a constant working day, constant labour intensity, and a rate of surplus-value varying solely through wages, "is the only one that meets Ricardo's assumption: 'Profits would be high or low, exactly in proportion as wages would be low or high.'" The whole architecture of the chapter is designed to show that this case is one among many — indeed, a special and restrictive one. Ricardo's proportionality holds only when the organic composition of capital and the three conditions of labour are held fixed. Once composition varies, the proportionality dissolves.

The Broader Intellectual Frame

The chapter belongs to the sequence: Volume 1 (production of surplus-value) → Volume 2 (circulation of capital) → Volume 3 (the process of capitalist production as a whole, i.e. the unity of production and circulation, and the concrete forms — profit, rent, interest — in which surplus-value appears to agents). Within Volume 3, Chapter 3's task is to perform the first transformation — the transformation of the rate of surplus-value into the rate of profit — at the level of the individual capital. The second transformation, the transformation of profit into average profit and of values into prices of production, is the business of Part Two. The third, the transformation of average profit into ground-rent, interest, and entrepreneurial profit, follows thereafter. Chapter 3 lays the algebraic floor of the entire structure.

It is also worth marking that Marx announces this as a mathematical investigation of relations among magnitudes, and that he treats the examples indifferently as successive states of one capital or as contemporaneous distinct capitals "from different branches of industry or from different countries." That dual reading is not a convenience. It is the bridge between the temporal dynamics of a single capital and the static comparison of many capitals — precisely the bridge that a theory of the general rate of profit requires.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Founding Abstraction: Profit ≡ Surplus-Value, and the Demarcation of the Field

The chapter opens by recalling the assumption made at the close of Chapter 2 and maintained throughout Part One: the profit accruing to a given capital is the same sum as the total surplus-value that this capital produces in a given period of circulation. Marx immediately flags what he is setting aside: "the division of this surplus-value into various subordinate forms: interest, ground-rent, taxes, etc., as also the fact that surplus-value by no means coincides in the majority of cases with profit, as the latter is appropriated by way of the prevailing rate of profit, which we shall return to in Part Two."

Two distinct exclusions are being made here, and they must not be conflated. The first exclusion is distributive: the surplus-value pump may be tapped by landlords, financiers, and the state before the industrial capitalist keeps his share. The second exclusion is systemic: under competition, capitals do not retain the surplus-value they individually extract but are credited with a share of the social total in proportion to the capital advanced — "the prevailing rate of profit." The second exclusion is the deeper one, because it is not merely a matter of splitting a fixed mass. It is a matter of reallocating it across branches, which will eventually force a divergence of the general rate of profit from the value composition of any individual capital, and thus force the transformation of values into prices of production.

The consequence of the assumption, Marx states, is that "its magnitude, and the magnitude of the rate of profit, are determined by simple numerical ratios, the numbers involved being given or definable in each individual case. Our investigation is firstly, therefore, a purely mathematical one." The phrase "firstly" is doing work: the mathematics is the skeleton; economic interpretation must be re-infused later. But the mathematics is indispensable because it reveals structural possibilities — for example, that the same profit rate can rest on entirely different rates of surplus-value — that no amount of intuitive reasoning about "exploitation" and "profit" would disclose.

2.2 The Primary Equations and the Proportionality p' : s' = v : C

The notation is inherited from Volumes 1 and 2. Total capital C divides into constant capital c and variable capital v, and produces surplus-value s. The ratio s/v is the rate of surplus-value, denoted s'. Hence:

s/v = s'   ⟹   s = s'v

The same surplus-value, related to the total capital instead of only to the variable capital, is called profit (p); the ratio p/C = s/C is the rate of profit, p'. So:

p' = s/C = s/(c + v)

and substituting s = s'v:

p' = s'v/C = s'v/(c + v)

Two things must be registered. First, p and p' are, at this stage, purely re-named surplus-value: the same magnitude viewed from a different denominator. Nothing has been added to the substance; only the frame of reference has shifted. Second, the equation can be read as a proportionality:

p' : s' = v : C

that is, "rate of profit is to rate of surplus-value as variable capital is to total capital." This is the master proportionality of the chapter, and it governs the whole of Section I.

From it, an immediate and much-quoted result follows. Because v is always smaller than C = v + c (so long as any constant capital at all is advanced), p' is always smaller than s'. The only exception is the case in which v = C — where the capitalist advances no constant capital, no means of production, only wages. Marx labels this case "impossible in practice." Note the editorial slip in the printed text at this point: the phrase rendered as "the sum of v + s, variable and constant capital" must be read as "the sum of v + c" — the two components of C, not v plus surplus-value. The logic of the inequality requires it.

The inequality carries an important interpretive message that the chapter will later complicate: the profit rate systematically understates the surplus-value rate, and the extent of the understatement is measured precisely by the share of constant capital in the total. The "camouflage" of exploitation built into the profit-form is thus not an illusion to be dispelled by argument alone; it is a determinate numerical ratio.

2.3 The Catalogue of Disturbing Factors: Money, Turnover, Productivity — and the n-Turnover Correction

Before proceeding, Marx notes that a further series of factors bears on the sizes of c, v, and s "in a decisive way," and must at least be named.

(i) The value of money. Taken as constant throughout. This is a standard simplification: if the value of money changed, all money magnitudes would be rescaled, and while the money rate of profit might shift, the value relations expressed in the ratios would not be altered in kind. Setting it constant isolates the real relations.

(ii) Turnover. "We shall ignore this factor completely, for the time being," because its influence on the profit rate is treated in a later chapter. But Marx inserts a crucial anticipation: p' = s'v/C is "strictly correct only for a single turnover period of the variable capital," while for the annual turnover "the simple rate of surplus-value s' has to be replaced by s'n, the annual rate of surplus-value, n standing for the number of turnovers that the variable capital makes in the course of a year" (with a cross-reference to Vol. 2, Ch. 16, added by Engels). This is a substantive qualification, not a footnote. The whole chapter proceeds on the fiction of a single turnover. Two capitals with identical value compositions and identical rates of surplus-value can have different annual profit rates purely because their capitals turn over at different speeds — a point developed in the later chapter and central to the analysis of merchant's capital and credit.

(iii) Productivity of labour. Its influence on the rate of surplus-value was already analysed in Volume 1, Part Four. But Marx adds a point about the individual capital: a capital operating above the social average productivity produces its commodities at a value below the social value and thereby realizes an extra profit (Vol. 1, Ch. 12, pp. 433ff.). This case is also set aside: "we also proceed from the assumption that commodities are produced under normal social conditions and are sold at their values."

But Marx then makes a move that is easy to miss and important to grasp. He insists that the value composition of capital and labour productivity are not independent variables in reality:

"The value composition of the capital applied in a particular branch of industry, i.e. a specific ratio between variable and constant capital, expresses in each case a definite level of labour productivity. Thus as soon as this ratio experiences any change that is not simply due either to a change in value of the material components of the constant capital, or to a change in wages, the productivity of labour must also have undergone a change."

This is the embryonic statement of the organic composition of capital: the value ratio c/v is the value-expression of a technical ratio (means of production per worker), and changes in it, unless traceable to revaluation or to wage changes, register changes in productivity. The whole subsequent exercise of "varying v/C" is therefore not a purely formal operation; each variation has an economic meaning indexed to productivity, to the length or intensity of the working day, or to wages. The chapter's abstraction is disciplined, not arbitrary.

2.4 The Three Remaining Determinants: Working Day, Intensity, Wages — and the Demonstration Examples

The same remark applies to the remaining three factors: the length of the working day, the intensity of labour, and wages. Their influence on the mass and rate of surplus-value was developed in Volume 1, Chapter 17. Marx summarises the directionality:

  • Wages affect the size and rate of surplus-value inversely: a rise in wages reduces surplus-value.
  • Length of the working day and intensity of labour affect it directly: an extension of the day and a greater intensity both increase surplus-value.

He then works three numerical demonstrations with a single capital of 100.

Baseline. 20 workers, 10-hour day, weekly wage bill 20:

80c + 20v + 20s;   s' = 100%;   p' = 20%

Total value product = 40 (20v + 20s). Note the composition: v/C = 20/100, so p' = 100% × 20/100 = 20%.

Demonstration 1 — extension of the working day. Extend the day to 15 hours without increasing wages. Total value produced rises from 40 to 60, since 10 : 15 = 40 : 60. The wage (v) remains 20; the surplus rises from 20 to 40:

80c + 20v + 40s;   s' = 200%;   p' = 40%

Here the denominator C remains 100 (c and v unchanged), so the entire effect on p' is transmitted through s' alone. p' doubles exactly as s' doubles.

Demonstration 2 — a fall in wages. The wage for the same 10 hours falls from 20 to 12. The total value product remains 40 (same quantity of labour performed), but it is distributed differently: v falls to 12, leaving 28 for s. Hence:

80c + 12v + 28s;   s' = 28/12 = 233⅓%;   p' = 28/(80 + 12) = 28/92 = 30 10/23%

Two things happen simultaneously. The rate of surplus-value rises steeply, because the same living labour is now paid less. But the total capital has also fallen, from 100 to 92, because the wage bill is smaller. This is a first glimpse of the fact that a change in v can alter the denominator as well as the numerator — and that p' responds both to the numerator's rise and to the denominator's fall. This is precisely why the percentage-composition convenience (introduced later) becomes treacherous in the analysis of a single capital.

The general lesson. Marx draws the conclusion:

"an extension of the working day (or, alternatively, an increase in the intensity of labour) and a fall in wages both raise the mass and with it the rate of surplus-value; conversely, a rise in wages, with other circumstances remaining the same, would reduce the rate of surplus-value. If v grows owing to a rise in wages, this does not express an increased quantity of labour but simply its dearer payment; s' and p' do not rise but fall."

And the crucial corollary: changes in the working day, intensity, and wages cannot occur without simultaneous changes in v and s and their relationship, and therefore in p'. The variables are not free. The three "conditions of labour" and the two magnitudes v and s are locked in a system of mutual determination.

2.5 The Organic Distinction between Constant and Variable Capital

At this point the chapter pivots from arithmetic to political economy proper, with a passage of concentrated theoretical importance on "the special organic connection that the variable capital has with the movement of the capital as a whole and its valorization, as well as its distinction from the constant capital."

Constant capital. "In so far as the formation of value is concerned, [it] is important only on account of the value that it has." It is immaterial whether £1,500 of constant capital represents 1,500 tons of iron at £1 a ton or 500 tons at £3. The quantity of actual material in which its value is expressed is completely unimportant for value formation and for the rate of profit. Therefore the rate of profit "varies in the opposite direction to the value of the constant capital" — irrespective of what relationship the increase or decrease in that value has to the mass of material use-values it represents. This is the theoretical basis for the treatment (in Case 3, below) of revaluation and technical change as equivalent in their effect on p': only the value of c matters.

Variable capital. "The case of the variable capital is completely different. What matters above all here is not the value that it actually has, the amount of labour objectified in it, but rather this value as a mere index of the total labour that it sets in motion, which is not expressed in it."

The claim is dense, and it must be unpacked carefully. The variable capital is the money advanced to buy labour-power. Its magnitude is the wage bill. But the value it brings into being is not the value of the labour-power; it is the value that living labour produces in the time that labour-power is at the capitalist's disposal. The wage bill is therefore a pointer — an index — to a quantity of living labour that exceeds it. The difference between the total labour set in motion and the labour objectified and paid for in the variable capital is the surplus-value. And:

"the difference between this total labour and the labour expressed and therefore paid for in the variable capital, i.e. the portion that forms surplus-value, is greater in proportion as the labour contained in the variable capital gets smaller."

Marx illustrates: assume a working day of 10 hours = 10 shillings. If necessary labour (the labour that replaces the wage, i.e. replaces v) is 5 hours, surplus-value = 5 shillings. If necessary labour is 4 hours = 4 shillings, surplus labour is 6 hours and surplus-value 6 shillings.

The conclusion is stated with unusual crispness:

"as soon as the value of the variable capital ceases to be an index of the mass of labour that it sets in motion, and the basis of this index itself changes, the rate of surplus-value changes in the opposite direction and in inverse proportion."

This is the theoretical heart of the distinction between constant and variable capital, and it explains why the composition variable v/C is so analytically volatile: c is sheer accumulated value, mute and inert; v is a cipher for living labour, and any change in the value of the cipher (a change in wages, say) does not proportionally change the labour it commands. The "index problem" is the source of all the complications in the case analysis that follows.

2.6 The Analytical Programme: Decomposing p' = s'v/C

Marx now lays out the plan with complete transparency. He intends to apply the equation p' = s'v/C to "the various possible cases," varying the individual factors of the product s'v/C successively and establishing the effect on the rate of profit. Crucially, he specifies that the examples may be read in two ways:

"either as successive changes in circumstances for the action of one and the same capital, or, indeed, as different capitals, existing simultaneously alongside one another, and brought in for purposes of comparison, e.g. from different branches of industry or from different countries."

And if some examples seem "forced or practically impossible" as chronological states of one capital, "this objection disappears as soon as they are viewed as the result of a comparison between separate capitals." This is a methodological statement of the first importance: comparative statics and inter-capital comparison are treated as formally equivalent representations of the same algebra. It is also, incidentally, the chapter's second quiet gesture toward the international comparison of profit rates at the end.

The programme is:

  1. Section I: hold s' constant and investigate the possible variations in v/C.
  2. Section II: hold v/C constant and put s' through its variations.
  3. Finally: treat all factors as variable — which, Marx asserts, exhausts all the cases from which the laws governing the profit rate may be derived.

2.7 Section I, General Formula — The Master Equations of Compositional Variation

The case s' constant, v/C variable comprises several subordinate cases and is covered by a general formula. Take two capitals C and C₁, with variable components v and v₁, a common rate of surplus-value s', and rates of profit p' and p'₁. Then:

p' = s'v/C        and        p'₁ = s'v₁/C₁

Suppose C₁ = EC and v₁ = ev, where E ≡ C₁/C and e ≡ v₁/v are the coefficients of variation of total capital and variable capital respectively. Substituting:

p'₁ = s' · (ev)/(EC) = s' · (e/E) · (v/C)

This is the master formula for Section I. It says that the new profit rate equals the old profit rate multiplied by the ratio of the two variation coefficients, e/E. Every case in Section I is a special case of this formula.

A second formula is obtained by converting the two original equations into a proportionality:

p' : p'₁ = s'(v/C) : s'(v₁/C₁) = v/C : v₁/C₁

Since the value of a fraction is unchanged if numerator and denominator are multiplied or divided by the same number, we may reduce both v/C and v₁/C₁ to percentages by taking C = C₁ = 100. Then v/C = v/100 and v₁/C₁ = v₁/100. Multiplying the proportionality by 100 to clear the denominators of 100:

p' : p'₁ = v : v₁

This yields the section's headline theorem:

Given any two capitals functioning with the same rate of surplus-value, the rates of profit stand in the same proportion as the variable components of the capitals, each calculated as a percentage of its total capital.

These two forms — the coefficient formula and the percentage proportionality — "cover all cases of variation in v/C."

2.8 The Percentage Form: Its Utility and Its Deceptiveness

Before working the subordinate cases, Marx pauses on the practice of expressing composition in percentages — taking c + v = 100. He notes that the percentage form is not immaterial for determining the mass of profit, but is immaterial for the rate of profit.

His illustration: a capital of 15,000, of which 12,000 is constant and 3,000 variable, produces a surplus-value of 3,000. Reduced to percentages:

15,000C = 12,000c + 3,000v (+ 3,000s)      ⟹      100C = 80c + 20v (+ 20s)

In both cases s' = 100% and p' = 20%. Likewise for comparison between two capitals:

12,000C = 10,800c + 1,200v (+ 1,200s)     ⟹      100C = 90c + 10v (+ 10s)

with s' = 100% and p' = 10%. The comparison is far easier to make in percentage form.

But the percentage form has a fatal defect when applied to one and the same capital over time. If a capital passes from 80c + 20v + 20s to 90c + 10v + 10s, we cannot tell whether the new composition arose from an absolute decline in v, an absolute rise in c, or both. Marx enumerates three distinct underlying movements that all yield the identical percentage composition 90c + 10v:

  1. 12,000c + 3,000v → 27,000c + 3,000v (increase in c, v constant);
  2. 12,000c + 3,000v → 12,000c + 1,333⅓v (reduction in v, c constant);
  3. 12,000c + 3,000v → 13,500c + 1,500v (change in both).

These three cases have different consequences for the rate of profit once mass matters, and they have entirely different economic meanings. The percentage form "almost always obliterates" these changes. Therefore the analysis must "dispense with the convenience of the percentage form," or apply it "only as a supplement to the main argument." This is a warning about a genuinely deep methodological trap: the ratio alone does not identify the path, and it is the path that carries the economic information about productivity, accumulation, and capital release.

2.9 Case 1: s' and C constant, v variable

If v changes while C remains unaltered, then c must change by the same amount in the opposite direction. If the capital was 80c + 20v = 100 and v falls to 10, then c must rise to 90: 90c + 10v = 100. In general, if v → v ± d, then c → c ∓ d.

Given s' unchanged and v changed, the mass of surplus-value must also change, since s = s'v and one of the factors of s'v has been given another value.

The equations:

p' = s'v/C          and          p'₁ = s'v₁/C

yield the proportionality:

p' : p'₁ = s'(v/C) : s'(v₁/C) = v : v₁

Therefore: with the rate of surplus-value and the total capital both remaining the same, the original profit rate is related to the new profit rate arrived at by a change in the variable capital, as the original variable capital is to the new variable capital.

A first numerical illustration: 

I.  15,000C = 12,000c + 3,000v (+ 3,000s)   s' = 100%,  p' = 20%
II. 15,000C = 13,000c + 2,000v (+ 2,000s)   s' = 100%,  p' = 13⅓%

and indeed 20% : 13⅓% = 3,000 : 2,000.

The rising-v example. Marx now takes a capital originally constituted as:

I.  100c + 20v + 10s;   C = 120,   s' = 50%,   p' = 8⅓%

and raises v to 30. Since C must remain 120, c falls from 100 to 90. At s' = 50%, the surplus rises to 15:

II. 90c + 30v + 15s;    C = 120,   s' = 50%,   p' = 12½%

Now Marx submits this to economic interrogation. What does the transition from I to II mean?

Reading A: wages unchanged. If wages are unchanged, then the increase in v from 20 to 30 can only mean that half again as many workers are employed. With the same working day and intensity, the total value produced rises by half, from 30 to 45, and it is divided just as before — two-thirds to wages, one-third to surplus-value. The arithmetic is consistent: v = 30 (two-thirds of 45), s = 15 (one-third of 45), s' = 50%.

But there is a snag, and Marx pounces on it: the constant capital has fallen from 100 to 90 at the very moment when more workers are being employed. "We have therefore a case of a decline in labour productivity combined with a simultaneous decline in constant capital. Is this case economically possible?" His answer is carefully calibrated. In agriculture and the extractive industries, a decline in productivity with more workers is easy to comprehend — but "within the confines of capitalist production, and on its basis," such a process "is linked not with a decline in constant capital but with an increase." Even where the decline in c is occasioned merely by a fall in price, an individual capital could make the transition from I to II only under quite exceptional conditions. With two independent capitals, however, invested in different countries or in different branches of agriculture or extractive industry, it would be "by no means unusual" for one to employ more workers with less expensive or less plentiful means of production. Here the dual reading (one capital through time vs. two capitals side by side) does decisive analytical work.

Reading B: wages rise by half. Now suppose the rise in v from 20 to 30 reflects a rise in the wage rate by one-half. Then the picture changes completely. The same number of workers (say 20) continue to work with the same or only insignificantly reduced means of production. If the working day remains 10 hours, the total value produced remains 30 — exactly as before. But that 30 would now be entirely absorbed in replacing the advanced variable capital of 30; surplus-value would vanish. Since s' was posited constant at 50%, this is impossible.

The only way out: extend the working day by half, to 15 hours. Twenty workers then produce a total value of 45 in a 15-hour day, and all the conditions are satisfied:

II. 90c + 30v + 15s;   C = 120,   s' = 50%,   p' = 12½%

Under this reading, the 20 workers need no more means of labour — no more tools or machinery — than in I. Only the raw or ancillary materials must increase by half. And, Marx adds, "If these materials fall in price, the transition from I to II would be much more possible as an economic phenomenon, given our assumptions, even for one and the same capital. And the capitalist would be compensated at least partially, by a bigger profit, for the loss that the devaluation of his constant capital would have caused him." (Note: the mass of profit rises from 10 to 15 and the rate from 8⅓% to 12½%.)

The falling-v direction. Reverse the example, taking II as the starting point and moving to I. "It is readily apparent that by this reversal, the rates of profit in the two cases and the conditions governing their mutual relationship are not changed in the slightest." Two readings again:

Reading A: v falls because fewer workers work with more means of production. This is "simply the normal case in modern industry: rising productivity of labour, the operation of greater quantities of means of production by fewer workers." And Marx adds a forward-looking promissory note of the highest theoretical importance: "And in Part Three of this volume we shall see how this movement is necessarily bound up with a simultaneous fall in the rate of profit." The chapter thus seeds the law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall at its very beginning.

Reading B: v falls because the same number of workers are paid a lower wage rate. If the working day is unchanged, the total value product remains 30v + 15s = 45. With v fallen to 20, surplus-value would rise to 25 and s' from 50% to 125% — against our assumption. To stay within the example, surplus-value at 50% must fall to 10, and thus the total value produced from 45 to 30. This is possible "only if the working day is cut by one-third":

100c + 20v + 10s;   s' = 50%,   p' = 8⅓%

Marx then issues the chapter's most explicit methodological caveat — and it is worth quoting in full, because it licenses everything that follows:

"We need hardly point out that a reduction in working hours of this kind combined with a fall in wages would not occur in practice. But this is beside the point. The rate of profit is a function of several variables, and if we want to know how these variables act on the profit rate we must investigate in turn the individual effect of each, irrespective of whether an isolated effect of this kind is economically possible or not in the case of one and the same capital."

The method is therefore not descriptive but analytical decomposition: isolate each partial derivative, then recombine. This is comparative statics avant la lettre, and it is a key to the chapter's structure.

2.10 Case 2: s' constant, v variable, C altered by the variation of v

This case "is different from the previous one only in degree." Instead of c moving by the same amount as v in the opposite direction, c now remains constant; consequently C moves as v moves. Marx notes the quantitative context: "under today's conditions of large-scale industry and agriculture, variable capital is only a relatively small portion of the total capital and hence any reduction or growth in the total capital that is brought about by a change in the variable capital is also relatively slight." (This is Marx's own observation of the historically rising composition of capital — a point that will matter enormously for the falling-profit-rate argument.)

Example:

I.  100c + 20v + 10s;   C = 120,   s' = 50%,   p' = 8⅓%
II. 100c + 30v + 15s;   C = 130,   s' = 50%,   p' = 15/130 = 11 7/13%

The opposite case of decline in v is the reverse transition from II to I. Marx judges the economic conditions "essentially the same as in the previous case, and hence need no further explanation." The transition from I to II involves a decline of one-third in the productivity of labour, or, equivalently, the operation of 100c requires half as much labour again in II as in I.

Editorial intervention (Engels's footnote). Marx wrote "a half" here; Engels corrects: this is "a slip arising from measuring the decline against the resulting figure rather than the original one." The arithmetic: labour rises from 20 to 30, an increase of one-half measured from the original 20; but productivity declines by one-third measured from the new 30 (10/30). This is exactly the sort of ratio-base confusion that the chapter is otherwise at pains to avoid, and Engels's correction is a small methodological service.

A second editorial note records a loose thread in the manuscript: "The manuscript has here: 'For later investigation, how this case is related to ground-rent.'" — a reminder that the abstract arithmetic of composition is already pointing toward the concrete forms of landed property.

The category of capital release and tying-up. Marx draws out the distinctive feature of this case relative to the previous one:

"Whereas in the previous case the total capital was held constant by the conversion of constant capital into variable or vice versa, now the increase in the variable portion means that extra capital is tied up, while a decrease involves the release of capital that had previously been needed."

This introduces a distinction that will become central in Volumes 2 and 3 (in the analysis of credit, of the circulation of capital, and of crises): changes in the composition of capital are not merely changes in ratios; they either absorb additional capital or liberate previously committed capital. The category of "released capital" is not a formal curiosity — it is a real quantity available for new investment, and thus a mechanism of accumulation and of cyclical movement.

2.11 Case 3: s' and v constant, c (and therefore C) variable

Here the equation:

p' = s'v/C

becomes:

p'₁ = s'v/C₁

and canceling on both sides gives:

p' : p'₁ = C : C₁

With the same rate of surplus-value and the same variable capital, the profit rate stands in inverse proportion to the total capital.

Illustration with three capitals (or three states of one capital):

I.    80c + 20v + 20s;   C = 100,  s' = 100%,  p' = 20%
II.  100c + 20v + 20s;   C = 120,  s' = 100%,  p' = 16⅔%
III.  60c + 20v + 20s;   C =  80,  s' = 100%,  p' = 25%

And indeed 20% : 16⅔% = 120 : 100; and 20% : 25% = 80 : 100.

In the general formula of Section I, p'₁ = s' · (ev)/(EC), this case corresponds to setting e = v₁/v = 1 (since v is unaltered), so that:

p'₁ = s' · v/(EC)

Because s'v = s and both s' and v are constant, the mass of surplus-value is also unaffected by any variation in C; it remains what it was before the change. Only the denominator moves.

Marx notes the limiting case: "If c were to fall to zero, we would have p' = s', the rate of profit equal to the rate of surplus-value." This is the same degenerate case flagged at the outset (v = C), now approached from the other direction.

Sources of the change in c — and their equivalence for value. The alteration in c may come about either: 1. from a change merely in the value of the material elements of the constant capital; or 2. from a changed technical composition of the total capital, i.e. a change in the productivity of labour in the branch in question.

Under the second heading, Marx reads the sequence of his three examples as a history of rising productivity: "the productivity of social labour, which rises with the development of large-scale industry and agriculture, would successively move from III to I and from I to II." He spells out the process: a quantity of labour paid 20 and producing a value of 40 starts by operating means of labour worth 60; as productivity rises, the means of labour operated grow first to 80, then to 100, "if their value remained the same." The reverse sequence registers a decline in productivity — the same quantity of labour sets less means of production in motion, and "the business would be cut back, as can well happen in agriculture, mining, etc."

Two further observations follow:

(a) Savings in constant capital. "A saving in constant capital both increases the rate of profit and releases capital as well, and this is important for the capitalist. We shall return to this point later, as well as investigating the effect of changes in the prices of the elements of constant capital, raw materials in particular." Engels's footnote identifies Chapters 5 and 6 as the locus of that promised treatment. This is a major fork in the road: the whole analysis of "moral depreciation," cheapening of raw materials, and the counteracting tendencies to the falling rate of profit will run through this issue.

(b) Value change vs. technical change. The section closes with the equivalence thesis:

"We see here again how a variation in constant capital has the same effect on the rate of profit, irrespective of whether this variation is brought about by an increase or decrease in the material components of c, or simply by a change in their value."

This is the counterpart, on the constant-capital side, of the "organic distinction" developed earlier: because constant capital matters only as value, the route by which its value changes is immaterial for the rate of profit.

2.12 Case 4: s' constant, v, c and C all variable

The general formula p'₁ = s'·(ev)/(EC) still applies. Marx extracts three results:

(a) The rate of profit falls if E > e — that is, if the constant capital is increased in such a way that the total capital increases more sharply than the variable capital. Example:

80c + 20v + 20s     →     170c + 30v + 30s

s' remains 100%, but v/C falls from 20/100 to 30/200, and p' accordingly falls from 20% to 15%.

(b) The rate of profit remains unchanged only if e = E — that is, if the fraction v/C retains the same value despite the apparent change; in other words, if both numerator and denominator are multiplied or divided by the same figure. Example:

80c + 20v + 20s     and     160c + 40v + 40s

Both have p' = 20%, because s' remains 100% and v/C = 20/100 = 40/200 exhibits the same value.

(c) The rate of profit rises if e > E — that is, if the variable capital rises more sharply than the total capital. Example:

80c + 20v + 20s     →     120c + 40v + 40s

with s' unaltered, v/C = 20/100 has risen to 40/160 = 1/4, so p' rises from 20% to 25%.

The reduction technique. Marx then supplies a general method for handling the case where v and C both move:

"Where v and C both change in the same direction, we can conceive this change in their magnitudes as if both vary to a certain extent in the same ratio, so that up to this point v/C remains unaltered. Beyond this point, then, only one of them varies, and we can thereby reduce this more complicated case to one or other of the previous simpler ones."

Illustration 1 (same direction). 80c + 20v + 20s changes to 100c + 30v + 30s. The ratio between v and c, and therefore between v and C, remains unaltered up to the intermediate point 100c + 25v + 25s (where v/C = 25/125 = 20/100, so p' is still 20%). From that starting point, v rises by 5, to 30, and C rises from 125 to 130 — this is Case 2. "The rate of profit, which was originally 20 per cent, is increased by this addition of 5v to 23 1/13 per cent, given the same rate of surplus-value" (check: 30/130 = 3/13 = 23 1/13%).

Illustration 2 (opposite directions). From 80c + 20v + 20s to 110c + 10v + 10s. An intermediate change to 40c + 10v + 10s would have kept the profit rate the same as originally (20%, since 10/50 = 20/100). The addition of 10c to this intermediate form makes it fall to 8⅓% (since 10/120 = 8⅓%). Again the example is reduced to a variation in one variable only — here c, i.e. Case 3.

Conclusion for the section: "Thus the simultaneous variation of v, c and C does not offer any new aspects, and always leads back in the last analysis to a case in which only one factor is variable."

2.13 The Residual Case: Numerical Constancy with Material Change

Marx closes Section I by disposing of the one remaining logical possibility: v and c retain the same numerical magnitudes, but their material elements undergo a change in value — so that v now represents a different quantity of labour set in motion, and c a different quantity of means of production.

Worked through: in 80c + 20v + 20s, the 20v might originally represent the wages of 20 workers for a 10-hour day. Suppose each worker's wage rises. Then 20v suffices to pay only 16 workers instead of 20. If the 20 workers had produced a value of 40 in their 200 hours' work (200 hours × 0.20 per hour), the 16 workers, in a 10-hour day amounting to 160 hours, produce a value of only 32. Subtracting 20v for wages leaves 12 for surplus-value; the rate of surplus-value would have fallen from 100% to 60% (12/20). But by assumption s' must remain constant. Therefore the working day must be extended by a quarter, from 10 hours to 12½ hours: sixteen workers at 12½ hours = 200 working hours, the same total labour time as before, hence the same value product, hence the same surplus-value of 20.

(Scholarly note on the printed text: the passage as printed reads "If 20 workers produce a value of 50 in a working day of 10 hours…", but this is inconsistent with the value product of 40 established two sentences earlier and with the requirement that the capital 80c + 20v still yield a surplus of 20 at s' = 100%. The coherent reading — and the one the argument requires — is a value product of 40. The essential result is unaffected either way: the same capital yields the same surplus-value only if the working day lengthens by exactly the factor by which the wage rate rose.)

Conversely, if wages fall so that 20v covers the wages of 30 workers, s' can remain constant only if the working day is reduced from 10 hours to 6⅔: since 20 × 10 = 30 × 6⅔ = 200 working hours.

Marx adds two qualifications. First, on the constant-capital side, he notes that the previous discussion had already explained "in essentials how c can retain the same value expression in money throughout all these conflicting assumptions, while representing the differing quantities of means of production which correspond to the changed conditions. This case would however be very exceptional in its pure form." Second, on revaluation of individual elements of c: "a change that increases or decreases certain elements while leaving their value sum c unaltered… disturbs neither the rate of profit nor the rate of surplus-value, as long as it does not bring with it any alteration in the magnitude of v." Again, only the sum matters.

2.14 The Limits of Compositional Variation

The section closes with a claim that is easy to skim and that is in fact the bridge to Section II:

"In this way we have dealt with all possible cases of variation of v, c and C in our equation. We have seen how the profit rate can fall, rise or remain the same, with the rate of surplus-value constant throughout, in so far as the slightest alteration in the ratio between v and c or C is sufficient to alter the profit rate as well.

It has also become evident that there is always a limit to the variation of v beyond which it is economically impossible for s' to remain constant. Since any unilateral variation of c must similarly reach a limit at which v can no longer remain constant, it is clear that limits are placed on all possible variations of v/C beyond which s' must also vary. In the case of these variations in s', which we shall now turn to investigate, the mutual interaction of the various different variables in our equation appears even more clearly."

The argument is that the variables cannot be varied independently beyond a point. There is a "feasible region" for compositional variation within which s' can be held fixed — and its boundary is set by the physical and institutional limits on the working day, on intensity, and on wages. Beyond that boundary, s' must move. This is an elegant and important structural claim: the abstract algebra is bounded by concrete conditions of labour.

2.15 Section II, General Formula — s' Variable

Section II turns to rates of surplus-value that vary, "irrespective of whether v/C remains constant or also varies." A general formula for the corresponding rates of profit is obtained by converting

p' = s'v/C        into        p'₁ = s'₁v₁/C₁

from which:

p' : p'₁ = s'(v/C) : s'₁(v₁/C₁)

and therefore

p'₁ = p' · (s'₁/s') · (v₁/v) · (C/C₁)

This is the master formula for Section II. It says that the change in the profit rate is the product of three ratios: the ratio of the new to the old rate of surplus-value, the ratio of the new to the old variable capital, and the inverse ratio of the new to the old total capital. Each subsequent case is this formula with one or more of the ratios set to unity.

2.16 Case II.1: s' variable, v/C constant — The Ricardo Case

Here:

p' = s'(v/C)      and      p'₁ = s'₁(v/C)

such that v/C has the same value in both cases. It follows that:

p' : p'₁ = s' : s'₁

The rates of profit for two capitals of the same composition are in direct proportion to their respective rates of surplus-value. And since the absolute magnitudes of v and C do not enter into the fraction v/C but only their ratio, this holds for all capitals of the same composition, whatever their absolute magnitude.

Example:

80c + 20v + 20s;   C = 100,   s' = 100%,   p' = 20%
160c + 40v + 40s;  C = 200,   s' =  50%,   p' = 10%

and 100% : 50% = 20% : 10%.

If the absolute magnitudes of v and C are the same in both cases, the profit rates also stand in the same ratio as the masses of surplus-value:

p' : p'₁ = s'v : s'₁v = s : s₁

Example:

80c + 20v + 20s;   s' = 100%,   p' = 20%
80c + 20v + 10s;   s' =  50%,   p' = 10%

and 20% : 10% = (100 × 20) : (50 × 20) = 20s : 10s.

The three mechanisms of an s' change. Given capitals of the same composition — either absolutely or relatively — the rate of surplus-value can vary only if one of three things varies: wages, the length of the working day, or the intensity of labour. Marx sets up three states:

I.   80c + 20v + 10s;   s' =  50%,   p' = 10%
II.  80c + 20v + 20s;   s' = 100%,   p' = 20%
III. 80c + 20v + 40s;   s' = 200%,   p' = 40%

with total value products of 30, 40, and 60 respectively. He then shows the three different mechanisms by which this can happen.

Mechanism 1 — variation in wages (a different number of workers at 20v). Assume 15 workers are employed in Case I for 10 hours at a wage of £1⅓ each, producing a value of £30, of which £20 replaces wages and £10 remains as surplus-value (check: 15 × £1⅓ = £20; each worker produces £2 of new value in 10 hours, so s' = 10/20 = 50%). If wages fall to £1, twenty workers are employed for 10 hours, producing a value of £40, of which £20 is wages and £20 surplus-value (s' = 100%). If wages fall further to £⅔, thirty workers are employed for 10 hours and produce a value of £60, of which £40 remains as surplus-value after subtracting £20 for wages (s' = 200%).

And now the polemical hammer-blow:

"This case, that of a constant percentage composition of capital, constant working day, constant intensity of labour, with changes in the rate of surplus-value brought about by changes in wages, is the only one that meets Ricardo's assumption: 'Profits would be high or low, exactly in proportion as wages would be low or high' (Principles, Chapter I, section iii)."

Engels's footnote supplies the Pelican pagination (p. 69) and notes that the emphasis is Marx's own. The point of the citation is to confine Ricardo's proposition to a special case: it holds only where the composition of capital is constant and the working day and intensity are fixed. Since composition varies systematically with the development of industry, the Ricardian proportionality cannot be the general law of profit.

Mechanism 2 — variation in the intensity of labour. Suppose 20 workers make 30 items in Case I, 40 in Case II, and 60 in Case III, working with the same means of labour for 10 hours a day, with each item representing a new value of £1 over and above the value of the means of production consumed. Since 20 items = £20 are always needed to replace wages, there remains for surplus-value 10 items = £10 in I, 20 items = £20 in II, and 40 items = £40 in III.

Mechanism 3 — variation in the length of the working day. If 20 workers work with the same intensity for 9 hours in I, 12 hours in II, and 18 hours in III, their total products stand in the ratio 9 : 12 : 18 = 30 : 40 : 60, and since wages are 20 each time, there remains 10, 20, or 40 for surplus-value.

The general regularity extracted: "A rise or fall in wages thus effects an opposite change in the rate of surplus-value, while a rise or fall in the intensity of labour, or an extension or reduction of the working day, both effect a change in the same direction, and with v/C constant, the rate of profit is therefore similarly affected." Three different causes, one and the same effect on the composition, three different signs of effect on s' and p'.

2.17 Case II.2: s' and v variable, C constant — The Three Sub-Cases

Here the proportionality from the Section II general formula reduces, with C = C₁, to:

p' : p'₁ = s'(v/C) : s'₁(v₁/C) = s'v : s'₁v₁ = s : s₁

The rates of profit stand in the same ratio as the respective masses of surplus-value.

Marx observes: "Variation in the rate of surplus-value, with variable capital remaining the same, means a change in the size and distribution of the value product. Simultaneous variation in v and s' similarly entails a different distribution, but not always a change in the magnitude of the value product." Three cases are possible.

(a) Opposite directions, by the same amount. Example:

80c + 20v + 10s;   s' =  50%,   p' = 10%
90c + 10v + 20s;   s' = 200%,   p' = 20%

Here the value product is the same in both cases — 20v + 10s = 10v + 20s = 30 — and so is the quantity of labour performed. The distinction is simply that in the first case 20 is paid for wages and 10 for surplus-value, while in the second case wages amount only to 10 and surplus-value is therefore 20. Marx flags: "This is the only case in which a simultaneous variation in v and s' leaves the number of workers, the labour intensity and the length of the working day unaffected."

Marx's footnote on the phrase "by the same amount." This is a piece of mathematical housekeeping with real analytical content. Marx concedes that v and s' "in no sense vary 'by the same amount' in this example, or even in the same proportion." What is really invariant is the value product, v + s. He then derives the exact formula. If the values of v, s, and s' after the variation are v₁, s₁, s'₁, and given that

v + s = v₁ + s₁

then substituting s = vs' on the left and s₁ = v₁s'₁ on the right:

v + vs' = v₁ + v₁s'₁

whence:

s'₁ = (v/v₁)(1 + s') − 1

(Verification with the example: v = 20, s' = 0.5, so v(1 + s') = 30; v₁ = 10, so 1 + s'₁ = 3, hence s'₁ = 2 = 200%. The formula reproduces the example exactly.)

Marx adds that "on the basis of this formula, case (b) below can similarly be reduced to an inequality." This is a small but telling piece of formalism: the qualitative taxonomy is underpinned by a quantitative criterion (equality, inequality, greater-than-or-less-than) applied to a single equation.

(b) Opposite directions, but not to the same extent. "Either the variation in v must predominate, or that in s'." Example:

I.   80c + 20v + 20s;   s' = 100%,      p' = 20%
II.  72c + 28v + 20s;   s' = 71 3/7%,   p' = 20%
III. 84c + 16v + 20s;   s' = 125%,      p' = 20%

(Check: I: p' = 20/100 = 20%; II: 20/28 = 71 3/7%, p' = 20/100 = 20%; III: 20/16 = 125%, p' = 20/100 = 20%.) The value products are 40, 48, and 36 respectively. Marx's inference: "Both the value product and the wages have altered; but an alteration in the value product means an alteration in the quantity of labour performed, and therefore either in the number of workers, the duration of labour, or its intensity, if not more than one of these three." So case (b) is not one in which the conditions of labour are unchanged; by the very fact that the value product changes, one of the three conditions must have changed.

(c) Same direction — mutual reinforcement. Example:

90c + 10v + 10s;   s' = 100%,   p' = 10%
80c + 20v + 30s;   s' = 150%,   p' = 30%
92c + 8v + 6s;     s' =  75%,   p' =  6%

Value products are 20, 50, and 14. "Here, too, the three value products are different… and this difference in the quantity of labour in each case can again be reduced to a difference in the number of workers, the duration or intensity of labour, or any combination of these factors."

2.18 Case II.3: s', v and C All Variable

"This case offers no new aspects and is settled by the general formula given under heading II." The formal completeness of the earlier construction means the general case adds nothing: the general formula p'₁ = p'(s'₁/s')(v₁/v)(C/C₁) already subsumes every case and every combination. The taxonomy is closed.

2.19 The Five-Fold Summary of s'→p' Effects

Marx now gathers the results into a five-part classification of how a change in the rate of surplus-value affects the rate of profit. This is the pivotal analytical summary of the chapter.

1. p' changes in the same ratio as s', if v/C remains constant.

80c + 20v + 20s;   s' = 100%,   p' = 20%
80c + 20v + 10s;   s' =  50%,   p' = 10%
100% : 50% = 20% : 10%

2. p' rises or falls in a higher ratio than s', if v/C moves in the same direction as s'.

80c + 20v + 10s;   s' = 50%,    p' = 10%
70c + 30v + 20s;   s' = 66⅔%,   p' = 20%

And indeed 50% : 66⅔% < 10% : 20%. Here both s' and v/C rise, so the two movements reinforce one another and the profit rate moves more than proportionally.

3. p' rises or falls in a lower ratio than s', if v/C changes in the opposite direction to s', but in a lower ratio.

80c + 20v + 10s;   s' =  50%,   p' = 10%
90c + 10v + 15s;   s' = 150%,   p' = 15%

And 50% : 150% > 10% : 15%. Here s' rises sharply while v/C falls; the composition change partially offsets the improvement, so p' rises only modestly.

4. p' rises even though s' falls, or falls even though s' rises, if v/C changes in the opposite direction to s', and in a higher ratio.

80c + 20v + 20s;   s' = 100%,   p' = 20%
90c + 10v + 15s;   s' = 150%,   p' = 15%

"Here s' has risen from 100 per cent to 150 per cent, while p' has fallen from 20 per cent to 15 per cent." This is the decisive case for the chapter's whole tendency: the two rates can move in contrary directions.

(Analytical note: cases 3 and 4 employ the same terminal capital — 90c + 10v + 15s — but from different starting points, 80c + 20v + 10s and 80c + 20v + 20s respectively. The construction makes an important point vividly: the same compositional shift, combined with the same s', can produce a rise in p' if s' has risen only modestly, or a fall in p' if s' has risen more dramatically. There is nothing contradictory here; it is precisely the compositional modulation that the chapter has been at pains to exhibit.)

5. p' remains constant even though s' rises or falls, if v/C changes in the opposite direction to s', but in exactly the same ratio.

2.20 The Exceptional Case of Constant p' with Varying s' — and the International Comparison

"It is only this last case that still requires some further discussion." Marx draws a crucial contrast between the two directions of analysis:

"We saw above, with the variations in v/C, how one and the same rate of surplus-value can be expressed in the most varied rates of profit. Here we see that one and the same rate of profit can be based on very different rates of surplus-value. But while with s' constant, any change whatsoever in the ratio of v to C is sufficient to induce a variation in the rate of profit, a change in s' must involve an exactly corresponding, but opposite, change in v/C, if the profit rate is to remain the same."

The asymmetry is the point. Compositional change is a cheap way to move the profit rate: any change suffices. Compensation by composition is an expensive requirement: it demands an exact, quantitatively matching, opposite movement. Hence: "This is possible only very exceptionally in the case of one and the same capital, or with two capitals in the same country."

The worked example. Take:

80c + 20v + 20s;   C = 100,   s' = 100%,   p' = 20%

Now let wages fall so that the same number of workers can be had for 16v as previously with 20v. With conditions otherwise unchanged, 4v is set free, giving:

80c + 16v + 24s;   C = 96,   s' = 150%,   p' = 25%

If p' is still to be 20%, as before, the total capital must increase to 120, and hence the constant capital to 104:

104c + 16v + 24s;   C = 120,   s' = 150%,   p' = 20%

But this "would be possible only if a change in the productivity of labour took place simultaneously with the fall in wages, and required this changed composition of capital; or alternatively, if the money value of the constant capital rose from 80 to 104 — in other words, a chance combination of conditions that only comes about in exceptional circumstances." Marx adds a clarification of the theoretical reach: "In actual fact a change in s' which is not simultaneously a change in v, thus also giving rise to a change in v/C, is conceivable only under quite special conditions, i.e. in those branches of industry in which only fixed capital and labour are applied, and the object of labour is provided by nature." That is a real economic specification — extractive industries where raw material is not purchased — and it is a useful delimitation of the theorem.

The international comparison. Then the decisive qualification that opens the way to Part Four of the volume:

"The position is different when comparing rates of profit in two countries. Here the same rate of profit expresses in most cases different rates of surplus-value."

The point is that the composition variable is not a compensating movement within a national economy in the same way as across national economies. Two countries may share a common profit rate on the world market while exhibiting quite different rates of surplus-value and quite different compositions. This is the seed of Marx's theory of international profit-rate equalization and of unequal rates of exploitation across national capitals — a theme that will only be developed much later, in the fragment on foreign trade and in the analysis of the world market.

The sweeping conclusion.

"It results from all these five cases, therefore, that a rising profit rate can correspond to a falling or a rising rate of surplus-value, a falling profit rate can correspond to a rising or a falling rate of surplus-value, and a rate of profit that remains the same can also correspond to a rising or a falling rate of surplus-value. We have already shown under heading I that a rising, falling or unchanged rate of profit can also correspond to a rate of surplus-value that remains the same. The rate of profit is thus determined by two major factors: the rate of surplus-value and the value composition of the capital."

This is the chapter's climax in a single sentence. Everything before has been the demonstration; this is the statement of the theorem.

2.21 The Closing Synthesis: Two Determinants and Six Summary Propositions

The final page consolidates the results, and — importantly — Marx now licenses the use of the percentage form for the summary, "since it is immaterial here in which of the two portions of capital the change originates" (i.e., for the equality/inequality propositions, only the ratio matters).

The rates of profit of two different capitals, or of one and the same capital in two successive and different states, are EQUAL:

  1. Given the same percentage composition and the same rate of surplus-value. (Trivially: same s', same v/C, hence same p'.)

  2. Given unequal percentage compositions and unequal rates of surplus-value, if the product s'v is the same in each case — that is, if the mass of surplus-value reckoned as a percentage of the total capital is the same; "in other words, when the factors s' and v stand in inverse proportion to one another in the two cases."

They are UNEQUAL:

  1. Given the same percentage composition, if the rates of surplus-value are unequal — in which case the profit rates stand in the same ratio as these rates of surplus-value.

  2. Given the same rate of surplus-value and different percentage compositions — in which case they stand in the same ratio as the variable portions of the capitals.

  3. Given different rates of surplus-value and different percentage compositions — in which case they stand in the same proportion as the products s'v, i.e. as the masses of surplus-value reckoned as a percentage of the total capital.

These six propositions are the chapter's formal yield. They constitute a complete, exhaustive classification of the conditions under which two capitals bear equal or unequal rates of profit.

Engels's final editorial note (footnote 10). The manuscript, we learn, "also contains further and very detailed calculations on the mathematical difference between rate of surplus-value and rate of profit (s' − p'), a difference which has all kinds of interesting properties, and whose movement presents cases in which the two rates draw apart and cases in which they converge. These movements can also be represented by curves." Engels refrained from reproducing this material, "since it is of little importance for the immediate aim of this book," but draws attention to it "for those readers who might wish to pursue it further." This closing note is significant for two reasons: (a) it confirms the chapter is unfinished in its mathematical ambitions; (b) it shows that Marx himself was aware of the gap s' − p' as a measurable, curve-representable quantity — an analytical counterpart of the qualitative theorem that the two rates are systematically decoupled. The gap is the "camouflage" quantified.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles

First, the rate of profit is a compound, not a simple index of exploitation. The chapter's central theorem is that p' = s' · (v/C). This means that the rate of profit is the product of two analytically independent determinations: the intensity with which living labour is exploited (s') and the value-compositional weight carried by living labour in the total capital advanced (v/C). Because it is a product of two factors, its movement is systematically ambiguous: it can rise while exploitation falls, fall while exploitation rises, or hold constant while both change. Anyone who reasons from profit rates to exploitation intensities, or from wage levels to profit levels, is making an inference that the algebra does not license. This is the chapter's most general and most consequential result, and it is the source of almost every subsequent theoretical puzzle in Volume 3.

Second, the decoupling is asymmetric and systemic, not accidental. Marx's crucial observation (§2.20) is that the "same profit rate" condition is cheap to satisfy with s' constant and expensive to satisfy with s' variable. Any change in composition whatsoever will move the profit rate when s' is fixed. But for a change in s' to leave the profit rate unchanged, an exactly matching, opposite compositional change is required. The practical implication is decisive: constant p' with widely divergent s' "is possible only very exceptionally in the case of one and the same capital, or with two capitals in the same country" — but is normal when comparing countries. This asymmetry expresses a real economic structure: the financial system's equalization of profit rates operates across national boundaries, so it is there, not within a single economy, that the divergence of the two rates becomes routine.

Third, the "organic" distinction between constant and variable capital is not just quantitative but categorical. Constant capital matters only through its value; its material form is irrelevant. Variable capital matters only through the living labour it commands, not through its own value. Therefore the two types of capital interact with the rate of profit in fundamentally different ways: changes in the value of c alter only the denominator; changes in the value of v alter the numerator and the denominator, and no linear mapping exists between changes in v and changes in s' when the wage rate moves (the "index problem," §2.5). This is a structural distinction that no purely aggregative accounting framework can capture.

Fourth, the laws governing the profit rate are bounded by the laws governing the conditions of labour. §2.14 establishes that there is "always a limit to the variation of v" beyond which s' cannot remain constant, and symmetrically a limit to the variation of c. The "free" variation of the ratio v/C is thus confined to a feasible region whose boundaries are set by the working day, intensity, and wages — themselves social and institutional magnitudes. The algebraic case enumeration therefore is not logically closed off from the sphere of class struggle; on the contrary, the boundaries of the case space are the boundaries of the struggle over conditions of labour.

Fifth, the method is comparative-static and case-exhaustive — deliberately so. Marx states the license explicitly: "The rate of profit is a function of several variables, and if we want to know how these variables act on the profit rate we must investigate in turn the individual effect of each, irrespective of whether an isolated effect of this kind is economically possible or not in the case of one and the same capital." The willingness to run "economically impossible" examples is not a sign of unrealism but of analytical decomposition: the total effect is the composition of partial effects, and the partial effects must be isolated first. This is the methodological tradition that will later be called comparative statics, and Marx's practice of it here is disciplined, systematic, and self-aware.

Broader Practical, Theoretical, and Philosophical Implications

For the theory of the falling rate of profit. The chapter plants the seed explicitly. Reading the falling-v case (Case 1, Reading A) as "simply the normal case in modern industry: rising productivity of labour, the operation of greater quantities of means of production by fewer workers," Marx writes: "And in Part Three of this volume we shall see how this movement is necessarily bound up with a simultaneous fall in the rate of profit." The connection is now transparent: rising productivity raises c/v, lowers v/C, and hence, with s' unchanged or even raised, lowers p'. The chapter's algebra is the technical shell of the falling-profit-rate argument; Part Three will fill in the historical dynamics, the counteracting tendencies, and the crises. The "release of capital" category introduced in Case 2 will matter directly for those counteracting tendencies — because capital released from existing lines can be reinvested, raising the mass of profit even as the rate falls.

For the theory of prices of production and the transformation problem. Chapter 3 establishes the base level of Volume 3's analysis: an individual capital's profit rate as a function of its own composition and its own rate of surplus-value. Part Two will then ask what happens when capitals of differing composition compete for investment and capital flows from low- to high-profit-rate branches, equalizing the rate. The result will be a general rate of profit, prices of production differing from values, and a systematic redistribution of surplus-value from labour-intensive to capital-intensive branches. The chapter is thus the point of departure for that redistribution — it identifies the determinants whose systematic dispersion across branches makes equalization both necessary and consequential.

For the critique of Ricardo and of classical political economy. The chapter's one direct citation of Ricardo (§2.16) is telling. Ricardo's "profits would be high or low exactly in proportion as wages would be low or high" is shown to hold only in the special case of a constant composition of capital with a constant working day and constant intensity of labour. Since capital composition is systematically transformed by accumulation, the Ricardian proposition is not the general law. This is the analytic content of Marx's critique of Ricardo — not that Ricardo was careless, but that his framework could not distinguish the two rates, and could not see that their relationship is mediated by a third term (composition). The chapter's whole case-enumeration is, in effect, a demonstration of why a one-factor theory of profit cannot survive scrutiny.

For the international comparison of profit rates and the theory of unequal development. The chapter's closing observation, that "the same rate of profit expresses in most cases different rates of surplus-value" as between countries, opens a research programme that Marx will only gesture toward in this volume. Its implication is enormous: the equalization of profit rates on world markets can coexist with systematically different rates of exploitation, and the composition variable is the hinge. This is the analytical foundation for later theories of unequal exchange, of imperial extraction, and of the divergence between high-wage, high-composition economies and low-wage, low-composition economies under conditions of global capital mobility.

For the theory of crises and the release of capital. The distinction between cases where capital is "tied up" and where it is "released" (§2.10) is not a marginal accounting nicety. Released capital is the raw material of credit expansion, of speculative bubbles, and of the reallocation of capital across branches. In the crisis theory that follows in Part Five of Volume 3, the sudden release or sudden immobilization of capital — traceable to just the sort of compositional shifts that this chapter catalogues — will become the mechanical trigger of crises of over-accumulation. The chapter's apparently dry algebra is thus the vocabulary in which the dynamics of the cycle will be written.

For the methodology of political economy. Three methodological lessons stand out. First, the chapter demonstrates the productive discipline of complete case enumeration: rather than settling for a few illustrative examples, Marx constructs the general formulas that cover every case and then demonstrates that the remaining candidate cases add nothing new. Second, it demonstrates the value of the dual reading — successive states of one capital and simultaneous distinct capitals are formally interchangeable, which licenses the comparative analysis of branches and countries without abandoning the temporal analysis of a single capital. Third, it demonstrates the limits of abstraction: the analysis is qualified throughout by reminders that turnover, price movements of constant capital, extra profits from above-average productivity, and the equalization of profit rates have all been bracketed off and must be re-imported to complete the theory. The chapter is deliberately incomplete, and aware of its incompleteness. Its value lies not in being a self-contained account of profit but in being a rigorous first transformation of surplus-value into profit — the indispensable foundation for everything that comes after.
