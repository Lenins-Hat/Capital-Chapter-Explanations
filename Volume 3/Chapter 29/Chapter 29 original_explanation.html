Volume 3 • Chapter 29: Component Parts of Bank Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 29, "Banking Capital's Component Parts," occupies a pivot-point in the architecture of Capital Volume III. It belongs to Part V, "The Division of Profit into Interest and Profit of Enterprise," and it opens after Marx has just dismantled a specific polemical manoeuvre: the transformation, by Fullarton and others associated with the Banking School, of the analytic distinction between money as means of circulation and money as means of payment (and, where a gold drain is at stake, money as world money) into a quite different distinction between circulation ("currency") and capital. Marx's opening move is to expose the symmetry of two opposed errors. Where "enlightened economics" laboured to insist that money is not capital, the "banker's economics" labours just as assiduously to insist that money is capital par excellence. The chapter's central problem is therefore not merely taxonomic but ideological: given that the credit system constitutes a vast apparatus of claims, titles, and book entries, how does this apparatus generate the systematic illusion that capital is a self-valorizing, self-multiplying thing — and what, precisely, is the material anatomy of the institution (banking capital) through which that illusion is produced and sustained?

The chapter's core thesis can be stated in three layers. First, banking capital has a determinate and surprisingly simple component structure: (1) cash, in the form of gold or notes; and (2) securities, which subdivide into commercial paper (bills of exchange falling due on specified dates, whose discounting is the banker's specific business) and public securities (government bonds, treasury bills, stocks — "interest-bearing paper," essentially different from bills of exchange), with mortgages also admissible. This structure, Marx emphasises, is invariant with respect to ownership: it makes no difference whether these components represent the banker's own capital or the deposits of others. The subdivisions remain identical whether he trades on his own capital or entirely on borrowed capital.

Second, the form of interest-bearing capital is generative of illusion as such. Any regular periodic revenue — from a national debt, from a share, from a piece of land, or even from a wage — can be capitalized: reckoned, at the average rate of interest, as the sum that a capital lent out at that rate would yield. This operation, which Marx names the formation of fictitious capital, severs every link between the value of the claim and the actual process of capital's valorization, "right down to the last trace," thereby "confirming the notion that capital is automatically valorized by its own powers." Marx drives this to its most scandalous limit with the wage: if a year's labour-power commands £50 and interest is 5 per cent, labour-power is declared to be a capital of £1,000 yielding that £50 — an absurdity in which the exploitation of labour-power is replaced by the claim that labour-power is mystical, interest-bearing capital.

Third, and crucially, Marx does not stop at the logical critique of illusion. He descends into the institutional mechanics of the credit system: the double role of deposits (lent out as interest-bearing capital yet simultaneously functioning as mere book entries cancelled by cheques), the clearing-house, the duplication and triplication of the same capital (with Engels's appended footnote on financial trusts and preferred/deferred shares), Adam Smith's account of money as a "deed of assignment," the infinite multiplication of the same £1,000 in deposits, and finally the regress of all reserve funds to the reserve fund of the Bank of England — itself of double existence, resting on the legal note-issue formula of the 1844 Bank Act, and liable to collapse (as in 1847 and 1857) while the Issue Department still holds millions. The chapter closes on the bill-brokers of London, carrying on enormous transactions with no cash reserve, and on the catastrophic ratios of their liabilities to their capital.

Its intellectual objective, then, is double-edged: to give a positive anatomy of banking capital, and to demonstrate that the "greater part of banker's capital is purely fictitious." It fits into the broader theoretical framework as the concrete institutional complement to the abstract theory of interest-bearing capital: the chapter in which fictitious capital, credit, and crisis are shown to be structurally inseparable, and in which the banker's balance sheet is revealed as a system of mutually referring claims rather than a stock of substance.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Polemical Premise: How the Bankers' Economics Inverts the Money/Capital Distinction

Marx opens not with a definition but with a boundary-marking against the immediately preceding analysis. He has "just seen" how Fullarton and others convert a specific and analytically necessary distinction — money as means of circulation versus money as means of payment, the latter extended to world money insofar as a gold drain is involved — into a different distinction, between circulation ("currency") and capital. This is the crux of the currency/banking school controversy, and Marx's point is that the substitution is not innocent. The distinction between means of circulation and means of payment pertains to the functions money performs within the circuit of capital; the distinction between currency and capital pertains to what is being hoarded, lent, or drained. By collapsing the first into the second, the Banking School writers could argue that a gold drain affects only "currency," not "capital," and thereby deflect the analysis away from the credit system's inherent instability.

Marx then identifies the reciprocal error. The special role that capital plays in this controversy means that "enlightened economics" — which insisted that money is not capital — is mirrored by the bankers' own apologetics, which tries "just as assiduously to insist that money is actually capital par excellence." Two mirror-image dogmas, both generated by the same polemical need. And he announces a forward-looking clarification that structures the remainder of the chapter: money capital in the sense of a transitional form of capital (alongside commodity capital and productive capital) is to be carefully distinguished from "moneyed capital" in the sense of interest-bearing capital. The former is a phase in the circuit of industrial capital; the latter is a separate social relation, the claim on surplus-value as a revenue. That the two get confused is precisely what makes the chapter necessary. The confusion is not a mere terminological slip — it is the conceptual vehicle by which the credit system appears to be a system of wealth-creation rather than a system of claims.

2.2 The Inventory of Banking Capital: Cash, Securities, and the Invariance of the Subdivisions

Marx now supplies the anatomy. Banking capital consists of (1) cash, in the form of gold or notes; (2) securities. The securities divide again:

  • Commercial paper — current bills of exchange that fall due on specified dates. Their discounting is "the specific business of the banker." A bill of exchange is a promise to pay issued by an industrial capitalist or merchant; discounting means the banker buys it before maturity, deducting interest for the period the bill has still to run, so that the deduction from its face value depends on the prevailing rate of interest. The bill is therefore, for the money-lender, interest-bearing paper — not because it bears a coupon, but because its purchase price is a present value discounted at the current rate.
  • Public securities — government bonds, treasury bills, stocks of all kinds: "interest-bearing paper" in a stricter sense, which Marx insists is essentially different from bills of exchange. Bills of exchange rest on an actual commercial transaction and mature in weeks or months; government stock rests on taxation and has no self-extinguishing term. This distinction matters enormously later, when Marx shows that the movement of the two classes of paper obeys different logics. Mortgages are admitted into this category as well.

Within this anatomy a further division is drawn: the banker's own invested capital versus the deposits that form his banking or borrowed capital; and, for banks with the right of issue, notes must be added. Marx explicitly brackets deposits and notes "to start with" — a deferral that the second half of the chapter will redeem.

The decisive claim in this section, however, is the invariance claim: "the actual components of banker's capital — money, bills of exchange and interest-bearing paper — are not affected by whether these various elements represent his own capital, or deposits, the capital of other people." This is a quietly radical proposition. It means that a bank's balance sheet does not disclose whose wealth it describes. The banker's own capital and the public's capital are chemically indistinguishable once they are inside the vault or on the ledger. The very identity of the constituent elements is ownership-blind — and it is precisely this ownership-blindness that makes the later phenomena of duplication, triplication, and fictitious capital possible. If the components were property-specific, the same £1,000 could not perform three functions at once; because they are not, it can.

2.3 The General Mechanism of Illusion: How Any Revenue Becomes Interest, and Interest Becomes Capital

Marx now states the general mechanism in a paragraph that functions as the chapter's logical engine. Under the form of interest-bearing capital, "any definite and regular monetary revenue appear[s] as the interest on a capital, whether it actually derives from a capital or not." The sequence runs in a specific order: first the money income is transformed into interest; then, "with the interest we then have the capital from which it derives." The relationship is inverted — the consequence generates its own supposed cause. And a parallel inversion applies to any sum of value: as soon as it is not spent as revenue, it appears as a "principal" in contrast to the possible or actual interest it can bear.

This is a generalisation of what Marx elsewhere calls the fetishism of interest-bearing capital: the social relation (the exploitation of labour, the appropriation of surplus-value) is occluded by a thing-like relation (money begetting money). The parenthetical phrase later in the chapter — "interest-bearing capital always being the mother of every insane form" — is the compact version of this diagnosis.

2.4 Case One — The National Debt: Capital That Was Never Capital

Marx now demonstrates the mechanism with a worked example. "Say that the average rate of interest is 5 per cent per year. A sum of £500, then, if transformed into interest-bearing capital, would bring in a yearly £25. Hence every fixed annual income of £25 is seen as the interest on a capital of £500." This is the arithmetic of illusion, and its algebraic form is:

C = R / i

where C is the fictitious capital value, R the periodic revenue, and i the rate of interest. Here £25 / 0.05 = £500.

But Marx immediately qualifies: this is "a purely illusory notion, except in the case where the source of the £25 — whether a mere title of ownership or claim, or an actual element of production such as a piece of land — is directly transferable, or assumes a form in which it is transferable." The qualifier is important: the illusion is not all-encompassing, because where a genuine transferable asset underlies the income (land, for instance), the capitalization corresponds to something real. The illusion proper arises where the underlying is either nonexistent or not the source of the income at all.

The national debt is the first case. The state pays its creditors interest on borrowed capital. The creditor cannot recall the capital; he can only sell the claim, his title of ownership. "The capital itself has been consumed, spent by the state. It no longer exists." What the state's creditor actually possesses is a three-part complex:

  1. the state's promissory note for, say, £100;
  2. a claim on the state's annual revenue — the proceeds of the year's taxation — to a certain amount, say £5 or 5 per cent;
  3. the freedom to sell this promissory note to anyone he likes.

Marx then shows that the sale is not a return of capital but a hand-over of the claim. If the interest rate is 5 per cent and the state's credit good, owner A can generally sell the note for £100 to B, "since it is the same thing for B whether he lends out £100 at 5 per cent per year or assures himself of an annual tribute of £5 from the state by paying out £100." But in all these cases "the capital from which the state's payment is taken as deriving, as interest, is illusory and fictitious." Two distinct grounds are given for the fiction: (a) the money lent has been consumed and no longer exists; and (b) more fundamentally, "It was never designed to be spent as capital, to be invested, and yet only by being invested as capital could it have been made into a self-maintaining value." The state's borrowing is consumption expenditure, not capital investment, so the very presupposition of the interest form — a self-maintaining, valorizing principal — is absent. For the original creditor A, the tax share is interest on his "capital" in the same sense that the usurer's cut from a spendthrift is interest; in neither case was the money laid out as capital. The possibility of selling the note "represents for A the potential return of his principal." For B, "from his own private standpoint his capital is then invested as interest-bearing capital. In actual fact, he has simply taken the place of A, and bought A's claim on the state."

And then the crucial nuance, which rescues the analysis from being a mere debunking: "No matter how these transactions are multiplied, the capital of the national debt remains purely fictitious, and the moment these promissory notes become unsaleable, the illusion of this capital disappears. Yet this fictitious capital has its characteristic movement for all that, as we shall see soon." This is the pivot of the entire chapter. Fictitious capital is not nothing; it behaves. It has prices, fluctuations, a market, a crisis-dynamic — even though its substrate is a fiction. The remainder of the chapter (and of the surrounding chapters) is precisely the study of that characteristic movement.

2.5 Case Two — Wages as Interest: The Apotheosis of the Absurdity, and the Slave Contrast

From the national debt, "where a negative quantity appears as capital" — Marx's aside here, "interest-bearing capital always being the mother of every insane form, so that debts, for example, can appear as commodities in the mind of the banker," is one of the chapter's sharpest formulations — he moves to labour-power. Here wages are conceived as interest, and hence labour-power as capital yielding that interest. The arithmetic: "If the wage for a year comes to £50, say, and the rate of interest is 5 per cent, one annual labour-power is taken as equal to a capital of £1,000." Again C = R / i = 50 / 0.05 = 1,000.

Marx calls this "the absurdity of the capitalist's way of conceiving things … at its climax," because it inverts the causal order of the entire theory of surplus-value: "instead of deriving the valorization of capital from the exploitation of labour-power, they explain the productivity of labour-power by declaring that labour-power itself is this mystical thing, interest-bearing capital." The labourer's exploitation, which is the real ground of profit, is transmuted into a property of the labourer — an internal fund of capital that yields interest.

Marx traces the notion historically: "In the second half of the seventeenth century (with Petty, for example) this was a favourite notion, but it is still used today, in all seriousness, by vulgar economists and especially by German statisticians." The footnote documents the vulgar usage with a quotation from Von Reden's Vergleichende Kulturstatistik (Berlin, 1848, p. 434): "The worker has a capital value which is found by considering the monetary value of his annual service as a payment of interest … If the average daily wage is capitalized at a rate of 4 per cent, we make the average value for an agricultural worker of the male sex to be 1,500 thalers in German Austria, 1,500 thalers in Prussia, 3,750 thalers in England, 2,000 thalers in France, and 750 thalers in Russia proper." The footnote is a specimen of the method in its statistical application — the same capitalization operation that produced £1,000 from a £50 wage is here used to build an international table of the "capital value" of human beings, with Russia's lower figure reflecting lower wages.

Two "inconvenient circumstances" refute the notion:

  1. The worker has to work in order to receive this interest. The "interest" is not a return on an asset he owns; it is the price of his activity, forthcoming only under the discipline of employment.
  2. He cannot turn the capital value of his labour-power into money by transferring it to someone else. Unlike the state's bondholder, unlike the shareholder, unlike the landowner, the worker has no transferable title. "Rather, the annual value of his labour-power is his average annual wage, and his labour has to replace its buyer with this value itself plus the surplus-value that is its valorization."

The contrast with slavery is exact and analytically pointed. Under the slave system the worker does have a capital value, namely his purchase price. And if he is hired out, the hirer must first pay the interest on this purchase price and on top of this replace the capital's annual depreciation. The slave owner, unlike the capitalist employer of free labour, really does own a capital asset in the person of the worker — which is why the "wages as interest" analogy, nonsensical for the free worker, has a genuine application to the slave. The comparison thus performs double duty: it exposes the free-labour notion as a false analogy, and it clarifies what a real capital value in a human being would require (transferability and a purchase price).

2.6 The Formal Definition of Capitalization and the Loss of the Valorization Process

Marx now names the operation formally: "The formation of fictitious capital is known as capitalization. Any regular periodic income can be capitalized by reckoning it up, on the basis of the average rate of interest, as the sum that a capital lent out at this interest rate would yield." His canonical example: "if the annual income in question is £100 and the rate of interest 5 per cent, then £100 is the annual interest on £2,000, and this £2,000 is then taken as the capital value of the legal ownership title to this annual £100." So C = 100 / 0.05 = 2,000.

For the purchaser of the ownership title, the annual £100 "does actually represent the conversion of the capital he has invested into interest." This is the subjective truth of the matter: for him, functionally, it is interest on his investment. But the objective result is that "all connection with the actual process of capital's valorization is lost, right down to the last trace, confirming the notion that capital is automatically valorized by its own powers." The phrase "right down to the last trace" is the key diagnostic: the illusion is not a partial misperception but a complete severance. Capital, which in Marx's theory is a social relation of exploitation expressed in a circuit (M–C…P…C'–M'), becomes a self-sufficient thing that yields revenue by nature. This is the completed fetishism of interest-bearing capital, the theoretical counterpart of the rentier's daily experience.

2.7 Joint-Stock Shares: Real Capital versus Its Ownership Title (The Non-Duplication Thesis)

Marx now extends the analysis to the case where the underlying is not a pure fiction — joint-stock capital, which "we have already seen" the credit system produces. Securities "purport to be ownership titles representing this capital." Shares in railway, mining, shipping companies, etc., represent real capital, i.e. capital invested and functioning in these enterprises, or the sum of money advanced by shareholders to be spent there as capital.

Two qualifications follow immediately:

  • Fraud is not ruled out. "It is in no way ruled out here that these shares may be simply a fraud."
  • But the capital does not exist twice over — "once as the capital value of the ownership titles, the shares, and then again as the capital actually invested or to be invested in the enterprises in question. It exists only in the latter form, and the share is nothing but an ownership title, pro rata, to the surplus-value which this capital is to realize."

This is the chapter's decisive anti-duplication thesis, and it is a direct assault on the appearance that the stock exchange creates wealth. The share is a claim, not a second capital. When A sells the title to B and B to C, "these transactions have no essential effect on the matter. A or B has then transformed his title into capital, but C has transformed his capital into a mere ownership title to the surplus-value expected from this share capital." The same real capital remains in the enterprise; what circulates is only the title, and the only person left holding a title rather than money is the last seller. The individual can indeed convert a claim into cash — but only by another's purchase, which is a transfer, not a creation.

2.8 The Autonomous Movement of Security Prices: The Formula of Inverse Variation with the Interest Rate

Marx now addresses the phenomenon that most strongly sustains the illusion: "The independent movement of these ownership titles' values, not only those of government bonds, but also of shares, strengthens the illusion that they constitute real capital besides the capital or claim to which they may give title." The titles become commodities with their own prices, their own market values, and a determination "differing from their nominal values, without any change in the value of the actual capital (even if its valorization does change)."

Two determinants of market value are distinguished:

(a) The level and security of the receipts to which the title legally entitles the holder. Marx's example: a share with nominal value £100 (the sum originally advanced), in an enterprise yielding 10 per cent instead of 5 per cent, rises in market value — "other circumstances being equal" — because, capitalized at 5 per cent, it now represents a fictitious capital of £200: C = 10 / 0.05 = 200. Someone who buys it for £200 obtains a revenue of 5 per cent on his capital outlay. The opposite holds when the enterprise's revenue declines. Note that the share's nominal value stays £100; what changes is the capitalization of its actual yield at the market rate.

(b) Speculation. "The market value of these securities is partly speculative, since it is determined not just by the actual revenue but rather by the anticipated revenue as reckoned in advance." This is a distinct and important caveat: the price is not a function of the present alone, but of expectations.

Marx then isolates the pure relationship by holding actual valorization constant — or, where no such capital exists (national debts), by taking the annual yield as fixed by law and sufficiently guaranteed. In that case: "the prices of these securities rise and fall in inverse proportion to the rate of interest":

  • If the rate of interest rises from 5 per cent to 10 per cent, a security yielding £5 now represents a capital of only £50 (5 / 0.10).
  • If the rate of interest falls to 2½ per cent, the same security represents a capital of £200 (5 / 0.025).

"Its value is always simply the capitalized yield, i.e. the yield as reckoned on an illusory capital at the existing rate of interest." This gives the chapter one of its cleanest analytical results: the price of a fixed-income security is inversely related to the rate of interest, and any movement in the bank rate is immediately a movement in the "value" of the nation's paper wealth — with no change whatsoever in the real capital or the real income stream.

From this Marx derives the crisis-dynamics: "In times of pressure on the money market, therefore, these securities fall in price for two reasons: first, because the interest rate rises, and second, because they are put up for sale in massive quantities, to be converted into money." The two causes are analytically distinct but empirically simultaneous: one is a re-capitalization at a higher discount rate, the other a supply shock from forced liquidation. The fall occurs irrespective of whether the yield is constant (government bonds) or whether the real capital's valorization is itself threatened by the disturbance in the reproduction process (industrial undertakings) — "In the latter case, we simply have a further devaluation besides that already mentioned," i.e. a genuine devaluation of the underlying added on top of the pure capitalization effect. The distinction is exactly the one Marx needs: some depreciation is real, some is purely fictional — and only the latter is recovered when the storm passes.

2.9 Crisis Depreciation as a Mechanism of Centralization, and the Measure of National Wealth

"Once the storm is over, these securities rise again to their former level, in so far as the undertakings they represent have not come to grief and are not fraudulent. Their depreciation in a crisis is a powerful means of centralizing money wealth." Two propositions are packed here. First, the resilience of fictitious value: if the underlying survives, the paper recovers. Second, and more importantly for Marx's political economy, the redistributive function of the crisis: those with cash (or with credit) buy depreciated titles; those forced to sell surrender claims cheaply. The crisis is thus an apparatus of concentration — which is why the banker with ready money, as the Rothschild anecdote in footnote 2 illustrates, is uniquely positioned. Engels's footnote reports that immediately after the February revolution of 1848, when commodities and securities in Paris were extremely devalued and totally unsaleable, a Swiss merchant in Liverpool, Mr E. Zwilchenbart, sold whatever he could for cash, travelled to Paris, and proposed a joint deal to Rothschild. Rothschild stared at him, seized him by both shoulders and asked: "Avez-vous de l'argent sur vous?" — "Oui, M. le baron." — "Alors vous êtes un homme!" ("Then you're the man for me!") "And they both made a handsome profit." The anecdote is not decorative: it is a concrete demonstration that in a crisis, liquidity — the one thing the fictitious system cannot manufacture — is the ticket to acquiring the claims of others.

Marx then makes the corresponding aggregate claim: "In so far as the rise or fall in value of these securities is independent of the movement in the value of the real capital that they represent, the wealth of a nation is just as great afterwards as before." He substantiates this with testimony from the Governor of the Bank of England: "The public stocks and canal and railway shares had already by the 23rd of October, 1847, been depreciated in the aggregate to the amount of £114,752,225" (Morris, evidence in the Report on Commercial Distress, 1847–8). Marx's comment is precise about the condition under which this loss is not a real national loss: "As long as their depreciation was not the expression of any standstill in production and in railway and canal traffic, or an abandonment of undertakings already begun, or a squandering of capital in positively worthless enterprises, the nation was not a penny poorer by the bursting of these soap bubbles of nominal money capital." The four listed conditions are precisely the cases in which the depreciation would index a real loss — a halt in production, abandonment of works, squandering in worthless ventures. Where none of these holds, the £114 million is a pure bookkeeping evaporation.

The section's summary statement is the theoretical core: "All these securities actually represent nothing but accumulated claims, legal titles, to future production. Their money or capital value either does not represent capital at all, as in the case of national debts, or is determined independently of the real capital value they represent."

2.10 The Accumulation of "Moneyed Capital" as the Accumulation of Claims

Marx draws the consequence for accumulation theory. "In all countries of capitalist production, there is a tremendous amount of so-called interest-bearing capital or 'moneyed capital' in this form. And an accumulation of money capital means for the most part nothing more than an accumulation of these claims to production, and an accumulation of the market price of these claims, of their illusory capital value." This is a direct strike at the appearance that the growth of financial assets measures the growth of wealth. Accumulation of moneyed capital, "for the most part," is the accumulation of claims and of the prices at which those claims trade — a phenomenon which can inflate indefinitely while the productive base stagnates, and which can collapse without the productive base being touched.

2.11 The Internal Composition of the Banker's Capital: Discount, Deposits, and the Flux of the Reserve

Marx now returns to the anatomy of §2.2 and fills in the deferred detail, running through the components once more with their functions:

  • One portion of banker's capital is invested in these so-called interest-bearing securities. Importantly, "This is actually part of the reserve capital and does not function in the banking business proper." Securities held by a bank are typically not its trading stock but its secondary reserve.
  • The most important portion consists of bills of exchange — promises to pay issued by industrial capitalists or merchants. For the money-lender these bills are interest-bearing paper; when he buys them he deducts interest for the period they still have to run, and the deduction from the face value of the bill thus depends on the rate of interest at the time. Discounting is therefore a direct transmission mechanism from the money market to the whole commercial system.
  • The final portion consists of the money reserve in gold or notes. Deposits, "unless tied up for a longer period by contract, are always at the depositors' disposal. They are in a state of perpetual flux. But if some depositors withdraw their deposits, others replace them, so that the overall average fluctuates only a little in times of normal business." This is the statistical premise of fractional reserve banking, stated as a fact of the deposit flux rather than as a policy.

Marx then generalises: "The banks' reserve funds, in countries of developed capitalist production, always express the average amount of money existing as a hoard, and a part of this hoard itself consists of paper, mere drafts on gold, which have no value of their own." The hoard is the money that has been withdrawn from circulation and lies idle; the banking system, as a whole, is the social organisation of that hoard — but even the hoard is partly paper. He then states the chapter's summary verdict in its most comprehensive form: "The greater part of banker's capital is therefore purely fictitious and consists of claims (bills of exchange) and shares (drafts on future revenues)."

The qualification that follows is dense and worth unpacking clause by clause. It "should not be forgotten" that this capital's money value, as represented by the paper in the banker's safe, is completely fictitious

  • even where the paper consists of drafts on certain assured revenues (government securities) or ownership titles to real capital (shares) — "their money value being determined differently from the value of the actual capital that they at least partially represent";
  • or, where they represent only a claim to revenue and not capital at all, "the claim to the same revenue is expressed in a constantly changing fictitious money capital."

So there are two distinct species of fictitiousness: (i) titles whose asset-base exists but whose price is determined independently of it; and (ii) titles with no asset-base at all, whose very "money capital" is a function of the interest rate and nothing else. To this is added a third consideration: this fictitious capital of the banker represents to a large extent not his own capital but rather that of the public who deposit with him, whether with interest or without. The ownership-blindness established in §2.2 now pays off: the bank's fictitious capital is largely other people's fictitious capital, and the bank's solvency is consequently a claim about a claim.

2.12 The Double Role of Deposits and the Clearing Mechanism

Marx now analyses deposits with an exactness that repays close reading. "Deposits are always made in money, gold or notes, or else in drafts on money." Except for the reserve fund, which contracts or expands according to the needs of actual circulation, these deposits are in fact always either

  • in the hands of industrial capitalists and merchants, serving to discount their bills of exchange and make them advances; or
  • in the hands of dealers in securities (stockbrokers), private persons who have sold securities, or the government (in the case of treasury bills and new loans).

So the deposited money, having been lent out, is held by specific classes of borrowers performing specific functions. This is the concrete counterpart of the abstract statement that money capital is a "transitional form."

"The deposits themselves play a double role." On the one hand, they are lent out as interest-bearing capital and are thus "not to be found in the banks' safes, figuring instead in their books as credits held by the depositors." On the other hand, they function as mere book entries in so far as the reciprocal credits of depositors are settled by cheques on their deposits and mutually cancelled. And here Marx adds a point that dismantles any residual notion of deposits as located wealth: "it is completely immaterial in this connection whether the deposits are with the same banker, so that they can cancel the various accounts against one another, or whether they are with different banks, which exchange their cheques on one another and simply pay the balances." The clearing-house — whether intra-bank bookkeeping or inter-bank balance settlement — performs the same function: extinguishing obligations without any movement of cash. The deposit is thus simultaneously a loan (asset side) and a debt record (liability side), and it discharges obligations by pure cancellation. Its "existence" consists in a set of mutually conditioned entries.

2.13 The Duplication and Triplication of Capital

Marx now raises his central rhetorical formulation of the chapter's second half: "With the development of interest-bearing capital and the credit system, all capital seems to be duplicated, and at some points triplicated, by the various ways in which the same capital, or even the same claim, appears in various hands in different guises." The verb is "seems" — the appearance is systematic but false. "The greater part of this 'money capital' is purely fictitious. With the exception of the reserve fund, deposits are never more than credits with the banker, and never exist as real deposits." Once lent out, they function as capital for the bankers; the bankers pay one another "reciprocal drafts on these non-existent deposits by balancing these credits against each other." The mechanism of settlement does not require the deposits to exist as anything other than an entry.

Engels's footnote 3 extends the analysis chronologically and institutionally: the duplication and triplication "has been taken very much further in recent years, e.g. through the financial trusts," which by Engels's time "already require a column of their own in the London stock-market reports." The mechanism described: a company is formed to purchase a class of interest-bearing paper (foreign government securities, English municipal loans, American public bonds, railway stocks, etc.); it raises, say, £2 million by share issue; the directors buy or speculate in the values; and they distribute the annual interest received among the shareholders as dividends after deducting expenses. Further, in certain joint-stock companies the practice has developed of dividing ordinary shares into two classes, "preferred" and "deferred": preferred shares receive a fixed interest payment, say 5 per cent, provided overall profit permits; any surplus goes to the holders of deferred shares. In this way "the more 'solid' capital investment in preferred shares is more or less separated from actual speculation, in deferred." And then the terminal development: "since certain large undertakings do not want to avail themselves of this new method, companies have come to be formed which invest a million pounds, say, or even a few million, in the shares of these firms, subsequently issuing new shares for the nominal value of the shares bought, but with one half preferred and the other deferred. In these cases the original shares are duplicated, by serving as the basis for a new share issue." This is fictitious capital raised to a second power: the same underlying shares now support two layers of shareholder claims, with the risk concentrated in the deferred tranche. Engels's note demonstrates that Marx's category is not a historical curiosity of the 1840s but a structurally expanding feature of finance capital.

2.14 Adam Smith on Money as "Deed of Assignment": Marx's Correction

Marx now enlists Adam Smith, quoting at length from Book II, Chapter IV (Pelican edition, p. 452). Smith's opening proposition: "Even in the monied interest, however, the money is, as it were, but the deed of assignment, which conveys from one hand to another those capitals which the owners do not care to employ themselves." These capitals "may be greater in almost any proportion than the amount of the money which serves as the instrument of their conveyance; the same pieces of money successively serving for many different loans, as well as for many different purchases." Smith's illustration: A lends £1,000 to W, who immediately buys £1,000 of goods from B; B, having no occasion for the money, lends the identical pieces to X, who buys £1,000 of goods from C; C lends them to Y, who buys from D. "In this manner the same pieces, either of coin or of paper, may, in the course of a few days, serve as the instrument of three different loans, and of three different purchases, each of which is, in value, equal to the whole amount of those pieces." Smith's conclusion: "What the three men, A, B and C, assign to the three borrowers, W, X and Y, is the power of making those purchases. In this power consist both the value and the use of the loans." The stock lent is equal to the value of the goods purchasable with it and is "three times greater than that of the money with which the purchases are made." The loans may be perfectly well secured because the goods purchased by the different debtors will in due time bring back, with a profit, an equal value of coin or paper. "And as the same pieces of money can thus serve as the instrument of different loans to three, or for the same reason, to thirty times their value, so they may likewise successively serve as the instrument of repayment."

Marx then corrects and deepens. He notes first the symmetry between circulation and lending: "Since the same piece of money can make several different purchases, depending on the velocity of its circulation, it can equally be used for various different loans, for purchases move it from one hand to another, and a loan is simply a transfer from one hand to another that is not mediated by any sale." There is, however, a decisive difference between the circulation-function and the loan-function:

  • In circulation, each seller receives money as the transformed form of his commodity. "Nowadays, when every commodity is expressed as a capital value, the money in its various loans successively represents different capitals, which is simply a different way of putting the previous statement that it can successively realize different commodity values."
  • In a loan, money is not transferred as a means of circulation. "As long as it remains in the lender's possession, it is not a means of circulation in his hands but the value-existence of his capital. And it is in this form that he transfers it to someone else in a loan."

And then the limit-case that disciplines the whole analysis: "If A had lent money to B, and B to C, without any purchases intervening, the same money would not represent three capitals but only one, just one capital value. How many capitals it actually does represent depends on how often it functions as the value form of various different commodity capitals." This is the crucial corrective. The multiplication of capitals in Smith's example is not a property of the money as such; it depends on the money serving, at each step, as the value-form of a different commodity capital — that is, on real commodities being bought and sold, i.e. on the reproduction process. Loan capital cannot multiply independently of production; where it appears to (pure chains of lending), the money represents one capital, not three. The chapter thus preserves the link to real accumulation even while exposing the fictional character of the paper.

2.15 Deposits as the Public's Loans to Bankers: The Infinite Multiplication of the Same £1,000

Marx now applies Smith's analysis to deposits: "What Adam Smith says about loans in general applies equally to deposits, this being simply a particular name for loans that the public make to the bankers. The same pieces of money can serve as instrument for any number of deposits." He then quotes The Currency Theory Reviewed (pp. 62–3): "It is unquestionably true that the £1,000 which you deposit at A today may be reissued tomorrow, and form a deposit at B. The day after that, reissued from B, it may form a deposit at C … and so on to infinitude; and that the same £1,000 in money may, thus, by a succession of transfers, multiply itself into a sum of deposits absolutely indefinite." Hence "It is possible, therefore, that nine-tenths of all the deposits in the United Kingdom may have no existence beyond their record in the books of the bankers who are respectively accountable for them." The empirical illustration: "Thus in Scotland, for instance, currency has never exceeded £3 million, the deposits in the banks are estimated at £27 million" — a ratio of one to nine, exactly matching the "nine-tenths" estimate. The passage continues: unless a run is made on the banks, the same £1,000 "would, if sent back upon its travels, cancel with the same facility a sum equally indefinite. As the same £1,000, with which you cancel your debt to a tradesman today, may cancel his debt to the merchant tomorrow, the merchant's debt to the bank the day following, and so on without end; so the same £1,000 may pass from hand to hand, and bank to bank, and cancel any conceivable sum of deposits."

The passage performs a precise theoretical role. It shows that the multiplication of deposits is a circulation-phenomenon of a single money sum — a function of velocity, not of quantity of value. Nine-tenths of the system's apparent wealth is the same money appearing in different books. And yet the system functions so long as the round of cancellations is not interrupted by a demand for cash — which is precisely what a run is.

2.16 The Reserve Fund and the Regress to the Bank of England

Having shown that securities, bills, and deposits are all, in their way, phantom-like, Marx turns to the "reserve fund," "where one might finally expect to lay hold of something solid." His method is to let the practitioners speak. Morris, Governor of the Bank of England: "The reserves of the private bankers are in the hands of the Bank of England in the shape of deposits … An export of gold acts exclusively, in the first instance, upon the reserve of the Bank of England; but it would also be acting upon the reserves of the bankers, in as much as it is a withdrawal of a portion of the reserves which they have in the Bank of England. It would be acting upon the reserves of all the bankers throughout the country" (Commercial Distress, 1847–8, Nos. 3639, 3642).

Marx's inference: "Ultimately, therefore, what these reserve funds actually boil down to is the reserve fund of the Bank of England." The hierarchy of reserves is really a single apex. And this apex, too, "has a double existence."

Engels's footnote 4 documents the empirical magnitude of this concentration with an official tabulation of the reserves of the fifteen largest London banks for November 1892, from the Daily News of 15 December. The table gives, for each bank, its liabilities, cash reserves, and the percentage:

The "solid" thing at the bottom of the pile has evaporated into a legal formula.

2.17 The Arithmetic of the Bank of England's Reserve and the 1847/1857 Crises

Marx now specifies that formula. The reserve fund of the Banking Department is equal to the excess of notes that the Bank is authorized to issue over the notes actually in circulation. The legal maximum note issue is £14 million (the amount for which no metal reserve is required, this being "the approximate sum of the government's debt to the Bank"), plus the Bank's total reserve of precious metal. So:

Issue capacity = £14m (fiduciary, non-metal-backed) + metal reserve

If the metal reserve is also £14 million, the Bank can issue £28 million in notes. If £20 million of these are already in circulation, the reserve fund of the Banking Department is £8 million. "This £8 million in notes is then the legal banking capital that the Bank has at its disposal and at the same time the reserve fund for its deposits."

Now the crisis mechanism:

"Should a drain of gold take place, reducing the metal reserve by £6 million — for which an equal sum in notes has to be destroyed — the Banking Department's reserve falls from £8 million to £2 million."

Note the double contraction: the gold loss destroys an equal quantity of notes (the note issue must be covered beyond the fiduciary £14m), so the reserve falls by the full £6 million. "On the one hand, the Bank increases its interest rate very sharply; on the other hand, the banks which have deposited with it, as well as its other depositors, see the reserve fund for their own credits with the Bank take a sharp drop." Two simultaneous shocks: a spike in the price of money, and a fall in the visible backing of everyone's claims.

Marx then gives the historical proof of the system's fragility. "In 1857, London's four largest joint-stock banks threatened that if the Bank of England did not obtain a 'government letter' suspending the Bank Act of 1844, they would call in their deposits and bankrupt the Banking Department." The threat was credible, and this is the meaning of Marx's next sentence: "Thus the Banking Department can fail to meet obligations, as in 1847, while there are still several millions in the Issue Department (e.g. £8 million in 1847), as guarantee for the convertibility of notes in circulation." The Bank's two departments are legally separated by the 1844 Act, and the separation is what allows the institution to be simultaneously solvent in one sense and insolvent in another. And Marx adds, cuttingly: "Though this is in turn an illusion." Even the Issue Department's £8 million guarantee — the last solid thing — is itself an illusion.

Engels's footnote 5 spells out what suspension of the 1844 Bank Act means: "The suspension of the 1844 Bank Act enables the Bank to issue an unlimited sum of banknotes, irrespective of the extent to which these are covered by its gold reserve; i.e. to create an unlimited amount of fictitious, paper money capital and use this to make advances to the banks and billbrokers, and through them to the world of commerce." The footnote is a definitional contribution: suspension of the Act does not create metal, it creates fictitious paper money capital — the legal sanction of what the chapter has been describing all along.

2.18 The Bill-Brokers: The Most Fragile Link

The chapter closes with the institution in which all these tensions are concentrated. Marx quotes the Report of Committee on Bank Acts, 1857–8, p. 5, Section 8: "That large portion (of deposits) for which the bankers themselves have no immediate demand passes into the hands of the bill-brokers, who give to the banker in return commercial bills already discounted by them for persons in London and in different parts of the country as a security for the sum advanced by the banker. The bill-broker is responsible to the banker for payment of this money at call." The structure is a three-tier chain of re-lending: depositors → bankers → bill-brokers → commerce. The crucial clause is "at call": the bill-broker's liability to the banker is repayable on demand, while his asset is a portfolio of bills maturing at specified future dates. This is a maturity mismatch in its purest form.

The scale is given by the Governor of the Bank of England, Neave: "'We know that one broker had 5 million, and we were led to believe that another had between 8 and 10 million; there was one with 4, another with 3½, and a third with above 8. I speak of deposits with the brokers.'" So a handful of private firms held £30 million or more of callable deposits.

And the practices: "The London bill-brokers carried on their enormous transactions without any cash reserve, relying on the run off of their bills falling due, or in extremity, on the power of obtaining advances from the Bank of England on the security of bills under discount." The phrase "without any cash reserve" is the chapter's terminal empirical point: the most leveraged nodal point of the whole credit system is also the one with no reserve at all, its viability resting on the regularity of maturities or, failing that, on the Bank of England as lender of last resort.

The evidence of both fragility and resilience follows: "Two bill-broking houses in London suspended payment in 1847; both afterwards resumed business. In 1857, both suspended again." And then the balance-sheet data, which show the leverage expanding across the cycle: "The liabilities of one house in 1847 were, in round numbers, £2,683,000, with a capital of £180,000; the liabilities of the same house, in 1857, were £5,300,000, the capital probably not more than one-fourth of what it was in 1847. The liabilities of the other firm were between £3,000,000 and £4,000,000 at each period of stoppage, with a capital not exceeding £45,000."

Pause on the ratios. The first house: liabilities £2,683,000 against capital £180,000 — approximately 15:1 — in 1847; and in 1857, liabilities £5,300,000 against a capital "probably not more than one-fourth" of £180,000, i.e. ~£45,000 — approximately 118:1. The second house: liabilities of £3–4 million against capital never exceeding £45,000 — a leverage ratio ranging up to nearly 89:1. And crucially, each firm weathered suspension twice, i.e. the failures were absorbed by the system. The chapter thus ends not on a definition but on a set of balance sheets demonstrating that the "enormous transactions" of the credit system were supported by capital amounting to a rounding error on their liabilities — the empirical confirmation of every claim made earlier in the chapter about fictitious capital, duplication, and the substitution of claims for substance.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Several fundamental principles emerge with unusual clarity from this chapter.

First, the principle of ownership-blindness in financial form. The components of banking capital — money, bills of exchange, interest-bearing paper — are unaffected by whether they represent the banker's own capital or the public's deposits. This is the formal condition of possibility for everything else in the credit system. Because the form does not register ownership, the same value can be simultaneously an asset and a liability, present and lent, reserve and loan.

Second, the principle of capitalization as the general formula of fictitious wealth. Any regular periodic revenue, whatever its source, can be converted into a capital value by division through the interest rate: C = R / i. This formula is source-agnostic, which is precisely its ideological power. It can transform a national debt into a capital, a share into a capital, a piece of land into a capital, and a wage into a capital. The chapter's treatment of the wage as interest-bearing capital shows that the formula recognises no distinction between a claim on surplus-value, a claim on taxation, and a price for labour-power. This indifference is the theoretical signature of the rentier's worldview.

Third, the principle of the autonomy of fictitious capital. Marx insists that although fictitious capital is illusory, "this fictitious capital has its characteristic movement for all that." Fictitious values behave: they fluctuate inversely with the rate of interest, they respond to anticipated revenue, they collapse in money-market pressure for two analytically distinct reasons (rate rise and forced liquidation), they recover after the storm if the real undertakings survive, and their collapse functions as "a powerful means of centralizing money wealth." The lesson is that fictitious capital is not a mere ideological error to be dispelled; it is a real social mechanism with real distributive consequences — most brutally, the transfer of claims from the forced seller to the liquid buyer.

Fourth, the principle of the strict limit of the multiplication. Against any view that credit creates capital ex nihilo, Marx's correction of Adam Smith establishes the boundary condition: if A lends to B and B to C without intervening purchases, the same money represents one capital, not three. The number of capitals that a money sum represents depends on how often it functions as the value-form of different commodity capitals, i.e. on the reproduction process. This is the anchor that keeps the analysis of fictitious capital tethered to production — and the reason the depreciation of securities is not a national loss "as long as" it does not express a standstill in production, an abandonment of undertakings, or squandering in worthless enterprises.

Fifth, the principle of the regress of reserves to a single apex, which is itself a claim. Private bankers' reserves are deposits at the Bank of England; an export of gold acts on the Bank's reserve and thereby on all reserves at once. But the Bank's own reserve is a double existence: the Banking Department's reserve is a legal formula (fiduciary issue of £14 million matched to the government's debt to the Bank, plus the metal reserve, minus notes in circulation). The 1892 figures in Engels's footnote show the whole system's £27.8 million of "cash" reserves resting on £25 million deposited at the Bank, whose bullion reserve was under £16 million. There is no ultimate solid ground: at every level the "reserve" is a claim on a claim, and the last level is a claim defined by statute. Suspension of the Bank Act then amounts to the deliberate creation of "an unlimited amount of fictitious, paper money capital."

Sixth, and most sobering, the principle of structural fragility at the point of maximum leverage. The bill-brokers, holding £30 million or more of callable deposits with "no cash reserve," relying on maturity roll-off or on the Bank as lender of last resort, with liabilities up to a hundred times their capital, are the emblematic institution of the credit system. They suspend in 1847, resume, suspend again in 1857 — and their leverage has meanwhile increased. The chapter's final image is not of a system that can be corrected by better policy or sounder judgement, but of a system whose normal functioning presupposes exactly the conditions that produce its periodic breakdowns.

Broader implications. For theory, the chapter supplies the conceptual instrumentation for a critique of finance that avoids two errors: the error of treating financial assets as real wealth, and the error of treating them as mere illusion with no causal efficacy. Marx's position is subtler than either. Fictitious capital is fictitious and real: it is real as a claim, real as a market with prices and movements, real as a mechanism of centralisation — but it is not real as capital, because it corresponds to no second valorisation process. For practice and policy, the chapter anticipates by well over a century the intuition that a financial system's stability depends on the liquidity of its most leveraged intermediaries and on the ultimate reserve that underwrites them — and that this ultimate reserve is itself a convention. And for method, the chapter is a model of Marx's procedure: begin with the polemical displacement of categories, proceed to the specific anatomy of the institution, expose the general mechanism by which form generates illusion, work through the extreme cases (debt, wages) to make the mechanism unmistakable, and then descend through deposits, clearing, trusts, the Bank of England, and the bill-brokers until the abstract category of fictitious capital is visible in a balance sheet with liabilities of £5,300,000 and a capital of £45,000.
