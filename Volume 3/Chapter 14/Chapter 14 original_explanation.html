Volume 3 • Chapter 14: Counteracting Influences

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 14, "Counteracting Factors," constitutes the analytical hinge of Part Three of Capital, Volume III. Its predecessor (Chapter 13) established the law of the tendential fall in the rate of profit; its successor (Chapter 15) will expound the "internal contradictions" of that law. Chapter 14 performs the indispensable mediating work between the two: it enumerates, classifies, and theorises the Gegenwirkungen — the counteracting influences — that prevent the law from manifesting itself as an immediate, mechanical decline.

The thesis is announced in the chapter's opening inversion, and it is a genuinely dialectical one. Classical political economy — Smith, Ricardo, and their predecessors — took the fall in the rate of profit as the phenomenon requiring explanation. Marx declares that, given "the enormous development in the productive powers of social labour over the last thirty years" (a footnote dates this span as 1835–65) and the "enormous mass of fixed capital" now absorbed in social production, the problem has reversed polarity: "instead of the problem that occupied previous economists, the problem of explaining the fall in the profit rate, we have the opposite problem of explaining why this fall is not greater or faster." The explanandum is therefore the slowness, not the fact, of the decline. This is the chapter's central problem, and its intellectual objective is to demonstrate that this slowness is itself a necessary result of the very same causes that produce the fall.

The second pillar of the thesis is a rigorous claim about the logical status of the law. Because counteracting forces "check and cancel" the effect of the general law, that law acquires "simply the character of a tendency, which is why we have described the fall in the general rate of profit as a tendential fall." Crucially, "tendency" here is not a probabilistic hedge nor a teleological promise. Later in the chapter Marx precisely defines its meaning: the law "operates more as a tendency, i.e. as a law whose absolute realization is held up, delayed and weakened by counteracting factors," and its "effect is decisive only under certain particular circumstances and over long periods." The observed profit rate is therefore a resultant — the vector sum of the tendency and its counter-tendencies — not the tendency itself.

The third, and philosophically most important, pillar is the identity of cause and counter-cause. Marx repeatedly insists that the factors which brake the fall are not external accidents, autonomous shocks, or political intrusions on an otherwise pure mechanism. They are generated by the same development of productive power that produces the fall. The formula recurs as a refrain: "the same factors that produce the tendency for the rate of profit to fall also moderate the realization of this tendency." This is a unity of opposites: rising productivity simultaneously (a) cheapens the elements of constant capital and raises the rate of surplus-value, and (b) raises the value-composition of capital and displaces living labour. The chapter is thus a masterclass in the logic of overdetermination, showing how a single causal process can issue in antithetical surface effects.

Structurally, the chapter delivers five enumerated counteracting factors — intensified exploitation of labour, wages forced below the value of labour-power, cheapening of constant capital, the relative surplus population, and foreign trade — followed by a general recapitulation with two clarifications, and then a supplementary sixth factor: the increase in share capital. Within the framework of Volume III as a whole, the chapter supplies the answer to the question of how the tendential law can coexist with the manifest facts of nineteenth-century capitalist prosperity, colonial super-profits, and persistently high measured profit rates. It also positions Marx decisively against Ricardo on foreign trade, and against wage-push explanations of falling profitability — a polemic whose methodological stakes ("only when the relationships that form the rate of profit have been understood will statistics be able to put forward genuine analyses of wage-rates") remain live.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Inverted Problem: Explaining Why the Fall Is Not Faster

The chapter opens with an empirical-historical observation that functions as a theoretical destabiliser. The productive powers of social labour have developed over "the last thirty years" — the footnote glosses this as 1835–65, precisely the period of the railway boom, the factory system's consolidation, and Britain's industrial hegemony — to an extent exceeding "all earlier periods" combined. Marx immediately broadens the reference beyond machinery proper to "the enormous mass of fixed capital involved in the overall process of social production," that is, to the entire value-architecture of buildings, infrastructure, and durable means of production. This is not decorative. It signals that the constant capital whose swollen proportions drive the fall is not merely the machine in the workshop but the whole social fixed-capital stock — precisely the category that reappears, strikingly, in the chapter's final section on railways.

From this observation Marx extracts the chapter's governing inversion. If constant capital has grown so colossally relative to living labour, the rate of profit (the ratio of surplus-value to total capital advanced) should have collapsed catastrophically and swiftly. That it has not is the mystery. Hence: "Counteracting influences must be at work, checking and cancelling the effect of the general law."

Two concepts require precise unpacking at this point, because the whole chapter depends on them.

First, the rate of profit. It is the ratio of the surplus-value (s) produced to the total capital advanced (C = c + v, constant plus variable capital). This differs structurally from the rate of surplus-value (s/v), which measures exploitation against variable capital alone — and, as Marx stresses later, is ultimately a ratio between two portions of living labour-time, "in actual fact on each working day individually." Because the rate of profit has in its denominator the entire capital, it is sensitive in a way the rate of surplus-value is not to the proportion in which that capital divides between dead and living labour — that is, to the organic composition of capital, the ratio c:v.

Second, the notion of a tendency. The word is doing technical work. A tendency in Marx's sense is a law of motion that is never observed in isolation; what is observed is the tendency plus its counter-tendencies. The tendential fall is thus not falsified by the empirical stability or even rise of measured profit rates — such stability is exactly what the theory predicts, given the counteracting factors enumerated here. The chapter's opening therefore sets a trap for naive empiricism: the very success of capitalism in postponing the fall is evidence for, not against, the law.

A crucial nuance appears immediately in the framing: the counteracting influences are "the most general of these factors" — the phrase conceding that the list is analytical rather than exhaustive, a catalogue of types rather than a closed inventory.

2.2 Counteracting Factor 1: The Intensification of Labour Exploitation

The first factor is the raising of the rate of surplus-value through more intense exploitation of labour, which the chapter divides along the lines already established in Volume I: prolongation of the working day (absolute surplus-value) and intensification of labour (relative surplus-value, in its intensive rather than merely technological mode).

Marx's treatment here is remarkable for its refusal to oversimplify. Intensification, he notes, is not a pure counteracting factor. "There are many aspects to the intensification of labour that involve a growth in the constant capital as against the variable, i.e. a fall in the rate of profit, such as when a single worker has to supervise a larger amount of machinery." So the very process that squeezes more surplus out of each worker simultaneously raises the capital each worker sets in motion — driving c/v upward and thus pushing the rate of profit down. Intensification is therefore internally split: it raises the rate of surplus-value and lowers the mass of surplus-value relative to the total capital advanced. Marx states the general rule with lapidary economy: "as also with most procedures that serve to produce relative surplus-value, the same reasons that bring about a rise in the rate of surplus-value can also involve a fall in its mass, taking given magnitudes of total capital applied."

He then draws a fine but vital distinction concerning the physical wear of machinery. Accelerated machine speeds consume more raw material in a given time and wear out fixed capital faster — but "as far as the fixed capital is concerned, the fact that this wears out the machines that much faster does not in any way affect the ratio of their value to the price of the labour that sets them in motion." The point is that accelerated depreciation is a change in the rate of turnover of capital value, not in the composition of the capital advanced at any moment. The value-ratio of machinery to wages is what governs the rate of profit; the speed at which that machinery is used up governs the annual rate of profit and the rhythm of replacement, a distinct issue. This is a recurring Marxian discipline: keeping the categories of composition and turnover analytically separate.

By contrast, the prolongation of the working day is characterised as the purest of the counteracting mechanisms. It "increases the amount of surplus labour appropriated without basically altering the ratio of the labour-power applied to the constant capital that this sets in motion, and which in point of fact rather reduces the constant capital in relative terms." Because the day is lengthened without adding machinery or materials proportionally, the ratio c/v falls. Prolongation is thus a counteracting factor twice over: it raises the numerator (surplus-value) directly, and enlarges the denominator's variable component relative to its constant component, raising the profit rate on both counts. Marx calls it "this discovery of modern industry" — a pointed phrase, since the lengthened day was a product of the factory system, not a pre-capitalist residue.

At this juncture comes the chapter's central theoretical disclosure. Marx identifies "the real secret of the tendential fall in the rate of profit" in the structure of relative surplus-value production itself: "the procedures for producing relative surplus-value are based, by and large, either on transforming as much as possible of a given amount of labour into surplus-value or on spending as little as possible labour in general in relation to the capital advanced; so that the same reasons that permit the level of exploitation of labour to increase make it impossible to exploit as much labour as before with the same total capital." This sentence is the pivot of the entire chapter. The secret is that the increase in exploitation per worker and the reduction in the number of workers per unit of capital are two faces of one process. Productivity growth means each worker, in a given time, processes more material with more machinery and produces more output; but it also means fewer workers are needed to work up a given capital. The rate of surplus-value rises; the mass of surplus-value producible by a given capital falls. Because the rate of profit is a ratio of mass to total capital, and mass is falling relative to capital, the rate falls — but only as a tendency, because the rising rate partially offsets the falling mass.

Marx adds female and child labour to this factor: the family as a whole now supplies capital with a greater quantity of surplus labour than before, "even if the sum of their wages increases, which is by no means always the case." The qualification matters — the counteracting effect operates through the quantity of labour-power mobilised, not through the wage bill's movement.

Two further sub-mechanisms are folded in. First, improvements in method that raise relative surplus-value "without a change in the magnitude of capital applied" — agriculture is the example: constant capital does not grow in proportion to variable, yet output rises relative to labour-power applied. Since agricultural products enter both the worker's consumption basket (cheapening the value of labour-power) and the elements of constant capital (raw materials), such improvements attack the problem from both directions. Second, the removal of "fetters": the freeing of productivity from "restraints on commerce, arbitrary restrictions, or limitations which have become irksome in the course of time, and generally from fetters of any kind," again without any initial impact on the c:v proportion. This is a pointedly political-institutional counteracting factor — free trade, deregulation, the demolition of customary restriction.

Finally, Marx addresses the innovation rent: the question whether the "temporary but ever repeated increases in surplus-value" accruing to the capitalist who adopts an invention before it is universally applied count among the counteracting factors. His answer is crisp: "This question must be answered in the affirmative." But he immediately adds a dialectical codicil — these factors "though in the final instance they always accelerate it further" inhibit the fall. The counteracting factor ultimately intensifies the tendency: the surplus profit of the pioneer is dissipated by general adoption, yet the accumulation it financed raises the social composition of capital. This forward-looking clause anticipates the identical claim Marx will make about foreign trade.

2.3 The Arithmetic of Compensation: Rate versus Mass of Surplus-Value

Having identified the mechanism, Marx subjects it to explicit quantitative analysis — one of the few passages in Volume III where the algebra is laid out almost formally. He begins from a decomposition: "The mass of surplus-value that a capital of given size produces is the product of two factors, the rate of surplus-value and the number of workers employed at this rate."

This yields the chapter's fundamental identity of the mass of surplus-value:

S = s′ × n

where S is the mass of surplus-value, s′ (= s/v, or more precisely surplus labour-time divided by necessary labour-time per worker) is the rate of surplus-value, and n is the number of workers employed at that rate. Equivalently, since the wage bill V is n times the wage per worker, S = s′ × V — the mass depends on the absolute size of variable capital and the rate of surplus-value. Marx states this equivalence explicitly.

He then poses the antinomy. "We have seen that the same factors that increase the rate of relative surplus-value lower the amount of labour-power applied on average." If S = s′ × V, and the movement of productivity raises s′ while depressing V relative to total capital C, then S's movement is indeterminate. Hence: "this effect can be greater or less, depending on the specific proportions in which this antithetical movement takes place."

Marx then clarifies with a return to the level of a capital of fixed magnitude — the abstraction, standard in Volume III, of capital = 100. "With respect to a capital of given magnitude, e.g. 100, the rate of surplus-value can grow while the average mass of surplus-value falls, since the rate is determined by the ratio in which the variable portion of the capital is valorized, while the mass is determined by the proportion that the variable capital forms in the total." This is the crux. Two quantities are being distinguished:

  • The rate of surplus-value s′ is a pure ratio internal to living labour — unpaid labour-time over paid labour-time. It is unaffected, in itself, by how much constant capital accompanies the workers.
  • The mass of surplus-value S = s′·v is mediated by the share of variable capital in the total, v/C. As c/v rises, v/C falls, and S shrinks even while s′ grows.

If we express the rate of profit in terms of these components, the underlying structure becomes transparent (a reconstruction faithful to Marx's definitions, not a formula he writes here): since p′ = s/C = s′v/C, dividing numerator and denominator by v gives

p′ = s′ / (1 + c/v)

This makes the chapter's logic algebraically legible: the rate of profit rises with the rate of surplus-value and falls with the value-composition of capital. A tendential fall requires the rise of c/v to outrun the rise of s′. Every counteracting factor in the chapter works on one or both of these variables.

Marx then supplies a concrete numerical illustration of the compensation mechanism — the worker doing the work of two, or replaceable by three. "If one worker is compelled to do work that it would really be rational for two to perform, and if this happens under circumstances in which this one worker can replace three, then one worker can now provide as much surplus labour as two did before, and to this extent the rate of surplus-value rises. But this one will not supply as much surplus labour as three did before, and this makes the mass of surplus-value fall. Its fall is compensated for or limited by the rise in the rate of surplus-value." Notice the precision: the rate rises to the level of two workers' surplus (a gain), but the mass falls short of three workers' surplus (a loss). Compensation is partial, not total. This is why the chapter speaks of the fall being "moderated or halted," never reversed.

He then extends the analysis from the individual capital to the social aggregate. "If the entire population is set to work at the increased rate of surplus-value, the mass of surplus-value rises, even though the population remains the same. Still more is this the case with a growing population; and even though this growth is linked with a relative fall in the number of workers employed, compared with the size of the total capital, the fall is still moderated or halted by the higher rate of surplus-value." Here lies the crucial distinction between the individual capital and social total capital. For the individual capitalist, the mass of surplus-value may fall; for society, employing the whole available population at a higher rate yields a larger mass. And since, taken across society, "the surplus-value it produces is the same as the profit," the social rate of profit can be maintained by the extension of exploitation across the entire workforce — the mechanism by which the counteracting factors operate at the level of the total, not the part.

Marx closes the section with a formal caution and a theorem. The caution: "the rate of surplus-value can rise, with a constant amount of capital, even though the mass of surplus-value falls — and vice versa." The theorem: "the mass of surplus-value is equal to the rate multiplied by the number of workers; but the rate is never calculated on the total capital, but only on the variable capital, in actual fact on each working day individually. Once the size of the capital value is given, however, the rate of profit can never rise or fall without a similar rise or fall in the mass of surplus-value." This last is trivially demonstrable: if p′ = S/C and C is fixed, then p′ and S must move together. The theorem's function within the argument is to forestall an error: the rate of surplus-value and the mass of surplus-value can diverge, but the rate of profit and the mass of profit cannot — wherefore any falling rate of profit necessarily entails a falling mass of profit for a given total capital, which is why the tendency is not merely a bookkeeping artefact.

2.4 Counteracting Factor 2: Wages Below the Value of Labour-Power

The second factor is dispatched in a single paragraph, and its brevity is itself the point. Marx writes: "We simply make an empirical reference to this point here, as, like many other things that might be brought in, it has nothing to do with the general analysis of capital, but has its place in an account of competition, which is not dealt with in this work." The reduction of wages below the value of labour-power is a deduction from the workers' customary subsistence — a redistribution of the wage in capital's favour that raises s′ directly and, since it lowers v's magnitude as well as its proportion, raises the rate of profit.

The theoretical scruple is significant. Marx places the systematic treatment of wage reductions under the heading of competition — the sphere of surface antagonism analysed in Volume I's discussion of the working day, but not part of the "general analysis of capital," which proceeds on the assumption that commodities (including labour-power) exchange at their values. Even so, he emphasises that this factor is "one of the most important factors in stemming the tendency for the rate of profit to fall." This creates a methodological tension that Marx is content to leave unresolved: the theory of the tendential fall is developed on the premise of value-equivalent exchange, while the most powerful counteracting factor in practice violates that premise. The chapter's own later polemic — "nothing is more absurd... than to explain the fall in the rate of profit in terms of a rise in wage rates" — cuts the other way here: wages below value slow the fall, but falling wages are not the law's cause, and rising wages are not its cause either. The causal arrow runs from productivity, not from the wage level.

2.5 Counteracting Factor 3: The Cheapening of the Elements of Constant Capital

The third factor is the most direct attack on the composition of capital: the falling value of constant capital's material elements, notwithstanding the growth of their mass. Marx explicitly cross-references "Part One of this volume" — the earlier analysis (Chapter 5) of the causes that raise the rate of profit while the rate of surplus-value remains constant, notably the economies in the use of constant capital and the cheapening of its elements through rising labour productivity.

The argument is advanced by a vivid historical comparison. "The quantity of cotton that a single European spinning operative works up in a modern factory has grown to a most colossal extent in comparison with that which a European spinner used to process with the spinning wheel. But the value of the cotton processed has not grown in the same proportion as its mass." The operative handles an enormously greater quantity of material, but the value of that material, measured against the wage, has grown far less than proportionally. The same holds for machinery and other fixed capital. Marx articulates the general principle: "the same development that raises the mass of constant capital in comparison with variable reduces the value of its elements, as a result of the higher productivity of labour, and hence prevents the value of the constant capital, even though this grows steadily, from growing in the same degree as its material volume." He adds the strong limiting case: "In certain cases, the mass of the constant capital elements may increase while their total value remains the same or even falls."

The dialectical inversion is exact and deserves emphasis: the same rise in productivity that increases the technical mass of constant capital per worker simultaneously cheapens each unit of that constant capital. Two opposing forces operate on c: its physical magnitude multiplies, its unit value diminishes. Because the rate of profit is governed by the value composition, not the physical composition, this cheapening is a powerful brake.

Marx then introduces the devaluation of existing capital — the destruction of the value of already-installed means of production by the arrival of more efficient ones (in later terminology, "moral depreciation"). This too "steadily operates to stay the fall in the rate of profit, even though in certain circumstances it may reduce the mass of profit by detracting from the mass of capital that produces profit." The caveat is important and again dialectical: devaluation can shrink the profit-producing capital base itself, so it is a two-edged instrument. But the chapter's refrain recurs: "We see here once again how the same factors that produce the tendency for the rate of profit to fall also moderate the realization of this tendency." The cheapening of constant capital is not an external rescue of the profit rate; it is the form in which the fall is produced, since the same productivity growth also swells the mass of constant capital.

2.6 Counteracting Factor 4: The Relative Surplus Population

The fourth factor is the creation of a relative surplus population — the reserve army of the unemployed, a concept developed in Volume I. Marx's framing here is explicitly circular, and he makes no apology for it: "The creation of such a surplus population is inseparable from the development of labour productivity and is accelerated by it, the same development as is expressed in the decline in the profit rate." The same productivity growth that raises c/v and depresses p′ expels living labour and thus produces a surplus population. The counteracting effect arises from the uses to which that population is put.

Marx identifies three consequences of the surplus population's existence. First, "the more the capitalist mode of production is developed in a country, the more strikingly does the relative surplus population obtrude there." Second, the availability of cheap, dismissible labour is "a reason why the more or less incomplete subordination of labour to capital persists in several branches of production, and longer indeed than would seem to correspond at first sight to the general level of development." That is, the reserve army retards the mechanisation of backward branches: why install machinery when cheap hands are plentiful? This is reinforced by "the greater resistance that many branches of production, by their nature, oppose to the transformation of manual work into machine production."

Third — and this is the analytically decisive point — new branches of production continually open up, "particularly in the field of luxury consumption, which precisely take this relative surplus population as their basis, a population often made available owing to the preponderance of constant capital in other branches of production." The composition of capital is thus uneven across the social economy: high-composition branches shed labour, and low-composition branches absorb it. These low-composition branches "base themselves in turn on a preponderance of the element of living labour, and only gradually pass through the same trajectory as other branches." Marx explicitly notes that this applies "in both cases" — both to backward branches and to newly opened luxury branches: "variable capital forms a significant proportion of the total and wages are below the average, so that both the rate and mass of surplus-value in these branches of production are unusually high."

The counteracting mechanism is then located precisely at the point of aggregation: "Now since the general rate of profit is formed by the equalization of the rates of profit in the various particular branches of production, here again the same reasons that produce the tendential fall in the rate of profit also produce a counterweight to this tendency, which paralyses its effect to a greater or lesser extent." The equalization of the general rate of profit is the mechanism by which capital flows between branches until profit rates are levelled. The persistence of branches with a high ratio of variable to constant capital — and thus unusually high s′ and rates of profit — pulls the social average upward, offsetting the tendency in the high-composition branches. This is a structural, not merely quantitative, counterweight: it depends on the heterogeneity of the social composition of capital and on the continuous regeneration of that heterogeneity by the surplus population. Note that this is also the section where Marx comes closest to a theory of the "informal sector" and of labour-intensive luxury production as functionally integrated into the reproduction of the total social capital.

2.7 Counteracting Factor 5: Foreign Trade, Surplus Profit, and the Dispute with Ricardo

The fifth factor is developed at greatest length and with the most explicit engagement with the history of political economy. Foreign trade operates on the profit rate through the same channels as factors 1 and 3, but with an external dimension. Marx writes: "In so far as foreign trade cheapens on the one hand the elements of constant capital and on the other the necessary means of subsistence into which variable capital is converted, it acts to raise the rate of profit by raising the rate of surplus-value and reducing the value of constant capital." Cheaper imported raw materials and machinery reduce c; cheaper imported foodstuffs reduce the value of labour-power, thereby raising s′. Additionally, foreign trade "permits the scale of production to be expanded," accelerating accumulation — but "it also accelerates the fall in the variable capital as against the constant, and hence the fall in the rate of profit." The duality of effect again: the same cause raises the rate now and deepens the tendency later.

Marx adds a historical-dialectical observation: "whereas the expansion of foreign trade was the basis of capitalist production in its infancy, it becomes the specific product of the capitalist mode of production as this progresses, through the inner necessity of this mode of production and its need for an ever extended market." Foreign trade is thereby not merely an environmental advantage but an internally generated requirement of capital — the imperative of the world market. He appends a sharp scholarly verdict: "(Ricardo completely overlooked this aspect of foreign trade.)" The footnote directs the reader to Chapter VII of Ricardo's Principles, "On Foreign Trade" — the locus classicus of the Ricardian claim that foreign trade benefits a country by reducing the value of its subsistence imports, but which treats this only as a static gain in the rate of surplus-value, missing the dynamic, contradictory effect on the composition of capital.

Marx then poses a further question that he declares "lies beyond the limits of our investigation": does foreign trade, and colonial trade in particular, raise the general rate of profit?

His analysis of how a higher rate of profit can arise in foreign trade proceeds in three steps.

Step one: the advanced-country price advantage. "Capital invested in foreign trade can yield a higher rate of profit, firstly, because it competes with commodities produced by other countries with less developed production facilities, so that the more advanced country sells its goods above their value, even though still more cheaply than its competitors." The apparent paradox — selling above value yet below competitors' prices — dissolves once we attend to the labour theory of value. "In so far as the labour of the more advanced country is valorized here as labour of a higher specific weight, the profit rate rises, since labour that is not paid as qualitatively higher is nevertheless sold as such." This is the theory of international value: more intensive or more productive national labour counts, on the world market, as labour of higher intensity, so a given quantity of it creates more value than the world average. The advanced country's workers produce more value per unit of labour-time, but their wages are not correspondingly higher — hence a higher rate of surplus-value, and a higher profit rate. The same relation holds with respect to the trading partner: "such a country gives more objectified labour in kind than it receives, even though it still receives the goods in question more cheaply than it could produce them itself." This is a clear statement of what later theory calls unequal exchange: a net transfer of objectified labour from the less to the more developed nation, masked by the fact that both parties gain in use-value terms.

Step two: the analogy of the innovation rent. Marx reinforces this with a comparison to the individual innovating capitalist: a manufacturer "who makes use of a new discovery before this has become general sells more cheaply than his competitors and yet still sells above the individual value of his commodity, valorizing the specifically higher productivity of the labour he employs as surplus labour. He thus realizes a surplus profit." The nation with superior production facilities occupies the same position vis-à-vis other nations as the innovating firm does vis-à-vis its competitors: its superior productivity yields a surplus profit — profit above the general rate. This links the foreign trade section back to the innovation-rent discussion under factor 1 and shows the two as instances of one logic.

Step three: the colonial case. "As far as capital invested in the colonies, etc. is concerned, however, the reason why this can yield higher rates of profit is that the profit rate is generally higher there on account of the lower degree of development, and so too is the exploitation of labour, through the use of slaves and coolies, etc." Here the mechanism is different: not superior national productivity, but the low organic composition and low level of development of the colonial economy, plus the extreme exploitation possible under slavery and indenture. This is a concentrated, historically specific statement of what later theory would call super-exploitation and colonial super-profit.

Marx then poses the aggregation question directly: "Now there is no reason why the higher rates of profit that capital invested in certain branches yields in this way, and brings home to its country of origin, should not enter into the equalization of the general rate of profit and hence raise this in due proportion, unless monopolies stand in the way." The qualifier "unless monopolies stand in the way" is the only condition. "There is in particular no reason why this should not be so when the branches of capital investment in question are subject to the laws of free competition."

At this point Marx introduces a footnote that is decisive for the chapter's polemical position: "Adam Smith is right here, as against Ricardo, who says: 'They contend, that the equality of profits will be brought about by the general rise of profits; and I am of the opinion, that the profits of the favoured trade will speedily subside to the general level.'" Marx sides with Smith. He then reconstructs Ricardo's argument fairly: "What Ricardo has in mind, on the other hand, is this: higher prices are obtained abroad; commodities are bought there and sent home in exchange; these commodities are therefore sold on the domestic market, so that the favoured spheres of production can have at most a temporary advantage over others." On Ricardo's account, the high profits of foreign trade are competed away: influx of commodities equalises prices, and the general rate returns to its former level.

Marx's rejoinder is an appeal to substance over appearance: "As soon as we take our leave of the money form, however, this semblance vanishes. The privileged country receives more labour in exchange for less, even though this difference, the excess, is pocketed by a particular class, just as in the exchange between labour and capital in general." The "money form" is the veil: in monetary terms, trade looks like an exchange of equivalents (commodity against commodity, price against price). But in labour-value terms, the advanced country receives a greater quantum of objectified labour than it gives — a real transfer, not a monetary illusion. And this excess accrues to a class, exactly as surplus-value does in the wage relation. The Ricardian equalisation is therefore not a return to the old level: "Thus in as much as the profit rate is higher because it is generally higher in the colonial country, favourable natural conditions there may enable it to go hand in hand with lower commodity prices. An equalization still takes place, but not an equalization at the old level, as Ricardo believes." The equalisation that occurs is equalisation at a higher general rate — Smith's position.

The section closes with the chapter's characteristic closing move: "But this same foreign trade develops the capitalist mode of production at home, and hence promotes a decline in variable capital as against constant, though it also produces overproduction in relation to the foreign country, so that it again has the opposite effect in the further course of development." Foreign trade counteracts the fall now; but by accelerating domestic accumulation and generating crises of overproduction in relation to export markets, it deepens the tendency in the longer run. This is the fullest statement in the chapter of the temporal structure of the counteracting factors: they are delaying mechanisms whose operation itself prepares the conditions of their reversal.

2.8 The General Summary and Two Clarifications

Before the supplementary sixth factor, Marx inserts a general recapitulation and two clarificatory points designed "to avoid any misunderstanding." The recapitulation is unqualified: "We have shown in general, therefore, how the same causes that bring about a fall in the general rate of profit provoke counter-effects that inhibit this fall, delay it and in part even paralyse it. These do not annul the law, but they weaken its effect." He then delivers the chapter's most celebrated rhetorical inversion: "If this were not the case, it would not be the fall in the general rate of profit that was incomprehensible, but rather the relative slowness of this fall." Slowness is the anomaly requiring explanation; the fall is the default. Hence: "The law operates therefore simply as a tendency, whose effect is decisive only under certain particular circumstances and over long periods."

The first clarification concerns a trap inherent in the theory: the cheapening of commodities must not be confused with the rising value-composition of capital. "The same process that leads to the cheapening of commodities as the capitalist mode of production develops leads to a change in the organic composition of the social capital applied in commodity production, and leads as a result to a fall in the profit rate." Both things happen simultaneously and from the same cause. Therefore "the reduction in the relative cost of the individual commodity, or even in the part of this cost that represents the wear and tear of the machinery, should not be confused with the rising value of the constant capital compared with the variable." A falling unit cost per commodity and a rising c:v ratio are compatible, indeed co-causal. Conversely — and this picks up factor 3 — "any reduction in the relative cost of the constant capital, with the volume of its material elements remaining the same or increasing, acts to increase the rate of profit, i.e. acts to reduce proportionately the value of the constant capital, compared with the variable capital that is applied on a scale which declines progressively." The phrase "on a scale which declines progressively" refers to variable capital's declining weight within the total: the counteracting effect consists in attenuating the value of the constant element whose relative weight is nonetheless rising.

The second clarification is the theoretical keystone of the chapter and its sharpest polemic. The fact that "an ever smaller quantity of additional living labour is objectified" in commodities — that the total living labour embodied in the commodity falls as social productivity develops — "does not affect the proportion in which the living labour contained in the commodity is divided between paid and unpaid. On the contrary. Even though the total amount of the additional living labour contained in it falls, the unpaid part still grows in proportion to the paid part, either by an absolute or a proportionate fall in this paid part; for the same mode of production that reduces the total mass of additional living labour in a commodity is accompanied by a rise in absolute and relative surplus-value." Two distinct quantities must be kept apart: the absolute quantity of living labour embedded in a commodity (which falls as productivity rises) and the division of that labour into paid and unpaid portions (which shifts in capital's favour). The tendential fall in p′ and the tendential rise in s′ are therefore not rivals; they are "particular forms."

The polemical payoff follows: "Nothing is more absurd, then, than to explain the fall in the rate of profit in terms of a rise in wage rates, even though this too may be an exceptional case." Marx thereby dismisses both the classical wage-fund-adjacent view and the proto-"profit squeeze" thesis, while conceding — "even though this too may be an exceptional case" — that rising wages can occur and can depress profitability episodically. The concession is precisely calibrated: exceptional, not law-constituting. He then states the methodological priority of theory over measurement: "Only when the relationships that form the rate of profit have been understood will statistics be able to put forward genuine analyses of wage-rates in different periods." Empiricism without theory is not merely incomplete but systematically misleading. And finally the chapter's most quoted theorem: "The profit rate does not fall because labour becomes less productive but rather because it becomes more productive. The rise in the rate of surplus-value and the fall in the rate of profit are simply particular forms that express the growing productivity of labour in capitalist terms."

2.9 Counteracting Factor 6: The Increase in Share Capital

The chapter appends a sixth and explicitly provisional factor: "The above five points can also be supplemented by the following one, though we cannot go any deeper into it at this point." As capitalist production advances, "one portion of capital is considered simply to be interest-bearing capital and is invested as such."

Marx distinguishes two senses of this, guarding against a trivial reading. It is not the sense in which a money-lending capitalist takes interest while the industrial capitalist takes entrepreneurial profit — that division "does not affect the level of the general rate of profit," because the general rate is constituted by the sum of all these revenue forms taken together: "profit = interest + profit of all kinds + ground-rent, its distribution between these particular categories being a matter of indifference." Whatever the split among claimants, the total is the same.

The substantive sense is different. Some capitals, "although invested in large productive enterprises, simply yield an interest, great or small, after all costs are deducted — so-called 'dividends'." Railways are the example. The crucial claim: "These do not therefore enter into the equalization of the general rate of profit, since they yield a profit rate less than the average. If they did go in, the average rate would fall much lower." Here Marx isolates a systematic understatement of the tendency. Large fixed-capital enterprises, precisely because their c:v ratio is extreme, earn rates of profit below the social average; but because they are funded as joint-stock investments paying a fixed dividend, their low profitability is excluded from the pool over which equalisation operates. The observed general rate of profit is therefore biased upward relative to the true social average. Marx draws the consequence: "From a theoretical point of view, it is possible to include them, and we should then obtain a profit rate lower than that which apparently exists and is really decisive for the capitalists, since it is precisely in these undertakings that the proportion of constant capital to variable is at its greatest."

The final clause — "really decisive for the capitalists" — is disconcerting and deliberate: the rate that is statistically apparent differs from the rate that is theoretically true, and Marx acknowledges that the apparent rate, however biased, is the one that actually governs capitalist behaviour and expectations. Two facts about modern capitalism are thus identified in a single paragraph: the tendency of large-scale enterprise to fall out of the equalising mechanism, and the systematic divergence between measured and true average profitability. Both are consequences of the very development that produces the fall.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter's first and deepest lesson is the identity of a law with its own counter-forces. Marx does not treat the counteracting factors as exogenous shocks — better technology, cheaper imports, political deregulation — that happen to arrive and soften a pre-existing tendency. He treats them as emanations of the same productivity dynamic that generates the fall. Rising productivity cheapens constant capital and raises the rate of surplus-value (counteracting); and it raises c/v and displaces living labour (tendency-producing). The "law" is therefore not a simple vector but a self-inhibiting process. This is why Marx can insist simultaneously that the counteracting factors "in the final instance... always accelerate it further" and that they "in part even paralyse it." The systemic principle here is that of the resultant: what is empirically observed is the net of opposed forces, and the theory's proper object is not the observed rate but the law that generates both the movement and its inhibition.

The second principle is the non-identity of rate and mass, and the subordination of both to the value-composition of capital. The chapter demonstrates rigorously that the rate of surplus-value can rise while the mass of surplus-value falls (S = s′·v, with v/C declining); that the rate of profit cannot, however, fall without the mass of profit falling for a given total capital (since p′ = S/C); and that the tendential fall in p′ coexists with a tendential rise in s′. Both movements are expressions of growing productivity. This dissolves a persistent pseudo-problem — how a rising rate of exploitation can accompany a falling profit rate — by showing that it is not a paradox but a necessity of the categories themselves.

The third lesson is methodological: theory precedes measurement. Marx's declaration that only once "the relationships that form the rate of profit have been understood" can statistics deliver "genuine analyses of wage-rates" is a general epistemic protocol. The flat dismissal of wage-push explanations of falling profitability — "nothing is more absurd" — is accompanied by the concession that such explanations may describe an "exceptional case." Marx thus distinguishes the lawful from the episodic without denying the latter's existence.

The chapter's broader implications radiate in three directions. For crisis theory, the fact that counteracting factors "in the final instance accelerate" the tendency integrates the long-run law with cyclical crisis. For the theory of the world market and imperialism, the foreign trade excursus supplies a fully internal Marxian account of unequal exchange, colonial super-profits, and the material basis of a privileged strata within the capitalist class — grounded in the labour theory of value and explicitly siding with Smith against Ricardo. The passage on "labour of higher specific weight," the "excess pocketed by a particular class," and the higher profit rates in the colonies is among the most structurally significant in Volume III. For the analysis of modern corporate capital, the sixth factor identifies in nuce the exclusion of large joint-stock enterprise from the equalisation process and the resulting upward bias of measured profitability — a phenomenon of obvious contemporary resonance in the era of gigafactories, utilities, and infrastructure funds.

Finally, the chapter bequeaths a philosophical caution: the tendency "is decisive only under certain particular circumstances and over long periods." Neither deterministic collapse nor empirical refutation follows from this. The law is a statement about the direction of gravity of a system that is perpetually being lifted by its own momentum. To confuse the tendency with a prediction is to repeat the error Marx diagnoses in classical political economy: mistaking the surface result for the underlying law — and thereby finding the fall, rather than its slowness, the mystery.
