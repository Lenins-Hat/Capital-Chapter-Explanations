Volume 3 • Chapter 22: Division of Profit. Rate of Interest

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 22 of Capital Volume 3, edited by Engels from Marx's manuscripts, is a compact but theoretically pivotal investigation into the form of interest-bearing capital. Its central problem is deceptively simple: interest is, by definition, merely a part of profit—a deduction the industrial capitalist pays to the money capitalist for the use of borrowed capital. Yet in the everyday functioning of the capitalist economy, interest appears as something entirely independent: a price, a uniform rate, a daily fact quoted in the financial press. How does a mere aliquot part of surplus-value acquire an autonomous existence, a "natural" appearance, and a life of its own? That is the chapter's guiding question.

Marx's answer unfolds through a double movement. First, he establishes the quantitative relation between interest and profit: the average rate of profit sets the upper limit of interest, and—given a fixed proportion—interest rises and falls with the general profit rate. This yields a striking corollary: because the rate of profit tends to fall with the development of capitalist production, the rate of interest tends, on this basis, to fall with industrial development. Second, Marx demonstrates that the actual level of interest—its average or "natural" rate—cannot be derived from any general law whatsoever. It is determined by competition between lenders and borrowers, by custom and legal tradition, by the supply of loanable capital generated by a growing rentier class, and by the credit system. There is, therefore, "no natural rate of interest" in the sense in which economists speak of a natural rate of profit or a natural rate of wages.

The chapter's deepest theoretical claim, signalled in Engels's inserted manuscript note (footnote 62), is methodological: interest represents the case in which a purely quantitative division of surplus-value generates a qualitative distinction. This is the exact inverse of the relation between surplus-value and wages (or between profit and rent), where a qualitative distinction between independent variables—labour-power and capital, capital and landed property—underlies a quantitative division. In the case of interest, the same capital functions only once and produces profit only once; the distinction between "capital-as-loan" and "capital-in-function" is a distinction between two titles to the same surplus-value, not between two productive agencies.

This formal analysis matters far beyond the chapter itself. It prepares the ground for the later treatment of profit of enterprise and wages of superintendence, for the theory of the credit system, and, most importantly, for the critique of the "trinity formula" in which capital appears as a self-expanding entity bearing fruit by nature. Interest-bearing capital (M—M′) is the most fetishised form of capital, the form in which capital appears to generate value without any mediating production process. Chapter 22 explains why this illusion arises and what determines the magnitude of the payment—thereby providing the conceptual scaffolding for the entire theory of credit and fictitious capital that follows. It is, in short, the chapter in which the appearance of interest as an independent revenue is shown to be the necessary form of appearance of a distributional relation grounded in the mere ownership of money capital.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Scope Problem: What This Chapter Refuses to Investigate

Marx opens with an explicit act of theoretical self-limitation. The chapter's object "cannot be investigated in detail," he warns, and he proceeds to list three crucial exclusions. First, the competition between lenders and borrowers and the resulting short-term fluctuations of the money market fall outside the scope of the discussion. Second, the circuit which the rate of interest describes during the industrial cycle can only be dealt with after the cycle itself has been analysed—and the cycle, too, is bracketed. Third, the approximate equalization of the rate of interest on the world market is postponed. What remains, and what constitutes the chapter's positive content, is narrow but decisive: "the independent form of interest-bearing capital and the way that interest acquires autonomy vis-à-vis profit."

This is a formal determination chapter, not a theory of finance. Marx is isolating a single question: how does a portion of surplus-value come to appear, and to function economically, as a price paid for a commodity? The exclusions are not evasions; they are methodological safeguards ensuring that the analysis of form is not contaminated by the analysis of fluctuation. The industrial cycle, the money market, and the world market will return in later chapters; here they function only as illustrations of a structural point.

2.2 The Limits of Interest: A Determinate Maximum, an Indeterminate Minimum

Since interest is simply a part of profit that the industrial capitalist must pay to the money capitalist, "the maximum limit of interest would seem to be the profit itself"—in which case the functioning capitalist's share would be zero. Marx refines this: leaving aside special cases in which interest actually exceeds profit (so that it cannot all be paid out of profit), the maximum limit is the whole profit minus that part reducible to "wages of superintendence," a category "to be developed later." This forward reference is important: Marx is already anticipating the analysis of Chapter 23, in which the industrial capitalist's supervisory function is shown to be partly a real function and partly an ideological disguise of his claim on surplus-value.

The minimum limit, by contrast, "is completely indeterminate. It could fall to any level, however low. But countervailing circumstances constantly enter to raise it above this relative minimum." Here we encounter the chapter's governing asymmetry: interest has a ceiling fixed by the profit rate, but no floor derivable from any economic law. The "countervailing circumstances"—custom, legal tradition, competition, the institutional habits of the money market—are not laws but empirical stabilisers.

Marx then cites two authorities to establish the general framework. The Economist (22 January 1853) states that "the rate of interest depends (1) on the rate of profit; (2) on the proportion in which the entire profit is divided between the lender and borrower." Massie (whose Essay on the Governing Causes of the Natural Rate of Interest, 1750, Marx repeatedly invokes) insists that "if that which men pay as interest for the use of what they borrow, be a part of the profits it is capable of producing, this interest must always be governed by those profits." These citations establish the two variables that structurate the entire chapter: the magnitude of profit and the division of that profit.

2.3 The Fixed-Proportion Hypothesis and Its Numerical Demonstration

Marx proceeds by assuming, as a heuristic, a fixed ratio between total profit and the portion paid as interest. Under this assumption, "the interest will rise or fall with the total profit," which is itself determined by the general rate of profit and its fluctuations. He supplies a series of concrete illustrations. If the average profit rate is 20 per cent and interest is one quarter of the profit, the interest rate is 5 per cent; if profit is 16 per cent, interest is 4 per cent. Varying the interest rate within a given profit rate produces a correspondingly varied industrial profit: with a 20 per cent profit rate, interest could rise to 8 per cent while the industrial capitalist still retains the same 12 per cent he would enjoy under a 16 per cent profit rate combined with a 4 per cent interest rate. If interest were only 6 or 7 per cent, he would in fact retain a greater share.

The crucial proposition follows: "if interest is a constant proportion of the average profit, it will follow that the higher the general rate of profit, the greater is the absolute difference between total profit and interest, and the greater therefore the total profit that accrues to the functioning capitalist, and vice versa." Marx's numerical series makes this vivid. Taking interest as one-fifth of the average profit:

  • one-fifth of 10 = 2; the difference between total profit and interest = 8
  • one-fifth of 20 = 4; the difference = 20 − 4 = 16
  • one-fifth of 25 = 5; the difference = 25 − 5 = 20
  • one-fifth of 30 = 6; the difference = 30 − 6 = 24
  • one-fifth of 35 = 7; the difference = 35 − 7 = 28

The interest rates of 4, 5, 6 and 7 per cent are here always one-fifth—i.e. 20 per cent—of the total profit. The mathematical point is that an unaltered proportional share of a growing magnitude yields a growing absolute residual. Hence: "With interest such a constant proportion, the industrial profit (the difference between the total profit and the interest) would be greater, the higher the general rate of profit, and vice versa."

This establishes what might be called the magnitude dependence of interest: interest is not an autonomous quantity but a function of the profit mass. All else equal—the ratio between interest and total profit being more or less constant—"the functioning capitalist will be able and willing to pay a higher or lower interest in direct proportion to the level of his profit rate." Massie's footnote (61) codifies this: "The natural rate of interest is governed by the profits of trade to particulars."

2.4 The Inverse Relation Between Interest and Industrial Development

The next move is one of Marx's boldest inferences. Since the level of the profit rate stands in inverse proportion to the development of capitalist production—the famous tendency of the rate of profit to fall—it follows that "the higher or lower rate of interest in a country stands in the same inverse proportion to the level of industrial development, particularly in so far as the variation in the rate of interest expresses an actual variation in the profit rate." Richer, more industrially developed countries should exhibit, other things equal, lower interest rates.

Marx immediately qualifies this with characteristic caution: "We shall see later on that this need by no means always be the case." The qualification is essential. Interest may vary independently of profit for reasons Marx will develop in the same chapter (the growth of rentiers, the credit system) and in later chapters (the world market, the state of the balance of payments, hoarding and dishoarding). Nonetheless, in this restricted sense, "interest is governed by profit, and more precisely by the general rate of profit. And this kind of regulation applies even to its average." The average rate of profit is therefore "ultimately determining the maximum limit of the interest."

2.5 Two Distinct Orders of Determination

Marx now sharpens the conceptual distinction that underlies the whole chapter. Where a given whole is to be divided in two, the first thing that matters is the size of the whole—and the magnitude of profit is determined by the average rate of profit. Once that rate is given (say on a capital of 100), interest "evidently varies in inverse proportion to the part of profit that remains to the functioning capitalist who operates with borrowed capital." But—and this is the decisive theoretical claim—"the circumstances that determine the magnitude of the profit to be divided, the value product of unpaid labour, are very different from those that determine its distribution among these two kinds of capitalist, and often operate in completely opposite directions."

Here Engels inserts a manuscript note (footnote 62) that illuminates the chapter's architecture: "The course of this chapter suggests it is better, before the laws of distribution of profit are investigated, to develop first of all the way in which the quantitative division becomes a qualitative one. No more is needed to make the transition from the previous chapter to this point, than to take interest as a portion of profit that is not yet determined more specifically." This is the methodological hinge of the chapter. The movement "from quantity to quality" is precisely what turns a share of profit into a distinct economic category—a price, a revenue, an autonomous magnitude.

2.6 The Industrial Cycle and the Barometric Behaviour of Interest

Marx returns to the industrial cycle he had initially bracketed, not to analyse it but to register the regularity of interest's behaviour within it. The cycle he names runs: "inactivity, growing animation, prosperity, overproduction, crash, stagnation, inactivity, etc." Within this cycle, three patterns stand out. First, "a low level of interest generally corresponds to periods of prosperity or especially high profit." Second, "a rise in interest comes between prosperity and its collapse." Third, "maximum interest up to extreme usury corresponds to a period of crisis." Gilbart's four-period schema (footnote 63) codifies the sequence: after pressure, money is abundant without speculation; then abundant with speculation; then demand for money rises as speculation declines; finally money is scarce and "a pressure arrives."

The empirical evidence is drawn from the 1840s. From summer 1843 there was marked prosperity. The rate of interest, which in spring 1842 was still 4½ per cent, fell in spring and summer 1843 to 2 per cent; in September 1843 it even fell to 1½ per cent (Gilbart). During the crisis of 1847 it rose to 8 per cent and more. But Marx immediately complicates the picture: "Yet low interest can also be accompanied by stagnation, and a moderate rise in interest by growing animation." The correspondence is not law-like; it is a tendency.

Tooke's explanation (footnote 64) is given for the low rates of the early 1840s: "the accumulation of surplus-capital necessarily accompanying the scarcity of profitable employment for it in previous years, by the release of hoards, and by the revival of confidence in commercial prospects." This is a supply-side explanation, and it already anticipates the two causes of the secular fall of interest developed below.

A crucial footnote (66) warns against reading a low interest rate as an index of sound conditions: "Since the rate of interest is determined by and large by the average profit rate, extraordinary swindling can very often go together with a low rate of interest. For example the railway swindle of summer 1844. The Bank of England's interest rate was only raised to 3 per cent on 16 October 1844." Low interest can coexist with fraud and speculative mania—an observation that will have great resonance in the later analysis of fictitious capital.

Marx then describes the crisis mechanism in which interest peaks. The rate of interest "reaches its highest level during crises, when people have to borrow in order to pay, no matter what the cost." Here interest is determined not by profitability but by the need for means of payment. And a rise in interest corresponds to a fall in the price of securities: "this is at the same time a very suitable opportunity for people with available money capital to buy up such interest-bearing securities at ridiculously low prices, and in the regular course of events these securities are bound to reach at least their average price again as soon as the interest rate falls." Footnote 65 gives the brutal illustration: a banker refused a loan on a £200,000 bond; the customer was about to announce suspension; "the banker would buy the bond at £150,000." The chapter thus records, in embryo, the speculative dynamic that turns crisis into a transfer of wealth to holders of ready money—a theme Marx will develop in the theory of the credit system.

2.7 The Secular Tendency of the Interest Rate to Fall

Marx now identifies "a tendency for the rate of interest to fall, quite independently of fluctuations in the rate of profit." Two major reasons are given.

(1) The growth of the rentier class (Ramsay). The argument, quoted at length from Ramsay's Essay on the Distribution of Wealth (pp. 201–2), is that even if capital were "never borrowed with any view but to productive employment," interest might vary without any change in the rate of gross profits. As a nation advances in wealth, "a class of men springs up and increases more and more, who by the labours of their ancestors find themselves in the possession of funds sufficiently ample to afford a handsome maintenance from the interest alone." Others, active in youth and middle age, retire to live quietly on the interest of accumulated sums. This class increases with national riches, since those who begin with a tolerable stock reach independence sooner than those who begin with little. Consequently, "in old and rich countries, the amount of national capital belonging to those who are unwilling to take the trouble of employing it themselves, bears a larger proportion to the whole productive stock of the society, than in newly settled and poorer districts." Ramsay concludes with the decisive identity: "As the class of rentiers increases, so also does that of lenders of capital, for they are one and the same." The supply of loanable capital grows faster than the demand arising from productive employment, pressing the rate of interest downward.

(2) The development of the credit system. Marx writes: "the ever growing control this gives industrialists and merchants over the monetary savings of all classes of society through the mediation of the bankers, as well as the progressive concentration of these savings on a mass scale, so that they can function as money capital, must also press down the rate of interest." He defers elaboration—"More on this later"—but the point is structural. The credit system socialises idle savings, converting scattered hoards into mass loanable capital. By expanding the supply of loan capital, it depresses its price.

2.8 Ramsay's Determinants and the Limits of Competition

Marx next quotes Ramsay's more general proposition: the interest rate "depends partly upon the rate of gross profits, partly on the proportion in which these are separated into profits of capital and those of enterprise. This proportion again depends upon the competition between the lenders of capital and the borrowers; which competition is influenced, though by no means entirely regulated, by the rate of gross profit expected to be realized." Two reasons are given why competition is not exclusively regulated by expected gross profit: "on the one hand many borrow without any view to productive employment; and, on the other, because the proportion of the whole capital to be lent, varies with the riches of the country independently of any change in gross profits." The first point encompasses consumption credit, speculative borrowing, and borrowing to meet payments (as in crises); the second restates the rentier/supply argument.

Marx then proposes the empirical method by which the average rate of interest might be ascertained: "(1) the average interest rate as it varies over the major industrial cycles; (2) the rate of interest in those investments where capital is lent for longer periods." The implication is that the average rate is a statistical and historical construct, not a theoretical magnitude derivable from first principles.

2.9 There Is No "Natural" Rate of Interest

Here the chapter reaches its central negative thesis: "The prevailing average rate of interest in a country, as distinct from the constantly fluctuating market rate, cannot be determined by any law. There is no natural rate of interest, therefore, in the sense that economists speak of a natural rate of profit and a natural rate of wages." Massie's formulation is quoted as fundamentally correct: the only question is "what proportion of these profits do of right belong to the borrower, and what to the lender; and this there is no other method of determining than by the opinions of borrowers and lenders in general; for right and wrong, in this respect, are only what common consent makes so."

Marx then launches a sustained methodological polemic against supply-and-demand explanations. "The coincidence of demand and supply means nothing at all here, even taking the average rate of profit as given." Where the supply-and-demand formula is legitimately used elsewhere (and "this is then correct for practical purposes"), it functions as a formula for finding the regulating limits or limiting quantities that are independent of competition and in fact determine it. It allows those caught up in competition to form some idea—"even if still a superficial one"—of the inner connection of economic relations. It is, in short, "a method of getting from the variations that accompany competition to the limits of these variations."

But this does not hold for the average rate of interest. "There is no reason at all why the average conditions of competition, of equilibrium between lender and borrower, should give the lender an interest of 3, 4, 5 per cent, etc. on his capital, or alternatively a certain percentage, 20 per cent or 50 per cent, of the gross profit." Where competition as such decides, "the determination is inherently accidental, purely empirical, and only pedantry or fantasy can seek to present this accident as something necessary."

Marx supports this with two polemical targets. Opdyke, in his Treatise on Political Economy (New York, 1851), made "a most unsuccessful attempt to explain the general phenomenon of a 5 per cent rate of interest in terms of eternal laws." But the more naive is Karl Arnd, in Die naturgemässe Volkswirthschaft (Hanau, 1845), who proposed what Marx derisively christens the "primordial forest rate of interest": the notion that the rate of interest is regulated by "the ratio by which the volume of timber in European forests increases through its annual growth," namely 3 or 4 to 100. Marx's sarcasm is withering—"(how curious of the trees to arrange their growth independently of their exchange-value!)"—and he recommends the author to posterity as the "philosopher of the dog tax." The point is serious: attempts to naturalise interest are absurd because interest has no natural magnitude.

Marx also mocks the parliamentary reports of 1857 and 1858, in which Bank of England directors, London and country bankers, and professional theorists "chatter back and forth about the 'real rate produced'," trading platitudes such as "the price paid for the use of loanable capital should vary with the supply of such capital" and "a high rate and a low profit cannot permanently exist." These are tautologies dressed as laws.

Custom and legal tradition are then admitted as genuine determinants: "Custom, legal tradition, etc. are just as much involved in the determination of the average rate of interest as is competition itself, in so far as this average rate exists not only as an average number but as an actual magnitude. An average interest must be assumed in many legal contexts where interest has to be reckoned."

The theoretical reason why no law can fix the limits of the average rate is then given: "the answer simply lies in the nature of interest. It is merely a part of the average profit. The same capital appears in a double capacity, as capital for loan in the hands of the lender, and as industrial or commercial capital in the hands of the functioning capitalist. But it functions only once, and produces profit only once. In the production process itself, the character of capital as loan capital does not play any role." The division between the two claimants is therefore "a purely empirical fact, pertaining to the realm of chance, just as the respective shares in the common profit of a business partnership are distributed among its various members."

The contrast with other divisions is decisive. In the division between surplus-value and wages, "two quite different elements are involved, labour-power and capital. It is the functions of two independent variables which set limits to one another, and the quantitative division of the value produced emerges from their qualitative distinction." The same structure governs the division of surplus-value between rent and profit. But "with interest, there is nothing of that kind. Here, on the contrary, the qualitative distinction proceeds from the purely quantitative division of the same piece of surplus-value." This is the chapter's deepest theoretical claim, and it justifies Engels's manuscript note about the transition from quantity to quality.

A related footnote (68) notes that the Bank of England raises and lowers its discount rate according to the inflow and outflow of gold, "though always of course with regard to the rate prevailing in the open market," and that "gambling in discounts, by anticipation of the alterations in the bank-rate, has now become half the trade of the great heads of the money centre"—i.e. the London money market.

2.10 Interest as a Uniform, Palpable Magnitude: The Price Analogy

The conclusion of the "no natural rate" argument contains a striking paradox. Although the average or middling rate of interest "cannot be given limits by a general law," the rate of interest itself—"whether it is the average rate or the market rate of the time"—nonetheless "appears as something quite different from the general rate of profit, as a uniform, definite and palpable magnitude." Steuart's observation (footnote 69) is cited: "The price of money, which we designate by the term interest, has a greater stability and uniformity than that of any other thing," because cash money is always of the same value, or assumed to be so.

Marx formalises the analogy: "The rate of interest is related to the profit rate in a similar way as the market price of a commodity is to its value." Insofar as the interest rate is determined by the profit rate, "this is always through the general rate of profit and not through the specific profit rates that may prevail in particular branches of industry, still less by the extra profit that the individual capitalist might make in a particular sphere of business." Massie's footnote (70) is invoked to show that the division of profits applies to lenders and borrowers in general: "remarkably great and small gains are the reward of skill and the want of understanding, which lenders have nothing at all to do with; for as they will not suffer by the one, they ought not to benefit by the other." The lender who charges the common rate of interest is entitled to no more than that rate, whatever profits the borrower makes with the money—a principle that underlines the lender's abstraction from the productive employment of capital.

Marx acknowledges that the interest rate differs according to the class of security provided by borrowers and the duration of the loan: "but for each of these categories, it is uniform at a given moment of time. This distinction, therefore, does not militate against the fixed and uniform character of the rate of interest." Footnote 71 (added by Engels) supplies a striking empirical snapshot of the London money market on 9 December 1889: the bank rate stood at 5 per cent; the market rate of discount on 60-day drafts at 3½ per cent; on 3-month drafts 3¼ per cent; on 6-month drafts 3⅜ per cent; loans to bill-brokers day-to-day at 1–2 per cent; for one week at 3 per cent; last rate for the fortnight on loans to stockbrokers at 4½–5 per cent; deposit allowance (banks) at 3½ per cent; and (discount houses) at 3–3½ per cent. "The minimum here is 1 per cent and the maximum 5 per cent"—on one and the same day. The point: sharp differentiation by category coexists with a single, quotable, uniform rate per category.

The average rate of interest is constant over long periods "because the general rate of profit changes only in the long run—despite constant change in the particular rates of profit, a change in one sphere being offset by an opposite change in another." The relative constancy of the profit rate is reflected in the relative constancy of the average rate of interest.

The market rate is fixed at any given moment "just like the market price of commodities, because on the money market all capital for loan confronts the functioning capital as an overall mass; i.e. the relationship between the supply of loan capital on the one hand, and the demand for it on the other, is what determines the market level of interest at any given time." This is all the more true, the more the development and concentration of the credit system gives loan capital "a general social character, and puts it on the money market all at once, simultaneously."

By contrast, the general rate of profit "only ever exists as a tendency, as a movement of equalization between particular rates of profit." The competition between capitalists consists in "their withdrawing capital bit by bit from those spheres where profit is below the average for a long period, and similarly injecting it bit by bit into spheres where it is above this." There is "a constant variation in the injection and withdrawal of capital vis-à-vis these various spheres, never a simultaneous effect on a mass scale as with the determination of the interest rate." The epistemic asymmetry is thus rooted in a real structural difference: interest is fixed by a market confrontation occurring en masse and simultaneously; the general profit rate is a resultant of countless dispersed, staggered movements.

2.11 Interest-Bearing Capital as a Commodity Sui Generis

Marx now draws the theoretical conclusion from the preceding analysis. Interest-bearing capital, although "a category absolutely different from the commodity," becomes "a commodity sui generis with interest as its price," and this price, like the market price of any commodity, "is fixed at any given time by demand and supply." The market rate of interest, "though in constant flux, thus appears at any given moment as every bit as fixed and uniform as the momentary market price of any commodity. The money capitalists supply this commodity, and the functioning capitalists buy it; they constitute the demand for it."

At the same time, Marx insists on the disanalogy: "This process of fixing the rate by supply and demand does not apply to the equalization that produces the general rate of profit." The equalisation of commodity prices to prices of production operates through "the expansion or contraction of production, i.e. an increase or decrease in the quantity of commodities that these industrial capitals put on the market, mediated by the immigration or emigration of capital with respect to these particular spheres of production." It "never appears and never can appear as if industrial or commercial capital as such were a commodity vis-à-vis a buyer, in the way that interest-bearing capital is." It appears only in the fluctuations that reduce market prices to production prices—"not as the direct establishment of an average profit."

The determinants of the general rate of profit are then enumerated systematically: "(1) by the surplus-value that the total capital produces; (2) by the ratio of this surplus-value to the value of the total capital; and (3) by competition, but only in so far as this is the movement through which the capitals invested in particular spheres of production seek to draw equal dividends from this surplus-value in proportion to their relative size." Hence: "The general rate of profit, in other words, obtains its determination in quite different and far more complicated ways than does the market rate of interest, which is directly and immediately determined by the relation of supply and demand, and it is therefore not a palpable and given fact in the way that the interest rate is."

The particular profit rates in the various spheres are "themselves more or less uncertain; but in so far as they do show themselves, it is not their uniformity that is apparent but rather their variation. The general rate of profit itself simply appears as the minimum limit of profit, not as an empirical and directly visible form of the actual profit rate." One crucial detail: the general profit rate appears as a minimum limit. This is because competition equalises such that capitals in any sphere will not permanently accept less than the general rate; the rate functions as a floor below which capital emigrates, not as an immediate description of what each capital actually realises.

2.12 Two Factors Favouring the Consolidation of the Interest Rate

Marx isolates two factors that, "in stressing this distinction between the interest rate and the profit rate," he had "so far left aside." Both help explain why interest wears such a solid, traditional, palpable appearance.

(1) The historical pre-existence of interest-bearing capital and the existence of a general rate of interest handed down by tradition. Interest-bearing capital is older than capitalist production proper. It existed in antiquity (usurer's capital) and in the medieval and early modern periods. This historical precedence means that a general rate of interest can be transmitted as a customary datum, quite independently of the current profit rate.

(2) The far stronger direct influence that the world market exerts on the establishment of the interest rate, "independently of the conditions of production in a country, as compared with its influence on the profit rate." The interest rate is directly sensitive to international movements of gold, exchange rates, and capital flows, whereas the profit rate remains anchored in national conditions of production. This gives interest an autonomy and international uniformity the profit rate lacks.

2.13 The Epistemic Asymmetry and the Money Market as Class Capital

Marx now summarises the cognitive asymmetry. "Average profit does not appear as a directly given fact, but rather as the end-product of an equalization of opposing tendencies that can only be established by investigation. With the interest rate it is different. Where it is a universal governing rule, which occurs at least locally, it is a fact fixed every day, a fact that even serves industrial and commercial capital as a presupposition and postulate in their operating calculations. It becomes a general property of any sum of £100 that it will yield 2, 3, 4, 5 per cent."

The metaphor is memorable: "Meteorological reports do not show more precisely the level of the barometer and thermometer than do stock-market reports the level of the interest rate, not for this capital or that, but rather for the generality of loan capital to be found on the money market."

The reason for this precision lies in the nature of the money market. "On the money market it is only lenders and borrowers who face one another. The commodity has the same form, money. All particular forms of capital, arising from its investment in particular spheres of production or circulation, are obliterated here. It exists in the undifferentiated, self-identical form of independent value, of money. Competition between particular spheres now ceases; they are all thrown together as borrowers of money, and capital confronts them all in a form still indifferent to the specific manner and mode of its application."

This produces a remarkable theoretical result: "Here capital really does emerge, in the pressure of its demand and supply, as the common capital of the class, whereas industrial capital appears like this only in the movement and competition between the particular spheres." On the money market, capital attains the form of class capital—capital abstracted from all particular employments, confronting the capitalist class as a whole in its money form. Money capital "really does possess the form in which it is distributed as a common element among these various spheres, among the capitalist class, quite irrespective of its particular application, according to the production requirements of each particular sphere."

This abstraction is further institutionalised with the development of large-scale industry: "money capital emerges more and more, in so far as it appears on the market, as not represented by the individual capitalist, the proprietor of this or that fraction of the mass of capital on the market, but rather as a concentrated and organized mass, placed under the control of the bankers as representatives of the social capital in a quite different manner to real production." Consequently, "as far as the form of demand goes, capital for loan is faced with the entire weight of a class, while, as far as supply goes, it itself appears en masse as loan capital." The chapter thus articulates, in compressed form, the theoretical foundation of the later analysis of bankers' capital, credit, and the socialisation of capital through the credit system. The money market is where capital becomes really social—not through production relations but through ownership in its most abstract form.

2.14 The Blurred Profit Rate and the Sharp Interest Rate; the Closing Note on Credit

Marx concludes with a restatement of the epistemic contrast. "These are some of the reasons why the general rate of profit presents a blurred and hazy picture compared with the sharply defined rate of interest, which although its level fluctuates always confronts the borrowers as fixed and given, because it fluctuates in the same way for them all."

Two analogies emphasise the point. First, "changes in the value of money do not prevent it from having the same value in relation to every commodity, and market prices of commodities fluctuate daily, although this does not prevent them from being noted every day in the reports." Second, "It is just the same with the rate of interest, which is noted just as regularly as the 'price of money.'" The reason: "capital itself is offered here as a commodity in the money form. The establishment of its price is therefore the establishment of its market price, just as with all other commodities; and so the rate of interest presents itself always as a general rate of interest, as so much for so much money, as quantitatively determined."

By contrast, "The rate of profit... can vary even within the same sphere, given the same market price, according to the different conditions in which individual capitals produce the same commodity; for the profit rate on an individual capital is not determined simply by the market price of the commodity, but rather by the difference between market price and cost price. And these various rates of profit, firstly within the same sphere and then within the various different spheres, can be equalized only through constant fluctuations." Profit rates are individual, dispersed, dependent on cost conditions, and equalised only through ceaseless fluctuation; interest is uniform, quotable, and simultaneous.

Marx closes the chapter with an unfinished note, marked "(Note for later elaboration.) A particular form of credit." Three points are sketched. (1) "We know that when money functions as means of payment instead of means of purchase, the commodity is alienated first and its value realized only later. If payment takes place only after the commodity has been re-sold, this sale does not appear as a consequence of the purchase, but rather it is by the sale that the purchase is realized. Sale, in other words, becomes a means of purchase." (2) "certificates of debt, bills, etc. become means of payment for the creditor." (3) "money is replaced by the settlement of outstanding debt certificates." These three observations sketch the germ of the credit system: the metamorphosis of the commodity that creates the seller as creditor, the circulation of debt certificates as means of payment, and the netting out of mutual debts that economises the use of money. They are the bridge to the later analysis of commercial credit, bank credit, and the credit system as a whole—an analysis Marx never fully completed.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Chapter 22 establishes a set of fundamental principles whose reach extends far beyond the technical question of the interest rate. The first is the primacy of the quantitative division in producing a qualitative distinction. In most economic distributions—surplus-value/wages, profit/rent—a qualitative difference between independent variables (labour-power vs. capital; capital vs. landed property) underlies and limits the quantitative split. In the case of interest, the reverse holds: the same capital functions once, produces profit once, and only afterwards is divided between two titles to it—the lender's and the functioning capitalist's. From this purely quantitative division emerges the qualitative appearance of interest as an independent revenue, a price, a thing. The chapter's theoretical punchline is precisely this inversion. It explains why interest can appear as a "natural" magnitude even though no law determines its level: because the division itself is arbitrary and empirical, the arbitrary result can congeal into the appearance of an objective price.

The second principle is the reality of appearance. The absence of a natural rate does not mean that interest is not experienced as fixed and given. On the contrary, the interest rate is the most palpable magnitude in the whole economic system: quoted daily, uniform across the class of borrowers, presupposed in every operating calculation, used in legal contexts as a fixed datum. Marx's contrast with the general rate of profit—the latter a mere tendency, an abstraction of investigation, a blurred resultant—shows that epistemic clarity and theoretical determinacy can diverge sharply. The more determinate, quantifiable, and palpable a form appears, the less it is actually determined by general laws. The chapter thereby offers a general methodological lesson: apparent precision may be the mask of substantive indeterminacy.

Third, the chapter identifies the dual determination of interest. Its maximum is set by the general rate of profit (through the profit mass to be divided); its actual level, in any given period, is set by competition between lenders and borrowers, by custom and legal tradition, by the supply of loanable capital generated by rentiers and the credit system, and by world-market conditions. This dual determination explains both the regularity of interest behaviour across the industrial cycle and its independence from profit in the long run. It also explains why the tendency of the rate of profit to fall may or may not be reflected in the interest rate—an argument Marx carefully qualifies.

Fourth, the chapter develops a secular theory of the falling interest rate grounded in the growth of a rentier class and in the socialisation of savings by the credit system. This supply-side theory complements, and in principle can counteract, the profit-based determination. It anticipates modern theories of the declining "rate of return on capital" arising from accumulation without commensurate investment opportunities, and it grounds them in class structure and institutional development rather than in technological or psychological factors.

Fifth, the chapter prepares the theory of fictitious capital and crisis. The observations on securities losing value during crises, on bankers' speculative purchases at knockdown prices, and on low interest rates coexisting with fraud ("the railway swindle of summer 1844") point forward to Marx's mature account of credit cycles, in which interest spikes, asset prices collapse, and wealth transfers from debtors to holders of liquidity.

The broader implications are both critical and constructive. Critically, the chapter demolishes every attempt to naturalise interest: the "eternal laws" of Opdyke, the "primordial forest rate" of Arnd, and the bankers' platitudes of the parliamentary reports are all shown to substitute tautology and metaphor for analysis. Interest has no natural magnitude because it is a distributional fact rooted in property titles, not a production coefficient. Constructively, the chapter lays the foundation for the theory of the credit system, the analysis of profit of enterprise, and—ultimately—the critique of the trinity formula in which capital appears as a self-generating source of revenue. In interest-bearing capital (M—M′), capital appears to generate money without any mediation by production: the money capitalist lends, and money returns augmented. This is the most fetishised form of capital, and the most ideologically potent. Chapter 22's demonstration that interest is only a part of profit, and that its level is empirically and institutionally determined, is the first essential step in dissolving that fetish—showing that what appears as a natural property of a thing (capital bearing interest) is in fact a social relation of distribution resting on the class division between owners of money capital and functioning capitalists, and ultimately on the exploitation of unpaid labour. The autonomy of interest is real as an appearance and as a practical datum, but it is an autonomy grounded in—and subordinated to—the fundamental relation between capital and labour. That is Marx's lasting lesson in this deceptively compact chapter.
