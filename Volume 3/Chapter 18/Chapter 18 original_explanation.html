Volume 3 • Chapter 18: The Turnover of Merchant's Capital. Prices

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 18 of Capital Volume 3 occupies a pivotal position in Marx's unfolding theory of the "transformation of commodity capital and money capital" into commercial capital (Kaufmannskapital). Its central problem is deceptively narrow and yet structurally enormous: what does "turnover" mean when the capital that turns over is not industrial but merely commercial, and what consequences follow from that difference for the formation of prices and the general rate of profit?

The chapter's core thesis can be stated in three interlocking propositions.

First, the turnover of commercial capital is not analogous to the turnover of industrial capital in kind, only in appearance. Industrial capital's turnover is the unity of production time and circulation time and therefore embraces the entire reproduction process — production, sale, purchase, and the resumption of production on a renewed scale. Commercial capital's turnover, by contrast, is "nothing but the movement of commodity capital that has become autonomous," and represents only the first phase of the commodity metamorphosis, C–M, read from the merchant's standpoint as the circuit M–C–M′. The merchant buys, sells, buys again — a movement that is externally repeated without ever passing through production.

Second, this asymmetry produces an illusion in the consciousness of the agents of circulation — and in the heads of bourgeois economists — that circulation as such determines price. Because the merchant's per-unit profit mark-up depends inversely on how many times his money capital turns over in a year ("small profits and quick returns"), it appears as though price is fixed by the merchant's arbitrary decision, or by the velocity of trade, rather than by the labour-time embodied in commodities and the general rate of profit. Marx's task is to dissolve that appearance and reduce it to the inner law.

Third, the real determination runs the other way. The merchant's two limits — the production price of the commodity (which he does not control) and the average rate of profit (which he also does not control) — are both given to him externally. All he chooses is whether to deal in expensive or cheap commodities. Turnover affects only the distribution of an already-determined aliquot mass of profit across commodity units, not the magnitude of that profit. Hence Marx's central theorem: the commercial price supplement stands in inverse proportion to the number of turnovers of the commercial capital in the branch concerned, and directly in proportion to the turnover time.

The chapter therefore serves as the epistemological hinge of Volume 3. It is where Marx shows why the "visible and merely apparent movement" of capitalist society necessarily generates upside-down ideas in the minds of merchants, stock-jobbers, bankers, and even manufacturers — and why "it is one of the tasks of science to reduce the visible and merely apparent movement to the actual inner movement." It also discharges a structural debt: having established in Part Two how the general rate of profit arises from surplus-value produced by industrial capital, and having in Chapter 17 introduced commercial capital as a deduction from that mass of profit, Marx must now show how turnover-time differences between branches of trade modulate commercial prices without in any way disturbing the law of value. The chapter thus closes the circle by demonstrating that the very phenomenon most hostile to the labour theory of value — the apparent arbitrariness of trading prices — is fully deducible from it.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Founding Distinction: Industrial Turnover as Totality, Commercial Turnover as Fragment

Marx opens with a definitional contrast that governs everything that follows. The turnover of industrial capital is "the unity of its production and circulation times and consequently embraces the entire production process." Turnover here is not merely repetition; it is the completed circuit M–C … P … C′–M′, in which money is transformed into means of production and labour-power, value is produced, and the enlarged result is realized. Note carefully that Marx's phrase is "unity" — production time and circulation time are not merely added together but fused into a single periodicity that includes the renewal of production itself.

The turnover of commercial capital is categorically different. It is "nothing but the movement of commodity capital that has become autonomous." Here Marx activates a distinction he constructed across Volume 2 and the preceding chapters of Volume 3: commodity capital (C′) is the form the finished product takes as it leaves the industrial enterprise and enters the market; when a specialized fraction of the total social capital takes on the function of buying and selling that commodity capital on its own account, commodity capital "becomes autonomous" as commercial capital. What the merchant turns over is therefore a single phase of industrial capital's circuit — the C–M phase (sale) — detached and made into a self-standing professional routine. From the merchant's own point of view, this routine presents itself as a complete circuit: M–C, then C–M, endlessly repeated: buy, sell, buy, sell. It is a turnover of capital in that the same money returns to its starting point enlarged — but it is a turnover that never leaves the circulation sphere.

This is the interpretive key to the chapter's title. Because the merchant's circuit excludes production, "prices" behave in his hands in a manner that is formally unlike the price behaviour of industrial capital — a difference that, if left unanalysed, produces the appearance of arbitrariness.

2.2 The Asymmetry of Metamorphosis: C₁–M–C₂ versus M–C–M′, and the £100 Example

Marx then sharpens the contrast through a meticulous analysis of what actually swaps hands.

Within the circulation sphere, industrial capital's metamorphosis "always presents itself as C₁–M–C₂." The commodity produced (C₁) is sold for money (M); that money is then used to buy new means of production (C₂). Crucially, Marx notes, this is "in fact an exchange of C₁ and C₂," and "the same money therefore changes hands twice." Money here is a pure intermediary: its movement mediates the exchange of two different kinds of commodities — the output of one production process and the inputs of the next. The money is not the destination; it is the vehicle. In the language of Volume 1, money functions as means of circulation.

In the merchant's circuit M–C–M′, the situation is inverted: "it is the same commodity that changes hands twice." The merchant's money buys a commodity, and that same commodity is resold — its second change of hands being the mechanism by which the merchant's money refluxes to him. Nothing is exchanged between two productive uses; there is no C₁ for C₂ substitution. The whole movement "simply mediates the reflux to him of his money." This is the precise formal locus of the merchant's function: he does not transform matter, he transfers title.

Marx illustrates with a deliberately round example: a merchant's capital of £100 used to buy commodities for £100 and later sold for £110 has "made his capital of £100 turn over once." The number of turnovers per year depends on how often M–C–M′ is repeated. From this simple arithmetic, two things must be held apart — and Marx here inserts a crucial methodological bracketing: "We are completely leaving aside here the costs that may be involved in the difference between the purchase price and the sale price, since these costs in no way affect the form we are initially concerned to analyse." These costs are designated K (costs of circulation, i.e., the portion of capital the merchant advances besides the sum laid out in purchasing commodities). By setting K aside, Marx isolates the form of the commercial circuit — the pure buy-cheap-sell-dear movement — before reintroducing complications. (He returns to this bracketing explicitly at 2.8 below, where he also sets aside ΔK, the additional profit earned on this additional capital, and defends the procedure as "the strictly logical and mathematically correct way of looking at things.")

2.3 Money as Means of Circulation: The Analogy, the Break, and the Credit System

Marx next draws an analogy that he immediately qualifies — a characteristic dialectical move. The number of turnovers of a given commercial capital is "completely analogous here with the repeated circuits of money as a simple means of circulation." Just as "the same shilling circulating ten times buys ten times its value in commodities," so the same merchant's capital of £100 "buys ten times its value in commodities, or realizes a total commodity capital of ten times its value, £1,000." The mechanism of velocity is the same: a given quantum of circulating medium can service a much larger volume of transactions if it moves faster, so that velocity substitutes for quantity.

But then the break, which is the point of the analogy: "with the circulation of money as means of circulation, the same piece of money passes through different hands… In the merchant's case, however, the same money capital… repeatedly returns to the same owner as M + ΔM." This is what "characterizes its turnover as a turnover of capital" — the defining feature is not mere velocity but reflux to the same owner as value plus surplus-value. "It always withdraws more money from circulation than it puts in."

Marx then anticipates the credit system: "as the turnover of commercial capital accelerates (and this is also where the function of money as means of payment predominates, with the development of the credit system), the same quantity of money also circulates more quickly." This is a compressed but important forward reference. Means of payment — as opposed to means of circulation — is the monetary function appropriate to deferred settlement (bills of exchange, credit sales). Where trade runs on credit, the physical money need not accompany each transaction; debts are netted and settled later, so a smaller money stock can support a faster and larger turnover. But precisely because settlement is deferred, the whole system becomes vulnerable to any interruption in the reflux — a vulnerability that becomes the material basis of the crisis mechanism described in 2.5.

2.4 What Turnover Expresses: Reproduction versus Repetition, and the Two Limits

Here Marx delivers the conceptual heart of the chapter: what does the repetition mean in each case?

"The repeated turnover of commercial capital, however, is never anything more than a repetition of buying and selling; whereas the repeated turnover of industrial capital expresses the periodicity and renewal of the entire reproduction process (including the process of consumption)." For industrial capital, the completion of a circuit is a material event: commodities have been produced and consumed productively, the means of production have been replaced, and production can resume on a possibly expanded scale. For commercial capital, the completion of a circuit is a commercial event with no material content of its own.

From this follows a dependency that Marx states bluntly: "For commercial capital, on the contrary, this is simply an external condition." The merchant's rapid turnover is possible only if industrial capital keeps "constantly put[ting] commodities on the market and withdraw[ing] them from it again." If the reproduction process is generally slow, so is the turnover of commercial capital. Hence Marx derives the first limit: commercial capital "certainly facilitates the turnover of productive capital; but it only does this in so far as it cuts down the latter's circulation time." It "has no direct effect on the production time, which also forms a barrier to the turnover time of industrial capital. This is the first limit to the turnover of commercial capital."

This is a crucial distinction. Industrial capital's turnover time is production time + circulation time. Commercial capital can attack only the second term: by taking over the selling function, by holding inventories, by having a dense network of correspondents, the merchant shortens the interval during which the manufacturer's capital lies frozen in unsold product. But no commercial arrangement shortens the time the baker's dough must rise, the crop must grow, or the wine must age. That residual is the hard limit.

The second limit is on the demand side: "quite apart from the barrier formed by reproductive consumption, this turnover is decisively restricted by the speed and volume of total individual consumption, since the overall part of the commodity capital that goes into the consumption fund depends on this." Reproductive consumption means the purchase of means of production by capitals (Department I buying from Department I and II); individual consumption means purchases by workers and capitalists for personal use (the destination of Department II output). The merchant's ability to keep turning his capital over is ultimately capped by how fast and how much people and firms actually consume — i.e., by the volume of final demand, which is itself anchored in the distribution of value and the prevailing wage.

2.5 Autonomy, Elasticity, Crisis, and the Location of Breakdown

Marx now introduces the chapter's most dialectically charged passage — the theory of how commercial capital's relative independence becomes the mechanism of generalized crisis.

He first brackets a distinct phenomenon: "the turnovers within the world of commerce, where one merchant after the other sells the same commodity, a kind of circulation which may present a very flourishing appearance in periods of speculation." This is the chain of resales between middlemen — pure circulation of titles, generating the appearance of vigorous business while nothing has reached a consumer. It is speculative circulation, and Marx sets it aside to isolate the structural point.

The structural point is threefold:

  1. Commercial capital abbreviates the C–M phase for productive capital — it shortens the manufacturer's circulation time (as established above).
  2. Under the modern credit system, it commands a large part of society's total money capital, so that "it can repeat its purchases before it has definitively sold what it has already bought." This is the critical mechanism. The merchant does not need his own realized sales to fund his next purchase; the banking system supplies the means. "In this connection it is immaterial whether our merchant has sold directly to the final consumer or whether there are twelve other merchants between the two."
  3. The reproduction process is "tremendously elastic" — "it can always be driven beyond any given barrier." Given credit, and given the physical and organizational flexibility of industry, the merchant "finds no barrier in production itself, or only a very elastic one."

From these three elements Marx derives the dynamic: "Besides the separation of C–M and M–C, which follows from the nature of the commodity, an active demand is now therefore created." That phrase — "an active demand is now therefore created" — is the pivot. The commercial credit system does not merely transmit demand; it manufactures it. Merchants order goods not because consumers are buying but because credit is cheap and expectations are buoyant, and this ordering itself registers as demand upon industrial capital, which responds by expanding output and employment, which in turn puts more wages and revenue into circulation, which seems to confirm the demand that generated it.

Now the dialectical formula, arguably the most quoted sentence of the chapter: "Despite the autonomy it has acquired, the movement of commercial capital is never anything more than the movement of industrial capital within the circulation sphere. But by virtue of this autonomy, its movement is within certain limits independent of the reproduction process and its barriers, and hence it also drives this process beyond its own barriers. This inner dependence in combination with external autonomy drives commercial capital to a point where the inner connection is forcibly re-established by way of a crisis."

Three terms must be held precisely: - Inner dependence: commercial capital's movement is ontologically parasitic on industrial capital — it can only sell what has been produced, and can only be repaid out of final sales. - External autonomy: it appears and operates as a self-standing business with its own rhythms, its own credit lines, its own profit calculations, and its own capacity to commit funds independently of realized sales. - Forcible re-establishment by way of crisis: the latent contradiction is resolved — never smoothly, always violently — when the accumulation of unsold goods and unpaid debts makes it impossible to continue pretending that circulation can outrun production.

This yields Marx's famous observational claim about the location of crises: "crises do not first break out and are not first apparent in the retail trade, which bears on immediate consumption, but rather in the sphere of wholesale trade, as well as banking, which places the money capital of the entire society at the wholesalers' disposal." The retail trade's turnover is anchored directly to daily purchases, so it cannot stretch as far. Wholesale trade, with its long supply chains and credit-financed inventories, is where the gap between apparent demand and real demand first becomes untenable — and banking is where it becomes financially fatal.

2.6 The Imperceptible Point: From Prosperity to Crash

Marx now narrates the concrete genesis of a crisis — the most empirically textured passage in the chapter.

He traces a chain: "The manufacturer may actually sell to the exporter, and the exporter to his foreign customer; the importer may sell his raw materials to the manufacturer, and the manufacturer sell his products to the wholesaler, etc." Along this chain, "at some particular imperceptible point the commodity lies unsold; or else the total stocks of producers and middlemen gradually become too high."

The paradox is that precisely then "consumption is generally at flood tide." Why? Two reasons given: "partly because one industrial capitalist sets a series of others in motion" — the expansion propagates through the inter-industry demand network, as Department I's orders for raw materials and machinery call forth production in other branches — "partly because the workers these employ, being fully occupied, have more than usual to spend." And: "The capitalists' expenditure increases with their revenue." So the boom is self-intensifying: expanded production generates expanded wages and profits, which generate expanded consumption, which justifies expanded production.

Marx then adds a structurally important amplifier, referring back to Volume 2, Part Three (pp. 498–501, 505–9): "a constant circulation between one constant capital and another (even leaving aside the accelerated accumulation) which is initially independent of individual consumption." This is the circulation within Department I — iron to steel to machinery to iron, so to speak. This inter-constant-capital circulation "is initially independent of individual consumption in so far as it never goes into this even though it is ultimately limited by it, for production of constant capital takes place never for its own sake but simply because more of it is needed in those spheres of production whose products do go into individual consumption."

The phrase "initially independent… ultimately limited" is the whole point. The inter-capital trade in constant capital can sustain itself for a long while on its own momentum, generating brisk business "stimulated by prospective demand," with "business proceeds very briskly, as far as both merchants and industrialists are concerned." But this is a circuit feeding on itself; its ultimate justification is the consumer goods sector, and if that sector's demand is not actually growing commensurately, the whole edifice is financing itself against a promise.

Then the moment of truth: "The crisis occurs as soon as the returns of these merchants who sell far afield (or who have accumulated stocks at home) become so slow and sparse that the banks press for payment for commodities bought, or bills fall due before any resale takes place. And then we have the crash, putting a sudden end to the apparent prosperity."

Note the precise mechanism. The trigger is not a collapse of value or a sudden material catastrophe. It is a timing mismatch: bills (credit instruments with fixed maturities) come due before the goods they financed have been resold. The merchant's turnover time exceeds the repayment period the credit system granted him. Banks, having advanced against that turnover, demand settlement. He cannot sell fast enough; he cannot pay; the failure propagates along the chain of mutual debts — and the "apparent prosperity" collapses all at once. This is why crises in Marx's account are characteristically abrupt, why they appear as financial events even when rooted in production, and why Marx insists on the credit system as an essential structural feature rather than an incidental one.

2.7 One Commercial Capital, Many Industrial Capitals: Mediating Opposing Phases

Marx then deepens the account of commercial capital's "superficial and irrational character": "the turnover of the same commercial capital can mediate the turnovers of very different productive capitals at the same time or in succession." A single wholesaler's £10,000 of revolving capital may successively fund the sale of a cotton spinner's output, a weaver's output, and a bleacher's service — three distinct production processes, each with its own turnover period, all serviced by the same revolving commercial fund, staggered in time.

The same merchant's capital, moreover, can mediate opposing phases of the metamorphosis. Marx gives a concrete example: "The merchant may for instance buy linen from the manufacturer and sell it to the bleacher. Here, therefore, the turnover of the same merchant's capital — in actual fact the same C–M, the realization of the linen — represents two opposite phases for two different industrial capitals." For the weaver, the merchant's purchase is the weaver's sale (C–M, realization); for the bleacher, the merchant's sale is the bleacher's purchase (M–C, the acquisition of raw material). "If the merchant sells for productive consumption, his C–M represents the M–C of one industrial capital and his M–C the C–M of another."

The consequence is that the merchant's own accounting — the single turnover M–C–M′ he experiences — is a composite of two opposite movements in two different industrial circuits. His "turnover" therefore has no one-to-one relationship to any single productive process, which is precisely why his turnover can appear to have a life of its own and why from his standpoint "turnover itself seems to determine price." The structural interlocking of many industrial circuits in one commercial circuit is the material ground of the epistemic illusion Marx will diagnose later.

2.8 The Exclusion of K and ΔK: The Methodological Rationale

Marx now justifies, with unusual self-consciousness, a simplification he has been operating under. He has been analysing the commercial circuit while leaving aside K, "the costs of circulation, i.e. the portion of capital that the merchant advances besides the sum laid out on the purchase of commodities." These are the merchant's real ancillary outlays — warehousing, transport, clerks' wages, insurance, shop fittings, bookkeeping.

He reasons that if K is excluded, then ΔK — the additional profit the merchant makes on this additional capital — must also be excluded. And he defends this as "the strictly logical and mathematically correct way of looking at things, if it is a question of seeing how profit and turnover of commercial capital affect prices." The justification is decomposability: the effect of pure purchase-and-resale turnover on price can be isolated from the effect of circulation-cost capital only if the latter is temporarily zeroed out. Once the pure case is established, the complication can be added without disturbing the analysis of the pure case — because K, at the level of the general rate of profit, earns the same average rate as any other capital advanced. This is a modelling decision in the classical sense: strip the phenomenon to its essential skeleton, then readmit qualifications.

2.9 The Sugar Example: Production Price and the Aliquot Part of Commercial Profit

Marx now constructs the chapter's key numerical demonstration. (Note: production price = cost price + average profit, the price at which commodities must sell if industrial capitals of differing organic composition are to earn the same general rate of profit; aliquot part = the proportional share, i.e., the per-unit slice of a total.)

Case 1. "If the production price of 1 lb. of sugar is £1, with £100 the merchant can buy 100 lb. of sugar. If this is the amount he buys and sells in the course of a year, and the average annual rate of profit is 15 per cent, he will add £15 to this £100, and 3 shillings to each £1, the production price of 1 lb. He will thus sell the sugar at £1 3s. a lb." The arithmetic is exact: 15% of £1 = 3 shillings.

Case 2. Now suppose the production price of sugar falls to 1 shilling per lb. With the same £100 the merchant now buys 2,000 lb. His annual profit on the £100 remains £15, distributed across 2,000 units instead of 100 — so the per-unit addition becomes 1.8d (the text renders it as a small fraction of a penny; the exact figure implied by £15 ÷ 2,000 lb = 0.15s = 1.8d). He sells each lb. at just over 1 shilling.

Marx draws the theoretical conclusion with precision: "The level of the price of production, whether high or low, has nothing to do with the profit rate; but it has a decisive effect on the aliquot part of the sale price of each 1 lb. of sugar that goes to form commercial profit; i.e. the addition to the price that the merchant makes on a certain quantity of commodity (product)." A footnote clarifies that Marx means here the absolute profit per lb., not the relative profit. This clarification matters enormously, because the relative mark-up (profit as a share of the purchase price) is 15% in both cases — the same annual rate. What changes is the absolute per-unit figure: 3 shillings versus roughly 1.8 pence. The rate is invariant; the per-unit expression varies by a factor of twenty.

Marx generalizes: "If the production price of a commodity is low, so is the sum that the merchant advances in its purchase price, i.e. for a given quantity, and so too, at a given rate of profit, is the amount of profit he makes on a given quantity of this cheaper commodity. Alternatively, and this comes to the same thing, he can buy a larger amount of this cheaper commodity with a given capital, of e.g. £100, and the overall profit of £15 which he makes on his £100 is then distributed in small fractions over the individual portions of this mass of commodities. And vice versa."

And he grounds the whole thing in productivity: "This depends completely on the higher or lower productivity of the industrial capital whose commodities he trades in." The merchant's per-unit profit margin is a downstream reflection of the productivity of the industrial capital upstream of him.

2.10 The Merchant's Two Limits, the Dutch East India Company, and the Four Sources of Illusion

Marx now turns to demolishing the "prevailing conception" that the merchant chooses between high-margin/low-volume and low-margin/high-volume strategies. Setting aside cases of monopoly — "where the merchant is a monopolist and also monopolizes production, as was the case with the Dutch East India Company in its day" — he declares: "nothing could be more ridiculous than the prevailing conception that it depends on the merchant whether he wants to sell many commodities at a low profit on the individual commodity, or a few commodities at a high profit."

The reason is structural: "The two limits to his sale price are, on the one hand, the production price of the commodity, which he has no control over; and on the other hand the average rate of profit, which he has no control over either." The merchant's only decision is which branch to enter — "whether he wants to deal in expensive commodities or in cheap ones" — and even that is conditioned by the size of his available capital and by other circumstances. "The attitude of the merchant therefore depends entirely on the degree of development of the capitalist mode of production and not on his own will."

The Dutch East India Company serves as the historical exemplar of the opposite: "as a purely commercial company having a monopoly of production, [it] imagined it could still pursue, under completely changed conditions, a method that corresponded at most to the beginnings of capitalist production." Its extraordinary margins were a monopoly rent on production, not a commercial strategy; once general capitalist production developed, that position became untenable.

Marx then identifies four sources of the popular prejudice — a prejudice which, "like all wrong ideas about profit, etc., arises from taking the viewpoint of trade alone and from commercial preconceptions":

  1. Phenomena of competition among merchants themselves — "which pertain simply to the division of commercial profit among the individual merchants, the shareholders in the total commercial capital; e.g. when one merchant sells more cheaply than another, so as to drive his competitor from the field." What is observed is a redistribution within commercial capital, but it is misread as a determination of price.
  2. Naive theoretical confusion, exemplified by "an economist of Professor Roscher's calibre," who "can still imagine, in Leipzig, that it was reasons of 'good sense and humanity' that produced the change in sale prices, and that this was not the result of a revolution in the actual mode of production." (Marx cites Roscher's Die Grundlagen der Nationalökonomie, 3rd edn., 1858, p. 192.) The sarcasm is aimed at the reduction of an objective economic transformation to moral sentiment.
  3. A genuine empirical co-movement misread as causation: "if production prices fall as a result of increases in the productivity of labour, and if sale prices therefore fall as well, then demand often rises still more quickly than supply, and with it market prices, so that the sale prices yield more than the average profit." Falling prices and rising demand occur together — but the sequence runs productivity → production price → sale price, not merchant decision → price → demand.
  4. A real mercantile practice: "a merchant may reduce the sale price (and this means nothing but a reduction in the standard profit that he adds to the price), in order to turn over a larger capital more quickly in his business." Marx grants that this occurs, but insists it belongs to the analysis of competition among merchants, not to the determination of value.

He concludes: "All these are matters that pertain simply to competition among the merchants themselves."

A footnote to this passage quotes Corbet, An Inquiry into the Causes, etc. of the Wealth of Individuals (London, 1841), p. 20: "Profit, on the general principle, is always the same, whatever be price; keeping its place like an incumbent body on the swelling or sinking tide. As, therefore, prices rise, a tradesman raises price; as prices fall, a tradesman lowers price." Marx's editorial gloss is careful: attention is paid only to ordinary trade, not to speculation, whose examination "lies outside the ambit of our discussion, together with everything that pertains to the division of commercial capital." Corbet's second proposition (p. 128) is that "the profit of trade is a value added to capital which is independent of price, the second" — i.e., speculation — "is founded on the variation in the value of capital or in price itself." The distinction between ordinary trade profit (independent of price level) and speculative gain (derived from price variation) is exactly the line Marx draws.

2.11 Recapitulation from Volume 1: Price Level, Surplus-Value, and the Historical Fall of Commercial Prices

Marx now reaches back to Volume 1, Chapter 17, to re-establish the foundations. The claim: "a high or low level of commodity prices determines neither the mass of surplus-value that a given capital produces nor the rate of surplus-value." What the price level does reflect is the quantity of labour objectified: "if only a little labour is objectified in many commodities, the price of the individual commodity will be low and so will be the surplus-value contained in it."

But the crucial analytical separation is this: "how the labour embodied in a commodity is divided into paid and unpaid labour, and what proportion of this price thus represents surplus-value, has nothing to do with this total amount of labour, i.e. with the price of the commodity." In other words: - Absolute surplus-value per unit depends on the total labour embodied (hence on productivity) and on the length/intensity of the working day; - Rate of surplus-value depends on the ratio of unpaid to paid labour — a relative magnitude.

Hence: "The rate can be high even though the absolute amount of surplus-value in each individual commodity is small." And: "This absolute amount of surplus-value in each individual commodity depends in the first place on the productivity of labour and only secondly on its division between paid and unpaid." Marx states this ordering explicitly and it is essential: productivity governs the absolute magnitude; the paid/unpaid split governs its decomposition. A cheap commodity may carry a tiny absolute surplus-value and still exhibit a high rate of exploitation.

Then the pivot to commerce: "As far as the commercial sale price is concerned, the production price is a given external assumption."

From this, Marx offers a historical account of why commercial commodity prices were high in earlier periods, with two causes: 1. High production prices consequent on the low productivity of labour — each commodity embodied more labour-time, hence cost more, hence the commercial capital required to carry a given physical quantity was larger. 2. The absence of a general rate of profit — "since commercial capital drew a far higher proportion of the surplus-value than would accrue to it in conditions of general mobility of capital." Where capital could not move freely between branches, commercial capital could sustain a rate of profit well above what equalization would permit. (This is the historical-theoretical ground of the Dutch East India Company case.)

"The cessation of this situation is… in both respects the result of the development of the capitalist mode of production." Rising productivity lowers production prices; capital mobility establishes a general rate of profit that disciplines commercial margins.

Marx then notes the empirical variation of turnover: "Turnovers of commercial capital are longer or shorter in various branches of trade, and the number of turnovers in the year thus more or less. Within the same branch of trade, the turnover is quicker or slower in different phases of the economic cycle. There is however an average number of turnovers, which is discovered by experience." This empirical average is the quantity that will enter the price determination in 2.14.

2.12 The Two Capitals Compared: Turnover and the Formation of the General Rate of Profit

Marx now consolidates the contrast between the two capitals with respect to profit determination — the analytical summit of the chapter.

Industrial capital. "Its turnover expresses on the one hand the periodicity of reproduction and depends therefore on the amount of commodities that are put on the market in a certain period of time. On the other hand, the circulation time also forms a limit, even if an extendable one, which may have a more or less constricting effect on the formation of value and surplus-value through its effect on the scale of the production process." The logic: a faster turnover means a given capital can pass through more production cycles per year, each cycle extracting surplus-value; so more surplus-value is produced annually. "Thus the turnover exerts its determining function on the mass of surplus-value annually produced, and hence on the formation of the general rate of profit, not as a positive factor but rather as a constricting one." The phrase is precise: turnover time does not create surplus-value; being a barrier, a reduction in it removes a restriction, permitting more surplus-value to be produced. It operates negatively, as the removal of a drag.

Commercial capital. "The average rate of profit, on the other hand, is a given magnitude as far as commercial capital is concerned. Commercial capital does not have a direct effect on the creation of profit or surplus-value and it enters as a determining element into the formation of the general rate of profit only in so far as it draws its dividends from the mass of profit that industrial capital produces, according to the proportion that it forms in the total capital."

Two consequences follow, and they must be distinguished:

(i) "The greater the number of turnovers made by an industrial capital… the greater is the mass of profit that it forms." Marx concedes the general-rate-of-profit objection and answers it: "it is true that the establishment of a general rate of profit means that this total profit is divided among the various capitals not according to the ratio in which they directly participate in its production, but rather according to the aliquot parts that they form in the total capital, i.e. in proportion to their size. But this does not alter the essence of the question." Why not? Because a greater mass of total profit, given a given total capital, still means a higher general rate of profit. The equalization rule only governs distribution; it does not abolish the effect of turnover on the magnitude of the divisible pool.

(ii) "It is different with commercial capital. Here the rate of profit is a given magnitude, determined on the one hand by the mass of profit that industrial capital produces and on the other by the relative size of the overall commercial capital, by its quantitative proportion in the total capital advanced in the production and circulation process." Commercial capital therefore has no causal role in producing profit — it receives a share. Its rate is a function of (a) the surplus-value pool and (b) its own share of the total capital.

2.13 The Relative Size of Commercial Capital and the Historical Tendencies of Its Development

But the merchant's turnover number does matter — via the size of the commercial capital required. "The number of its turnovers, however, has a determining effect on its relationship to the total capital, or the relative size of the commercial capital needed for circulation, in that it is evident that the absolute size of the commercial capital required stands in inverse proportion to the speed of its turnover."

Marx's worked illustration: suppose the total capital is £10,000; then if commercial capital is one-tenth of it, that is £1,000. If the total capital is £1,000, one-tenth is £100. So far the relative magnitude is constant (one-tenth) while the absolute magnitude varies with the total. Now let turnover change. "Given a rapid turnover, its absolute size may be £1,000… in the first case, £100 in the second case, so that its relative size is one-tenth. With a slower turnover, its absolute size may be £2,000 in the first case and £200 in the second. Its relative magnitude would have grown from one-tenth of the total capital to one-fifth."

So the causal chain is: turnover speed → absolute commercial capital required → relative share of total capital → share of the surplus-value pool deducted from industrial capital's profit → the general rate of profit. Marx draws the policy-relevant conclusion: "Circumstances that shorten the average turnover of commercial capital, such as the development of means of transport, for example, reduce in the same proportion the absolute magnitude of this commercial capital and hence raise the general rate of profit. And vice versa." Railways, steamships, telegraphs, and improved roads do not produce surplus-value, but by shrinking the commercial capital that must be advanced they reduce the deduction from the surplus-value pool and thus raise the general rate of profit. This is a striking and important structural result: improvements in circulation raise the rate of profit even though they are unproductive of value.

Marx then states the double tendency of developed capitalism upon commercial capital:

"the same amount of commodities are turned over with a smaller amount of actually functioning commodity capital; while on account of the more rapid turnover of this commercial capital and the greater speed of the reproduction process on which it depends, the ratio of commercial capital to industrial capital is reduced."

But the counter-tendency: "with the development of the capitalist mode of production all production becomes commodity production, and hence the whole of the product comes into the hands of agents of circulation." In earlier modes, "quite apart from the mass of products that were directly consumed in kind by the producers themselves and the mass of services that were performed in kind too, a very large proportion of the producers sold their commodities directly to their consumers or worked to their personal orders." (Marx is describing the natural-economy residue — direct consumption, payment in kind, bespoke production for known customers.)

He then lays out three numbered propositions about commercial capital in earlier vs. later modes:

(1) In earlier modes commercial capital is smaller in absolute terms but relatively greater. It is absolutely smaller "because an incomparably smaller part of the entire product is produced as a commodity, has to go into circulation as commodity capital, and comes into the hands of merchants." It is relatively greater (i) because of its slower turnover — more capital locked up per unit of goods sold; and (ii) because "the price of this mass of commodities, and also therefore the commercial capital that has to be advanced for it, is greater as a result of the lower productivity of labour compared with capitalist production, so that the same value is expressed in a smaller amount of commodities." Lower productivity → higher unit prices → more money capital needed to carry the same physical stock.

(2) Under the capitalist mode, a greater mass of commodities is produced (with lower value per unit, which must be taken into account), and "the same mass of products, e.g. of corn, forms a greater mass of commodities; i.e. more and more of it comes into commerce." Subsistence goods that once never entered the market now do. The consequence: "not only does the mass of commercial capital grow, but so too does that of all the capital invested in circulation, e.g. in shipping, railways, telegraphs, etc." — the entire circulation-infrastructure apparatus.

(3) Non-functioning or semi-functioning commercial capital also grows with capitalist development — "with the increased ease of entry into the retail trade, with speculation and a surplus of unoccupied capital." Marx flags this as an aspect "to be discussed when we come to 'Competition among Capitals'" — a projected study he never completed. A footnote records: "In 1865, when the manuscript of Volume 3 was written, Marx evidently still intended to devote a special study to the phenomena of competition. See above, pp. 10–11, and the Introduction to the Pelican Marx Library edition of Capital Volume 1, pp. 27–8." This is an important textual marker of the work's incompleteness. The substantive content of (3) is that a portion of social capital is absorbed into retail, speculation, and idle reservoirs — capital that neither produces surplus-value nor efficiently circulates it, and which therefore operates as a drag on the general rate of profit.

2.14 The Central Theorem: The Price Supplement in Inverse Proportion to Turnover

Marx now reaches the proposition toward which the entire argument has been building.

His framing: "taking the magnitude of the commercial capital in relation to the total capital as given, the variations in turnover between various branches of commerce do not affect the total profit that accrues to the commercial capital, nor do they affect the general rate of profit. The merchant's profit is determined not by the mass of commodity capital he turns over, but rather by the amount of money capital he advances in order to mediate this turnover."

His demonstration: "If the general annual rate of profit is 15 per cent and the merchant advances £100, then, if his capital turns over once a year, he will sell his commodities at £115. If his capital turns over five times a year, he will sell a commodity capital with a purchase price of £100 five times a year at a price of £103, and in the whole year therefore a commodity capital of £500 at £515. This gives him, as before, an annual profit of £15 on the capital of £100 he has advanced."

Note the internal logic: the annual return is invariant at £15; what changes is how £15 is spread. Turned over once, the whole £15 is tacked onto one £100 transaction (a 15% mark-up per transaction). Turned over five times, £15 is spread across five £100 transactions (a 3% mark-up each), and the merchant sells £500 worth of goods over the year for £515.

Marx then gives the reductio: "If this were not the case, commercial capital would yield a far higher profit than industrial capital in relation to the number of its turnovers, and this would contradict the law of the general rate of profit." This is a decisive constraint. The equalization of the rate of profit across all capitals — industrial and commercial — forbids the merchant from multiplying his return by multiplying his turnovers. If he could, capital would flood into high-velocity trades, and the rate of profit would be driven down by competition anyway. The inverse relation between turnover and the per-unit supplement is the equalization mechanism in the commercial sphere.

The law stated formally: "The number of turnovers of commercial capital in various branches of commerce thus has a direct effect on the commercial prices of commodities. The level of the commercial price supplement, that is to say the aliquot part of the commercial profit on a given capital that is added to the production price of the individual commodity, stands in inverse proportion to the number of turnovers or the speed of turnover of the commercial capital in the particular line of business in question." And the illustration: "If a commercial capital turns over five times a year, it adds to the same value of commodity capital only a fifth the increase that another commercial capital, able to turn over only once a year, adds to a commodity capital of equal value."

The general statement follows: "The way that sale prices are affected by the average turnover time of capitals in various branches of commerce can be reduced to the principle that, according to the velocity of this turnover, the same mass of profit that is determined by the general annual profit rate for a given amount of commercial capital — determined independently, that is, of the particular character of this capital's commercial operations — is differently distributed over commodity masses of the same value, adding for example 15/5 = 3 per cent when it turns over five times a year, as against 15 per cent when it turns over only once."

And the cross-branch consequence: "Thus the same percentage of commercial profit in different lines of business raises the sale prices of the commodities in question by quite different percentages, calculated on the values of these commodities, in direct proportion to the differences in the turnover times." A jeweller and a greengrocer may both earn the same annual rate on capital, yet the jeweller's small volume of high-value, slow-turning stock bears a large price supplement per unit relative to the greengrocer's rapid-turnover perishables. The same rate of profit, differently expressed in prices. This is the theorem's practical punch-line.

2.15 The Industrial Contrast and the "Arbitrary Price" Illusion

Marx now contrasts the industrial case to isolate what is peculiar about the commercial case.

"As far as industrial capital is concerned, on the other hand, its turnover time has no effect on the value of the individual commodities produced, even though it does affect the mass of the values and surplus-values that a given capital produces in a given time, via the mass of labour exploited." Turnover speed does not change how much labour is embodied in one coat; it changes how many coats a given capital can make per year, and therefore how much surplus-value that capital extracts, and therefore — with the other capitals — the general rate of profit. Marx admits this is "concealed and appears as something different when we look at production prices, but that is simply because the production prices of various commodities diverge from their values, according to the laws already developed." Production prices deviate systematically from values as a function of organic composition; but "taking the production process as a whole, and the total mass of commodities produced by industrial capital, the general law is immediately confirmed." In the aggregate, price sums equal value sums, and the law of value holds.

From this contrast, Marx draws the chapter's epistemological conclusion:

"while a closer consideration of the influence of turnover time on value formation in the case of the individual capital leads back to the general law and the basis of political economy, viz. that commodity values are determined by the labour-time they contain, the influence of the turnover of commercial capital on commercial prices exhibits phenomena which, in the absence of a very far-reaching analysis of the intermediate stages of the process, seem to presuppose a purely arbitrary determination of prices, i.e. a determination simply by the fact that capital happens to have made up its mind to make a certain amount of profit per year. It seems in particular, through this influence of the turnover, as if the circulation process as such determines the prices of commodities, and that this is within certain limits independent of the process of production. All superficial and distorted views of the overall reproduction process are derived from consideration of commercial capital and from the notions that its specific movements give rise to in the heads of the agents of circulation."

This is the chapter's deepest claim. The illusion is not merely an intellectual error; it is a necessary appearance generated by the real structure of commercial capital's operation. Because the merchant does set a mark-up, and because that mark-up does vary inversely with turnover in a way he can partially control (by choosing his line, by moving his shop, by cutting prices to accelerate turnover), price appears to him to be his own decision. And because he never sees the production process—only the circulation of titles—he has no access to the labour-time determinism underneath. The illusion is overdetermined by his social position.

2.16 Competition, the Methodological Coda, and "Small Profits and Quick Returns"

Marx then delivers a methodological observation of first importance:

"As the reader will have recognized in dismay, the analysis of the real, inner connections of the capitalist production process is a very intricate thing and a work of great detail; it is one of the tasks of science to reduce the visible and merely apparent movement to the actual inner movement."

The "in dismay" is characteristic self-irony — Marx acknowledges the reader's fatigue — but the methodological claim is serious. Science's task is the reduction of appearance to essence. From this it follows that "in the heads of the agents of capitalist production and circulation, ideas must necessarily form about the laws of production that diverge completely from these laws and are merely the expression in consciousness of the apparent movement." And specifically: "The ideas of a merchant, a stock-jobber or a banker are necessarily quite upside-down. The ideas of the manufacturers are vitiated by the acts of circulation to which their capital is subjected and by the equalization of the general rate of profit."

A footnote reinforces the point with a charmingly concrete source — Feller and Odermann, Das Ganze der kaufmännischen Arithmetik, 7th edn., Leipzig 1859, p. 451: "Thus the fact that one and the same commodity is to be obtained from different sellers at essentially different prices also has its basis very frequently in an incorrect calculation." Marx comments: "The following observation, if very naive, is at the same time quite correct… This shows how the determination of price becomes purely theoretical, i.e. abstract." That is, in commercial practice, price determination consists of arithmetic operations performed on given data — a technical exercise — and so appears wholly detached from any social relation of production.

Marx then makes a decisive theoretical point about competition's role: "If the limits of value and surplus-value are given, it is easy to perceive how the competition between capitals transforms values into prices of production and still further into commercial prices, transforming surplus-value into average profit. But without these limits, there is absolutely no way of seeing why competition should reduce the general rate of profit to one limit rather than to another, to 15 per cent instead of 1,500 per cent. It can at most reduce it to one level. But there is absolutely no element in it that can determine this level itself."

This is the logical refutation of the "competition explains everything" position. Competition is an equalizing mechanism: it drives rates toward uniformity. But a purely equalizing mechanism cannot specify the level at which equality occurs. It can flatten variation but cannot set the mean. The level must be determined elsewhere — namely, by the mass of surplus-value produced relative to total capital advanced. Without the labour theory of value, competition theory has no answer to the question "why 15% and not 1,500%?" This is Marx's classic argument, deployed here against the commercial-capital standpoint specifically.

Then the epistemic inversion summarized with full clarity: "From the standpoint of commercial capital, therefore, turnover itself seems to determine price." Contrast the two capitals: for industrial capital, faster turnover "enable[s] a given capital to exploit more or less labour" and hence "has a determining and delimiting effect on the mass of profit, and hence on the general rate of profit as well." For commercial capital, "the rate of profit [is] something external to it, and this rate's inner connection with the formation of surplus-value is completely obliterated."

Marx then offers a vivid industrial illustration of the difference: "If the same industrial capital, with other circumstances remaining the same, and particularly with the same organic composition, turns over four times a year instead of twice, it produces twice as much surplus-value and thus profit; and this is palpably evident whenever the industrial capital in question possesses the monopoly of an improved mode of production, which permits it this accelerated turnover for as long as this monopoly lasts." Organic composition = the ratio of constant capital (machinery, raw materials) to variable capital (wages), which governs how much living labour a given capital sets in motion — holding it fixed isolates the pure turnover effect. The industrial monopolist who turns over twice as fast visibly doubles his surplus-value: the connection between turnover and profit is transparent to him.

But in commerce the relation appears inverted: "The differing turnover time in different branches of commerce, however, does manifest itself inversely, in this way: the profit made on the turnover of a certain commodity capital stands in inverse proportion to the number of turnovers of the money capital that turns over this commodity capital." Hence the shopkeeper's motto: "'Small profits and quick returns', in other words, appears to the shopkeeper as a principle that he follows on principle." He believes he has chosen a strategy. In fact he is executing the law of the general rate of profit in the only form available to him. The maxim is the ideology of a structural necessity.

Finally, Marx sets the boundaries of the law: "It is readily apparent, of course, that this law applies only to the turnovers of commercial capital in a particular line of business, and, leaving aside the mutually compensatory alternation of quicker and slower turnovers, holds only for the average turnover made by the whole commercial capital applied in this branch."

The individual qualifications then follow. Within a branch: "The capital of A, involved in the same branch as B, may make more or less than the average number of turnovers. In this case, the others conversely make less or more. This in no way affects the turnover of the total mass of commodity capital invested in this branch. But it is of decisive importance for the individual merchant or retailer." An above-average turnover yields super profit — the commercial analogue of the industrialist producing under more favourable conditions than the average. "If competition compels it, he can sell more cheaply than his fellows without reducing his profit below the average." And then the final, characteristically Marxian twist: "If the conditions that enable him to have a quicker turnover can themselves be purchased, e.g. the location of his sales outlet, he may pay extra rent for this; i.e. a part of his surplus profit is transformed into ground-rent."

With this closing sentence, Marx connects the differential turnover of commercial capital to ground-rent — the same category he had analysed earlier in Volume 3 for agriculture. Advantageous commercial location (a busy corner, a prime retail pitch) is a natural or socially-created monopoly, and its scarcity price captures a portion of the differential surplus profit arising from faster turnover. The chapter thus ends not with a self-contained result but with a bridge to the theory of rent — and a demonstration that even the most apparently "arbitrary" mercantile price premium is decomposable by the same analytical machinery that governs the whole of capitalist production.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

First lesson: turnover is a category that changes meaning with the nature of the capital performing it. For industrial capital, turnover is real — it measures the periodic renewal of a material process and constrains how much surplus-value a given capital can extract per year. For commercial capital, turnover is formal — a mere repetition of purchase and sale that presupposes the industrial process it cannot affect. This asymmetry is not a curiosity of accounting; it is the entire source of the chapter's theoretical complications. It also establishes a hierarchy: commercial capital "is never anything more than the movement of industrial capital within the circulation sphere," and industrial capital remains the locus of value and surplus-value creation.

Second lesson: autonomy-within-dependence is the general form of a distinctively capitalist pathology. The formula — "inner dependence in combination with external autonomy drives commercial capital to a point where the inner connection is forcibly re-established by way of a crisis" — is the most general proposition in the chapter. It can be restated as a systemic law: any subsystem whose operations are structurally determined by another subsystem, yet conducted according to its own independent logic and financed independently, will accumulate a discrepancy that is resolved abruptly rather than gradually. The credit system supplies the financing that permits the discrepancy to grow; the bill of exchange supplies the fixed maturity that triggers the resolution; and the location of the explosion — wholesale trade and banking rather than retail — follows directly from where the discrepancy is concentrated. The corollary is that crises appear as financial and commercial events even though their substance lies in the relation between production and its realization.

Third lesson: the paradox of appearance and essence. The chapter is, in effect, Marx's most sustained demonstration that the necessary illusions of a social system are generated by its real structure, not by error or ignorance. The merchant's belief that price is arbitrary, or that "small profits and quick returns" is a business principle he has chosen, is not stupidity; it is the accurate representation of how the system appears from the vantage point he occupies. "All superficial and distorted views of the overall reproduction process are derived from consideration of commercial capital and from the notions that its specific movements give rise to in the heads of the agents of circulation." Science's remit — "to reduce the visible and merely apparent movement to the actual inner movement" — is accordingly a critical as well as a descriptive task.

Fourth lesson: the decisive refutation of the circulation-determines-price thesis. Marx's argument here is subtle and worth restating sharply. It is not that circulation has no effect on prices; it does. But the effect is bounded and fully specifiable: turnover velocity redistributes a given profit mass across commodity units. It cannot determine the profit mass itself. The limits — production price and average rate of profit — are given externally to the merchant. Hence the argument: competition can equalize but cannot specify the level it equalizes to; if values and surplus-values were not prior, no determinant for 15% versus 1,500% would exist. This is the analytical point at which the apparently most contingent sphere of the capitalist economy — merchant pricing — is shown to be law-governed by the labour theory of value.

Fifth lesson: circulation costs matter, and unproductive capital is a real burden. Because commercial capital draws its profit from the same surplus-value pool as industrial capital, its size relative to the total capital fixes its share. Therefore anything that shrinks the commercial capital required — improved transport, better communications, faster turnover generally — raises the general rate of profit. Conversely, the growth of semi-functioning and non-functioning commercial capital (point (3) in Marx's triad), and the growth of the whole circulation infrastructure (shipping, railways, telegraphs — point (2)), operate as a drag on the rate of profit. Marx thus anticipates, in outline, a theory of the unproductive absorption of social capital, formulated in 1865 but of ongoing analytical relevance to the study of financialization, logistics overheads, and the retail sector's capital intensity.

Sixth lesson: the incompleteness of the argument is itself signposted. The chapter repeatedly defers: costs of circulation K and their profit ΔK are set aside; speculation and the division of commercial capital are excluded; a promised study of "Competition among Capitals" is never delivered (footnoted as an intention of 1865). These deferrals are not evasions but honest markers of a research programme. Reading the chapter well means reading its brackets and its footnotes as carefully as its arguments.

Broader implications. Theoretically, Chapter 18 completes the architecture of Volume 3's account of profit distribution by showing that even the swerve from value into commercial price preserves the invariants of the labour theory of value: turnover redistributes; it does not create. Historically, it explains the decline of the great mercantile fortunes of the early modern period not as a moral or commercial failure but as the necessary consequence of rising productivity and the equalization of profit rates. Practically, it provides the analytical basis for understanding why commercial booms can persist for extended periods independently of final demand; why financial and wholesale sectors are the locus of crisis onset; and why retail "strategy" — pricing, location, velocity — is largely a behavioural expression of structural position. And philosophically, it offers one of Marx's cleanest statements of his critical epistemology: that the task of science is precisely to show how the ordinary, real, quotidian experience of social agents — the merchant's ledger, the shopkeeper's rule of thumb, Roscher's "good sense and humanity" — can be simultaneously true as experience and false as explanation.
