Volume 3 • Chapter 38: Differential Rent: General Remarks

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 38, "Differential Rent in General," opens Part VI of Marx's Capital, Volume 3 — the section devoted to "The Transformation of Surplus Profit into Ground-Rent." Its central problem is posed with surgical precision at the outset: How can ground-rent exist at all if agricultural (and mining) products are sold not above, but exactly at, their prices of production? This is a question of enormous theoretical consequence. If commodities exchange at values determined by socially necessary labour-time, and if competition between capitals equalizes profit rates into a general average, then there seems to be no room for a landlord's cut. Any rent would appear to be either a deduction from the capitalist's normal profit or a surcharge on the consumer — in either case a mere transfer, not a distinct economic category with its own law of origin. Marx's declared task is to show that a rent can arise without any violation of the law of value, without monopoly pricing, and without any absolute rise in productivity.

His answer is the concept of differential rent, introduced here in its most general and abstract form. Marx constructs a deliberately simplified, quasi-experimental model: a country in which most factories run on steam-engines, but a minority run on natural waterfalls. Because the waterfall is a free gift of nature that enters no one's cost accounts, the waterfall-using manufacturer produces his commodities more cheaply. Yet he sells them at the same market price of production as everyone else — the price governed by the average conditions of the sphere. The gap between his lower individual price of production and the higher general price of production is a surplus profit. So long as the waterfall is a monopolizable, place-bound, non-reproducible natural force attached to a piece of the earth's surface, and so long as that surface has an owner, this surplus profit is intercepted by the landowner and thereby transformed into ground-rent.

The chapter's intellectual objective is therefore twofold. First, it isolates the general concept of differential rent — a surplus profit generated by the sheer differential in natural productive conditions, before any consideration of agriculture proper. Second, it establishes a set of rigorous distinctions that will discipline everything that follows: between absolute and relative productivity gains; between a natural force as the basis and as the source of surplus value; between the creation of surplus profit, which landed property does not perform, and the appropriation of it, which landed property alone makes possible. Marx closes with five numbered conclusions and a demonstration that the "price" of a waterfall is nothing but capitalized rent — an "irrational expression" that conceals a real social relation.

Within the broader architecture of Capital, this chapter functions as the load-bearing analytical gateway to the entire theory of ground-rent. Everything that follows in Part VI — differential rent in agriculture (Forms I and II), absolute rent, the critique of Ricardo's and Malthus's rent theories — presupposes the model built here. The chapter's final line signals this explicitly: "Now that we have established the general concept of differential rent in this way, we turn to consider this rent in agriculture proper. What will be said of agriculture applies on the whole also to mining."

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

38.1 The Methodological Premise: Rent Without a Monopoly Price

Marx begins by fixing an assumption that he insists operates throughout the analysis: the products that pay rent — "which means that a part of their surplus-value — and therefore also a part of their total price, is reducible to rent" — are sold just like all other commodities at their prices of production. He adds that for present purposes he need consider only agricultural products, though mining products would serve equally well. This is not a throwaway stipulation; it is the pivot on which the entire argument turns. A price of production is defined here as the sum of two components: the cost elements — "the value of the constant and variable capital consumed" — plus a profit determined by the general rate of profit calculated on the total capital advanced, whether used up or not.

That last clause is a technical precision of real importance. Marx distinguishes between the capital consumed (the portion actually used up in production, which enters the cost price) and the capital advanced (the total capital tied up, whether used up or not, upon which the profit rate is calculated). This distinction recurs when the waterfall manufacturer's "cost price" falls from 100 to 90 while his profit is still reckoned on a capital of £100. The concept of cost price (k) therefore denotes the value of consumed constant and variable capital, while the price of production (P) = k + the average profit on total capital advanced.

Having set this premise, Marx states the problematic with maximum clarity: "The question then arises how a ground-rent can develop on this assumption, i.e. how a portion of profit can be transformed into ground-rent, so that a part of the commodity price thus accrues to the landowner." The wording transformed is deliberate and anticipates the chapter's conclusion: differential rent does not add anything to the commodity's value; it redistributes a surplus profit that already exists.

38.2 The Heuristic Model: Steam versus Waterfall and the Price of Production

"To demonstrate the general character of this form of ground-rent," Marx asks us to assume a country in which factories are powered "predominantly by steam-engines, but a certain minority by natural waterfalls." He assigns numbers: the branches of industry using steam have a production price of 115 for a quantity of commodities for which a capital of 100 is consumed, the 15 per cent profit being calculated on the total capital applied, not merely on the consumed capital of 100. He then immediately qualifies the status of this number: "the particular numerical figures are completely immaterial here."

Two conceptual clarifications follow. First, the production price is "determined not by the individual cost price of any one industrialist producing by himself, but rather by the price that the commodity costs on average under the average conditions for capital in that whole sphere of production." It is thus the market price of production — the average market price considered as distinct from its oscillations. Second, Marx notes that this price appears "always in the form of the market price," and specifically as the governing market price. It is through this form that the nature of commodity value presents itself.

38.3 The Digression on Socially Necessary Labour-Time

At this point Marx inserts a compact but foundational statement of value theory. Commodity value is "determined not by the labour-time needed by a certain individual producer to produce a certain quantity of a commodity... but by the socially necessary labour-time." He defines this as "the labour-time required under the given average social conditions of production to produce the total socially required quantity of the species of commodity available on the market."

This digression is not ornamental. It does two jobs. First, it explains why the waterfall producer's privately cheaper commodity still sells at the general price: value is social, not individual. Second, it grounds the later insistence that ordinary industrial surplus profits are self-abolishing — because the law of socially necessary labour-time exerts "a compulsion to produce commodities under the same favourable conditions," it constantly erodes the advantage of any particular producer. The abstract theory of value is thus the hidden engine driving the concrete conclusion about rent.

38.4 The Waterfall's Cost Price and the Birth of Surplus Profit

Marx now introduces the differential: the cost price in the water-powered factories comes to only 90 instead of 100. Since the production price governing the market is 115, and the profit is 15 per cent, the water-powered factories "will also sell at 115, i.e. at the market price as governed by the average price." Their profit consequently amounts to 25 instead of 15 — a surplus profit of 10 per cent. This arises, Marx stresses, "not because they sell their commodities above the price of production but because they sell them at this price," because their capital functions under exceptionally favourable conditions "that stand above the average level prevailing in this sphere."

Note the structural elegance: the surplus profit is generated entirely on the side of production (lower cost), yet realized entirely on the side of circulation (selling at the general price). Neither element alone would suffice. The market does not reward the waterfall producer for virtue; it simply fails to lower its price to his level.

38.5 What Is Immediately Evident (I): The Two Limits of the Difference

"Two things are immediately evident here." The first is that the surplus profit behaves at first exactly like any other surplus profit — not "the chance result of transactions in the circulation process, of accidental fluctuations in market price." It equals the difference between the individual price of production of the favoured producers and the general social price of production governing the market — that is, "the excess of the general production price of the commodity over its individual production price." Marx then specifies the two governing limits of this excess: on one side the individual cost price (hence the individual production price), on the other the general production price.

The mechanical derivation follows: the value of the waterfall-produced commodities is lower because less labour is required — "less labour enters in the objectified form, as a portion of the constant capital." The labour applied is more productive: it needs a smaller quantity of objectified labour (no coal to be paid for) and a smaller quantity of living labour ("since the water-wheel does not need to be heated"). The industrialist experiences this as a lower cost price for him personally: he has less objectified labour to pay for and fewer wages for less living labour-power. Consequently his individual production price is lower.

Marx gives the arithmetic explicitly: the general ratio 100 : 115 = 90 : 103½, so his individual production price is "only 103½ instead of 115." Here a subtle internal tension deserves flagging. If the general capital advanced is 100 and profit is reckoned on total capital advanced, then an individual cost price of 90 with a 15 per cent profit on the advanced capital of 100 yields an individual production price of 105, and a surplus profit of 115 − 105 = 10 — which is precisely the figure Marx uses everywhere else in the chapter ("a surplus profit of 10 per cent"; the £10 paid to the landowner). The parenthetical proportional calculation yields 103½ and hence a difference of 11½. Marx himself defuses this: "the particular numerical figures are completely immaterial here." The conceptual point stands regardless — the surplus profit equals the difference between the individual and the general production price, and that difference is bounded by the two magnitudes named.

The bound matters because it makes the surplus profit conditional. If coal becomes cheaper, the difference between the waterfall producer's individual cost price and the general one declines, "and so therefore does his surplus profit." If he had to sell at his individual value or at the production price determined by it, "the difference would disappear."

38.6 The Social Result: Productivity of Labour Presented as Productivity of Capital

Marx closes this first point with a decisive social observation. The surplus profit is the result, on the one hand, of the commodity being sold at its general market price — "the price at which competition balances the individual prices" — and on the other hand, of the fact that "the greater individual productivity of the labour that he sets in motion does not benefit the workers, but, like the productivity of labour in general, their employer; i.e. it presents itself as the productivity of capital."

This is a compressed restatement of the theory of surplus value: the gain from heightened productivity accrues to whoever owns the means of production. The waterfall's advantage is not a technical accident but a social relation — the natural force becomes "the productivity of capital" because capital owns the labour process. And since one limit of the surplus profit is the general price of production, whose component the general rate of profit is, the surplus profit "can arise only from the difference between the general and the individual production prices, and hence from the difference between the individual and the general rate of profit. An excess over and above this difference would presuppose the sale of the product above the price of production governed by the market."

38.7 What Is Immediately Evident (II): Identity with Ordinary Surplus Profit

The second evident point is that, so far, the waterfall manufacturer's surplus profit "has not so far been distinguished in any way from all other surplus profit." Marx defines normal surplus profit (excluding accidental business deals or market fluctuations) as determined by the difference between the individual production price of the commodities produced by this particular capital and the general production price that governs the market — for the total capital invested in the sphere. Up to this point the waterfall case is merely an instance of the general formula.

Then comes the pivot, announced in one of the chapter's most effective rhetorical moves: "But now comes the difference." Marx poses the question directly: "To what circumstances does the manufacturer in the present case owe his surplus profit?"

38.8 Nature's Gift versus Capital's Achievement

The first answer is: a natural force — the motive power of water, "which is provided by nature itself and is not itself the product of labour, unlike the coal that transforms water into steam, which has value and must be paid an equivalent, i.e. costs something." The waterfall is "a natural agent of production, and no labour goes into creating it."

But Marx immediately refuses to let this stand as a sufficient explanation. "But this is not all." The manufacturer operating a steam-engine also applies natural forces that cost him nothing — the ability of water to change its aggregate state, the elasticity of steam — and these make labour more productive and, insofar as they cheapen the production of the workers' means of subsistence, increase surplus-value and hence profit. The monopolization of natural forces is thus common to all capital operating with steam-engines. Such monopolization may increase the general rate of profit, but it "does not create any surplus profit, for this consists precisely in the excess of individual profit over and above the average profit." A general advantage is a general advantage; it raises the average, not the deviation from it.

An editorial note here corrects the text: the phrase "the increase in labour-power that they bring about" should presumably read "the productivity of labour." This is worth noting because the correction preserves the coherence of the argument — what the natural force increases is the productivity of labour, not labour-power itself.

Marx draws the conclusion pointedly: if in our case the application of water-power does create surplus profit, "this cannot arise simply from the fact that the increased productivity of labour is due here to the use of a natural force. Further modifying factors must intervene." A footnote at this juncture refers the reader to an Inquiry of 1821 (London) written against Malthus — signaling that Marx is consciously composing his account of surplus profit in polemical opposition to Malthusian rent theory, even where he does not spell out the disagreement.

38.9 The Normal Sources of Surplus Profit and Their Self-Abolition

Marx now broadens the field to enumerate the ordinary sources of surplus profit within a sphere, in order to demonstrate by contrast what is unlike the waterfall case. Divergences in profit rate between particular spheres are constantly balanced out into an average; what remains, apart from accidental divergences, arises from a reduction in the cost price due to either:

  1. Scale of capital applied — capital operating on a greater-than-average scale reduces the faux frais of production (the incidental or overhead costs), and the general causes of rising labour productivity (cooperation, division of labour) can operate "to a greater extent and with more intensity, because over a larger field of operation"; or
  2. Superior methods — "better methods of work, new inventions, improved machines, trade secrets in chemistry, etc." — the application of new, improved, above-average means and methods of production.

The critical characterization follows: "The reason for the surplus profit in this case is thus inherent in the capital itself (including the labour that it sets in motion)." Whether from sheer magnitude of capital or from more efficient application, "nothing inherently prevents all capital in the same sphere of production from being invested in the same way." Competition between capitals "actually tends to the contrary, it tends to cancel out these distinctions more and more." The determination of value by socially necessary labour-time inexorably produces the cheapening of commodities and "the compulsion to produce commodities under the same favourable conditions." What begins as an exceptional advantage becomes the norm, and the surplus profit evaporates. This is why such advantages are "cancelled out as soon as equally large amounts of capital are employed in the average case," or "as soon as the exceptional manner of production becomes universal, or is overtaken by one still more advanced."

38.10 The Waterfall's Exceptional Character: Monopolizable, Place-Bound, Non-Reproducible

"Things take a different form with the surplus profit of the manufacturer who makes use of the waterfall." Here Marx delivers the analytic core of the chapter. The heightened productivity of his labour arises "neither from the capital and labour themselves nor from the simple application of a natural force distinct from capital and labour but incorporated into the capital." It arises from "the greater natural productivity of a labour linked with the use of a natural force, but a natural force that is not available to all capital in the same sphere of production, as is for example the elasticity of steam."

The contrast is stark and decisive. Elasticity of steam is available to any capital as soon as it invests in the sphere; water-power is not. What is at issue is "a monopolizable natural force which, like the waterfall, is available only to those who have at their disposal particular pieces of the earth's surface and their appurtenances." Marx insists: "It is in no way just up to the capital to call into being this natural condition of greater labour productivity, in the way that any capital can transform water into steam." The condition exists "in nature only at certain places, and where it is not found it cannot be produced by a particular capital outlay." It is not bound up with products labour can produce — machines, coal — but "with particular natural conditions on particular pieces of land."

This gives rise to exclusion: "Those manufacturers who possess waterfalls exclude those who do not possess them from employing this natural force, because land is limited, and still more so land endowed with water-power."

Marx then acknowledges a genuine complication with intellectual honesty. It is "not ruled out" that although the number of natural waterfalls in a country is limited, the amount of water-power industry can use may still be increased — a waterfall can be artificially channelled, a water-wheel improved, turbines substituted where the ordinary wheel is unsuited. This concession does not weaken but sharpens the argument, because it shows that the barrier is not a fixed quantity of physical energy but the exclusive ownership of particular sites. Hence: "Possession of this natural force forms a monopoly in the hands of its owner, a condition of higher productivity for the capital invested, which cannot be produced by capital's own production process; the natural force that can be monopolized in this way is always chained to the earth." It belongs neither to "the general conditions of the sphere of production in question" nor to "those of its conditions that are generally reproducible."

38.11 Landed Property and the Transformation of Surplus Profit into Ground-Rent

Having established the existence of a monopoly, Marx introduces the legal and social form that converts it into rent. "If we now imagine that these waterfalls, together with the land on which they are located, are in the hands of subjects who are accepted as the proprietors of these portions of the globe, as landowners, then these are in a position to prevent the application of capital to the waterfall and its utilization by capital." Their power is essentially negative: "They can either allow this use or refuse it." But capital cannot conjure a waterfall from its own resources.

The conclusion follows: "The surplus profit that arises from this use of the waterfall thus arises not from the capital but rather from the use by capital of a monopolizable and monopolized natural force. Under these conditions, the surplus profit is transformed into ground-rent, i.e. it accrues to the owner of the waterfall."

Marx illustrates with concrete magnitudes. If the manufacturer pays the landowner £10 a year for the waterfall, his profit comes to £15 — "15 per cent on the £100 which is now the amount of his production costs." (The £100 comprises his £90 real cost plus the £10 rent, which now enters his individual cost accounting.) He remains "in a position just as good as, if not better than, the other capitalists in his sphere." And crucially: "Nothing is altered if the capitalist owns the waterfall himself. He still draws the surplus profit of £10 not as a capitalist, but as the owner of the waterfall; and for the precise reason that this excess arises not from his capital as such, but rather from his disposal over a natural force that is limited in scope, separable from his capital, and monopolizable, it is transformed into ground-rent." The three defining traits of the monopoly — limited, separable from capital, monopolizable — are the criteria that separate ground-rent from ordinary surplus profit. Ownership of the waterfall and ownership of capital are conceptually distinct titles, even when united in one person.

38.12 The Five Conclusions

Marx now consolidates the argument into five numbered propositions.

First: "this rent is always a differential rent." It does not contribute to determining the general production price of the commodity but "takes this as given." It always arises from the difference between the individual production price of the particular capital that commands the monopolized natural force and the general production price for capital invested in the sphere. The defining marker of differential rent is therefore parasitic passivity with respect to price formation: it presupposes the general price and feeds on deviation from it.

Second: the rent derives from no absolute rise in productivity. Such a rise "can only ever reduce the value of commodities." What is at issue is "the greater relative returns from certain particular capitals invested in a sphere of production, as compared with those capital investments that are excluded from these exceptional, favourable conditions of productivity which have been created by nature." Marx illustrates with a counterfactual: if the use of steam conferred an overwhelming advantage — even though coal costs money and water-power costs nothing — and that advantage more than compensated for coal's cost, "water-power would not be used and could not produce any surplus profit, nor therefore any rent." This is an elegant reminder that differential rent depends on the relative superiority of the privileged producer; if the privileged position were not superior, the rent would vanish.

Third: the natural force is the basis, not the source, of the surplus profit. This is the most theoretically dense of the five. The natural force provides "the natural basis of the exceptionally increased productivity of labour." Marx then sets out a general principle of political economy: "Use-value is altogether the bearer of exchange-value but not its cause. If the same use-value could be obtained without labour, it would have no exchange-value." Conversely, "a thing cannot have exchange-value without having use-value, i.e. without being such a natural bearer of labour." The waterfall is a use-value — a bearer of productivity — but it is not a source of value, because value is human labour in its social determination.

Marx then completes the thought with a conditional that ties the argument back to his value theory: "If the various different values did not balance out into production prices and the various individual production prices into a general production price that governs the market, a rise in labour productivity resulting from the use of a waterfall would simply lower the price of the commodities produced with the waterfall without raising the portion of profit contained in these commodities." Value theory and the theory of price of production are therefore joint preconditions of differential rent. Without the equalization of profit rates, the waterfall's advantage would benefit consumers rather than producers. And in the same manner, "increased labour productivity in general would not be transformed into surplus-value if capital did not appropriate as its own the productive power, natural and social, of the labour applied."

Fourth: landed property does not create the surplus profit. "This surplus profit exists even if there is no landed property, if for example the land on which the waterfall is located can be used by the manufacturer as unclaimed land." Landed property simply enables the landowner "to entice this surplus profit out of the manufacturer's pocket and into his own. It is not the cause of this surplus profit's creation, but simply of its transformation into the form of ground-rent, hence of the appropriation of this portion of profit or commodity price by the landowner or waterfall-owner." This is the chapter's most important negative claim, directed against any theory that treats private property in land as the origin of rent. Property in land is the cause of appropriation, not of creation.

Fifth: the price of the waterfall. If the landowner sells it — to a third party or to the manufacturer himself — the price "does not at first go directly into the production price of the commodities concerned, even though it does go into the individual price for the manufacturer." For rent in this case arises from the production price of those commodities of the same kind produced by steam-engines, "which is determined independently of the waterfall."

38.13 The Price of the Waterfall: Capitalization and the "Irrational Expression"

From the fifth proposition Marx develops a striking critique of landed-price fetishism. "The price of the waterfall, besides, is altogether an irrational expression concealing a real economic relationship." The reasoning flows directly from the labour theory of value: "The waterfall, like the earth in general and every natural force, has no value, since it represents no objectified labour and hence no price, this being in the normal case nothing but value expressed in money. Where there is no value, there is eo ipso nothing to be expressed in money."

What then is the price of a waterfall? "This price is nothing but capitalized rent." Marx unfolds the mechanism: "Landed property enables the proprietor to lay hold of the difference between the individual profit and the average profit; the profit captured in this way, which is renewed every year, can be capitalized and then appears as the price of the natural force itself." His worked example is exact: if the surplus profit is £10 per year and the average interest rate is 5 per cent, then this £10 represents the interest on a capital of £200. "This capitalization of the annual £10 that the waterfall empowers its owner to extract from the manufacturer then appears as the capital value of the waterfall itself."

The irrationality of this expression is then exposed by an internal contradiction in the calculation. The "price" of £200 "simply expresses the product of the surplus profit of £10 multiplied by twenty years" — that is, the capitalization formula 10/0.05 = 200 corresponds to twenty years' purchase. But "if circumstances remain otherwise the same, the same waterfall actually enables its owner to extract this annual £10 for an indefinite time, thirty or a hundred years." Conversely, Marx adds the equally asymmetrical possibility on the downside: "if a new method of production that cannot make use of water-power lowers the cost price of the commodities produced by steam from £100 to £90, the surplus profit would disappear, and the rent and the price of the waterfall along with it." The capitalized "value" of the waterfall is thus a fiction pinned to a contingent, mutable social and technical differential — a differential which the very progress of capitalist technique may abolish. What appears as the intrinsic worth of a piece of nature is a capitalized claim on a social surplus profit, dependent for its very existence on the continuing backwardness of the excluded capitals.

38.14 Transition to Agriculture

The chapter closes with a programmatic hand-off: "Now that we have established the general concept of differential rent in this way, we turn to consider this rent in agriculture proper. What will be said of agriculture applies on the whole also to mining." The waterfall has served its heuristic purpose. It was never itself meant to be a typical case of ground-rent; it is a clean, minimal example that isolates the differential mechanism by stripping away all complicating factors such as soil fertility differences and their relation to successive investments of capital. The general concept — surplus profit born of a monopolizable, place-bound natural differential, transformed into ground-rent by landed property — is now available to be applied to the far murkier terrain of agricultural rent proper, where differential rent will be shown to operate under both extensive and intensive forms of cultivation.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter yields a set of principles that stand as fundamental laws of Marx's theory of ground-rent and, more broadly, of his critique of distribution.

Differential rent is a surplus profit, not a price surcharge. Its distinguishing feature is passivity before the price system: it "takes the general production price as given" and feeds on the deviation of an individual price of production below that standard. This is the deep reason why differential rent can coexist with the law of value. Anyone who claims that rent is merely a monopoly mark-up on consumers, or a deduction from normal profit, is describing something else — some non-differential form of rent — not this.

The natural basis of rent is not its source. The waterfall is a use-value, a bearer of productivity; it is not a source of value. This distinction, expressed in the formula "use-value is altogether the bearer of exchange-value but not its cause," blocks any naturalistic or physiocratic theory that traces value (or rent) to the earth's bounty. Nature gives differential advantages in productivity, but only the social appropriation of labour — capital's monopolization of labour's productive power — turns those advantages into surplus-value. The natural force is the occasion, not the cause.

Landed property appropriates rather than creates. No claim in the chapter is more consequential. The surplus profit exists whether or not the waterfall has an owner; unclaimed land would still yield it. Landed property's distinctive power is the ability to exclude — to "either allow this use or refuse it" — and therefore to divert an already-existing surplus into rent. This theorem disarms at a stroke all doctrines that would treat rent as a payment for a contribution made by the landowner. The landowner contributes nothing to production; he commands a position from which nothing can be produced without his permission.

Ordinary surplus profits are self-abolishing; natural ones are not (as long as the monopoly persists). The chapter's contrast between steam and waterfall is ultimately a contrast between advantages inherent in capital and advantages chained to the earth. Capital-borne advantages — scale, improved machinery, better methods, trade secrets — are "cancelled out" by competition's tendency to equalize conditions, because "nothing inherently prevents all capital in the same sphere of production from being invested in the same way." But the waterfall is available only at certain places and cannot be produced by capital outlay. Its scarcity is not a temporary imperfection of competition but a permanent structural feature. This makes differential rent far more durable than industrial surplus profit — yet not eternal: a technological revolution that renders water-power superfluous would lower steam costs, eliminate the differential, "and the rent and the price of the waterfall along with it."

The price of land is capitalized rent — a fetish. The chapter's closing movement supplies one of Marx's sharpest illustrations of fetishism. The waterfall has no value, because it embodies no labour. Yet it acquires a price, which is discovered to be simply the annual surplus profit divided by the interest rate — that is, a capitalized claim on a social relation. The fact that the capitalization reflects twenty years' purchase while the rent stream may run for thirty or a hundred years, or collapse to nothing overnight, exposes the "price" as a reckoning rooted in expectations, conventions, and the prevailing rate of interest rather than in any property intrinsic to the thing. What appears as the value of nature is the mirror image of a surplus extracted from labour.

Theoretical context and forward trajectory. This chapter is the conceptual hinge of Part VI. It establishes the abstract form of differential rent before the concrete complications of agriculture are introduced. Its method is exemplary: begin from the strictest possible assumption (products sell at prices of production), construct a minimal model, extract the general concept, and only then proceed to historical and empirical complexity. The reader who has followed the argument now possesses the analytical instrument — surplus profit + land monopoly + landed property — that will be applied to the two forms of agricultural differential rent and to absolute rent, and that underpins Marx's critique of Ricardo, Malthus, and the rent theories of classical political economy. The chapter's philosophical message is that under capitalism even the gifts of nature become sources of private revenue — not because nature is productive of value, but because a class of owners stands, by historical and legal arrangement, at the gateway through which everything produced from the earth must pass.
