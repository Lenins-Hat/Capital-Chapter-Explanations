Volume 3 • Chapter 41: Differential Rent II — First Case: Constant Price of Production

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 41 of Capital, Volume Three, belongs to Part VI — "The Transformation of Surplus Profit into Ground-Rent." It follows Marx's prior construction of Differential Rent I (rent arising when equal capitals are applied to lands of unequal fertility, treated in Chapter 39) and the opening of Differential Rent II (rent arising when unequal capitals are applied to the same land, introduced in Chapter 40). Chapter 41 conducts the first systematic analysis of the latter by isolating one controlling variable: the price of production is held constant. The chapter thus assumes that the market price continues to be governed by the capital invested on the worst, rentless land, A, so that A's production price — capital advanced plus average profit — remains the "regulating" bench-mark against which every additional outlay on the better lands B, C and D must be measured.

The central thesis is stated almost immediately and then systematically demonstrated through a four-case taxonomy (I–IV). Whenever additional capital is invested on rent-bearing land while the price of production remains fixed, the absolute mass of rent per acre rises — regardless of whether the supplementary capital is as barren as land A (Case I: no change), yields a product strictly proportional to its size (Case II: proportional rise), yields surplus profit at a declining rate (Case III: absolute rise, sub-proportional), or produces more than the original capital (Case IV: super-proportional rise). This rising level of rent per acre — as opposed to the aggregate rental — Marx calls "a phenomenon that is peculiar to differential rent II and distinguishes it from differential rent I."

Methodologically, the chapter is a controlled arithmetical thought-experiment. Marx takes Table I of Chapter 39 — four acres (A, B, C, D), each receiving £2½ of capital at 20 per cent average profit, producing 1, 2, 3 and 4 quarters respectively, all sold at £3 per quarter — and transforms it into Table II (capital doubled everywhere, per-unit productivity maintained) and Table III (capital doubled, but the second portion of outlay yields a smaller physical increment). The entire demonstration proceeds from these schedules rather than from historical data, allowing Marx to separate the effect of capital-intensification from price movements.

Beyond rent theory proper, the chapter performs three intellectual tasks whose significance radiates outward. First, it argues that the arithmetic difference between land-type revenues — decisive for Differential Rent I — becomes analytically immaterial under Differential Rent II, since it merely reflects the pattern of further investment rather than a natural property of the soil. Second, it develops a two-theorem analysis of capital release and the productivity of labour, including a celebrated cloth example that distinguishes the release of capital from the sparing of accumulation, and a sharp critique of the capitalist's inverted perception that living labour is the most expensive element of production. Third, it formulates the spatial-versus-temporal distinction: identical total capital produces a higher rent per acre when concentrated successively on a restricted area than when scattered over a wider surface — a result explainable neither by natural fertility nor by quantity of labour, but "exclusively in terms of the different kind of capital investments."

Positioned at the threshold of the later DR-II cases (in which the price of production itself changes), Chapter 41 supplies the base case for all subsequent rent theory: the demonstration that capital intensification alone, with prices constant, is sufficient to raise rent per acre. It also anticipates themes — released capital, accumulation, the falling rate of profit paired with a rising absolute mass of profit — that recur throughout the volume.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

Framing the Problem: Why the "First Case" Must Hold Production Prices Constant

Marx opens with a methodological stipulation that silently governs everything that follows: "This assumption implies that the market price continues to be governed by the capital invested on the worst land A." In Marx's rent theory, the production price of agricultural output is determined by the worst land in cultivation, because that land must be farmed to meet effective demand and therefore its production price — cost of production plus average profit — becomes the regulating price at which every other (better) parcel sells its output. Because A yields no surplus profit at this price, it defines the level above which the better lands B, C and D earn surpluses, and these surpluses are the differential rent. Freezing this regulating price is thus not arbitrary: it is the device that lets Marx isolate the effect of varying the capital applied without contaminating the result with a simultaneous shift in the price scale.

The analytical division of labour between the two differential rents accordingly sharpens. Under Differential Rent I, capital per acre is held constant and the land types differ; rent is the product of equal capitals earning unequal returns because the soil differs. Under Differential Rent II, the land type is held constant and the capital applied varies; rent is the product of unequal capitals earning varying returns on a single plot. Chapter 41 asks the base-case question of DR-II: what happens to rent when extra capital is poured onto rent-bearing land, given that the governing price never moves?

Case I — The Nil Case: Additional Capital as Barren as Land A

The first possibility explored is deliberate sterility. If additional capital invested on any of B, C or D "is only as productive as the same capital on land A, i.e. if at the governing price of production it yields only the average profit and thus no surplus profit, the effect on rent is nil." The mechanics are transparent once differential rent is understood as surplus over the average profit earned by the regulating capital. Rent exists only because a better parcel yields more output per unit of capital at the same selling price, and that excess output converts into a surplus profit at the ruling price. If the incremental outlay produces no more per unit of capital than A does, then it earns exactly the average profit and no surplus, so no differential surplus crystallizes. Marx's characterization is exact: "It is the same as if a number of acres of quality A, the worst land, had been added to the area previously cultivated." The comparison between B, C, D and A — the very ratio from which rent is calculated — remains untouched, and rent stands where it was. The point is that rent is a differential magnitude, not an absolute yield; barren additions cannot move it.

Case II — Proportional Investment: Table II and the Demotion of the Arithmetic Difference

The second case assumes the opposite extreme short of improvement: "additional capitals produce extra products in proportion to their size." Here the rate of productivity per unit of capital is preserved. Table I of Chapter 39 thus becomes Table II. In Table I, each acre received £2½ of capital, incurred £½ of average profit at 20 per cent, and therewith a price of production of £3; A brought in 1 quarter, B 2, C 3 and D 4, all sold at £3 per quarter, so A's proceeds of £3 just covered its price of production and yielded nothing, while B earned £3 of surplus (1 quarter), C £6 (2 quarters) and D £9 (3 quarters) — corresponding to surplus-profit rates of 120 per cent, 240 per cent and 360 per cent on the capital of £2½.

Table II simply doubles the capital to £5 per acre, generating £1 of profit, a price of production of £6, and outputs of 2, 4, 6 and 8 quarters on A, B, C and D. Proceeds become £6, £12, £18 and £24; rents become 2, 4 and 6 quarters (£6, £12, £18); and — crucially — the rates of surplus profit remain 120 per cent, 240 per cent and 360 per cent, exactly as before. Marx stresses that "it is unnecessary here for the capital investment on all types of land to double"; the law only requires that production on each type increase in the same ratio as the capital. Rent therefore "rises simply as a result of increased capital investment on the land and in proportion to this increase of capital."

Marx then draws a consequential equivalence. Because the outcome — in respect of mass and value of product and surplus product — is identical whether the extra £2½ is added to the same acre or laid out on a second acre of the same quality, the whole surface-level distinction between a temporal addition of capital and a spatial addition of land temporarily dissolves: "the result would remain the same if the additional capital of £2½ per acre were invested on a second acre each of B, C and D." This equivalence is precisely what Case III will disrupt.

The sharp analytic nuance of this section is the demotion of arithmetic differences. In Differential Rent I, the difference in productivity between land types — measured at the same capital outlay — is what generates rent, and a change in that arithmetic difference signals a change in rent. Under DR-II, however, if the additional capital is not distributed uniformly, the simple arithmetical gaps between land revenues can shift markedly without any corresponding change in rent-determining productivity. Marx's example: if the extra capital goes solely to B and D, the difference between D and A in total output becomes 7 quarters (against 3 before), between B and A becomes 3 quarters (against 1), and between C and B turns negative — −1 instead of +1. Yet "this arithmetical difference, which is decisive in the case of differential rent I, in so far as it expresses the difference in productivity for the same capital investment, is here quite immaterial, since it is simply the result of different further investment or non-investment of capital, given the same difference for each equal portion of capital on the various lands." The lesson is durable: under DR-II, one must read the per-unit-of-capital proportional differences, not the totals, because the totals now encode the uneven deployment of capital rather than any natural property of the soil.

Case III — Declining Returns: Table III, the Falling Rate and the Rising Mass of Surplus Profit

The third case introduces the realistic scenario in which supplementary capital still produces surplus profit but "at a declining rate and not in proportion to their increase." This is the analytically richest case, and it generates Table III. Capital on B, C and D rises from £2½ to £5 per acre, but the second portion of outlay is less productive: B's second £2½ yields only 1½ quarters (instead of the 2 that strict proportionality would produce), C's yields 2 (instead of 3), and D's yields 3½ (instead of 4). Total outputs become 1 (A), 3½ (B), 5 (C) and 7½ (D); proceeds at £3 per quarter become £3, £10.50, £15 and £22.50; prices of production become £6 per acre on the better lands. Rents therefore become 1½, 3 and 5½ quarters — £4.50, £9 and £16.50.

Now compare the surplus-profit rates with Table I: B falls from 120 to 90 per cent; C from 240 to 180 per cent; D from 360 to 330 per cent. Every rate declines — yet every absolute rent rises (B from £3 to £4.50; C from £6 to £9; D from £9 to £16.50; the total from £18 to £30). Marx articulates the general law with a deliberate parallel to the falling tendency of the general rate of profit: "The rate of surplus profit declines, in relation both to the extra capital and the total capital invested on the land, but the absolute amount of surplus profit increases; just as the falling rate of profit on capital in general is usually combined with an increasing absolute mass of profit." He illustrates with land B: its average surplus profit is now 90 per cent on the capital deployed, against 120 per cent for the first outlay alone — yet the total surplus profit rises from 1 quarter to 1½ quarters, from £3 to £4.50. "The total rent taken by itself — and not in relation to the doubled sum of capital advanced — has risen absolutely."

Marx then emphasizes the generality of the result by stripping away all incidental detail: it is immaterial "whether the extra investments of capital put in second time round fall uniformly on the various types of land or not; whether the declining production of surplus profit proceeds in equal or unequal proportions; whether the additional capital investments all fall on the same rent-bearing type of land; or whether they are distributed, uniformly or not, on rent-bearing lands of different quality." The only assumption needed is that extra capital yields surplus profit, but in declining proportion.

He then fixes the bounds within which the decline must move. On the same capital investment, the maximum product attainable is 4 quarters (£12) — the product of the original capital on the best land D; the minimum is 1 quarter (£3) — the product of the same capital on the worst land A, which bears no rent and gives no surplus profit. Any marginal investment on a rent-bearing parcel must produce somewhere between 1 and 4 quarters if it is to yield some surplus (above 1 quarter) but less than the original (below the D-level maximum). Marx strengthens the point by redescribing Case III in the language of Case II: whereas Assumption II "implies that new plots of land of the same quality as the better types are added to the cultivated area," Assumption III "implies that additional plots of land are tilled whose degree of fertility varies between D and A, between that of the best land and that of the worst." The reason is intuitive: successive capital applications on a single parcel (say D) can traverse, one after another, productive capacities equivalent to the spectrum D → A — hence the equivalence to cultivating "virtual" new plots of intermediate quality. Successive investments confined to C, by contrast, can encompass only the differences between C and A and C and B; confined to B, only the difference between B and A.

The law of Case III is therefore that "the rent on all these types of land grows absolutely, even if not in proportion to the additional capital invested." And a final nuance caps the case: the differences between the rents of the several types, and their mutual ratios, may alter — but "this change in these differences is here the result of the wider spread of rents and not its cause." The shifting ratios are a symptom of differential intensification, not an independent source of rent; the underlying cause remains the augmented capital applied at a constant price.

Case IV, the Two Meanings of "Saving," and Released Capital

The fourth case — "extra capital investments on the better types of land produce a greater product than the original" — is dismissed briskly as self-evident: "It is immediately comprehensible how on this assumption the rents per acre rise, and in a higher ratio than the extra capital." The additional outlay is combined with an improvement, so rent rises faster than capital because both the base productivity and the marginal productivity have risen.

Marx then pauses to make a subtle distinction within Case IV: the case in which a small extra capital produces the same or greater effect than a previous, larger addition. Though it might be conflated with Case II, it is not identical, and the difference matters "for all capital investments." Suppose £100 yields £10 of profit (10 per cent), and £200 yields £40 (20 per cent). The rate has risen from 10 to 20 per cent — "and in this respect it is the same as if £50, applied in a more effective way, gave a profit of £10 instead of £5." The surface resemblance to Case II is that both show a rising rate; the real difference is that "in the one case I have to double the capital, while in the other I produce the doubled effect with the same capital as before."

To fix the distinction, Marx enumerates three possible configurations, all with the same value-cost outcome but radically different implications for wealth:

  1. the same product as before with half the living and objectified labour;
  2. double the previous product with the same labour;
  3. four times the previous product with twice the labour.

In the first configuration, labour is "set free" in either living or objectified form and can be applied elsewhere. Marx draws a conclusion of broad economic significance: "The release of capital (and labour) is in itself an increase in wealth; it has exactly the same effect as if this extra capital was obtained by accumulation, but it spares the task of accumulation." Release and accumulation are functionally equivalent in their wealth effect, but release is cheaper to obtain — it does not require the prior act of saving. This is one of the chapter's most portable analytical insights.

He then crystallizes the three configurations in a cloth example. A capital of £100 produces 10 metres of cloth; with equal constant and variable capital plus profit, the cost is £10 per metre. If the same £100 can produce 20 metres, the cost per metre falls to £5. If instead £50 can produce the same 10 metres, the cost is also £5 per metre — and a capital of £50 is now released, "in so far as the former supply is still sufficient." If, finally, £200 is required to produce 40 metres, the cost is still £5 per metre — but no capital is released. The distinction is that "in the first case capital is released; in the second case extra capital is spared, given that twice the production is required; in the third case the increased product can be obtained only by a growth in the capital advanced, although not in the same proportion as if the increased product had had to be supplied at the old level of productivity." Marx explicitly defers the value-and-price aspects of this analysis to "Part One" — acknowledging that his immediate concern is the rent-relevant distribution of the released resources, not the theory of value itself.

The Colonist's Innocence and the Capitalist's Inverted Cost Perception

Marx now shifts from the arithmetic of released capital to its ideological and subjective dimension. "Considered from the standpoint of capitalist production," he writes, a fall in the cost price has the same effect on the capitalist's profit as a rise in surplus-value itself, since profit is the residue of selling price over cost of production. It therefore appears "always cheaper to employ constant capital rather than variable." This presupposes "the development of credit and the abundance of loan capital that corresponds to the capitalist mode of production."

He sharpens the point with a value comparison. Take an additional constant capital of £100 — "the product of five workers over a year" — and an additional variable capital of £100. If the rate of surplus-value is 100 per cent, the five workers set in motion by the variable £100 produce £200 of value, whereas the £100 of constant capital is worth £100 (or perhaps £105 as a capital-claim if the rate of interest is 5 per cent). "The same sums of money express very different values." A further subtlety reinforces the point: if the £100 of constant capital is fixed capital, only its wear and tear enters the value of the commodity, whereas the £100 paid in wages must be completely reproduced in the commodity's value. Both facts make constant capital appear "cheaper" to the individual capitalist.

Contrast the colonist or independent petty producer, who has no access to capital or only at usurious rates. For such a producer, the portion of the product that represents wages is revenue, not a capital advance. The outlay of labour appears to be an indispensable precondition for the proceeds of that labour, and any labour beyond necessary labour realizes itself as excess product that appears to have "cost them nothing, as it has not cost any objectified labour." Only the expenditure of objectified labour is felt as "an alienation of wealth." Hence "even a sale below value and the capitalist price of production still seems to them a profit, as long as this profit is not anticipated by incurring any debt, mortgage, etc."

For the capitalist, in contrast, both constant and variable capital are advances. And because the relatively greater advance of constant capital reduces the cost price (and also reduces the value of commodities), the capitalist is led to the conclusion — Marx calls it a "capitalistically distorted form" — that living labour is "the most expensive element in his production costs, which should be reduced to the smallest possible minimum." Marx's verdict is normative and analytic at once: this is "simply a capitalistically distorted form of the correct statement that the relatively greater application of past labour, compared with living, means an increase in the productivity of social labour and greater social wealth." The correct statement is a statement about social productivity; the distorted form converts it into a statement about individual cost minimization, which then misidentifies the source of profit (living labour) as the item most to be economized. "This is how everything appears from the standpoint of competition: incorrectly, and standing on its head."

The Boundary Case of Land A and the Consistency of the Governing Assumption

Marx next specifies the limits of what can happen on land A itself, given the assumption of constant production prices. On the better lands (B and above), extra capital may be applied with constant, increasing or decreasing productivity. On A, however, only two cases are compatible with the constant-price premise: either productivity stays unchanged — in which case A "would continue to bear no rent" — or productivity increases, in which case "one part of the capital invested on land A would then bear rent, the other not." It would be "impossible on the assumption that A's productivity declines, for in that case the production price would not remain constant, but would rise." This is a self-consistency check on the chapter's own framework: if A — the regulating parcel — becomes less productive, its production price (cost plus average profit, per quarter) rises, and with it the whole price level; the chapter's foundational assumption collapses. The scope of what can happen to A is thus strictly bounded, and the boundary is set by the chapter's own premise, not by any empirical accident of soil.

Concentration versus Scattering: Why Successive Investment Raises Rent per Acre

From these preliminaries Marx extracts the chapter's most striking general conclusion. "Under all these circumstances," he writes — whether the surplus product brought in is proportionately above, below or equal to the proportion of capital added, and thus whether the surplus-profit rate stays constant, rises or falls — "the surplus product and the surplus profit per acre corresponding to it increase, and so too therefore, potentially, does the rent, in corn and in money." The reason is arithmetic but conceptually fundamental: rent per acre is the mass of surplus profit reckoned on a constant unit of land. As capital invested on the acre grows, the mass of surplus profit per acre grows with it (whether proportionally, more than proportionally, or less), and because the denominator — the acre — is fixed, the level of rent per acre necessarily rises. "The growth in the simple mass of surplus profit or rent, reckoned per acre ... is expressed as a growth in the proportion."

Crucially, the degree of the rise can be amplified or dampened by whether productivity is constant, falling or rising, but "the fact that it does grow" is independent of those factors: "The latter factors modify the degree to which the level of rent per acre grows, but not the fact that it does grow." This is the phenomenon peculiar to DR-II.

Marx drives the point home with a spatial-versus-temporal comparison. If additional capital is invested "alongside one another in space on new additional land of the appropriate quality," instead of "successively in time on the same land," then while the mass of rental and the average rent over the whole cultivated area both rise, the rent per acre — the rent level — does not. The old acres still yield what they always yielded; new acres add to the aggregate rather than the per-acre intensity. "With the result remaining the same, as far as the mass and value of the total production and the surplus product are concerned, the concentration of capital increases the level of rent per acre on a more restricted area, whereas under the same conditions its scattering over a greater area, with other factors remaining the same, could not produce this effect." Marx then derives a historical tendency: "The more the capitalist mode of production develops, however, the more the concentration of capital on the same area increases, so that the rent per acre rises." Rising rent per acre becomes, on this account, a structural feature of capitalist agricultural development, not merely a consequence of soil fertility or price movements.

He generalizes this into a cross-country theorem. Suppose two countries share the same production prices, the same land-type differences and the same total capital invested, but in one the capital is applied "more in the form of successive investments on a restricted area" and in the other "more in the form of coordinated investments on a wider area." Then "the rent per acre and therefore the land price would be higher in the first country and lower in the second, even though the total rental in both countries was the same." Since land price is essentially capitalized rent, higher rent per acre translates into a higher land price. "This difference in the levels of rent could thus be explained neither in terms of a difference in the natural fertility of the land types nor in the amount of labour applied, but exclusively in terms of the different kind of capital investments." This is the chapter's most theoretically aggressive claim: two economies could be identical in natural endowment, price level and aggregate capital, and yet exhibit different rents per acre and land prices, purely as a function of the organizational form — intensive versus extensive — of capital deployment.

Terminological Caveat: Surplus Product as an Aliquot Portion

The chapter closes with a terminological clarification that guards against a recurring confusion. "In speaking of a surplus product here, we mean the aliquot portion of the product in which the surplus profit is expressed." The word "aliquot" here carries its precise mathematical sense: a proportional part of a whole — the fraction of the physical output that corresponds to the surplus profit specifically, i.e., to rent. In general usage, however, "surplus product" means "the portion of the product in which the total surplus-value is expressed, or in particular cases the portion that represents the average profit." Because rent theory uses the narrower, rent-specific sense — the aliquot part embodying only the surplus over average profit — while general surplus-value theory uses the broader sense, "the specific meaning that this term obtains in the case of rent-bearing capital can give rise to misunderstandings, as we saw previously." The caveat is not pedantry; it is a warning that the reader must not conflate two distinct quantities — the portion of output representing rent (an aliquot differential) and the portion representing total surplus-value (average profit plus rent) — each of which might casually be called the "surplus product."

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter establishes a principle that operates almost as a systemic law within Marx's rent theory: differential rent is a function of the relationship between the mass of capital applied at a fixed price and the fixed unit of land on which rent is measured. Because rent per acre divides the surplus profit yielded on a given plot by that plot's constant extent, any increase in capital applied — under constant prices — mechanically raises the denominator-relative numerator, i.e., the rent level. This is why the direction of productivity change matters for degree but not for the fact of rising rent per acre. Case II (proportional productivity), Case III (declining productivity) and Case IV (increasing productivity) all generate higher rents per acre; they differ only in how much higher. Case I is the exception that proves the rule: with no surplus profit earned, no rent can rise. Marx's parallel to the falling rate of profit with rising absolute mass of profit is not decorative; it signals that rent theory obeys the same dialectic of rate and mass that governs capital in general — a rate may fall while an absolute magnitude rises.

Two further principles emerge. The first is the demotion of arithmetic differences under DR-II: the simple gap between land revenues that determines rent under DR-I ceases to determine anything once capital deployments diverge, because the gap then encodes the pattern of investment rather than native soil quality. The second is the equivalence of released capital with accumulation: a productivity gain that frees capital has "exactly the same effect as if this extra capital was obtained by accumulation," yet spares the social labour of accumulation — an insight that anticipates modern treatment of technical progress as a form of capital-saving growth.

The chapter's most important broad implication is the spatial-versus-temporal theorem and its corollary: two economies identical in price level, land-type distribution and aggregate capital may nevertheless differ in rent per acre and land price solely because of differences in the organizational form of capital — intensive concentration versus extensive scattering. This has two striking consequences. First, land price cannot be reduced to natural fertility or to quantity of labour; it is partly an artifact of capital-organization form. Second, since capitalist development intensifies capital concentration on a given area, the historical tendency is toward rising rent per acre and rising land prices — an endogenous, not exogenous, result of the mode of production itself. Third, and most subversively, Marx exhumed an ideological structure within the cost-accounting perception of the capitalist: because constant capital enters the cost price only as wear and tear (if fixed) and because its advance is smaller relative to output than the advance of wages, the capitalist perceives living labour as the most expensive element of production — the very element, Marx insists, that is the source of profit. This is the "capitalistically distorted form" of the true proposition that increasing past labour relative to living raises the productivity of social labour. The chapter thus ties the technical arithmetic of rent to a critique of the capitalist's form of appearance: what appears as cost minimization and profit maximization is, on inspection, an inverted representation of the wealth-generating role of living labour and of the social productivity gains embodied in past labour.

Finally, the terminological caveat about the "aliquot portion" cautions that the very vocabulary of surplus product is polysemous, and that rent theory's localized use of the term must be kept distinct from its general use in surplus-value theory. Read as a whole, Chapter 41 is not only the analysis of the first case of Differential Rent II; it is a hinge — an analytic bridge from DR-I's fertility-difference logic to DR-II's capital-intensity logic — and a demonstration that some of the most consequential economic magnitudes (rent level, land price) depend on how capital is organized in space and time, not merely on how much of it exists.
