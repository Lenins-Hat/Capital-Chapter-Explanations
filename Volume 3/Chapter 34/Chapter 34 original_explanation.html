Volume 3 • Chapter 34: The Currency Principle and the English Bank Legislation of 1844

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 34 of Capital Volume 3 — "The Currency Principle and the English Bank Legislation of 1844" — constitutes one of Marx's most concentrated exercises in the critique of political economy, operating simultaneously on three interlocking fronts: theoretical (the demolition of Ricardo's quantity theory of money), doctrinal (the exposure of the Currency School's dogma as a tautology dressed in causal clothing), and institutional (the empirical indictment of Sir Robert Peel's Bank Acts of 1844–45 as a mechanism for transferring the profits of industry into the pockets of usurers). The chapter's central problem is deceptively narrow — how did a specific, flawed monetary theory acquire the force of law and govern the banking system of the world's most advanced capitalist economy? — but its intellectual objective is far broader: to demonstrate that the appearance of scientific rigor in bourgeois economics frequently conceals a self-serving circularity, and that the translation of such circularity into legislation produces not stability but intensified crisis.

The chapter's core thesis can be stated as follows. Ricardo's theory of the value of money — that the value of metal money is determined by labour-time objectified in it, so long as the quantity of money stands in the right proportion to the quantity and price of commodities to be exchanged — contains a genuine insight (the possibility that the aggregate means of circulation may appreciate or depreciate as a whole) that was pressed into service by Lord Overstone, Colonel Torrens, Norman, Clay, Arbuthnot, and the "currency school" as the foundation for Peel's Bank Acts. But the theory, as deployed by the currency school, is not a causal explanation at all: it is a tautology masquerading as one. "Prices rise and fall periodically, because periodically there is too much or too little money in circulation." The Currency School's practical program — to regulate the issue of banknotes strictly according to the import and export of precious metals, thereby imitating the supposed laws of a purely metallic currency — rests on Ricardo's wrong assumption "that gold is simply specie," i.e., that all imported gold augments the money in circulation and thus raises prices, and all exported gold decreases the quantity of specie and thus lowers prices. In fact, Marx argues, gold flows function primarily as fluctuations in the rate of interest, not as direct determinants of commodity prices; and the Bank Act, by separating the Bank of England into an Issue Department and a Banking Department and by rigidly linking the note issue to the metal reserve, withdraws means of circulation at the very moment when they are most urgently needed, thereby "directly provoking the entire world of commerce into meeting the outbreak of a crisis by putting aside a reserve stock of banknotes," accelerating and intensifying the crisis and driving the interest rate "up to a previously unheard-of level." Instead of abolishing crises, the Act intensifies them to a point at which either "the entire world of industry has to collapse, or else the Bank Act" — and on two occasions (25 October 1847 and 12 November 1857), the government was compelled to suspend the Act to save the economy.

The chapter fits into the broader architecture of Capital Volume 3 as a bridge between the theory of credit (developed in the preceding chapters, particularly Chapter 26 on the banking system and Chapter 28 on the relationship between money and capital) and the analysis of the "division of profit into interest and profit of enterprise" (the section of which this chapter is a part). It is a case study — indeed, the most detailed historical case study in the volume — of the way in which the specific forms of the credit system (the separation of money-dealing capital from industrial capital, the transformation of gold into a "reserve," the operation of the discount rate) generate ideological illusions that then feedback into legislation, which in turn exacerbates the very contradictions of the capitalist mode of production. The chapter thus operates at the intersection of economic theory, intellectual history, and political economy: it shows how a theory becomes an "ideology" in the precise Marxian sense — a set of ideas that reflects and serves particular class interests while claiming universal validity.

Crucially, the chapter is also a sustained exercise in empirical argumentation. Marx does not merely assert that the currency theory is false; he mobilizes the testimony of bankers, merchants, and manufacturers before the House of Commons Committee of 1857 (B. A. 1857), the House of Lords Committee of 1848 (C. D. 1848–57), and produces tabular data (Hubbard's tables of 1834–43 and 1844–53) to demonstrate that commodity prices were "entirely independent of the amount of circulation" and that the only systematic relationship was between gold flows and the rate of interest. The chapter's rhetorical structure — alternating between theoretical critique, institutional description, and witness testimony — is designed to produce a cumulative, overwhelming effect, culminating in the bitter irony of Overstone's dithyramb to the Committee on 14 July 1857 and its "antistrophe" on 12 November 1857, when the government suspended "the miracle-working act of 1844, in order to save what could still be saved."

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Opening Frame: Ricardo's Quantity Theory and Its "Discovery"

The chapter opens with an editorial parenthesis marked "(F. E.)" — indicating the hand of Friedrich Engels, who edited Volume 3 after Marx's death — that refers back to Marx's earlier work, A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859), specifically pages 169ff. Engels explains that Marx had already investigated Ricardo's theory of the value of money in relation to commodity prices in that earlier text, so the present chapter can confine itself to "what is most essential." This is not merely a scholarly cross-reference; it establishes the genealogical character of the chapter. Marx (or Engels, in this editorial framing) is tracing a specific theoretical lineage: Ricardo's monetary doctrine → the Currency School → Peel's Bank Acts. The point is to show that the legislation of 1844 is the practical implementation of a theoretical error, and that the error can only be understood by returning to its origin.

The opening paragraph lays out Ricardo's position with precision. According to Ricardo, the value of (metal) money is determined by the labour-time objectified in it, but only as long as the quantity of money stands in the right proportion to the quantity and price of the commodities to be exchanged. This "but only as long as" clause is the hinge on which everything turns. If the quantity of money rises above this proportion, its value falls and commodity prices rise; if it falls below the right proportion, its value rises and commodity prices fall — "as long as other factors remain the same." The mechanism of correction is the international flow of gold: a country with a surplus of gold (whose value has fallen) exports gold and imports commodities; a country where gold is priced above its value will see gold flow in, while its under-valued commodities flow to other markets where they can obtain normal prices.

Engels then quotes Marx's earlier formulation directly: "Since on these assumptions 'even gold in the form of coin or bullion can become a value-token representing a larger or smaller value than its own, it is obvious that any convertible banknotes that are in circulation must share the same fate.'" This is a crucial step. Although banknotes are convertible and their real value corresponds to their nominal value, "the aggregate currency consisting of metal and of convertible notes" may appreciate or depreciate if the total quantity rises above or falls below the level determined by the exchange-value of the commodities in circulation and the metallic value of gold. This depreciation — not of notes in relation to gold, but of gold and notes taken together, i.e., of the aggregate means of circulation of a country — is identified as "one of Ricardo's main discoveries, which Lord Overstone and Co. pressed into their service and turned into a fundamental principle of Sir Robert Peel's bank legislation of 1844 and 1845."

Here we encounter the first important conceptual distinction in the chapter. The depreciation Marx is describing is not the familiar phenomenon of inconvertible paper money losing value relative to gold (the phenomenon Hume confronted in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and Ricardo confronted during the Napoleonic wars). It is something subtler and, Marx argues, theoretically confused: the idea that the aggregate of gold plus convertible notes can appreciate or depreciate as a whole. This is what allows the Currency School to argue that even a purely metallic system can experience general price movements caused by "too much" or "too little" money, and that the same logic applies to a mixed system of gold and banknotes. Engels notes explicitly: "We do not need to repeat here the demonstration of the absurdity of this theory of Ricardo's that is given in that text. All that concerns us now is the form and manner in which Ricardo's doctrines were taken up and elaborated by the school of banking theorists who dictated Peel's Bank Acts." The chapter's scope is thus deliberately narrowed — not to refute Ricardo again, but to show how his doctrine was weaponized.

2.2 The Historical Shift: From Single Phenomena to World-Market Storms

The next conceptual beat marks a historical transition. "The commercial crises of the nineteenth century, and in particular the great crises of 1825 and 1836, did not lead to any further development of Ricardo's currency theory, but rather to new practical applications of it." This is a precise and important distinction. The crises did not generate new theory; they generated new uses for existing theory. The reason lies in the changed character of the phenomena. It was "no longer a matter of single economic phenomena — such as the depreciation of precious metals in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries confronting Hume, or the depreciation of paper currency during the eighteenth century and the beginning of the nineteenth confronting Ricardo — but of big storms on the world market, in which the antagonism of all the elements in the bourgeois process of production explodes." The crises of 1825 and 1836 were not local or sectoral; they were systemic convulsions of the world market. And characteristically, "the origin of these storms and the means of defence against them were sought within the sphere of currency, the most superficial and abstract sphere of this process."

This phrase — "the most superficial and abstract sphere of this process" — is a compressed statement of Marx's materialist method. Currency is "superficial" because it is the phenomenal form in which deeper contradictions (overproduction, the falling rate of profit, the anarchy of production) appear; it is "abstract" because it is the most mediated and generalized expression of the totality of capitalist relations. To seek the cause of crises in the sphere of currency is therefore to mistake the surface for the essence — a methodological error that is not innocent, because it directs attention away from the real causes of crisis (the antagonisms of the bourgeois process of production) and toward technical fixes (banking legislation) that cannot possibly address them.

The "theoretical assumption which actually serves the school of economic weather experts as their point of departure" is "the dogma that Ricardo had discovered the laws governing purely metallic currency." Marx's term "economic weather experts" (ökonomische Wetterkundige or similar) is a mocking designation for the Currency School theorists, who treated economic crises as meteorological events to be predicted and managed rather than as structural necessities. It was "thus left to them to subsume the circulation of credit money or banknotes under these laws."

2.3 The Tautology Critique: Ricardo's Theory as Circular Reasoning

The heart of Marx's theoretical critique comes in the discussion of the "most common and conspicuous phenomenon accompanying commercial crises": "a sudden fall in the general level of commodity prices occurring after a prolonged general rise of prices." Marx notes that a general fall of commodity prices may be expressed as a rise in the value of money relative to all other commodities, and a general rise of prices may be defined as a fall in the relative value of money. But — and here is the logical pivot — "Either of these statements describes the phenomenon but does not explain it... The different terminology has just as little effect on the task itself as a translation of the terms from German into English would have."

The critique is devastating in its simplicity. "Ricardo's monetary theory proved to be singularly apposite since it gave to a tautology the semblance of a causal relation." What is the cause of the general fall in commodity prices? It is the periodically occurring rise in the relative value of money. What is the cause of the recurrent general rise in commodity prices? It is the recurrent fall in the relative value of money. But "It would be just as correct to say that the recurrent rise and fall of prices is brought about by their recurrent rise and fall."

Once the tautology is converted into a causal relationship — "once the transformation of the tautology into a causal relationship is taken for granted, everything else follows easily." The rise in commodity prices is due to a fall in the value of money; the fall in the value of money is due to excessive currency (i.e., the amount of money in circulation rises above the level determined by its own intrinsic value and the intrinsic value of commodities); and the general fall of commodity prices is due to the value of money rising above its intrinsic value as a result of insufficient currency. "Prices therefore rise and fall periodically, because periodically there is too much or too little money in circulation."

Marx then exposes the unfalsifiability of this schema — a point that anticipates modern philosophy of science critiques of pseudoscience. "If it is proved, for instance, that the rise of prices coincided with a decreased amount of money in circulation, and the fall of prices with an increased amount, then it is nevertheless possible to assert that, in consequence of some reduction or increase — which can in no way be ascertained statistically — of commodities in circulation, the amount of money in circulation has relatively, though not absolutely, increased or decreased." The theory is rescued by the introduction of an unobservable variable: the quantity of commodities in circulation, which can always be invoked ad hoc to reconcile the theory with contrary evidence. This is the structure of a metaphysical rather than an empirical theory.

Marx then returns to Ricardo's own account of the purely metallic case to show that, even on Ricardo's own terms, the mechanism is self-correcting. An insufficient amount of currency brings about a fall in commodity prices; the fall stimulates an export of commodities; the export leads to an influx of money; the influx causes a rise in commodity prices. When there is excessive currency, the reverse occurs. "Since notwithstanding these general price movements, which arise from the very nature of Ricardo's metallic currency, their severe and vehement form, the form of crisis, belongs to periods with developed credit systems, it is clear that the issue of banknotes is not exactly governed by the laws of metallic currency." This is a crucial analytical step: the empirical phenomenon of crisis is qualitatively different from the theoretical phenomenon of metallic-currency price oscillation. The former is violent and destructive; the latter is gentle and self-correcting. The attempt to explain the former by the latter therefore fails on Ricardo's own premises.

2.4 The Currency School's Practical Program and Its Critics

From this theoretical demolition, Marx turns to the practical program. The remedy applicable to metallic currency is the import and export of precious metals, which are immediately thrown into circulation as coin, causing commodity prices to fall or rise. "The banks must now artificially exert the same influence on commodity prices by imitating the laws of metallic currency." If gold flows in from abroad, it is a proof that there is an insufficient amount of currency, that the value of money is too high and commodity prices too low — and banknotes must therefore be thrown into circulation in accordance with the newly imported gold. Conversely, banknotes must be taken out of circulation in accordance with an outflow of gold. "In other words the issue of banknotes must be regulated according to the import and export of the precious metals or according to the rate of exchange."

The theoretical error on which this practical program rests is now named explicitly: "Ricardo's wrong assumption that gold is simply specie and that consequently the whole of the imported gold is used to augment the money in circulation thus causing prices to rise, and that the whole of the gold exported represents a decrease in the amount of specie and thus causes prices to fall." This assumption is "turned into a practical experiment by making the amount of specie in circulation correspond always to the quantity of gold in the country."

The school's roster is then named: "Lord Overstone (Jones Loyd, the banker), Colonel Torrens, Norman, Clay, Arbuthnot and numerous other writers known in England as the 'currency school'." They "have not only preached this doctrine, but have made it the basis of the present English and Scottish banking legislation by means of Sir Robert Peel's Bank Acts of 1844 and 1845." Marx notes that "the analysis of the ignominious fiasco they suffered both in theory and practice, after experiments on the largest national scale, can only be made in the section dealing with the theory of credit." This is again a delimitation of scope: the full theoretical treatment of credit is elsewhere; what is pursued here is the historical and empirical indictment.

The chapter then notes the critics: "Thomas Tooke, James Wilson (in The Economist of 1844–7) and John Fullarton." Marx is careful to note that "We have already seen several times, particularly in Chapter 28 of this volume, how inadequately these critics penetrated the nature of gold and how unclear they were as to the relationship between money and capital." This is an important qualification: Marx is not simply aligning himself with the "banking school" against the "currency school." The critics, too, failed to grasp the fundamental issues. The remainder of the chapter is organized around witness testimony before the parliamentary committees, beginning with the House of Commons Committee of 1857 on the Peel Bank Acts (B. A. 1857).

2.5 Hubbard's Evidence and the Two Tables: The Independence of Commodity Prices

J. G. Hubbard, a former Governor of the Bank of England, testifies (no. 2400) that "The effect of the export of bullion... has no reference whatever to the prices of commodities. It has an effect, and a very important one, upon the price of interest-bearing securities, because, as the rate of interest varies, the value of commodities which embodied that interest is necessarily powerfully affected." This is a direct empirical refutation of the currency theory: gold flows affect the rate of interest, and through it the prices of securities (i.e., capitalized claims to income), but not the prices of commodities as such.

Hubbard produces two tables for the years 1834–43 and 1845–56 (the chapter presents the first two, 1834–43 and 1844–53), covering fifteen of the most important items of commerce. The items are: raw cotton, cotton yarn, cotton fabrics, wool, woollen cloth, flax, linen, indigo, pig-iron, tin, copper, tallow, sugar, coffee, and silk. For each date, the tables record the bullion reserve of the Bank, the market rate of discount, and the number of items whose price increased, decreased, or remained unchanged.

Table I (1834–43) shows, for example: on 1 March 1834, bullion reserve £9,104,000, discount rate 2¾%; on 1 March 1835, bullion £6,274,000 (a decrease), discount rate 3¾% (an increase), with 7 items rising, 7 falling, 1 unchanged; on 1 March 1836, bullion £7,918,000, discount 3¾–4%, 11 rising, 3 falling, 1 unchanged; on 1 March 1837, bullion £4,077,000, discount 5%, 5 rising, 9 falling, 1 unchanged. Table II (1844–53) shows: 1 March 1844, bullion £16,162,000, discount 2¼%; 1 Dec. 1845, bullion £13,237,000, discount 4½%, 11 rising, 4 falling; 1 Sept. 1846, bullion £16,366,000, discount 3%, 7 rising, 8 falling; 1 Sept. 1847, bullion £9,140,000, discount 6%, 6 rising, 6 falling, 3 unchanged; 1 March 1850, bullion £17,126,000, discount 2½%, 5 rising, 9 falling, 1 unchanged; 1 June 1851, bullion £13,705,000, discount 3%, 2 rising, 11 falling, 2 unchanged; 1 Sept. 1852, bullion £21,853,000, discount 1½%, 9 rising, 5 falling, 1 unchanged; 1 Dec. 1853, bullion £15,093,000, discount 5%, 14 rising, 1 unchanged.

Hubbard's comment on these tables is decisive: "As in the years 1834–43, so in the period 1844–53, the upward and downward movements in the bullion of the Bank have invariably been accompanied with a decrease or increase in the loanable value of money advanced on discount; and, as in the earlier period, so in this, the variations in the prices of commodities in this country exhibit an entire independence of the amount of circulation as shown in the fluctuations in bullion of the Bank of England." In other words: bullion flows move inversely with the discount rate (gold out = interest rate up; gold in = interest rate down), but commodity prices show no systematic relationship to either.

Marx's commentary draws the methodological conclusion. "Since the demand and supply of commodities regulates their market price, it is evident here how false is Overstone's identification of the demand for loanable money capital (or rather the gap between its demand and supply), as expressed in the discount rate, with the demand for real 'capital'." This is a critical conceptual distinction: the demand for loanable money capital (reflected in the discount rate) is not the same as the demand for real capital (means of production and labor-power). The currency school obscures the difference by claiming that fluctuations in the discount rate express fluctuations in the demand for "actual material capital, as distinct from money capital." Marx refers back to Chapter 26, where Norman and Overstone maintained this before the committee and "to what weak subterfuges they were forced to resort, particularly the latter until he was finally cornered."

The "old humbug" is then stated plainly: "the old humbug that changes in the quantity of gold, since they increase or decrease the amount of means of circulation in a country, must necessarily raise or lower commodity prices there." The currency theory's logic would imply that if gold is exported, commodity prices in the gold-importing country rise, and the value of the gold-exporting country's exports rise on the gold-importing country's market, while the latter's exports fall in value. But "In actual fact, a decline in the amount of gold simply raises the rate of interest, while an increase lowers it; and if these fluctuations in the interest rate did not come into account in establishing the cost price, or determining demand and supply, commodity prices would be completely unaffected."

2.6 The Silver Drain to Asia: N. Alexander's Testimony

Marx next introduces the testimony of N. Alexander, head of a big firm in the India trade, on the sharp drain of silver to India and China in the mid-1850s. This drain had two causes: partly the Chinese civil war, which stopped the supply of English fabrics to China, and partly the silkworm disease in Europe, which sharply reduced silk production in Italy and France.

Alexander testifies (no. 4337) that the silver was sent to India, where much of it was used to buy opium, which then went on to China "to lay down funds for the purchase of the silk"; and that "the state of the markets in India (in spite of the accumulation of silver there) makes it a more profitable investment for the merchant to lay down silver than to send piece-goods or English manufactures." (No. 4338) A great drain of silver from France also occurred. (No. 4344) "Instead of bringing in silk from France and Italy, we are sending it there in large quantities, both from Bengal and from China."

Marx's analytic point is sharp: "Thus silver was sent to Asia, where it is the money metal, instead of commodities, not because the price of these commodities had risen in the country that produced them (England), but because it had fallen — owing to excessive imports — in the country which imported them; even though England had to obtain this silver from France, and to pay for it partly with gold." This is a direct empirical refutation of the currency theory's prediction. "According to the currency theory, prices should have fallen in England and risen in India and China as a result of such imports." In fact, commodities were not sent because their prices were too high in England; silver was sent because commodity prices in the importing countries had fallen due to excessive imports. The direction of causation runs opposite to the theory.

2.7 Wylie's Testimony: The Cotton Example and the Self-Interest of Overstone's "Wisdom"

Wylie, one of the leading Liverpool merchants, testified before the House of Lords Committee (C. D. 1848–57). His testimony (no. 1994) describes the cotton-spinning boom of 1845: "At the close of 1845 there was no trade that was more remunerating, and in which there were such large profits" than cotton spinning. The stock of cotton was large and good; cotton could be bought at 4d. per pound; good secunda mule twist no. 40 was made at an expense not exceeding 8d. per pound in all to the spinner. This yarn was largely sold and contracted for in September and October 1845 at 10½ and 11½d. per pound, "and in some instances the spinners realized a profit equal to the first cost of the cotton." (No. 1996) The trade continued remunerative until the beginning of 1846. (No. 2000) "On 3 March 1844, the stock of cotton" (627,042 bales) "was more than double what it is this day" (on 7 March 1848, 301,070 bales), "and yet the price then was 6¼d. per pound dearer" (6¼d. as against 5d.). At the same time, yarn (good secunda mule twist no. 40) had fallen from 11½–12d. to 9½d. per lb. in October, and to 7¾d. at the end of December 1847; yarn was sold at the purchase price of the cotton from which it had been spun (nos. 2021, 2022).

Marx draws the inference: "This exposes the self-interest in Overstone's wisdom that money is 'dear' because capital is 'scarce'." The data show that the Bank rate stood at 3 per cent on 3 March 1844; rose to 8 and 9 per cent in October and November 1847; and fell to 4 per cent on 7 March 1848. "Owing to the complete stagnation in sales and the panic, with its correspondingly high rate of interest, cotton prices had been driven far below the level corresponding to the supply." The result was a tremendous decline in imports in 1848 and a decline in production in America, hence a new rise in cotton prices in 1849. "According to Overstone, the reason why commodities were too dear was that there was too much money in the country."

Wylie's testimony (no. 2002) states: "The late decline in the condition of the cotton manufacturers is not to be ascribed to the want of the raw material, as the price seems to have been lower, though the stock of the raw material is very much diminished." Marx comments: "But Overstone conveniently confuses the price or value of a commodity with the value of money, i.e. the rate of interest." This is a conceptual confusion central to the currency theory: the "dearness" of money (high interest rate) is conflated with the "dearness" of capital (scarcity of real capital).

In answer to question 2026, Wylie delivers his overall verdict on the currency theory: "These principles seemed to me to be of a nature that would give an artificial high value to money and an artificial and ruinously low value to all commodities and produce." This verdict was relevant because Cardwell and Sir Charles Wood (the latter, Sir Charles Wood, later Viscount Halifax, was Chancellor of the Exchequer at the time; Edward, Viscount Cardwell, was a Peelite) in May 1847 "asserted the necessity of carrying out the Bank Act of 1844 in its full and entire integrity." (The editorial footnote explains these identities.) Wylie goes on to say of the effects of the Bank Act on the general state of business: "As bills at four months, which is the regular course of drafts, from manufacturing towns on merchants and bankers for the purchase of goods going to the United States, could not be discounted except at great sacrifices, the execution of orders was checked to a great extent, until after the Government Letter of 25 October" (suspension of the Bank Act), "when those four months' bills became discountable" (2097). The suspension of the Bank Act "brought salvation for the provinces, too." (No. 2102) "Last October [1847] there was scarcely an American buyer purchasing goods here who did not at once curtail his orders as much as he possibly could; and when our advices of the dearness of money reached America, all fresh orders ceased." (No. 2134) "Corn and sugar were special. The corn market was affected by the prospects of the harvest, and sugar was affected by the immense stocks and imports." (No. 2163) "Of our indebtedness to America... much was liquidated by forced sales of consigned goods, and I fear that much was cancelled by the failures here." (No. 2196) "If I recollect rightly, 70 per cent was paid on our Stock Exchange in October 1847."

2.8 The Ideological Conditions for the Bank Act's Passage

An editorial parenthesis (F. E.) inserts an assessment of the historical conditions that allowed the currency school's dogma to be translated into practice. "The crisis of 1837 with its long aftermath, added to which was a further regular crisis in 1842, and the self-interested blindness of the industrialists and merchants, who were resolved not to notice any overproduction — this was all nonsense and an impossibility, said the vulgar economists! — all this finally brought about the mental confusion that allowed the currency school to translate their dogma into practice on a national scale. And thus the Bank legislation of 1844–5 went through." The passage is significant because it identifies the ideological mechanism: the refusal to recognize overproduction (which the "vulgar economists" treated as impossible) created a "mental confusion" that made the currency school's technical solution seem plausible. If crises cannot be caused by overproduction, they must be caused by something else — and the most superficial and abstract sphere, the sphere of currency, is the natural candidate.

2.9 The Mechanics of the 1844 Bank Act

Marx then provides a detailed institutional description of the 1844 Bank Act. The Act divides the Bank of England into two departments: the Issue Department and the Banking Department. The Issue Department receives securities — for the most part government stock — for £14 million, as well as the entire metal reserve, of which no more than one quarter may consist of silver, and issues a sum of notes corresponding to this total amount. "In so far as these are not in the hands of the public, they remain in the Banking Department and form its ever ready reserve, together with the small amount of coin needed for everyday use (about £1 million)." The Issue Department gives the public gold for notes and notes for gold; all other dealings with the public are the concern of the Banking Department. Private banks in England and Wales entitled in 1844 to issue their own notes retain this right, but their note issue is fixed. If one of these banks ceases to issue its own notes, the Bank of England can increase its own fiduciary note issue by two-thirds of the quota thus made available; in this way its issue was increased, by 1892, from £14 million to £16½ million (£16,450,000 to be precise).

The mechanical operation is stated in a formula-like passage: "Thus, for every £5 that leaves the Bank's reserve in gold, a £5 note comes back to the Issue Department and is destroyed; for every five sovereigns that come into the reserve, a new £5 note is put into circulation. This is how Overstone's ideal paper circulation, governed precisely by the laws of metal circulation, is carried out in practice, and in this way, according to the currency people's contention, crises are made impossible once and for all."

The critical inversion follows immediately. "In reality, however, the separation of the Bank into two independent departments withdrew the directors' power of free disposal of their entire available means at decisive moments, so that situations could come about in which the Banking Department was faced with bankruptcy while the Issue Department still had several millions in gold, and besides this its £14 million intact in securities." This "could happen all the more easily in so far as every crisis has a point marked by a sharp drain of gold abroad, which has principally to be covered by the Bank's metal reserve." But for every £5 that flows abroad, a £5 note is withdrawn from circulation at home, "so that the amount of means of circulation is reduced at the very moment when most is required, and with the greatest urgency at that." The Act thus "directly provokes the entire world of commerce into meeting the outbreak of a crisis by putting aside a reserve stock of banknotes, thereby accelerating and intensifying the crisis." By artificially intensifying the demand for monetary accommodation at the same time as the supply is declining, it drives the interest rate in crisis times up to a previously unheard-of level.

The result is the dialectic of the suspension. "Instead of abolishing crises, it rather intensifies them to a point at which either the entire world of industry has to collapse, or else the Bank Act." On two occasions — 25 October 1847 and 12 November 1857 — the crisis reached such a height that the government freed the Bank from the restriction on its note issue by suspending the Act, "and this was sufficient on both occasions to curb the crisis." In 1847, "the certainty that banknotes could now once more be had in exchange for first-class securities was sufficient to bring £4–£5 million in notes back to the light of day and into active circulation; in 1857 rather less than £1 million in notes were issued above the legal quota, but only for a very short time."

2.10 The Fear of Banknote Depreciation and the 1825 Precedent

An editorial parenthesis (F. E.) notes that "some parts of the legislation of 1844 reflected the memory of the first twenty years of the century, the time when the Bank suspended cash payment and banknotes depreciated in value. The fear that banknotes might lose their credit is still very much apparent here; a very excessive fear, since as early as 1825 the issue of a rediscovered supply of old £1 notes, which had been taken out of circulation, curbed the crisis and thereby showed that even at that period the credit of these banknotes remained unimpaired, even at a time of most general and pronounced lack of confidence. And this is quite comprehensible, for in fact the entire nation and its credit stands behind these tokens."

This is an important conceptual point about the nature of credit money. The "credit" of banknotes does not rest on the gold reserve backing them; it rests on "the entire nation and its credit." The 1825 episode demonstrates that even in a moment of "most general and pronounced lack of confidence," the rediscovery and reissue of £1 notes could curb the crisis — evidence that the notes' credit was intact. The fear that motivates the 1844 Act is therefore both empirically unfounded and conceptually confused: it mistakes the form of convertibility (the gold reserve) for the substance of credit (the productive power of the nation).

2.11 John Stuart Mill's Testimony and Engels' Critique

Marx now turns to "a few witnesses on the effect of the Bank Act." The first is John Stuart Mill, who "believed that the Bank Act of 1844 had kept down over-speculation." Mill gave his evidence on 12 June 1857; "Four months later, the crisis broke out." The juxtaposition is deliberate and devastating. Mill "literally congratulated the 'bank directors and the commercial public generally' on the fact that they 'understand much better than they did the nature of a commercial crisis, and the extreme mischief which they do both to themselves and to the public by upholding over-speculation'" (B. A. 1857, no. 2031).

Mill also testified (no. 2066) that if £1 notes were issued "as advances to manufacturers and others, who pay wages... the notes may get into the hands of others who expend them for consumption, and in that case the notes do constitute in themselves a demand for commodities and may for some time tend to promote a rise of prices." Marx responds with a series of rhetorical questions: "Does Mr Mill thus assume that the manufacturers would pay higher wages if they paid them in paper instead of in gold? Or does he believe that if the manufacturer obtained his advance in £100 notes and exchanged these for gold, these wages would then form less demand than if paid in £1 notes? Does he not know that in certain mining districts, for example, wages actually are paid in notes from local banks, so that several workers receive a £5 note together? Does this increase their demand? Or are bankers easier with manufacturers in small notes, advancing more money than in larger denominations?"

The editorial parenthesis (F. E.) explains Mill's position: "This peculiar fear that Mill had of £1 notes would be inexplicable if his entire work on political economy did not exhibit an eclecticism which never flinches from any contradictions. On the one hand, he supports Tooke on many points against Overstone, while on the other hand he believes commodity prices are determined by the amount of money present. He is thus in no way convinced that for each £1 issued — all other things remaining the same — a sovereign finds its way into the Bank's reserve; he fears that the quantity of means of circulation might be increased and thereby devalued, so that commodity prices would rise. It is this and nothing else that is hidden behind his above caution." Mill is thus presented as an eclectic who combines elements of the banking school (Tooke) with elements of the currency theory, and whose fear of £1 notes reveals a persistent attachment to the quantity theory. The critique of Mill is significant because Mill was the most authoritative economist of the mid-nineteenth century; his inability to break with the quantity theory demonstrates the ideological hold of the currency doctrine.

2.12 Tooke and Newmarch: The Separation of Departments and the Fluctuations of Interest

Tooke makes the following statements to the C. D. 1848–57. The greater fluctuations of the interest rate in 1847, as compared with 1837 and 1839, are due "solely to the separation of the Bank into two departments" (3010). The safety of banknotes was affected neither in 1825 nor in 1837 and 1839 (3015). The demand for gold in 1825 was aimed only at filling the vacuum created by the complete discredit of the £1 notes of the country banks; this vacuum could be filled only by gold, until the Bank of England also issued £1 notes (3022). In November and December 1825, "not the slightest demand existed for gold for export purposes" (3023). "In point of discredit at home as well as abroad. A failure in paying the dividends and the deposits would be of far greater consequence than the suspending of the payment of banknotes" (3028). In response to question 3035 — whether any circumstance endangering the convertibility of the note would add serious difficulty in a moment of commercial pressure — Tooke answers: "Not at all." And (3058): "In the course of 1847 ... an increased issue from the circulating department might have contributed to replenish the coffers of the Bank, as it did in 1825."

Before the Committee on B. A. 1857, Newmarch states (no. 1357): "The first mischievous effect ... of that separation of departments" (of the Bank) "and ... a necessary consequence from the cutting in two of the reserve of bullion has been that the banking business of the Bank of England, that is to say, the whole of that part of the operation of the Bank of England which brings it more immediately into contact with the commerce of the country, has been carried on upon a moiety only of its former amounts of reserve. Out of that division of the reserve has arisen, therefore, this state of things, that whenever the reserve of the Banking Department has been diminished, even to a small extent, it has rendered necessary an action by the Bank upon its rate of discount. That diminished reserve, therefore, has produced a frequent succession of changes and jerks in the rate of discount." (No. 1358) "The alterations since 1844" (until June 1857) "have been some sixty in number, whereas the alterations prior to 1844 in the same space of time certainly did not amount to a dozen."

The statistical comparison — sixty changes in the discount rate since 1844 versus fewer than a dozen before — is an empirical demonstration of the Act's destabilizing effect. The separation of departments, by halving the effective reserve, subjects the banking business to frequent "changes and jerks" in the rate of discount, which is precisely the opposite of the stability the Act was supposed to ensure.

2.13 Palmer's Evidence: The Intention to Make Money Dear

Palmer's evidence to the Lords Committee (C. D. 1848–57) is particularly weighty because he had been a director of the Bank of England since 1811 and Governor for a period. His testimony is a detailed historical demonstration of what would have happened had the 1844 Act existed at earlier moments of crisis. (No. 828) "In December 1825, there was about £1,100,000 of bullion remaining in the Bank. At that period it must undoubtedly have failed in toto, if this Act had been in existence" (meaning the Act of 1844). "The issue in December, I think, was 5 or 6 millions of notes in a week, which relieved the panic that existed at that period." (No. 825) The first period since 1 July 1825 when the present Act would have failed was on 28 February 1837, when there were £3,900,000 to £4,000,000 of bullion in the Bank's possession, and the Bank would have been left with £650,000 only in the reserve. Another period is 1839, from 9 July to 5 December. (No. 826) "The reserve was minus altogether £200,000 upon the 5th of September. On the 5th of November it rose to about a million or a million and a half." (No. 830) "The Act of 1844 would have prevented the Bank giving assistance to the American trade in 1837." (No. 831) "There were three of the principal American houses that failed... Almost every house connected with America was in a state of discredit, and unless the Bank had come forward at that period, I do not believe that there would have been more than one or two houses that could have sustained themselves." (No. 836) "The pressure in 1837 is not to be compared with that of 1847. The pressure in the former year was chiefly confined to the American trade." (No. 838) Early in June 1837, the management of the Bank discussed the question of overcoming the pressure: "Some gentlemen advocated the opinion ... that the correct principle was to raise the rate of interest, by which the price of commodities would be lowered; in short, to make money dear and commodities cheap, by which the foreign payment would be accomplished."

This last statement is crucial: it reveals the class program behind the "technical" theory. The Bank's directors consciously advocated a policy of making money dear and commodities cheap. This is not a neutral monetary rule; it is a mechanism for transferring income from debtors (manufacturers, merchants) to creditors (bankers, money-capitalists). (No. 906) "The establishment of an artificial limitation of the powers of the Bank under the Act of 1844, instead of the ancient and natural limitation of the Bank's powers, namely, the actual amount of its specie, tends to create artificial difficulty, and therefore an operation upon the prices of merchandise that would have been unnecessary but for the provisions of the Act." (No. 968) "You cannot, by the working of the Act of 1844, materially reduce the bullion, under ordinary circumstances, below nine million and a half. It would then cause a pressure upon prices and credit which would occasion such an advance in the exchange with foreign countries as to increase the import of bullion, and to that extent add to the amount in the Issue Department." (No. 996) "Under the limitation that you" (the Bank) "are now subject to, you have not the command of silver to an extent that you require at a time when silver would be required for an action upon the foreign exchanges." (No. 999) In response to the question of what was the object of the regulation restricting the Bank as to the amount of silver to one-fifth, Palmer states: "I cannot answer that question."

Marx comments: "The intention was to make money dear." This is the analytical key to the entire chapter: the Act's provisions — the £14 million ceiling on fiduciary note issue, the administration of the Banking Department as an ordinary bank (forcing interest down when money is plentiful, up when scarce), the restriction of the silver reserve (the principal means of rectifying exchange rates with the Continent and Asia), the regulations for Scottish and Irish banks (which never need gold for export but must now keep it under the pretext of a convertibility of their notes which is "pure illusion") — all "lead to raising the rate of interest." The Act of 1845 produced the first run on the Scottish banks for gold, in 1857. The legislation "also makes no distinction between a drain of gold abroad and a domestic drain, even though the effects of these two things are self-evidently quite different. Hence the constant and violent fluctuations in the market rate of interest."

On silver, Palmer says twice (992, 994) that the Bank can buy silver in exchange for notes only if the exchange rate is in England's favour, so that there is an excess of silver. (No. 1003) "The only object in holding a considerable amount of bullion in silver is to facilitate making the foreign payment so long as the exchanges are against the country." (No. 1004) "Silver is ... a commodity which, being money in every other part of the world, is therefore the most direct commodity ... for the purpose" (of payments abroad). "The United States latterly have taken gold alone." In Palmer's opinion, the Bank did not need to raise the interest rate above its old level of 5 per cent in times of pressure, as long as gold was not drawn abroad by an unfavourable rate of exchange. Were it not for the Act of 1844, all first-class bills could still be discounted without difficulty at the old rate (1018–20). But with the 1844 Act and the situation in which the Bank found itself in October 1847, "there was no rate of interest which the Bank could have charged to houses of credit, which they would not have been willing to pay to carry on their payments." Marx comments: "And this high rate of interest was precisely the aim of the Act."

Palmer draws (no. 1029) "a great distinction which I wish to draw between the action of the rate of interest upon a foreign demand" (for precious metal) "and an advance in the rate for the object of checking a demand upon the Bank during a period of internal discredit." (No. 1023) "Previously to the Act of 1844 ... when the exchanges were in favour of the country, and positive panic and alarm existed through the country, there was no limit put upon the issue, by which alone that state of distress could be relieved." Marx introduces these statements with the observation: "These are the words of a man who was a director of the Bank of England for thirty-nine years."

2.14 Twells: The Private Banker's Perspective

Marx next introduces Twells, "a private banker, a partner in Spooner, Attwood and Co. since 1801." He is described as "the only one out of all these witnesses before the B. A. 1857 who provides us with any insight into the actual state of affairs in the country, and sees the crisis impending." In other respects, however, "he is a kind of Birmingham 'little shilling man', like his partners, the Attwood brothers, who are the founders of this school." (The reference is to the Birmingham currency school of the 1820s–30s, which advocated a depreciated currency to stimulate industry. See Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, p. 82.)

Twells states (no. 4488): "How do you think that the Act of 1844 has operated? — If I were to answer you as a banker, I should say that it has operated exceedingly well, for it has afforded a rich harvest to bankers and" (money-) "capitalists of all kinds. But it has operated very badly to the honest industrious tradesman who requires steadiness in the rate of discount, that he may be enabled to make his arrangements with confidence ... It has made money-lending a most profitable pursuit." (No. 4489) "It" (the Bank Act) "enables the London joint-stock banks to return from 20 to 22 per cent to their proprietors? — The other day one of them was paying 18 per cent and I think another 20 per cent; they ought to support the Act of 1844 very strongly." (No. 4490) "The little tradesmen and respectable merchants, who have not a large capital ... it pinches them very much indeed ... The only means that I have of knowing is that I observe such an amazing quantity of their acceptances unpaid. They are always small, perhaps ranging from £20 to £100, a great many of them are unpaid and go back unpaid to all parts of the country, which is always an indication of suffering amongst ... little shopkeepers."

This is a class analysis in the testimony of a banker: the Act benefits "bankers and money-capitalists of all kinds" (with returns of 18–22% to joint-stock bank proprietors) while "pinching" small tradesmen and merchants. In 4494, Twells explains that business is presently unprofitable. His remarks are important because "he saw the latent presence of the crisis when none of the others suspected it." (No. 4494) "Things keep their prices in Mincing Lane, but we sell nothing, we cannot sell upon any terms; we keep the nominal price." (No. 4495) He relates a particular case: a Frenchman sends a broker in Mincing Lane commodities for £3,000 to sell at a given price. The broker cannot obtain this price, and the Frenchman cannot sell below the price. The commodities are still around, but the Frenchman needs money. The broker therefore advances him £1,000, the Frenchman drawing a three-month bill of exchange on the broker for £1,000, with the goods as security. Three months later the bill falls due, but the commodities are still unsaleable. The broker then has to pay the bill, and although he has security for £3,000, he cannot cash this and gets into difficulties. "Thus one person drags another down with him."

(No. 4496) "With regard to the large exports ... where there is a depressed state of trade at home, it necessarily forces large exportation." (No. 4497) In response to the question of whether home consumption has diminished: "Very much indeed ... immensely ... the shopkeepers are the best authorities." (No. 4498) "Still the importations are very large, does not that indicate a large consumption? — It does, if you can sell; but many of the warehouses are full of these things; in this very instance which I have been relating, there is £3,000 worth imported, which cannot be sold." (No. 4514) "When money is dear, would you say that capital would be cheap? — Yes."

Marx comments: "This fellow thus in no way shares Overstone's opinion that high interest is the same thing as dear capital." Twells's testimony on business practice (no. 4616) is also important: "Others are going to a very great extent, carrying on a prodigious trade in exports and imports, to an extent far beyond what their capital justifies them in doing; there can be no doubt of all of that. These men may succeed; they may by some lucky venture get large fortunes, and put themselves right. That is very much the system in which a great deal of trade is now carried on. Persons will consent to lose 20, 30, and 40 per cent upon a shipment; the next venture may bring it back to them. If they fail in one after another, then they are broken up; and that is just the case which we have often seen recently; mercantile houses have broken up, without one shilling of property being left." This describes the speculative, over-leveraged character of mid-Victorian trade — a structural feature of the credit system that the Bank Act does nothing to address and arguably exacerbates.

(No. 4791) "The low rate of interest" (during the previous ten years) "operates against bankers, it is true, but I should have very great difficulty in explaining to you, unless I could show you the books, how much higher the profits" (his own) "are now than they used to be formerly. When interest is low, from excessive issues, we have large deposits; when interest is high, we get the advantage in that way." (No. 4794) "When money is at a moderate rate, we have more demand for it; we lend more; it operates in that way" (for us, the bankers). "When it gets higher, we get more than a fair proportion for it; we get more than we ought to do." This is a frank admission of the bankers' class interest in interest-rate volatility: whether rates are high or low, the banking system profits.

2.15 Brown and Capps: The Reserve as Waste and the Act as Usurers' Scheme

Marx notes: "We have seen how all the experts consider the credit of Bank of England notes as unshakeable. Nevertheless, the Bank Act ties up absolutely some £9 to £10 million in gold, for the convertibility of these notes. The sanctity and inviolability of the reserve is thereby carried much farther than among the hoarders of old." W. Brown (Liverpool) testifies (C. D. 1848–57, no. 2311): "This money" (the metal reserve in the Issue Department) "might as well have been thrown into the sea from any use that it was of at that time, there being no power to employ any of it without violating the Act of Parliament."

The building contractor E. Capps, "whom we already met with earlier, and from whose evidence we took the description of the modern building system in London (Volume 2, Chapter 12)," gives his opinion of the 1844 Bank Act: (No. 5508) "Then upon the whole ... you think that the present system" (of bank legislation) "is a somewhat adroit scheme for bringing the profits of industry periodically into the usurer's bag? — I think so. I know that it has operated so in the building trade." This is perhaps the single most direct statement of the chapter's political-economic thesis: the Bank Act is "a somewhat adroit scheme for bringing the profits of industry periodically into the usurer's bag." The phrase "periodically" is important: the mechanism operates through the cycle of crisis, in which high interest rates transfer income from industrial capital to money-capital.

2.16 The Scottish Banks and the 1845 Act

The 1845 Bank Act forced the Scottish banks into a system closer to the English. They were obliged to hold gold in reserve for any note issue beyond a limit fixed for each bank. The effect is shown by witnesses before the B. A. 1857. Kennedy, director of a Scottish bank, testifies (no. 3375): "Was there anything that you can call a circulation of gold in Scotland previously to the passing of the Act of 1845? — None whatever." (No. 3376) "Has there been any additional circulation of gold since? — None whatever; the people dislike gold." (No. 3450) The sum of about £900,000 in gold which the Scottish banks are compelled to keep since 1845 "can only be injurious in his opinion and 'absorbs unprofitably so much of the capital of Scotland'."

Anderson, director of the Union Bank of Scotland, testifies (no. 3588): "The only pressure upon the Bank of England by the banks in Scotland for gold was for foreign exchanges? — It was; and that is not to be relieved by holding gold in Edinburgh." (No. 3590) "Having the same amount of securities in the Bank of England" (or in the private banks of England), "we have the same power that we had before of making a drain upon the Bank of England."

Finally, Marx quotes an article from The Economist (Wilson): "The Scotch banks keep unemployed amounts of cash with their London agents; these keep them in the Bank of England. This gives to the Scotch banks, within the limits of these amounts, command over the metal reserve of the Bank, and here it is always in the place where it is needed, when foreign payments are to be made." This system was upset by the 1845 Act. In consequence of the Act of 1845 for Scotland, "of late a large drain of the coin of the Bank has taken place, to supply a mere contingent demand in Scotland, which may never occur ... Since that period there has been a large sum uniformly locked up in Scotland, and another considerable sum constantly travelling back and forward between London and Scotland. If a period arrives when a Scotch bank expects an increased demand for its notes, a box of gold is brought down from London; when this period is past, the same box, generally unopened, is sent back to London" (The Economist, 23 October 1847). The image of the unopened box of gold travelling back and forth between London and Scotland is a perfect emblem of the Act's irrationality: it immobilizes capital, adds nothing to the security of the note issue, and destroys the centralized reserve that made the Bank of England effective in meeting foreign payments.

2.17 Overstone's Dithyramb and Its Antistrophe

The chapter concludes with an editorial parenthesis (F. E.) presenting Overstone's defense of the Act and its ironic reversal. Overstone had already repeated before the Lords Committee of 1848 on Commercial Distress that "pressure, and a high rate of interest, caused by the want of sufficient capital, cannot be relieved by an extra issue of banknotes" (1514) — "even though the mere permission for an increased note issue in the government letter of 25 October 1847 had sufficed to blunt the edge of the crisis." He still maintains (1604) that "the high rate of interest and the depression of the manufacturing interests was the necessary result of the diminution of the material capital applicable to manufacturing and trading purposes." But "the depressed condition of manufacturing industry for months back meant that material commodity capital overflowed the warehouses and was actually unsaleable, while for that very reason material productive capital lay either completely or partially idle, so as not to produce still more unsaleable commodity capital." This is the decisive refutation of Overstone's "dear capital" theory: capital was not scarce; it was over-abundant and hence unsaleable. The high interest rate was not a reflection of capital scarcity but a mechanism for transferring value to creditors.

Before the Bank Acts Committee of 1857, Overstone delivers his dithyramb (no. 4189): "By strict and prompt adherence to the principles of the Act of 1844, everything has passed off with regularity and ease, the monetary system is safe and unshaken, the prosperity of the country is undisputed, the public confidence in the wisdom of the Act of 1844 is daily gaining strength, and if the Committee wish for further practical illustration of the soundness of the principles on which it rests, or of the beneficial results which it has ensured, the true and sufficient answer to the Committee is, look around you, look at the present state of the trade of this country, ... look at the contentment of the people, look at the wealth and prosperity which pervades every class of the community, and then having done so, the Committee may be fairly called upon to decide whether they will interfere with the continuance of an Act under which those results have been developed." The dithyramb — a term for an ecstatic, frenzied hymn in ancient Greek drama — is dated 14 July 1857.

Marx concludes: "The antistrophe to this dithyramb that Overstone sang to the Committee on 14 July came on 12 November the same year, in the form of the letter to the Bank directors in which the government suspended the miracle-working act of 1844, in order to save what could still be saved." The "antistrophe" is the response of the chorus in Greek drama, moving in the opposite direction. In this case, the response is the suspension of the Act — precisely the measure Overstone had denounced as unnecessary and harmful — four months after his confident prediction of permanent prosperity. The irony is complete, and it constitutes the chapter's final, devastating verdict on the currency principle.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter establishes several fundamental principles of Marx's analysis of the credit system and its relationship to the capitalist mode of production.

First, the priority of production over circulation. The chapter's most general theoretical lesson is that the causes of commercial crises are not to be sought in the sphere of currency — "the most superficial and abstract sphere" of the bourgeois process of production — but in the deeper antagonisms of the mode of production itself. The Currency School's error was simultaneously an analytical error (mistaking the surface for the essence) and an ideological error (misdirecting attention away from overproduction, the falling rate of profit, and the anarchy of production). The consequence was a body of legislation that could not address the real causes of crisis and, in fact, intensified them.

Second, the distinction between money and capital. Throughout the chapter, Marx insists on the distinction between loanable money capital (whose "price" is the rate of interest) and real capital (means of production and labor-power). Overstone's identification of high interest with scarce capital is false: in 1847, commodity capital overflowed the warehouses and was unsaleable, while the interest rate soared. The high interest rate was not a reflection of material scarcity but a mechanism for redistributing value from industrial capital to money-capital. This distinction — which the critics Tooke, Wilson, and Fullarton failed to grasp adequately — is essential to any scientific analysis of credit.

Third, the nature of credit money. The chapter's discussion of the 1825 episode and the "credit" of banknotes establishes that the stability of credit money rests not on the gold reserve but on "the entire nation and its credit." The fear that motivated the 1844 Act — the fear of banknote depreciation — was therefore both empirically unfounded and conceptually confused. By tying up £9–£10 million in gold for the convertibility of notes whose credit was already unshakeable, the Act immobilized capital, centralized nothing, and destroyed the flexible reserve system that had made the Bank of England effective in meeting foreign payments.

Fourth, the class character of monetary legislation. The chapter demonstrates, through witness testimony (Twells, Capps, Palmer), that the Bank Act systematically benefited bankers and money-capitalists at the expense of industrialists, merchants, and small tradesmen. Twells's admission that the Act "has afforded a rich harvest to bankers and money-capitalists of all kinds" while "pinching" little tradesmen; Capps's characterization of the Act as "a somewhat adroit scheme for bringing the profits of industry periodically into the usurer's bag"; and Palmer's statement that the intention "was to make money dear" — these together constitute a class analysis of the 1844 legislation. The Act was not a neutral technical reform but a mechanism for the redistribution of income from productive to financial capital.

Fifth, the dialectic of reform and crisis. The chapter's narrative structure — the Act's passage, its operation, the crises of 1847 and 1857, the suspensions, the resumption — illustrates a general pattern of capitalist regulation: a reform designed to prevent crises in fact intensifies them, forcing the suspension of the very rules that were supposed to provide stability. The government's suspension of the Act on 25 October 1847 and 12 November 1857 is the empirical proof of the Act's failure and the concrete negation of the currency principle. This dialectic — the self-negation of bourgeois reform through its own operation — is a recurring theme in Marx's work.

The broader implications of the chapter extend beyond monetary theory. They concern the relationship between economic theory and class interest; the way in which a flawed theory can acquire practical dominance through its service to powerful interests; the structural tendency of the capitalist credit system to generate crises and to redistribute their costs; and the inability of technical fixes to address the fundamental contradictions of the mode of production. The chapter is, in this sense, not merely a historical study of the English Bank Legislation of 1844 but a case study in the political economy of reform — a demonstration that within the framework of capitalist production, the attempt to manage the system through legislation tends to reproduce and intensify the system's contradictions rather than resolve them. The final image — Overstone's dithyramb and its antistrophe — encapsulates this lesson: the confident celebration of a "miracle-working act" is answered, four months later, by the government's letter suspending it "in order to save what could still be saved."
