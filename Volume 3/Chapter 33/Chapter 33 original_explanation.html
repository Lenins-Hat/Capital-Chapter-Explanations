Volume 3 • Chapter 33: The Currency under the Credit System

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem: How Does Credit Govern the Circulation of Money?

Chapter 33 of Capital, Volume 3, entitled "The Means of Circulation under the Credit System," is a pivot point in Marx's analysis of the credit system. Having established that interest is a portion of profit, and that the credit system allocates loanable capital across the economy, Marx now turns to a narrower but technically ferocious question: what is the actual relationship between the quantity of circulating media (banknotes, coin, bills of exchange) and the credit system that both produces and constrains them? The chapter opens with a quotation from an anonymous 1840s pamphlet, The Currency Theory Reviewed, which declares: "The great regulator of the velocity of the currency is credit. This explains why a severe pressure upon the money-market is generally coincident with a full circulation" (p. 65). Marx seizes on this sentence and resolves it into a double proposition — credit is the regulator of velocity in two distinct senses — and this doubling constitutes the chapter's organizing principle.

The first sense is structural: every institutional device that saves means of circulation — the clearing house, the offsetting of bills, the deposit-and-loan cycle of banknotes, the system of reciprocal claims settled by bookkeeping transfer — is founded on credit. The second sense is dynamic: credit mediates and accelerates the velocity with which a given piece of money changes hands, so that the same note can accomplish more purchase-and-payment transactions in a given time than it would if each transaction required an actual sale and purchase. These two senses are not parallel; they interlock, and their interlocking is what makes the credit system simultaneously an engine of economization and a source of catastrophic fragility.

The Intellectual Objective: Demolishing the Currency Theory from Within

Marx's deeper objective in this chapter is polemical and forensic. He is intervening in the great mid-Victorian monetary controversy that had produced the Bank Charter Act of 1844 and the Bank Acts of 1845 — legislation that tied the issue of banknotes to the metal reserve and that had been framed on the assumptions of the "Currency School" (Overstone, Torrens, and their allies). The Currency School held that the quantity of notes in circulation determines prices and that over-issue of notes is the cause of crises; hence note issue should be strictly regulated by the gold reserve. Against this, Marx deploys an immense battery of empirical evidence — parliamentary testimony from the Bank Acts Committee of 1857–58, the House of Lords Committee on Commercial Distress of 1848, and the Bank Acts Committee of 1858 — to demonstrate that the note-issuing banks cannot in fact determine the quantity of notes in circulation as long as those notes are convertible, and that the absolute quantity of circulation has no determining effect on the rate of interest except in moments of acute pressure.

The chapter's central thesis, then, can be stated as follows: the quantity of means of circulation under the credit system — both notes and bills of exchange — is determined by the needs of commerce (the prices and volume of transactions, together with the velocity and economy of circulation), not by the will of the banks or the state of the gold reserve; and the interest rate is determined by the demand for and supply of loanable capital, not by the quantity of circulating media. Where the quantity of circulation does appear to govern the interest rate — during panics — this is an illusion generated by the collapse of credit into a demand for means of payment. The crisis, in Marx's famous formulation near the end of the chapter, is the moment when "the sudden collapse of the credit system into the monetary system" occurs.

Method: The Dialectic of Circulation and Capital

The chapter's method is characteristically Marxian: it holds together two levels of analysis that bourgeois political economy kept separate. On one level, Marx analyses the technical mechanics of circulation — how a note is deposited, discounted, redeposited, rediscounted, and returned to its issuer. On another level, he analyses the social relations embodied in these mechanics — the way in which the credit system converts a national saving on metal money into a private profit, the way in which a class of "parasites" acquires the power to "decimate the industrial capitalists periodically" and to "interfere in actual production in the most dangerous manner." The technical and the social are not two arguments but one: the very mechanisms that economize on circulating media (the clearing house, the bill of exchange, the fiduciary note) are the mechanisms that concentrate power in the hands of the banks and big money-lenders, and that make the crisis both possible and profitable.

This chapter fits into the broader architecture of Volume 3 as the moment when Marx transitions from the analysis of interest-bearing capital as a form (Part V's opening chapters) to the analysis of the credit system as an institutional totality. It prepares the ground for the later treatment of banking capital, the role of the Bank of England, and — most famously — the "fictitious capital" that pervades the credit system. It is also, importantly, a chapter in which Marx's engagement with The Economist (Wilson), with Gilbart, with Tooke, and with the testimony of practising bankers (Chapman, Gurney, Neave, Hubbard) becomes the substance of the argument. Marx does not merely cite these sources; he reads them against the grain, letting the bankers expose their own system even as they defend it.

The Focal Point: The Collapse of Credit into Money

The chapter's dramatic and theoretical climax is the crisis. In ordinary times, the note circulation is governed by the needs of trade; the interest rate moves according to the demand for loanable capital; the bill of exchange circulates as a substitute for money; and the banks appear as mere intermediaries. But in a crisis, everything inverts. The bill circulation "completely collapses"; no one will accept a promise to pay; each person hoards banknotes; the same notes that were "so abundant the day before have vanished from the market"; the rate of interest soars not because capital has become scarce but because means of payment have become scarce; and the entire credit structure reveals itself to rest on a metallic base that is utterly inadequate to its superstructure. The chapter thus ends with a vision of the credit system as "one enormous centralization" that gives a class of parasites "fabulous power" — a power that the Bank Act of 1844, far from restraining, actually intensifies by compelling the Bank to contract its note issue at the precise moment when the world is crying out for notes.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Proposition: Credit as the Regulator of Velocity in a Double Sense

Marx begins by taking the quoted sentence — "the great regulator of the velocity of the currency is credit" — and insisting that it must be read in a double sense. The first sense is that "all methods that save means of circulation are founded on credit." This is a claim about the institutional foundations of economization. The clearing house, the offsetting of bills, the deposit system, the practice of settling by bookkeeping transfer — none of these would be possible unless the parties involved already stood in relations of credit to one another. The second sense is that credit increases the velocity of circulation, i.e. it allows a given mass of money to perform more transactions in a given time. This is a claim about the dynamics of circulation.

To make the second sense concrete, Marx offers the famous example of a £500 note. Suppose A gives this note to B today in payment for a bill of exchange; B deposits it the same day with his banker; the banker uses it the same day to discount a bill for C; C pays it into his bank; this bank gives it to the billbroker as an advance, and so on. The note is "serving to make purchases or payments" at every step, but the speed with which it circulates "is mediated by the speed with which it returns time after time to someone or other in the form of a deposit and is transferred to someone else in the form of a loan." Here Marx is making a subtle but crucial point: the note's velocity in real transactions (purchases and payments) is a function of its velocity in credit transactions (deposits and loans). The note is not merely a means of purchase; it is simultaneously the vehicle of a series of credit operations. The same piece of money is alternately a deposit (a claim on the banker) and a loan (a claim by the banker on someone else), and it is because it is passed back and forth between these two forms that it can be used so intensively.

The Clearing House and the Two Forms of Settlement

Marx now turns to the "purest" form of economizing on means of circulation: the clearing house. Here the "simple exchange of bills falling due" takes place, and "the principal function of money as means of payment is simply to settle the balances." In the clearing house, the enormous mass of reciprocal claims is cancelled against one another, and only the net difference is discharged in money. This is the most highly developed form of the economization that credit makes possible.

But Marx immediately adds a crucial qualification: "the existence of these bills of exchange itself depends on the credit that the industrialists and merchants give one another." If this credit declines, so does the number of bills, "particularly the long-term ones," and so too does the effectiveness of this method of settlement. Here the second sense of credit's role (mediating velocity) folds back into the first (founding the methods that save circulation): the very existence of the bills that are cleared depends on credit, so that a contraction of credit simultaneously reduces the number of bills and the efficacy of the clearing house.

Marx then distinguishes two kinds of economization, apart from "the greater or lesser development of technique in the concentration of these payments." The first is the case where "reciprocal claims, represented by bills of exchange or cheques, are balanced by the same banker, who simply transfers the claim from one person's account to the other's." The second is the case where "the various bankers settle them among themselves." In the first case, the economization is internal to a single bank's bookkeeping; in the second, it is inter-bank. Marx notes that the concentration of £8–£10 million in bills of exchange in the hands of a bill-broker such as Overend, Gurney & Co. was "one of the principal means for expanding the scope of this settlement at the local level." This detail is important: it shows that the bill-broker is not a mere intermediary but a structural node in the system of economization. The bill-broker's ability to concentrate claims in his own hands is what allows the clearing mechanism to operate on a larger scale.

Credit as the Mediator and Accelerator of Velocity: The Five-Transaction Example

Marx now unpacks the second sense of credit's role with an extended comparison. He asks us to imagine a single piece of money that accomplishes five transactions in a given period. In the first scenario, without credit, A buys from B, B from C, C from D, D from E, and E from F — the money passes from hand to hand "mediated simply by actual purchases and sales." In this case, the money "may effect only five transactions, for instance, remaining for longer in each individual hand."

In the second scenario, with credit, the same piece of money performs five purchases but in a different way. B deposits with his banker the money received from A; the banker passes it to C in discounting a bill of exchange; C buys from D; D deposits it with his banker; the latter lends it to E; E buys from F. Here the money's velocity as a "mere means of circulation (means of purchase)" is mediated by four credit operations: B's depositing, the banker's discounting for C, D's depositing, and the banker's discounting for E. Without these credit operations, "the same piece of money would not have performed five purchases successively in a given period of time." The crucial point is that the fact that the money "changed hands without the mediation of actual purchase and sale — as a deposit and by discounting — means that its change of hands in the series of real transactions is accelerated." In other words, the credit operations do not replace the real transactions; they accelerate them by allowing the same money to be used more frequently.

Marx adds a further refinement: "one and the same banknote can form deposits with different bankers. In the same way, it can form different deposits with the same banker." The example given is that a banker discounts B's bill with the note A has deposited, B pays to C, and C deposits the note with the same banker who gave it out. Here the same note returns to its point of origin, having mediated a discount and a payment. This is a miniature illustration of the circulation of banknotes within a credit system: the note is issued, used, and returned, and in the process it has facilitated a series of transactions.

The Law of Quantity: Prices, Transactions, Velocity, Economy

Having established the mechanics of credit's role in circulation, Marx now restates the fundamental law governing the quantity of money in circulation. He refers back to Volume 1, Chapter 3, Section 2, where he had shown that "the quantity of money actually circulating, taking the velocity of circulation and economy of payment as given, is determined by the price of commodities and the number of transactions." This is the classical quantity-relation, but with a crucial qualification: velocity and economy of payment are taken as given. The same law, Marx says, "prevails in the case of note circulation." This is a direct challenge to the Currency School, which held that note circulation is governed by a different law (the law of the gold reserve). Marx insists that notes, so long as they are convertible, obey exactly the same law as coin.

To substantiate this, Marx presents a table of Bank of England note circulation from 1844 to 1857, subdivided by denomination. The table shows the yearly average of notes in the hands of the public, broken down into £5–£10 notes, £20–£100 notes, and £200–£1,000 notes, with percentages of the total. The total sum of banknotes in circulation "underwent a positive decline between 1844 and 1857, even though trade more than doubled," as shown by export and import figures. The low-denomination notes (£5 and £10) increased from £9,263,000 in 1844 to £10,659,000 in 1857, while the high-denomination notes (£200 to £1,000) declined from £5,856,000 in 1852 to £3,241,000 in 1857 — a decline of more than £2.5 million.

Marx explains this decline by reference to a change in the clearing arrangements. On 8 June 1854, the private bankers of London admitted the joint-stock banks to the clearing house, and "shortly afterwards the final clearing was adjusted in the Bank of England." The daily clearances were now effected by transfers in the accounts which the banks kept at the Bank of England. As a result, "the large notes which the bankers formerly employed for the purpose of adjusting their accounts are no longer necessary." This is a concrete illustration of the economization of means of circulation: the same clearing operations can be performed with fewer and smaller notes once the mechanism of settlement is improved. Marx notes that the low minimum to which the use of money in wholesale trade had been reduced can be seen by comparing the table reproduced in Volume 1, Chapter 3, p. 238, note 54, submitted to the Bank Acts Committee by Morrison, Dillon & Co. — one of the largest London wholesalers where a retail trader can purchase his entire stock of commodities.

Marx then cites the evidence of W. Newmarch (B. A. 1857, no. 1741) on other factors contributing to the saving on means of circulation: the penny post, railways, telegraphs — "in short the improved means of communication." As a result, England "can do approximately five or six times the amount of business with the same note circulation." Newmarch attributes this "essentially" to the withdrawal of notes of more than £10 from circulation, and sees it as a "natural explanation" for the fact that in Scotland and Ireland, where £1 notes also circulate, the note circulation has risen by approximately 31 per cent. Marx gives the figures: the total circulation of banknotes in the United Kingdom, including the £1 notes, is £39 million; the gold circulation is £70 million. In Scotland, the note circulation in 1834 was £3,120,000; in 1844, £3,020,000; in 1854, £4,050,000.

The Impossibility of Over-Issue by Note-Issuing Banks

From this empirical material, Marx draws a striking conclusion: "it is in no way in the power of the note-issuing banks to increase the number of notes in circulation, as long as these notes are exchangeable at any time against metal money." This is the heart of his argument against the Currency School. If notes are convertible, any excess of notes will immediately return to the issuer for redemption in metal. Hence "the amount of notes in circulation is governed by the needs of commerce, and each superfluous note immediately finds its way back to its issuer."

Marx adds a parenthetical footnote (marked as an editorial insertion by Engels, "F. E.") clarifying that he is "not referring at all here to inconvertible paper money." Inconvertible banknotes "can become general means of circulation only where they are in actual fact supported by the state's credit, as is the case today in Russia, for example." Such notes "fall under the laws of inconvertible state paper money, as already developed: Volume 1, Chapter 3, 2, c: 'Coin. The Symbol of Value'." This footnote is important because it delimits the scope of Marx's argument: he is analysing a system of convertible notes, not fiat money. The distinction matters because the laws governing the two are different: convertible notes are tied to metal and therefore subject to the law of metallic circulation, whereas inconvertible notes are governed by the laws of state paper money.

Marx then notes that in England, "it is only the notes of the Bank of England that enjoy general circulation as legal tender," so "we can ignore here the insignificant and simply local note circulation of the provincial banks." This is a matter of institutional fact: the Bank of England's notes are the general currency of the realm, and it is therefore the Bank's circulation that is relevant to the analysis.

The Parliamentary Evidence: Neave, Tooke, and Wright

Marx now introduces the evidence of Mr Neave, Governor of the Bank of England, before the Bank Acts Committee of 1858. Neave testifies that "in ordinary times, the uses of the public seem to want about £20,000,000" of notes, with special periodic moments when they rise by another £1,000,000 or £1,500,000. He states that "if the public wanted more, they could always take it from the Bank of England." During a panic, "the public have, as I believe, the full power of helping themselves as to notes; and of course, as long as the Bank has a liability, they may use that liability to take the notes from the Bank." Neave estimates the average legal-tender requirement at "from £19,000,000 to £20,000,000."

Marx then cites Thomas Tooke's evidence to the House of Lords Committee on Commercial Distress (C. D. 1848–57, no. 3094): the Bank "has no power of its own volition to extend the amount of its circulation in the hands of the public; but the Bank has the power of reducing the amount of the notes in the hands of the public, not however without a very violent operation." This is a precise statement of the asymmetry that Marx wants to emphasize: the Bank cannot increase circulation at will, but it can decrease it — and only through a violent operation (raising the discount rate, calling in loans, etc.).

J. C. Wright, a Nottingham banker of thirty years' experience, testifies that "any excess of circulation will go into the deposits and thus assume a different name." This is an important observation: an excess note issued by the Bank does not remain in circulation as a note; it is deposited, and thus appears as a deposit liability rather than as circulating currency. The note's "name" changes — from circulation to deposit — but the underlying reality is that the note has returned to the banking system.

The Scottish and Irish Evidence: Kennedy and Anderson

Marx extends the argument to Scotland, where "there is almost nothing but paper in circulation," since £1 notes are permitted and "the Scotch hate gold." Kennedy, a director of a Scottish bank, declares that the banks "could never reduce their note circulation" and that "so long as there are internal transactions requiring notes or gold to perform them, bankers must, either through the demands of their depositors or in one shape or another, furnish as much currency as those transactions require." The Scottish banks "can restrict their transactions, but they cannot control their currency." This is a crucial distinction: the banks can control the volume of their lending (their transactions), but they cannot control the quantity of currency in circulation, because that is determined by the needs of trade.

Anderson, director of the Union Bank of Scotland, adds a further point (ibid., no. 3578): the system of exchanges between the Scottish banks "prevents any over-issue on the part of any one bank," but there is "a more powerful preventive than the system of exchanges" — namely "the universal practice in Scotland of keeping a bank account." Everybody who has any money at all "has a bank account and puts in every day the money which he does not immediately want, so that at the close of the business of the day there is no money scarcely out of the banks except what people have in their pockets." This is a striking observation about the institutional conditions of note circulation: in a society where everyone has a bank account and deposits their surplus daily, the notes return to the banks every evening, and the banks can therefore control the quantity in circulation to a much greater degree than would otherwise be possible. But even here, the control is indirect: it works through the return flow of notes, not through the banks' arbitrary decision to issue or not issue.

Marx notes that the same applies to Ireland, citing the evidence of the Governor of the Bank of Ireland, MacDonnell, and the director of the Provincial Bank of Ireland, Murray.

Independence from the Gold Reserve: The Kinnear Example

Marx now makes a further claim: "the note circulation is not only independent of the will of the Bank of England, it is equally independent of the state of the gold reserve in the Bank's vaults, which is what ensures the convertibility of these notes." He cites the example of J. G. Kinnear (The Crisis and the Currency, London, 1847, p. 5): on 18 September 1846, the circulation of the Bank of England was £20,900,000 and the bullion in the Bank was £16,273,000; on 5 April 1847, the notes in circulation were £20,815,000 and the bullion was £10,246,000. Thus "£6 million of gold were exported, without any contraction of the currency of the country." This is a powerful empirical refutation of the Currency School's claim that note circulation is determined by the gold reserve. The gold reserve fell by £6 million, but the note circulation was essentially unchanged.

Marx adds the qualification: "Naturally this holds good only under the conditions prevailing in England today, and even then only in so far as legislation does not enforce a different ratio between note issue and metal reserve." This is a crucial caveat: the Bank Act of 1844 does enforce a different ratio, by separating the Issue Department from the Banking Department and requiring that notes above a fixed fiduciary limit be backed by gold. Marx is saying that in the absence of such legislation, the note circulation is independent of the gold reserve; but the legislation artificially ties the two together, which is precisely why the Act is pernicious.

Seasonal Fluctuations: The Harvest and the Quarterly Dividends

Marx now turns to the periodic fluctuations in circulation that repeat themselves each year. He cites Newmarch (B. A. 1857, no. 1650): "the circulation is high in one month, and it is low in another month, and in a certain other month occurs a medium point." The first of these is the harvest. In August of each year, "a few £ millions, mostly in gold, pass from the Bank of England into domestic circulation, to pay for the costs of the harvest." Since this involves principally the payment of wages, banknotes are used but little in England; the money then flows back to the Bank "up to the end of the year." In Scotland, where £1 notes are given almost invariably in place of sovereigns, "it is the note circulation that expands in the corresponding case, in fact twice a year, in May and November, by some £3 to £4 million; fourteen days later the reflux has already set in, and within a month it is almost complete." (Anderson, op. cit., nos. 3595–3600.)

The Bank of England's note circulation also undergoes "a temporary fluctuation each quarter as a result of the quarterly payment of 'dividends,' i.e. interest on the national debt." Notes are first withdrawn from circulation and then distributed among the public, but "these flow back very quickly." Weguelin (B. A. 1857, no. 38) puts the total fluctuation at £2.5 million. Chapman, of Overend, Gurney and Co., reckons the disturbance to be much higher: "When you abstract from the circulation £6,000,000 or £7,000,000 of revenue in anticipation of dividends, somebody must be the medium of supplying that in the intermediate times" (B. A. 1857, no. 5196).

The Industrial Cycle and the Crisis: Samuel Gurney's Evidence

Marx now turns to "far more important and persistent" fluctuations: those that correspond to the various phases of the industrial cycle. He cites Samuel Gurney, partner in Overend, Gurney & Co., testifying before the House of Lords Committee (C. D. 1848–57, no. 2645). In October 1847, the amount of banknotes in the hands of the public was £20,800,000, and there was "great difficulty in getting possession of banknotes in the money-market," due to "the alarm of not being able to get them in consequence of the restriction of the Act of 1844." By March 1848, the amount was £17,700,000, but "there being now no commercial alarm whatsoever, it is much beyond what is required." There was "no banking house or money-dealer in London, but what has a larger amount of banknotes than they can use." Gurney further testifies (no. 2650) that the amount of banknotes "out of the custody of the Bank of England affords a totally insufficient exponent of the active state of the circulation, without taking into consideration likewise ... the state of the commercial world and the state of credit." And (no. 2651): "The feeling of surplus that we have under the present amount of circulation in the hands of the public arises in a large degree from our present state of great stagnation. In a state of high prices and excitement of transaction £17,700,000 would give us a feeling of restriction."

Marx interpolates a crucial theoretical summary at this point (in a paragraph marked as an editorial insertion by Engels). As long as the state of business is such that the returns on advances come in regularly and credit remains unimpaired, "the expansion and contraction of circulation is governed simply by the needs of the industrialists and merchants." Since in England gold does not come into the picture for wholesale trade, and the gold circulation — apart from seasonal fluctuations — can be seen as "a magnitude that is fairly constant over a longish period of time," the Bank of England's note circulation provides "a sufficiently exact measurement of these changes." In the quiet period after a crisis, transactions are at their lowest; with the revival of demand there is a greater requirement for means of circulation, which rises with increasing prosperity; the quantity reaches its high point in the period of "over-exertion and over-speculation." Then the crisis breaks out, and "overnight the banknotes that were still so abundant the day before have vanished from the market, and with them the discounter of bills, the advancer on securities, the buyer of commodities." The Bank of England is supposed to help, "but even its powers are soon exhausted, and the Bank Act of 1844 compels it to contract its note circulation at the very moment when the entire world is crying out for banknotes, when the owners of commodities cannot sell and yet are supposed to pay, and are ready to make any sacrifice, so long as they can obtain banknotes."

Marx then cites banker Wright (op. cit., no. 2930): "During an alarm, the country requires twice as much circulation as in ordinary times, because the circulation is hoarded by bankers and others." This introduces the crucial concept of hoarding in a crisis. In so far as a crisis breaks out, "it is then simply a question of means of payment." But since each person is dependent on someone else for the arrival of these means of payment, and no one knows whether the other will be in a position to pay on the due date, "a real steeplechase breaks out for those means of payment that are to be found in the market, i.e. for banknotes." Each person hoards as many as he can get his hands on, "so that the notes vanish from circulation the very day they are most needed." Samuel Gurney estimates a figure of £4–£5 million for the banknotes put under lock and key at the moment of panic in October 1847.

Chapman's Evidence on Market Manipulation

Marx now introduces the evidence of Chapman, Gurney's partner, before the 1857 Bank Acts Committee, which he describes as "highly interesting." Chapman testifies (no. 4963) that he does not think it is "a proper condition of things that the money-market should be under the power of any individual capitalist (such as does exist in London), to create a tremendous scarcity and pressure, when we have a very low state of circulation out." He adds that "there is more than one capitalist, who can withdraw from the circulating medium £1,000,000 or £2,000,000 of notes, if they have an object to attain by it." A big speculator can sell £1 or £2 million worth of Consols "and thus take money out of the market" (no. 4965), creating "a very violent pressure." The notes are then "quite unproductive" (no. 4967), but "that is nothing, if it effects his great object; his great object is to knock down the funds, to create a scarcity, and he has it perfectly in his power to do so."

Chapman gives a concrete example. One morning there was a great demand for money from the stock exchange, and no one knew the cause. Someone asked Chapman to lend him £50,000 at 7 per cent; Chapman was surprised, as his rate was much lower, but agreed. The man returned and took a further £50,000 at 7.5 per cent, then £100,000 at 8 per cent, and wanted more at 8.5 per cent. Chapman "then got worried himself." It subsequently emerged that "a major sum of money had suddenly been withdrawn from the market." Chapman: "I did lend a large sum at 8 per cent; I was afraid to go beyond; I did not know what was coming."

Marx draws a crucial distinction here: "It should never be forgotten that although a fairly constant sum of £19–£20 million in notes is ostensibly in the hands of the public, yet the portion of these notes that is actually circulating, on the one hand, and the portion that lies unoccupied in the banks as a reserve, on the other, are both constantly and substantially changing." If the reserve is large (i.e. the actual circulation low), it is said from the standpoint of the money market that "the circulation is full, or money is plentiful"; if the reserve is small (i.e. the actual circulation full), the money market calls it "low" and says "money is scarce," i.e. "only a small amount represents unoccupied loan capital."

This is a subtle but essential point. The total quantity of notes in the hands of the public may be roughly constant, but the distribution of those notes between active circulation and idle reserve is constantly changing, and it is this distribution — not the total — that determines whether the money market feels "full" or "low." When the reserve is large, there is plenty of unoccupied loan capital; when it is small, loan capital is scarce. The apparent paradox — that a "full" circulation means plentiful money and a "low" circulation means scarce money — is resolved once we understand that the money market is concerned not with the total circulation but with the quantity of loanable money, i.e. the reserve.

Technical Fluctuations: Taxes and Dividends

Marx distinguishes these industrial-cycle fluctuations from "a genuine expansion or contraction of circulation independent of the phases of the industrial cycle — one in which the amount that the public needs remains the same." Such fluctuations are "only to be found for technical reasons, e.g. at the payment date of taxes or of interest on the national debt." With tax payment, notes and gold flow into the Bank of England "in more than their customary measure," and circulation contracts "irrespective of the need for it." Conversely, when dividends are paid out on the national debt, circulation expands. In the first case, loans are taken out from the Bank in order to obtain means of circulation; in the latter case, the rate of interest charged by the private banks falls "on account of the temporary growth in their reserves."

Marx emphasizes that this "has nothing to do with the absolute total of means of circulation, but simply with the banking house that puts these means of circulation into circulation, for which this process appears as an alienation of loan capital and which therefore pockets the profit on it." In the one case, there is "simply a temporary displacement of the circulating medium," which the Bank of England adjusts by making short-term advances at low interest shortly before the due date of the quarterly taxes and the likewise quarterly dividends. The additional notes paid out "first of all fill the gaps that the payment of taxes gives rise to, while their repayment to the Bank immediately afterwards brings back the surplus notes which the paying out of dividends has placed with the public." In the other case, "a low or full circulation is never more than a different distribution of the same mass of means of circulation between active circulation and deposits, i.e. as an instrument for loans."

The Interest Rate and the Absolute Quantity of Circulation

Marx now addresses the central question directly: does the absolute quantity of circulation determine the rate of interest? His answer is a qualified no. He begins with the case of a gold influx leading to an increase in the number of notes given out by the Bank of England. These notes "help the business of discounting outside the Bank and flow back in the repayment of loans, so that the absolute volume of notes in circulation is only temporarily increased." If the circulation is full on account of an expansion of business (which is only possible given relatively low prices), the rate of interest "may be relatively high on account of the demand for loan capital that results from rising profits and increased new investments." If it is low on account of a contraction of business or greater ease of credit, the rate of interest "may be low even if prices are high." (Marx refers to Hubbard for this.)

The absolute quantity of circulation has a determining effect on the rate of interest "only in periods of pressure." In this case, either the demand for a full circulation is "merely a demand for means of hoarding" (apart from the reduced velocity with which the money circulates, and with which the same identical pieces of money are constantly converted into loan capital), on account of the lack of credit, as in 1847, when the suspension of the Bank Act "did not lead to any expansion in the circulation but was sufficient to bring hoarded notes to light again and make them circulate actively." Or else more means of circulation may really be required, "in the way that the circulation really did grow for some time in 1857 after the suspension of the Bank Act."

In other cases, the absolute quantity of circulation has no effect on the rate of interest, for two reasons. First, taking the economy and velocity of circulation as constant, it is determined by the price of commodities and the volume of transactions (in which case one element generally counteracts the effect of the other) and ultimately by the state of credit, "whereas it in no way conversely determines the latter." Second, "commodity prices and interest do not stand in any necessary relationship."

Marx then provides a series of historical examples to substantiate this claim. Under the Bank Restriction Act (1797–1820), there was a surplus of "currency," with the rate of interest "always far higher than since the resumption of cash payment." It later fell sharply with the restriction of note issue and rising bill quotations. In 1822, 1823, and 1832, the total circulation was low and the rate of interest similarly so. In 1824, 1825, and 1836, the circulation was high and the rate of interest rose. In summer 1830, the circulation was high and the rate of interest low. Since the gold discoveries, monetary circulation has expanded throughout Europe, and the interest rate has risen. "The interest rate thus does not depend on the amount of money in circulation."

The footnote on the Bank Restriction Act explains its historical context: it was "designed to cope with the needs of the British government in the wars with France" and "freed the Bank of England from the obligation of exchanging its notes for gold." This is important because it explains why the period 1797–1820 is anomalous: the notes were inconvertible, and therefore subject to the laws of state paper money rather than metallic circulation. The high interest rates during this period were not caused by the surplus of currency but by the government's enormous borrowing demands.

The Distinction between Issuing Means of Circulation and Lending Capital

Marx now makes a theoretical distinction that is central to the chapter: "The distinction between the issue of means of circulation and the lending of capital is best shown in connection with the actual reproduction process." He refers back to Volume 2, Part Three, where he had shown how the various components of production are exchanged one for another. Variable capital consists materially of the means of subsistence of the workers, a portion of their own product, but it is paid out to them "bit by bit in money." The capitalist has to advance this money, and "it depends very much on the organization of the credit system whether he can pay out the new variable capital again the next week with the old money that he paid out the week before." It is similar in the acts of exchange between the various components of a total social capital, e.g. between means of consumption and the means of production of these. The money for their circulation "must be advanced by one or both of the exchanging parties. It then remains in circulation, but returns time and again, after completing its exchange, to the person who advanced it, since it was advanced by him over and above the industrial capital he actually employed." (See Volume 2, Chapter 20.)

When the credit system is developed, so that money is concentrated in the hands of the banks, "it is they who advance it, at least nominally. This advance is only related to the money in circulation. It is an advance of circulation, not an advance of the capitals it circulates." This is the crucial distinction: the bank's advance of money is not the same as an advance of capital. The bank advances means of circulation; it does not thereby advance capital. The capital being circulated already exists; the bank merely provides the money that allows it to circulate. This is a point that the Currency School consistently confused, treating the issue of notes as an advance of capital to the economy.

Chapman on Credit, Regional Money Flows, and "Money at Call"

Marx returns to Chapman's evidence. Chapman testifies (no. 5062) that "there may be times, when the notes in the hands of the public, though they may be large, are not to be had." Marx comments: "There is money, even during a panic; but everyone takes good care not to transform it into loanable capital, loanable money; each holds on to it for actual needs of payment." This is a precise statement of the hoarding phenomenon: the money exists, but it is not available as loanable capital. Chapman confirms (nos. 5099–5101) that country bankers in rural districts send their unemployed balances to London houses such as his, while the Lancashire and Yorkshire districts require discounts from him for the use of their trades. "Then by that means the surplus money of one part of the country is made available for the demands of another part of the country? — Precisely so." This is the geographical dimension of the money market: the surplus of the agricultural districts flows to London and is redistributed to the industrial districts.

Chapman adds that the banks' custom of investing their surplus money capital short-term in Consols and treasury bills "has greatly increased in recent times, since it became the custom to lend out this 'money at call' (i.e. money whose repayment may be demanded at any time, from one day to the next)." Chapman himself sees the purchase of this kind of paper as "most unsuitable for his business." He therefore invests it in good bills of exchange, "with one portion falling due each day, so that he always knows how much ready money he can count on." (5101–5105.) This is a revealing detail about the internal management of a bill-broking house: Chapman avoids "money at call" because it is unreliable, preferring a portfolio of bills that mature in a staggered fashion. It also reveals the extent to which the money market is a market in liquidity, where the ability to convert assets into cash at short notice is the decisive consideration.

Discounting as Purchase: The Export Trade Debate

Marx now turns to Chapman's evidence on the export trade. The very growth of exports "presents itself as a growing demand on the domestic money market, which however is felt as such only in times of pressure." In periods when exports are increasing, long-term bills of exchange are generally drawn by manufacturers on the export merchant, against consignments of British manufactures. (5126.) Wilson asks Chapman whether bills are not "redrawn from time to time" (5127); Chapman replies that "that is a thing which they keep from us; we should not admit any bill of that sort ... I dare say it is done, but I cannot speak to a thing of the kind." Marx calls him "the innocent Chapman" — an ironic comment on Chapman's professed ignorance of a practice (kite-flying, or the rolling over of bills) that was in fact widespread.

Wilson then asks (5129) whether a large increase in exports leads to "a great demand for capital for the discount of bills representing those exports." Chapman agrees (5130), noting that "this country gives an immense credit; but then it takes credit for its raw material. We are drawn upon from America always at 60 days, and from other parts at 90 days. On the other hand we give credit; if we send goods to Germany, we give two or three months." Wilson asks (5131) whether bills are not already drawn on England against imported raw materials and colonial goods at the same time as they are loaded, and whether the goods do not arrive simultaneously with the bills of lading. Chapman "believes that this is the case, but knows nothing of this 'commercial' business; better informed people should be asked." In the export trade to America, "the goods are symbolized in transit" (5133); Marx calls this "gibberish," which "is supposed to mean that the English export merchant draws a four-month bill against the commodities on one of the major American banking houses in London, and the banking house receives collateral from America."

Chapman explains (5136) that "the more remote transactions" are conducted by the merchant, "who waits for his capital until the goods are sold." There may be houses of great private wealth who can afford to lay out their own capital without taking any advance on the goods, "but the most part are converted into advances by the acceptances of some well-known established houses." These houses are resident in London, Liverpool, or elsewhere (5137–5138). "Therefore, it makes no difference, whether the manufacturer lays out his money, or whether he gets a merchant in London or Liverpool to advance it; it is still an advance in this country? — Precisely. The manufacturer in few cases has anything to do with it" (but in 1847 in almost every case).

Chapman then gives a detailed example: a man dealing in manufactured goods at Manchester will buy his goods and ship them through a house of respectability in London; when the London house is satisfied that they are packed according to the understanding, he draws upon this London house for six months against these goods to India or China; then the banking world comes in and discounts that bill for him; so that, "by the time he has to pay for those goods, he has the money all ready by the discount of that bill." Wilson asks (5139) whether, although he has the money, "the banker is laying out of his money." Chapman: "The banker has the bill; the banker has bought the bill; he uses his banking capital in that form, namely, in discounting commercial bills." Marx comments: "Thus Chapman, too, sees the discounting of bills not as an advance, but rather as a purchase of commodities." This is a significant point: in Chapman's view, the banker is not lending capital but buying a commodity (the bill), and thus his activity is commercial rather than financial. Marx will later expose the naivety of this view.

Chapman continues (5140): "No doubt; it is the substantial occupation of the money-market and of the Bank of England. The Bank of England are as glad to get these bills as we are, because they know them to be good property." (5141) "As the prosperity of the country increases, we' (the Chapmans) 'partake of it." (5142) "Then when these various fields for the employment of capital increase suddenly, of course, the natural consequence is that the rate of interest is higher? — No doubt about it."

In 5143 Chapman cannot "quite understand, that under our large exports we have had such occasion for bullion." In 5144 Wilson asks: "May it not be that we give larger credits upon our exports than we take credits upon our imports?" Chapman: "I rather doubt that point myself. If a man accepts against his Manchester goods sent to India, you cannot accept for less than ten months. We have had to pay America for her cotton' (that is perfectly true) 'some time before India pays us; but still it is rather refined in its operation." (5145) "If we have had an increase, as we had last year, of £20 million in our exports of manufactures we must have had a very large increase of imports of raw material previously to that' (and in this way over-exports are already identified with over-imports, and overproduction with over-trading) 'in order to make up that increased quantity of goods? — No doubt." (5146) "We should have to pay a very considerable balance, that is to say, the balance, no doubt, would run against us during that time, but in the long run, with America ... the exchanges are in our favour, and we have been receiving for some time past large supplies of bullion from America."

In 5148 Wilson asks Chapman "whether he does not consider his high interest rate as a token of great prosperity and high profits." Marx calls Wilson a "sycophant" and says Chapman, "evidently astonished by the naivete," naturally confirms this, "but is honest enough to make the following qualification: 'There are some, who cannot help themselves; they have engagements to meet, and they must fulfil them, whether it is profitable or not; but, for a continuance' (of the high rate of interest), 'it would indicate prosperity.'" Marx comments: "Both men forget that a high rate of interest can also indicate, as was the case in 1857, that the knights errant of credit are making the country unsafe." In this case, they can pay high rates of interest "because they pay out of other people's pockets (though in this way they help to determine the interest rate for everyone), meanwhile living in style on anticipated profits." At the same time, "precisely this can be a very profitable business for manufacturers, etc. The system of advances makes returns absolutely deceptive." This also explains why the Bank of England, when interest rates are high, "discounts at a lower rate than the others."

Chapman testifies (5156) that "our discounts, taking the present moment, when we have had for so long a high rate of interest, are at their maximum." Marx notes that Chapman said this on 21 July 1857, "a few months before the crash." (5157) "In 1852' (when the interest rate was low) 'they were not nearly so large." Marx comments: "Because at that time business was still much healthier." This is a nice example of Marx's use of hindsight: Chapman's confident testimony in July 1857 is juxtaposed with the crash that followed a few months later, exposing the fragility of the boom.

The Convertibility of Bills versus the Convertibility of Banknotes

Marx now turns to what he calls "a particularly amusing aspect of Chapman's evidence": "how these people actually view the public's money as their own property and believe they have a right to it, to ensure the permanent convertibility of the bills they discount." The questions and answers "show great naivete." It turns out to be "the duty of legislation to ensure the permanent convertibility of the bills of exchange accepted by the major firms and to make sure that the Bank of England will rediscount them for the billbrokers under all circumstances." Marx notes that 1857 saw the bankruptcy of three such bill-brokers, "to the tune of some £8 million, their own capital being infinitesimal in comparison with these debts."

Chapman testifies (5177) that it would be "a very lamentable thing" that bills accepted by Barings or Loyds "should not be discountable; a most extraordinary position, that a man should stop payment, who had the acceptances of Smith, Payne & Co., or Jones, Loyd & Co. in his hands, because he could not get them discounted." (5178) Wilson asks: "Is not the engagement of Messrs Baring an engagement to pay a certain sum of money when the bill is due?" Chapman: "That is perfectly true; but Messrs Baring, when they contract that engagement, and every other merchant who contracts an engagement, never dream that they are going to pay it in sovereigns; they expect that they are going to pay it at the Clearing House." (5180) "Do you think that there should be any machinery contrived by which the public would have a right to claim money before that bill was due by calling upon somebody to discount it? — No, not from the acceptor; but if you mean by that that we are not to have the possibility of getting commercial bills discounted, we must alter the whole constitution of things." (5182) "Then you think that it' (commercial bill) 'ought to be convertible into money, exactly in the same way that a Bank of England note ought to be convertible into gold? — Most decidedly so, under certain circumstances." (5184) "Then you think that the provisions of the currency should be so shaped that a bill of exchange of undoubted character ought at all times to be as readily exchangeable against money as a banknote? — I do." (5185) "You do not mean to say that either the Bank of England or any individual should, by law, be compelled to exchange it? — I mean to say this, that in framing a bill for the currency, we should make provision to prevent the possibility of an inconvertibility of the bills of exchange of the country arising, assuming them to be undoubtedly solid and legitimate."

Marx's comment: "This is the convertibility of the commercial bill of exchange against the convertibility of the banknote." He is pointing to a striking inconsistency in Chapman's position: Chapman wants commercial bills to be as convertible as banknotes, yet he does not want any legal obligation to convert them. The convertibility he demands is not a legal right but a market convention — the assurance that good bills will always find a discounter. But this assurance is precisely what fails in a crisis, and it is this failure that exposes the fragility of the entire system. Chapman's demand amounts to a plea that the Bill-brokers be guaranteed liquidity by the Bank of England — a plea that was answered, in a sense, by the Bank Act's provision for rediscounting, but only at the cost of tightening the screw when it was least needed.

Marx adds (5190): "The money-dealers of the country only, in point of fact, represent the public" — as Chapman did later at the Assizes in the Davidson case. Marx cites "the Great City Frauds" (Seton Laing, New Series of the Great City Frauds of Cole, Davidson and Gordon, 5th edn, London, 1869) as evidence of the money-dealers' fraudulent dealings. The point is that Chapman's claim to represent the public is self-serving; the money-dealers represent themselves.

Chapman continues (5196): "During the quarters' (when the dividends are paid) 'it is ... absolutely necessary that we should go to the Bank of England. When you abstract from the circulation £6,000,000 or £7,000,000 of revenue in anticipation of the dividends, somebody must be the medium of supplying that in the intermediate time." Marx comments: "In this case the question at issue is the supply of money, not of capital or loan capital." This is another instance of the distinction between money and capital: the Bank is asked to supply money, not capital.

(5169) "Everybody acquainted with our commercial circle must know that when we are in such a state that we find it impossible to sell Exchequer bills, when India bonds are perfectly useless, when you cannot discount the first commercial bills, there must be great anxiety on the part of those whose business renders them liable to pay the circulating medium of the realm on demand, which is the case with all bankers. Then the effect of that is to make every man double his reserve. Just see what the result of that is throughout the country, that every country banker, of whom there are about 500, has to send up to his London correspondent to remit him £5,000 in banknotes. Taking such a limited sum as that as the average, which is quite absurd, you come to £2,500,000 taken out of the circulation. How is that to be supplied?" This is a vivid illustration of the hoarding dynamic: in a crisis, every banker doubles his reserve, and the aggregate demand for notes — £2.5 million at Chapman's "absurdly low" estimate — represents a massive withdrawal of means of circulation from active use.

Chapman adds (5195) that those private capitalists who have money "do not want to let go of it whatever the interest," for "we would rather have no interest at all, than have a doubt about our getting the money in case we require it." This is a precise statement of the liquidity preference that operates in a crisis: the demand for money becomes absolute, and no rate of interest, however high, is sufficient to induce holders to part with it.

(5173) "Our system is this: That we have £300,000,000 of liabilities which may be called for at a single moment to be paid in the coin of the realm, and that coin of the realm, if the whole of it is substituted, amounts to £23,000,000, or whatever it may be; is not that a state which may throw us into convulsions at any moment?" Marx comments: "Hence the sudden collapse of the credit system into the monetary system in times of crisis." This is one of the most famous formulations in the chapter. The credit system is a vast structure of claims — £300 million in liabilities — resting on a tiny metallic base of £23 million. In normal times, the claims circulate as substitutes for money, and the disproportion between claims and base is invisible. In a crisis, the claims are converted into a demand for money, and the disproportion becomes catastrophic.

Marx adds: "Apart from the domestic panic during crises, we can speak of the quantity of money only in so far as metal world money is involved. And it is precisely this that Chapman excludes, speaking only of £23 million in banknotes." This is a crucial theoretical point: in the international arena, money functions as metallic world money, and the credit system must ultimately rest on this metallic base. Chapman's focus on the domestic note circulation obscures the role of world money.

Chapman testifies (5218): "The primary cause of the derangement of the money-market' (in April and later in October 1847) 'no doubt was in the quantity of money which was required to regulate our exchanges, in consequence of the extraordinary importations of the year." Marx comments: "Firstly, this reserve of world-market money was at that time reduced to its minimum. Secondly, it served at the same time as security for the convertibility of credit money, banknotes. It thus combined two completely different functions, although both of these arise from the nature of money, since real money is always world-market money, and credit money always depends on this world-market money." This is a dense theoretical passage. The gold reserve serves a double function: (1) as a reserve for international payments (world money), and (2) as the guarantee of the convertibility of domestic credit money (banknotes). These two functions are distinct but both arise from the nature of money as world money. In a crisis, the demand for gold for international payments coincides with the demand for gold to guarantee the convertibility of notes, and the reserve is overwhelmed.

Chapman testifies (5221) that in 1847, without the suspension of the 1844 Bank Act, "the clearing houses could not have been settled." He had some inkling of the impending crisis (5236): "There are certain conditions of the money-market (and the present is not very far from it), where money is exceedingly difficult, and recourse must be had to the Bank." (5239) "With reference to the sums which we took from the Bank on the Friday, Saturday and Monday, the 19th, 20th, and 22nd of October, 1847, we should only have been too thankful to have got the bills back on the Wednesday following; the money reflowed to us directly the panic was over." On Tuesday, 23 October, the Bank Act was suspended, "and the crisis thereby curbed." This is a crucial observation: the mere suspension of the Act — without any actual increase in circulation — was sufficient to end the panic, because it restored confidence and brought hoarded notes back into circulation. This confirms Marx's argument that the crisis is fundamentally a crisis of confidence, not of physical scarcity.

Chapman believes (5274) that the bills of exchange running on London "amount at any one time to some £100–£200 million." This does not include local bills on provincial centres. (5287) "Whereas in October 1856, the amount of the notes in the hands of the public ran up to £21,155,000, there was an extraordinary difficulty in obtaining money; notwithstanding that the public held so much, we could not touch it." This was due to "the anxiety produced by the straits in which the Eastern Bank had found itself for a while (March 1856)." (5290) As soon as the panic is over, "all bankers deriving their profit from interest begin to employ the money immediately." (5302) Chapman explains the disquiet at the decline in the bank reserve "not from fear for the deposits, but rather because all those who might suddenly have to pay large sums of money knew very well that they could be driven to the Bank as the last resort in time of pressure on the money market; and 'if the banks have a very small reserve, they are not glad to receive us; but on the contrary'."

Marx then provides a summary of the reserve's function. "It is very pleasant, incidentally, to observe how the reserve dwindles away as an actual magnitude." The banks keep a minimum for their current business, partly with themselves and partly with the Bank of England. The bill-brokers hold "the loose bank money of the country" without a reserve. And all the Bank of England has to set against its liabilities for deposits is simply the reserves of the bankers and others, besides "public deposits," etc. It allows this reserve to fall to the lowest possible point, e.g. some £2 million. "Apart from this £2 million in paper, therefore, the entire swindle has absolutely no other reserve than the metal reserve in times of pressure (and these periods reduce the reserve, because notes that come in against the metal that leaves the Bank must be destroyed). Hence any reduction of the metal reserve adds to the crisis with a drain of gold." This is a damning picture of the fragility of the system: the entire credit structure rests on a reserve of £2 million in paper plus the metal reserve, and in a crisis the metal reserve itself is depleted because notes returned to the Bank must be destroyed. The "swindle" — Marx's word for the credit system — is exposed in all its precariousness.

Chapman on the Currency and the Collapse of Credit

Chapman testifies (5306): "If there should not be currency to settle the transactions at the clearing house, the only next alternative which I can see is to meet together, and to make our payments in first-class bills, bills upon the Treasury, and Messrs Smith, Payne, and so forth." (5307) Wilson asks: "Then, if the government failed to supply you with a circulating medium, you would create one for yourselves?" Chapman: "What can we do? The public come in, and take the circulating medium out of our hands; it does not exist." (5308) "You would only then do in London what they do in Manchester every day of the week? — Yes." Marx comments: "Chapman has a very good answer to the question put to him by Cayley (a Birmingham man of the Attwood school) concerning Overstone's conception of capital." Cayley asks (5315): "It has been stated before this Committee, that in a pressure like that of 1847, men are not looking for money, but are looking for capital; what is your opinion in that respect?" Chapman: "I do not understand it; we only deal in money; I do not understand what you mean by it." (5316) "If you mean thereby' (commercial capital) 'the quantity of money which a man has of his own in his business, if you call that capital, it forms, in most cases, a very small proportion of the money which he wields in his affairs through the credit which is given him by the public" — through the mediation of the Chapmans. This is a sharp exchange. Cayley is invoking Overstone's distinction between money and capital; Chapman, the practical man of the money market, professes not to understand it. Marx's point is that Chapman's inability to understand the distinction is itself revealing: the money-dealer is so immersed in the circulation of money that he cannot see the capital that lies behind it.

(5339) Chapman: "Is it the want of property that makes us give up our specie payments? — Not at all ... It is not that we want property, but it is that we are moving under a highly artificial system; and if we have an immense superincumbent demand upon our currency, circumstances may arise to prevent our obtaining that currency. Is the whole commercial industry of the country to be paralysed? Shall we shut up all the avenues of employment?" (5338) "If the question should arise whether we should maintain specie payments, or whether we should maintain the industry of the country, I have no hesitation in saying which I should drop." This is a remarkable statement: Chapman prefers to suspend specie payments rather than paralyse industry. Marx's comment is implied rather than stated: the choice between specie payments and industry is a false one, created by the artificial constraints of the Bank Act.

As to the hoarding of banknotes "with a view to aggravate the pressure and to take advantage of the consequences" (5358), Chapman says that this can happen very easily. Three major banks would suffice. (5383) "Must it not be within your knowledge, as a man conversant with the great transactions of this metropolis, that capitalists do avail themselves of these crises to make enormous profit out of the ruin of the people who fall victims to them? — There can be no doubt about it." Marx comments: "And we may well believe Mr Chapman here, even though he finally broke his own neck, commercially speaking, in an attempt to make 'enormous profit out of the ruin of the victims'." For if his partner Gurney says that any change in business is advantageous for someone who is well informed, Chapman says: "The one section of the community knows nothing of the other; one is the manufacturer, for instance, who exports to the Continent, or imports his raw commodity; he knows nothing of the man who deals in bullion" (5046). And this is how it happened that "one day Gurney and Chapman were themselves not 'well informed', and fell into a notorious bankruptcy." The irony is that the speculators who profited from the ignorance of others were themselves ignorant of the impending crash, and they too fell victim to the system they exploited.

Tooke on Advances of Capital without an Increase in Notes

Marx now returns to Tooke's evidence before the House of Lords Committee of 1848 on Commercial Distress. Tooke testifies (3099) that the Bank of England "might enlarge its advances greatly, and yet lead to no additional issue of notes." One of the most striking instances was in 1835, when the Bank made use of the West India deposits and of the loan from the East India Company in extended advances to the public, and "at that time the amount of notes in the hands of the public was actually rather diminished." Something like the same discrepancy is observable in 1846 at the time of the payment of the railway deposits into the Bank: "the securities' (in discount and deposits) 'were increased to about thirty million, while there was no perceptible effect upon the amount of notes in the hands of the public." This is a powerful empirical confirmation of the distinction between advancing capital and issuing means of circulation: the Bank can increase its advances without increasing the note circulation, because the notes it issues are immediately returned as deposits.

Bills of Exchange as the Second Means of Circulation

Marx now turns to the bill of exchange, "a second and far more important means of circulation" in wholesale trade. He recalls Chapman's insistence that "good bills are always taken in payment under all circumstances" and quotes Heine's lines: "Gilt nicht mehr der Tausves Jontof, was soll gelten, Zeter, Zeter!" — "If the Tausves-Jontof's worthless, / What is any worth? God save us!" (Heine, Disputation). The Tausves-Jontof was a medieval commentary on the Hebrew Talmud; Marx uses the quotation ironically to suggest that the bill of exchange has become the new sacred text of the money market, the ultimate standard of value.

The question Marx now poses is: "How then are these two means of circulation related?" He cites Gilbart (An Inquiry into the Causes of the Pressure, etc., p. 31): "The reduction of the amount of the note circulation uniformly increases the amount of the bill circulation. These bills are of two classes — commercial bills and bankers' bills ... when money becomes scarce, the money-lenders say, 'draw upon us and we will accept'. And when a country banker discounts a bill for his customer, instead of giving him the cash, he will give him his own draft at twenty-one days upon his London agent. These bills serve the purpose of a currency." This is a crucial point: there is an inverse relationship between note circulation and bill circulation. When notes are scarce, bills take their place. The bill of exchange is a substitute for money, and its quantity expands when money is tight.

Newmarch confirms this in modified form (B. A. 1857, no. 1426): "There is no connection between the variations in the amount of bill circulation and the variations in the banknote circulation ... the only pretty uniform result is ... that whenever there is any pressure upon the money-market, as indicated by a rise in the rate of discount, then the volume of the bill circulation is very much increased, and vice versa." The bills drawn in a time of pressure, however, "are in no way the short-term bank bills that Gilbart mentions. On the contrary, they are to a large extent bills of accommodation, which do not represent any real transactions at all, or only such as are embarked upon simply so as to draw bills on them." Marx notes that he has already given sufficient examples of both kinds. This introduces the concept of the accommodation bill — a bill drawn not to finance a real transaction but merely to create a means of payment. These bills are pure credit instruments, backed by nothing but the mutual confidence of the drawers and acceptors.

The Economist (Wilson) comments on the security of such bills in comparison with banknotes: "Notes payable on demand can never be kept out in excess, because the excess would always return to the bank for payment, while bills at two months may be issued in great excess, there being no means of checking the issue till they have arrived at maturity, when they may have been replaced by others. For a people to admit the safety of the circulation of bills payable only on a distant day, and to object to the safety of a circulation of paper payable on demand, is, to us, perfectly unaccountable" (The Economist, 1847, p. 575). This is a striking argument: bills are more dangerous than notes, because notes payable on demand are self-limiting (any excess returns to the issuer) while bills at two months can be issued in excess without any check until maturity. The Currency School's obsession with note issue thus misses the more dangerous form of credit money.

Marx concludes: "The amount of bills in circulation, therefore, just like the amount of banknotes, is determined solely by the needs of commerce; in the 1850s, the U.K. circulation in ordinary times, besides £39 million in banknotes, came to some £300 million in bills of exchange, of which £100–£120 million were on London alone." The scale of bill circulation has no influence on the volume of note circulation and is influenced by the latter "solely in times of tight money, when the quantity of bills increases and their quality deteriorates." Finally, "at the moment of crisis, the bill circulation completely collapses; no one has any use for promises to pay, each wanting only to accept cash payment; only the banknote still keeps its ability to circulate, at least up till now in England, since the Bank of England is backed by the entire wealth of the nation." This is the culminating statement of the section: in a crisis, the hierarchy of monies reasserts itself. Bills of exchange are rejected; only banknotes — backed by the Bank of England, which is in turn backed by the wealth of the nation — retain the ability to circulate. The credit system collapses back into the monetary system.

The Power of Big Money Capitalists

Marx returns to Chapman's complaint that "there were several large money capitalists in London who were strong enough to bring the entire money market into disorder at a given moment and in this way fleece the smaller money-dealers most shamelessly." There were "several great sharks of this kind who could significantly intensify a difficult situation by selling £1 or £2 millions worth of Consols and in this way taking an equivalent sum of banknotes (and thereby available loan capital) out of the market." The collaboration of three big banks in such a manoeuvre "would suffice to turn a pressure into a panic." The biggest capital power in London is of course the Bank of England, "but its position as a semi-state institution makes it impossible for it to assert its domination in so brutal a fashion. None the less, it too is sufficiently capable of looking after itself — particularly since the 1844 Bank Act."

The Bank of England's Capital and Position

Marx now gives the Bank of England's balance sheet data. It has a capital of £14,553,000, and besides this disposes of a "balance" of some £3 million, i.e. undistributed profits, as well as all monies that the government receives in taxes, etc., which have to be deposited with it until they are used. If we add to this the sum of other deposits (in ordinary times some £30 million) and the banknotes issued without reserve backing, then Newmarch seems quite moderate in his assessment when he says (B. A. 1857, no. 1889): "I satisfied myself that the amount of funds constantly employed in the (London) money-market may be described as something like £120,000,000; and of that £120,000,000 a very considerable proportion, something like 15 or 20 per cent, is wielded by the Bank of England."

Marx then makes a crucial theoretical point: "In as much as the Bank issues notes that are not backed by the metal reserve in its vaults, it creates tokens of value that are not only means of circulation, but also form additional — even if fictitious — capital for it, to the nominal value of these fiduciary notes. And this extra capital yields it an extra profit." This is the theory of fiduciary issue as the creation of fictitious capital. When the Bank issues notes beyond the metal reserve, it creates value tokens that function as capital for the Bank, even though they are not backed by real metal. This is the source of the Bank's extra profit. Wilson asks Newmarch (1563): "The circulation of a banker, so far as it is kept out upon the average, is an addition to the effective capital of that banker, is it not?" Newmarch: "Certainly." (1564) "Then whatever profit he derives from that circulation is a profit derived from credit, and not from a capital which he actually possesses?" Newmarch: "Certainly."

Marx notes that the same holds for note-issuing private banks. In his answers nos. 1866–1868, Newmarch considers two-thirds of all these issued notes (for the last third, these banks must have metal reserves) as "the creation of so much capital," because this amount of metal money is saved. The banker's profit on this "may not be greater than the profit of other capitalists. The fact remains that he draws his profit from this national saving on metal money. But the fact that a national saving appears as a private profit is in no way shocking to the bourgeois economist, since profit in general is the appropriation of the nation's labour." This is a biting piece of sarcasm: the bourgeois economist accepts that a national saving (the economizing on metal money) should appear as a private profit (the banker's profit), just as he accepts that profit in general is the appropriation of the nation's labour. Marx then asks: "Is there anything more crazy than that between 1797 and 1817, for example, the Bank of England, whose notes only had credit thanks to the state, then got paid by the state, i.e. by the public, in the form of interest on government loans, for the power that the state gave it to transform these very notes from paper into money and lend them to the state?" This is a devastating irony: the Bank's notes had value only because the state guaranteed them, and yet the Bank charged the state interest on the loans it made with these very notes. The Bank was, in effect, paid by the public for the privilege of lending the public its own credit.

How Banks Create Credit and Capital

Marx now summarizes the ways in which banks create credit and capital: "(1) by issuing their own banknotes; (2) by writing drafts on London running for up to twenty-one days, which will however be paid to them in cash immediately they are written; (3) by re-issuing bills of exchange, whose creditworthiness is created first and foremost by the endorsement of the bank, at least for the district in question." He explains that the provincial banks are obliged to send their surplus funds (i.e. Bank of England notes) to London bill-brokers, who send them in exchange discounted bills. It is these bills which the provincial banks use to serve their customers, since their general rule is not to re-issue bills of exchange received from local clients, "so that their business operations do not become known in their own locality." These bills of exchange received from London can be issued not only to clients who have direct payments to make in London, in case these do not prefer to get the bank's own draft on London; "they also serve to settle payments in the provinces, for the banker's endorsement secures them local credit. In Lancashire, for example, they have driven out of circulation all the notes of the local banks and a great part of the Bank of England's notes" (ibid., nos. 1568–1574). This is a concrete illustration of the way in which bank credit creates a circulation medium: the endorsed bill becomes a local currency, displacing the banknotes that would otherwise be required.

Bank of England Power and Its Limits

Marx now turns to the Bank of England's power to regulate the market rate of interest. "In times when business runs its normal course, it may happen that the Bank of England cannot check a moderate drain of gold from its metal reserve by raising its discount rate, since the demand for means of payment is satisfied by the private and joint-stock banks, and by bill-brokers, who have acquired a great deal of capital power in the last thirty years." It then has to employ other means. But for critical moments, what banker Glyn (of Glyn, Mills, Currie & Co.) testified to the Committee on Commercial Distress (1848–57) still holds true: (1709) "Under circumstances of great pressure upon the country the Bank of England commands the rate of interest." (1710) "In times of extraordinary pressure ... whenever the discounts of the private bankers or brokers become comparatively limited, they fall upon the Bank of England, and then it is that the Bank of England has the power of commanding the market rate."

Marx adds an important qualification: as a public institution under state protection, and endowed with state privileges, the Bank "cannot exploit its power relentlessly, as a private firm can allow itself to do." This is why Hubbard stated to the Bank Acts Committee of 1857 (2844): "That will always be the case, because the Bank of England never goes quite so low as its competitors, and when the rate is highest, it is never quite as high." The Bank's rates are always between those of its competitors: it is a ceiling in times of low rates and a floor in times of high rates. This reflects its semi-public character: it cannot compete aggressively for business, but neither can it exploit a crisis to the fullest extent.

The footnote by Engels (footnote 12) gives an example from the stockholders' meeting of the Union Bank of London on 17 January 1894, where the chairman Mr Ritchie recalled how the Bank of England had put up its discount rate from 2.5 per cent (July) to 3 per cent and 4 per cent in August, and since despite this fully £4.5 million in gold had been lost, to 5 per cent, on which gold flowed back and the Bank rate was reduced to 4 per cent in September and 3 per cent in October. The point is that a moderate rise in the Bank rate did not stop the gold drain; only a more drastic rise did. Engels also notes that the Bank rate was not recognized in the market: "When the bank-rate was 5 per cent, the discount rate was 3.5 per cent, and the rate for money 2.5 per cent; when the bank-rate fell to 4 per cent, the discount rate was 2.5 per cent and the money rate 1.5 per cent; when the bank-rate was 3 per cent, the discount rate fell to 1.5 per cent and the money rate to something below that" (Daily News, 18 January 1894). This illustrates the gap between the Bank rate and the market rate, and the limits of the Bank's control.

The Screw and Its Effects: Gilbart

Marx quotes Gilbart (An Inquiry into the Causes of the Pressure on the Money-Market, London, 1840, p. 35): "As soon as the Bank puts on the screw, all purchases for foreign exportation immediately cease ... the exporters wait until prices have reached the lowest point of depression, and then, and not till then, they make their purchases. But when this point has arrived, the exchanges have been rectified — gold ceases to be exported before the lowest point of depression has arrived. Purchases of goods for exportation may have the effect of bringing back some of the gold which has been sent abroad, but they come too late to prevent the drain." The "screw" is the Bank's practice of raising the discount rate sharply in order to stop a gold drain. Its effect is to halt purchases for export, which lowers domestic prices, which in turn corrects the exchanges. But the correction comes only after a period of depression, and the gold has already left the country. Gilbart adds (p. 40): "Another effect of regulating the currency by the foreign exchanges is that it leads in seasons of pressure to an enormous rate of interest." And (p. 52): "The cost of rectifying the exchanges falls upon the productive industry of the country, while during the process the profits of the Bank of England are actually augmented in consequence of carrying on her business with a less amount of treasure." This is a precise statement of the distributive consequences of the Bank's policy: the burden of adjustment falls on industry, while the Bank's profits increase.

The Profits of the Bank of England and the Bank of Ireland

Marx quotes Samuel Gurney: "The great fluctuations in the rate of interest are advantageous to bankers and dealers in money — all fluctuations in trade are advantageous to the knowing man." And he adds: "even if the Gurneys skim off the cream by a ruthless exploitation of the commercial distress, while the Bank of England cannot do this with the same freedom, quite handsome profits fall its way too — not to speak of the private profits that come the way of the gentlemen directors as a result of their exceptional opportunity for knowing the overall state of business." Marx then presents the data on the Bank of England's profits for the period 1797–1817, from D. Hardcastle, Banks and Bankers, 2nd edn, London, 1843, p. 120. The totals are: bonuses and increased dividends £7,451,136; new stock divided among proprietors £7,276,500; increased value of capital £14,553,000; total £29,280,636 — "this being for nineteen years on a capital of £11,642,400." If we calculate the total profits of the Bank of Ireland, which also suspended cash payment in 1797, according to the same principle, we arrive at: dividends as by returns due 1821 £4,736,085; declared bonus £1,225,000; increased assets £1,214,800; increased value of capital £4,185,000; total £11,360,885 — "on a capital of £3 million" (ibid., pp. 363–4). Marx's comment: "Talk about centralization!"

These figures are striking: the Bank of England, on a capital of £11.6 million, earned profits of £29.3 million over nineteen years — a return of more than 250 per cent. The Bank of Ireland, on a capital of £3 million, earned £11.4 million — a return of nearly 380 per cent. These profits were earned during the period of the Bank Restriction, when the Bank's notes were inconvertible and the Bank was lending these notes to the government at interest. Marx's point is that the credit system, far from being a neutral mechanism for allocating capital, is a machine for the centralization of wealth in the hands of a parasitic class.

The Centralization of the Credit System

Marx concludes the chapter with a powerful statement of the credit system's class character: "The credit system, which has its focal point in the allegedly national banks and the big money-lenders and usurers that surround them, is one enormous centralization and gives this class of parasites a fabulous power not only to decimate the industrial capitalists periodically but also to interfere in actual production in the most dangerous manner — and this crew know nothing of production and have nothing at all to do with it." This is the culminating social judgment of the chapter. The credit system is not merely a technical mechanism; it is a structure of power. It centralizes wealth in the hands of a class that is external to production — "this crew know nothing of production and have nothing at all to do with it" — and that uses its power to "decimate the industrial capitalists periodically" and to "interfere in actual production in the most dangerous manner." The Acts of 1844 and 1845 are "proof of the growing power of these bandits, added to whom are the financiers and stock-jobbers."

The Banker as a Moral Institution: The Closing Irony

Marx closes the chapter with an ironic quotation from G. M. Bell, a Scottish bank director, in The Philosophy of Joint-Stock Banking, London, 1840, pp. 46–47: "Banking establishments are ... moral and religious institutions ... How often has the fear of being seen by the watchful and reproving eye of his banker deterred the young tradesman from joining the company of riotous and extravagant friends? ... What has been his anxiety to stand well in the estimation of his banker? ... Has not the frown of his banker been of more influence with him than the jeers and discouragements of his friends? Has he not trembled to be supposed guilty of deceit or the slightest misstatement, lest it should give rise to suspicion, and his accommodation be in consequence restricted or discontinued? ... And has not that friendly advice been of more value to him than that of priest?"

Marx's irony is devastating. The banker, who in the preceding pages has been exposed as a parasite and a bandit — who manipulates the money market for his own profit, who exploits the distress of the industrialists, who creates fictitious capital — is here presented by his own apologist as a "moral and religious institution," a guardian of the young tradesman's virtue. The juxtaposition is the point. The moral pretensions of the banking class are a mask for its predatory reality.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles Revealed

Several fundamental principles emerge from this chapter. First, and most importantly: the quantity of circulating media is determined by the needs of trade, not by the will of the banks or the state of the gold reserve. This is the chapter's central technical claim, and Marx substantiates it with an impressive array of empirical evidence, from Neave's testimony that the public can always take notes from the Bank, to Kinnear's demonstration that £6 million in gold could be exported without any contraction of the currency, to Tooke's observation that the Bank could increase its advances without increasing the note circulation. The Currency School's premise — that note issue determines prices and that over-issue causes crises — is refuted by the simple fact that, as long as notes are convertible, any excess is immediately returned to the issuer.

Second: the rate of interest is determined by the demand for and supply of loanable capital, not by the quantity of circulation. The chapter provides a series of historical examples (1797–1820, 1822–23, 1824–25, 1830, 1832, 1836, and the post-1851 gold discoveries) in which high circulation coexists with low interest and vice versa. The apparent dependence of the interest rate on the quantity of circulation in periods of pressure is an illusion; what is really happening is that the demand for loanable money has become absolute because credit has collapsed, and the quantity of circulation appears to determine the rate only because the hoarding of notes reduces the available loanable capital.

Third: credit is the regulator of the velocity of circulation in a double sense — it founds all methods of economizing on circulating media, and it mediates and accelerates the velocity of the money that circulates. The clearing house, the bill of exchange, the deposit-and-loan cycle — all rest on credit; and the same piece of money can perform more transactions in a given time when its movement is mediated by credit operations. The corollary is that credit and money are not rivals but complements: credit does not replace money so much as it allows a given quantity of money to do more work.

Fourth: the credit system rests on a metallic base that is utterly inadequate to its superstructure. Chapman's £300 million in liabilities against £23 million in coin is the emblematic statement of this disproportion. In normal times, the disproportion is invisible because credit functions as a substitute for money; in a crisis, the disproportion becomes catastrophic because credit collapses back into a demand for money. This is the principle that later theorists — from Marx to Keynes and Minsky — would recognize as the fundamental instability of a monetary economy.

Fifth: the credit system is a machine for the centralization of wealth in the hands of a parasitic class. This is the social judgment that Marx weaves into the technical analysis. The credit system is not neutral; it concentrates power in the hands of the banks, the bill-brokers, and the big money-lenders, and it enables them to exploit the industrial capitalists and to interfere in production. The Bank Act of 1844, far from restraining this power, intensifies it by compelling the Bank to contract its issues at the moment of greatest need. The profits of the Bank of England (£29.3 million over nineteen years) and the Bank of Ireland (£11.4 million) are the material expression of this power.

The Broader Theoretical Implications

The chapter has profound implications for the theory of money and credit. It establishes that money and credit are not separate systems but a single system with a hierarchical structure. At the apex is metallic world money, which serves as the ultimate reserve and as the guarantee of the convertibility of credit money. Below it is banknote money, which circulates as a substitute for metal and which, in England, is backed by the entire wealth of the nation. Below that is the bill of exchange, which circulates as a substitute for banknotes and which, in times of tight money, expands even as the note circulation contracts. At the bottom are accommodation bills and other pure credit instruments, which have no counterpart in real transactions. In a crisis, the hierarchy is reversed: the lower forms are rejected, and the demand concentrates on the higher forms. This is what Marx means by the "collapse of the credit system into the monetary system."

The chapter also establishes the essential point that the issuance of means of circulation is not the same as the lending of capital. When a bank discounts a bill, it advances money, not capital. The capital being circulated already exists; the bank merely provides the medium through which it circulates. This distinction is crucial to understanding why the Bank's increased advances need not increase the note circulation (Tooke's evidence), and why the demand for money in a crisis is not a demand for capital (Chapman's exchange with Cayley). The confusion between money and capital is the fundamental error of the Currency School, and Marx's insistence on the distinction is one of his most enduring contributions to monetary theory.

The Practical and Political Implications

The practical implications of the chapter are political. If the quantity of circulation is determined by the needs of trade, then the Bank Act of 1844 is unnecessary and harmful. It is unnecessary because the note circulation regulates itself; it is harmful because it compels the Bank to contract its issues precisely when the world is crying out for notes. Marx's evidence from 1847 and 1857 — that the mere suspension of the Act was sufficient to end the panic, without any actual increase in circulation — demonstrates that the Act is a self-inflicted wound. The choice that Chapman poses between specie payments and the industry of the country is a false choice, created by the Act.

The deeper political implication is that the credit system gives a class of parasites "fabulous power" over production. This class does not produce anything; it lives on the credit that it controls. It uses its power to "decimate the industrial capitalists periodically" and to exploit the distress of the community. The moral pretensions of the banker, exposed in Bell's homily, are a mask for this predatory reality. Marx's analysis thus points beyond the critique of monetary policy to the critique of the credit system as a whole, and to the necessity of bringing it under social control.

The Lessons for Today

For the modern reader, the chapter offers several lessons. First, it teaches the importance of distinguishing between money and credit, and between the quantity of money and the rate of interest. The chapter's empirical demonstration that the interest rate does not depend on the quantity of circulation is a permanent contribution to monetary theory, and it anticipates the modern understanding of the interest rate as determined by the supply and demand for loanable funds.

Second, it teaches the essential instability of a credit economy. The chapter's account of the crisis — the hoarding, the steeplechase for means of payment, the collapse of the bill circulation, the sudden disappearance of banknotes from the market — is a vivid and enduring description of financial panic. The disproportion between the vast structure of credit and the slender metallic base on which it rests is the fundamental source of this instability, and it is a lesson that the financial crises of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries have repeatedly confirmed.

Third, it teaches the political economy of the money market. The chapter's exposure of the Bank of England's power, its profits, and its manipulation of the interest rate is a case study in the relationship between economic power and political power. The Bank is a "semi-state institution" that uses its state privileges to enrich its shareholders and to discipline the economy. The "screw" is the instrument of this discipline, and its cost falls on "the productive industry of the country."

Fourth, it teaches the importance of reading the sources critically. Marx's method in this chapter — reading the bankers' testimony against the grain, exposing their self-interest, and drawing out the implications of their own admissions — is a model of critical scholarship. The bankers tell us more than they intend to; they reveal the inner workings of the credit system even as they defend it.

Finally, the chapter teaches the enduring truth that the credit system is not a neutral mechanism but a structure of power. The bankers and bill-brokers who dominate the money market are not passive intermediaries; they are active participants in the exploitation of the industrial capitalists. The "moral and religious institution" of the banker is a façade for a system of exploitation, and the task of political economy is to penetrate that façade. This is the deepest lesson of the chapter, and it is the one that gives it its enduring relevance.
