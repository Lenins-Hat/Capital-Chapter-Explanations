Volume 3 • Chapter 36: Pre-Capitalist Relations

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS:

Chapter 36 of Capital, Volume III constitutes Marx's most sustained historical-materialist investigation of what he calls the "antediluvian forms of capital": merchant's capital and its twin, usurer's capital (interest-bearing capital in its archaic guise). The chapter's immediate position within the broader architecture of Volume III is significant. Having just established, in the immediately preceding chapters, that industrial capital is the form of capital par excellence — the form from which profit of enterprise, interest, and ground-rent are all ultimately differentiated as tributary portions of a single surplus-value — Marx here steps back to examine the historical genesis and persistence of interest-bearing capital in the modes of production that precede capitalism and that also survive within and alongside capitalism as subordinate, parasitic residuals. The central thesis can be stated precisely: usurer's capital is a mode of exploitation without a corresponding mode of production. It is not a transitional form that itself produces capitalism but rather a dissolving solvent that, under specific historical conditions, may help to decompose pre-capitalist relations and thereby help to clear the ground for a capitalist mode of production — yet which, absent those other conditions, merely impoverishes, cripples, and perpetuates backwardness. The chapter thus provides a decisive historical counterweight to a persistent error, both popular and semi-theoretical, namely the tendency to treat interest-bearing capital as the fundamental or "purest" form of capital as such.

The intellectual objective the author pursues is threefold. First, Marx wants to explain why interest-bearing capital appears historically prior to industrial capital yet cannot be its determining form; he must show that this priority in time is not a priority in conceptual or logical determination. Second, he must distinguish sharply between the interest-bearing capital that is an organic element of the capitalist mode of production — the loanable capital that presupposes and rests upon wage-labour, the expropriation of the producer from the conditions of labour, and the equalization of the general rate of profit — and the usurer's capital that operates on entirely different presuppositions: the continued ownership (real or nominal) of their conditions of labour by small producers, and the consumption-borrowing of luxurious landed proprietors. Third, the chapter serves as the historical foundation for the subsequent analysis of credit, since the modern credit system is defined, from its origins, as a reaction against usury — the subordination of interest-bearing capital to the requirements of the capitalist mode of production.

How does this chapter fit into the broader subject or theoretical framework? Capital, Volume III as a whole is organized around the "trinity formula" — the division of surplus-value into the three great revenue forms: profit of enterprise (which in turn splits into the industrial profit and interest), ground-rent, and wages. Chapter 36 confronts the most primitive and historically earliest of these forms — interest — and asks what makes it capital at all, and what distinguishes its pre-capitalist career from its capitalist career. In doing so, the chapter also prepares the ground for Volume III's deeper analysis of the credit system as the "most artificial and elaborate product brought into existence by the capitalist mode of production," a system that simultaneously socializes and privately monopolizes the means of production, and that Marx identifies as one of the most powerful levers for driving capitalist production beyond its own barriers — and thus, inherently, as a form pregnant with the abolition of capital itself. The chapter thus sits at a crucial hinge: between the abstract theory of the division of profit and the concrete historical and institutional analysis of credit; between pre-capitalist economic formations and the mature capitalist world market; and between the critique of political economy and the critique of the socialist (Saint-Simonian, Proudhonian) fantasies about credit.

Finally, a word on the author's method, which the reader must grasp to follow the argument. Marx proceeds dialectically and historically at once. He insists that the same forms — usury during the Republic of Rome, usury in the twelfth-century Rhineland, usury in Polish bill-broking of the eighteenth century, the usury of the English pawnshop in the nineteenth — recur both in pre-capitalist formations and within the developed capitalist system, but that their character is entirely determined by the social conditions under which they function and by the class position of the borrower who confronts the money-lender. The chapter therefore continually warns against comparing forms that share a name but differ in determining content. It is this methodological imperative — never confuse a form with its function, a temporal priority with a logical primacy, or a quantitative rate with the social relations that establish its meaning — that organizes every section.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS:

§1 The Antediluvian Twins: Usurer's Capital and Merchant's Capital as Pre-Capitalist Forms

Marx opens the chapter with a definition of the form under examination: "Interest-bearing capital, or, to describe it in its archaic form, usurer's capital, belongs together with its twin brother, merchant's capital, to the antediluvian forms of capital." The word "antediluvian" — pre-Flood, in the biblical idiom — signals that these are forms existing before the deluge that reshaped the world into a system of wage-labour and industrial profit. Both forms "long precede the capitalist mode of production and are to be found in the most diverse socio-economic formations." The essential point is that these forms do not require capitalism; they require only two minimal conditions. First, "at least a portion of the products is transformed into commodities" — that is, some products must become exchange-values, not merely use-values. Second, "money in its various functions develops concurrently with trade in commodities" — money must exist and perform its roles (measure of value, means of purchase, means of payment, hoard) as an accompaniment to commodity exchange. Nothing more is strictly required. In particular, capital as a relation of production (wage-labour exploited for surplus-value, with the producers divorced from their conditions of labour) is not required. This is the note that will sound through the entire chapter.

A crucial analytical distinction is introduced here between two kinds of lending, distinguishable by the purpose and social position of the borrower. "The merchant borrows money to make a profit with it, to use it as capital, i.e. to lay it out." This is the productive borrower who borrows to valorize — a borrower whose relation to the money-lender, Marx says, is "precisely the same way as" in relation to the "modern capitalist." This specific relationship — the lending of money to a borrower who will use it as capital to make a profit — was even recognized by the Catholic universities. Marx quotes Augier's Le Crédit public: the universities of Alcalá, Salamanca, Ingolstadt, Freiburg im Breisgau, Mainz, Cologne and Trier "successively acknowledged the legality of interest on commercial loans," with the first five approvals deposited in the consulate archives of Lyons. The point is that even the Church's own intellectual institutions had to admit that productive, commercial borrowing occupies a different moral and economic category from consumption-lending to the distressed — anticipating an argument that will be developed later in the chapter.

Marx grounds the very possibility of interest in the social relations of production rather than in any intrinsic property of money. He gives the case of the slave economy of the later Greco-Roman era, "not patriarchal slavery, but rather that of the later phases." Where slave economy exists "as a means of enrichment," where money is the means of appropriating other people's labour by purchase of slaves, land, etc., then "money can be valorized as capital and comes to bear interest precisely because it can be invested in this way." Interest is thus not a property of money but a consequence of the productive relations in which money can be deployed. This is a decisive methodological claim, and it recurs throughout the chapter as the solvent of all the superficial analogies between ancient and modern rates of interest.

§2 The Two Characteristic Forms of Usurer's Capital

Marx then isolates two forms whose longevity and theoretical importance justify the word "characteristic": "firstly, usury by lending money to extravagant magnates, essentially to landed proprietors; secondly, usury by lending money to small producers who possess their own conditions of labour, including artisans, but particularly and especially peasants, since, wherever pre-capitalist conditions permit small autonomous individual producers, the peasant class must form their great majority."

Both forms, Marx says, "lead to the formation and concentration of large money capitals" — but this process has completely different consequences from the process by which capitalism concentrates capital. Indeed, "the extent to which this process abolishes the old mode of production, as was the case in modern Europe, and whether it establishes the capitalist mode of production in its place, depends entirely on the historical level of development and the conditions that this provides." This is the decisive caveat: usury is a necessary but by no means sufficient condition of the formation of capitalism. Its effects are contingent on the presence or absence of other conditions.

The reason usurer's capital is the characteristic form corresponding to petty production is developed dialectically. "Usurer's capital, as the characteristic form of interest-bearing capital, corresponds to the predominance of petty production, of peasants and small master craftsmen working for themselves." Under developed capitalism, by contrast, "the conditions of production and the product of labour confront the worker as capital," so the worker "does not have to borrow any money in his capacity as a producer." When he does borrow, it is "for personal necessity, as at the pawnshop." But when the worker is "the proprietor of his conditions of labour and his product, in reality or in name, then it is as a producer that he relates to the money-lender's capital, which confronts him as usurer's capital." The form of the loan follows the form of the producer.

Marx then turns to a criticism of F. W. Newman's Lectures on Political Economy (London, 1851), which claimed that the banker is respected and the usurer hated because the former lends to the rich and the latter to the poor. Marx calls this "rather inane." The real distinction is not rich borrower versus poor borrower but "a difference between two social modes of production and the social arrangements corresponding to them." The apparent paradox is that usury's exploitation of the poor is inseparable from usury's ruin of the rich: "the usury that impoverishes the poor petty producer goes hand in hand with the usury that ruins the rich landed proprietor." Both processes concentrate monetary wealth, and both decompose the existing mode of production. The historical illustration is Rome: "As soon as the usury of the Roman patricians had completely ruined the Roman plebeians, the small farmers, this form of exploitation came to an end, and the petty-bourgeois economy was replaced by a pure slave economy." The dialectic of the chapter is precisely that usury, in ruining the small producers, may also ruin the conditions of its own continuation and be replaced by another, more brutal form of exploitation.

§3 The Level of Pre-Capitalist Interest and the Absurdity of Direct Comparison

This leads to one of the chapter's most important quantitative arguments. In the form of interest, "the usurer can in this case swallow up everything in excess of the producers' most essential means of subsistence, the amount that later becomes wages (the usurer's interest being the part that later appears as profit and ground-rent)." That is to say, in petty production the usurer's interest absorbs the entire surplus-value — everything above bare subsistence. It is therefore "quite absurd to compare the level of this interest ... with the level of the modern interest rate, where interest, at least the normal interest, forms only one part of this surplus-value." The modern wage-labourer produces and yields to the capitalist "profit, interest and ground-rent, in short the entire surplus-value," of which the interest paid to a money-lender is only one deducible portion. In pre-capitalist usury, the usurer alone appropriates the whole surplus. Comparing rates across such radically different distributions is a category mistake.

Marx here attacks Carey, who "makes this absurd comparison in order to show the great advantage for the worker of the development of capital and the accompanying fall in the interest rate." This is the apologetic use of a quantitative false equivalence — a sleight of hand that treats a lower interest rate as evidence of the worker's benefit, when in fact the fall is only possible because surplus-value has been diffused across profit, interest, and rent, and appropriated by capital in the process of production, leaving the worker with wages. The chapter's critical force is very much aimed at such apologetics.

Marx then examines the outcome when usury goes beyond extracting surplus labour and begins to extract the conditions of labour themselves. "If the usurer, not content with extracting his victim's surplus labour, gradually obtains the ownership title to his conditions of labour themselves — land, house, etc. — and consistently sets out to expropriate him in this way, it should still not be forgotten that this complete expropriation of the worker from his conditions of labour is not a result towards which the capitalist mode of production tends, but rather the given presupposition from which it proceeds." Here we encounter one of the most theoretically precise propositions in the chapter: capitalist production begins from the expropriation of the producer; it does not produce it as a tendency internal to itself. The expropriation is the historical precondition, not the result. Consequently, "the wage-slave is just as much excluded by his position as the slave proper from being a debt slave, at least in his capacity as producer; if he can become so at all, it is in his capacity as consumer." This clarifies the earlier point about the pawnshop: the modern worker may be caught by usurious consumer credit, but he cannot be caught by usurious producer credit, because he produces nothing of his own to be stripped from him.

The mode of production governed by usurer's capital of the second type, where the producer remains the owner of his conditions of labour, is characterized precisely: "Usurer's capital, in this form where it actually appropriates all the surplus labour of the direct producer, without altering the mode of production; where the producers' ownership or possession of their conditions of labour (and the isolated petty production corresponding to this) is an essential precondition; where capital therefore does not directly subordinate labour, and thus does not confront it as industrial capital — this usurer's capital impoverishes the mode of production, cripples the productive forces instead of developing them, and simultaneously perpetuates these lamentable conditions in which the social productivity of labour is not developed even at the cost of the worker himself, as it is in capitalist production." The key contrast is between developing the productive forces (as capitalist accumulation does, even at the worker's expense) and crippling them (as usury does, without compensating development).

§4 Usury's Double Effect on Pre-Capitalist Property

Marx now states the double movement of usury clearly: "Usury thus works on the one hand to undermine and destroy ancient and feudal wealth, and ancient and feudal property. On the other hand it undermines and ruins small peasant and petty-bourgeois production, in short all forms in which the producer still appears as the owner of his means of production." These are not two separate processes but two sides of the same solvent. But — and here is the crucial distinction — in the developed capitalist mode of production, "the worker is not the owner of his conditions of production," and "this alienation of the conditions of production from the producer ... corresponds here to a real revolution in the mode of production itself." That revolution consists in the bringing together of isolated workers into the large workshop, in specialized and interlocking activity, in the replacement of the tool by the machine. "The mode of production itself no longer permits the fragmentation of the instruments of production that is linked with petty property, any more than it permits the isolation of the workers themselves." Once this revolution has occurred, "usury can no longer divorce the conditions of production from the producer, since they are already divorced."

This gives us one of the chapter's most famous and vivid images: "Where the means of production are fragmented, usury centralizes monetary wealth. It does not change the mode of production, but clings on to it like a parasite and impoverishes it. It sucks it dry, emasculates it and forces reproduction to proceed under ever more pitiable conditions." The parasitical relation is here fully theorized: usury is not a developmental agency but a consuming one. The reason for the popular hatred of usury — most intense in the ancient world — is now explained on material grounds: "the producer's ownership of his conditions of production was at the same time the basis for political relations, for the independence of the citizen." Usury attacks not merely an economic arrangement but the material foundation of political citizenship; hence the political intensity of ancient hostility to usury.

The consequences of usury for the exploiting class are then traced. "As long as slavery prevails, or the surplus product is consumed by the feudal lord and his retinue, the mode of production still remains the same even though slaveowner or feudal lord fall prey to usury; it simply becomes harsher for the workers." Here we encounter the paradox that when the exploiting class is itself squeezed by usury, it simply intensifies its extraction — "The indebted slaveowner or feudal lord takes more out of them, since more is taken from him." Ultimately, the exploiting class may be displaced: "he may be completely replaced by the usurer, who himself becomes a landowner or slaveowner as the knights did in ancient Rome. In place of the old exploiter, whose exploitation was more or less patriarchal, since it was largely a means of political power, we have a hard, money-grubbing upstart. But the mode of production itself remains unaltered." This is an important formulation of a principle that runs through the chapter: the personnel of the exploiting class may change without a change in the form of exploitation.

§5 The Historical Conditions of Usury's Revolutionary Effect

Marx now formulates with precision the conditions under which usury actually plays a revolutionary role: "Usury has a revolutionary effect on pre-capitalist modes of production only in so far as it destroys and dissolves the forms of ownership which provide a firm basis for the articulation of political life and whose constant reproduction in the same form is a necessity for that life." Note the word "revolutionary" is used in a strictly negative and dissolving sense. Usury dissolves forms; it does not constitute new ones. The Asiatic forms illustrate the limited negative power: "In Asiatic forms, usury can persist for a long while without leading to anything more than economic decay and political corruption." Here usury is stagnant — it dissolves without generating. Only where "the other conditions for the capitalist mode of production are present" does usury appear "as one of the means of formation of this new mode of production, by ruining the feudal lords and petty production on the one hand, and by centralizing the conditions of labour on the other." Even then, it is only one of the means.

This is the chapter's most important historical-theoretical claim about causality: usury is a catalyst of already-potentially-present tendencies, not their originating cause. Marx's rigor here guards against any reading of the history of capitalism as the simple outcome of usury's destructive work.

§6 Medieval Interest Rates and the Absence of a General Rate

Marx now pivots to the historical record of medieval usury. "In the Middle Ages, there was no generally prevailing interest rate in any country. The Church prohibited all interest dealings from the start. Laws and courts gave little security for loans. All the higher was the interest rate in particular cases." The explanation is grounded in the rudimentary state of circulation: "The low monetary circulation and the need to make most payments in cash compelled people to borrow money, and all the more so, the more undeveloped the system of bills of exchange." The undeveloped credit instrument raises rather than lowers the cost of borrowing relative to a developed system. "There was great variation in both the rate of interest and the concept of usury."

Concrete illustrative data are given. "In Charlemagne's time, it was considered usurious to take 100 per cent interest. At Lindau am Bodensee in 1344, some local burghers took 216! per cent." (The exclamation mark and the particular figure are worth noting: the very notion of a "rate" becomes elastic when enforcement is not only weak but conceptually contested.) "In Zurich the town council fixed 43½ per cent as the legal interest. In Italy, 40 per cent had to be paid on occasion, though from the twelfth century to the fourteenth the rate did not usually exceed 20 per cent. Verona settled on 12½ per cent as the legal interest. Emperor Frederick II fixed 10 per cent, but this was only for the Jews. He would not decree for Christians. 10 per cent was already common in the German Rhineland in the thirteenth century." The source cited is Hüllmann's Geschichte des Städtewesens, II, pp. 55–7. These are not decorative numbers. They establish the enormous dispersion of interest rates in a system without a unified money market, and they illustrate Marx's earlier point that without the equalization of profit across branches of production, there can be no general rate of interest.

§7 Usurer's Capital: Mode of Exploitation Without Mode of Production

The next formulation is a compressed dialectical thesis: "Usurer's capital has capital's mode of exploitation without its mode of production." This is the definition of all pre-capitalist and residual usury. "This relationship also recurs within the bourgeois economy in backward branches of industry, or those that are struggling against the transition to the modern mode of production." The methodological consequence is immediate and characteristic: "In comparing the English rate of interest with the Indian, for example, we must not take the Bank of England's interest rate but rather that charged, for instance, by people lending small machines to petty producers in domestic industry." Here Marx warns against the very comparison (English versus Indian rates) that Adam Smith and later apologists made to argue for the civilizing effect of British colonialism in India. The proper comparison is between like functions, not like names. This is the methodological imperative announced in the introduction and now executing.

§8 Usury as an Instrument of Monetary-Wealth Formation

Marx now identifies usury's historical importance in terms of its relation to monetary wealth. "Usury is historically important, in contrast to wealth devoted entirely to consumption, as being itself a process giving rise to capital." The formula "giving rise to capital" is carefully qualified. It does not mean that usury creates capital as a relation of production; it means that usury accumulates monetary wealth independent of landed property: "Usurer's capital and mercantile wealth bring about the formation of a monetary wealth independent of landed property."

The argument now turns on the character of money in undeveloped systems. "The less developed is the character of the product as a commodity, the less exchange-value has taken command of production in its whole breadth and depth, the more does money appear as wealth as such, wealth proper, wealth in general, as against its restricted form of appearance in use-values. Hoard formation depends on this." Here we have a dialectic of wealth and its forms. In a system where most products remain use-values rather than commodities, money appears not as one commodity among others but as the commodity — as the universal equivalent standing in glittering isolation. Hoarding, the accumulation of money as such, is grounded in this.

Marx then specifies the functions of money that are central to the development of interest. "Leaving aside money as world money and as hoard, it is particularly in the form of means of payment that it emerges as the absolute form of the commodity." This is a precise theoretical claim: the function of means of payment — the function in which money is transferred after the transfer of goods, to settle a debt — is the function that cultivates the soil for interest. "And it is particularly its function as means of payment that develops interest and with it money capital. What wealth for extravagance and corruption wants is money as money, money as a means to buy everything. (Also for paying debts.) What the petty producer needs money for above all is for payment. (The transformation of services in kind and deliveries to landlords and the state into money rents and money taxes plays a major role here.) In both cases money is needed as money."

So long as the producer can satisfy his obligations in kind, he is not drawn into the money relation; but with the spread of money rents and money taxes, he must find money on a fixed date. It is then that usury can seize him. This historical mechanism is essential to Marx's overall story of the genesis of capitalism from the transformation of the form of rent.

And here is the crucial theoretical point about usury's function within hoard formation: "it is only in usury that hoard formation becomes a reality for the first time and fulfils its dreams. What is sought from the hoard owner is not capital but rather money as money; but through interest he transforms this money hoard, as it is in itself, into capital — into a means by which he takes partial or complete command of surplus labour, and in this way of a portion of the conditions of production themselves, even if these nominally still confront him as someone else's property." This is why usurer's capital is the "form of capital par excellence" to the popular mind, as Marx will later say — because it converts a passive hoard into a claim on future surplus labour without any apparent productive activity in between.

The famous image follows: "Usury seems to live in the pores of production, like the gods in Epicurus's intermundia." (The reference is to Epicurus's conception, in the intermundia or spaces between the material worlds of the cosmos, of the gods living apart from the world's affairs; Marx borrowed the image from Lucretius and uses it here to figure usury's peculiar position — present in all the gaps of production, but never organizing the productive process itself.) "It is all the more difficult to get money, the less the commodity form has become the general form of the product. The usurer therefore does not come up against any barrier except the incapacity of those in need of money to pay or their capacity to resist." The barrier to usury is not competition among lenders (which requires developed credit) but merely the physical limits of the borrower's capacity to pay or to resist.

§9 The Specific Terrain: Money as Means of Payment

Marx proceeds to isolate the specific terrain on which usury sits. "The proper, principal and specific terrain of usury is still the function of money as means of payment. Any monetary obligation — rent, interest, tribute, tax, etc. — that falls due at a certain date brings with it the need for a money payment." This is why in Roman and early modern Europe, usurers were so often tax farmers: "This is why usury so generally attaches to tax farmers, fermiers généraux, receveurs généraux, from ancient Rome through to modern times. With trade and the generalization of commodity production, purchase and payment become separate in time. Money has to be provided at a particular date." The implication is that even today "money capitalist and usurer merge into one" in modern money crises — moments when the temporal separation of purchase and payment becomes acute.

The chapter then gives a dialectical observation of considerable sophistication: "This very usury, however, becomes a major means of extending the need for money as means of payment, since it drags the producer deeper and deeper into debt and destroys his customary means of payment in that the interest burden itself makes his regular reproduction impossible. Here usury springs from money as means of payment, and broadens this function of money, its most specific terrain." Usury thus has a self-deepening character: it arises from the function of money as means of payment, and in the process expands and intensifies that very function. This is why, in a system where money has not yet become the general form of the product, the usurer faces no functional barrier to his expansion.

§10 The Credit System as Reaction Against Usury

Marx now makes the chapter's most important conceptual link to the subsequent analysis of credit: "The credit system develops as a reaction against usury." But he immediately qualifies: "But this should not be misconstrued, nor by any means taken in the sense of the ancient writers, the Fathers of the Church, Luther or the early socialists. It means neither more nor less than the subordination of interest-bearing capital to the conditions and requirements of the capitalist mode of production." The verb "subordination" is precise. The credit system does not abolish interest-bearing capital; it disciplines it. In the modern credit system, interest-bearing capital "becomes adapted on the whole to the conditions of capitalist production." Usury proper, however, "not only continues to exist, but in countries of developed capitalist production it is freed from the barriers that former legislation had always placed to it." So the modern credit system does not eliminate usury; it unshackles one form of it, while subordinating the other.

Marx lists the cases where borrowing retains the form of usurer's capital even under capitalism: "(1) where borrowing in the sense appropriate to the capitalist mode of production does not and cannot occur; (2) where borrowing results from individual need, as at the pawnshop; (3) where borrowing is for extravagant consumption; (4) where the producer is a non-capitalist producer, a small peasant, artisan, etc., i.e. is still the possessor of his own conditions of production as a direct producer; (5) finally where the capitalist producer himself operates on so small a scale that his situation approaches that of those producers who work for themselves." This is an important recognition that the categories of usury and modern interest are not historically exclusive but coexist as different forms of a functionally identical capital, distinguished by the social conditions of their operation.

§11 The Form versus Function Distinction

Marx now clarifies what does not distinguish the two forms. "What distinguishes interest-bearing capital in so far as it forms an essential element of the capitalist mode of production, from usurer's capital is in no way the nature or character of this capital itself. It is simply the changed conditions under which it functions, and hence also the totally transformed figure of the borrower who confronts the money-lender." This is a crucial theoretical claim. The capital is not intrinsically one thing or another; its social character is determined by the conditions under which it is lent and the class position of the borrower. Even where a man without means obtains credit as an industrialist or merchant, "it is given in the expectation that he will function as a capitalist, will use the capital borrowed to appropriate unpaid labour. He is given credit as a potential capitalist."

§12 The Apologetic Argument and the Catholic Church Analogy

Marx now addresses a well-known apologetic argument — that capitalism allows a man of talent but no wealth to become a capitalist, thereby "widening the basis" of capital. "And this fact so very much admired by the economic apologists, that a man without wealth but with energy, determination, ability and business acumen can transform himself into a capitalist in this way ... actually reinforces the rule of capital itself, widens its basis and enables it to recruit ever new forces from the lower strata of society." The apparent openness of the system is not evidence of its liberality but of its ability to absorb and appropriate the energies of the dominated classes.

Then comes a striking historical analogy: "The way that the Catholic Church of the Middle Ages built its hierarchy out of the best brains in the nation, without regard to status, birth or wealth, was likewise a major means of reinforcing the rule of the priests and suppressing the laity." And the axiom: "The more a dominant class is able to absorb the best people from the dominated classes, the more solid and dangerous is its rule." This analogy is not decoration; it identifies a general mechanism of class rule, and it makes the connection between the ideological rationale for credit ("open to all who merit it") and the material function of credit (reproducing capital's domination over labour).

§13 Reaction Against Usury in Its Various Forms

Marx now distinguishes the several historical reactions against usury. First, he dismisses the mere moralizing reaction: "Instead of anathema against interest-bearing capital in general, the founders of the modern credit system proceed from its express recognition." Second, he explains the institutional reaction of the Church and of the poor's protectors. The Monts-de-piété ("mounts of piety"), first at Sarlins in Franche-Comté in 1350 and later at Perugia and Savona in 1400 and 1479, were charitable pawn institutions aimed at protecting the poor from usury. But, Marx says with characteristic irony, they display "the historical irony which turns pious wishes into their very opposite when they are realized." He cites Tuckett's History of the Past and Present State of the Labouring Population (London, 1846, I, p. 114) on the pawnshops of England: there are about 240 licensed pawnbrokers in the metropolis and nearly 1,450 in the country; the capital employed somewhat exceeds one million pounds sterling; "this capital is turned round thrice in the course of a year, and yields each time about 33½ per cent on an average; according to which calculation, the inferior orders of society in England pay about one million a year for the use of a temporary loan, exclusive of what they lose by goods being forfeited." The bishop's charitable institution has become the pawnbroker's monopoly, and the charitable gesture has become 100% a year.

Marx next dismisses the "credit fantasies" of Dr. Hugh Chamberleyne and John Briscoe, who in the last decade of the seventeenth century "tried to emancipate the English aristocracy from usury by way of a land bank with paper money based on landed property." A quotation from their titles announces their purpose: "the general good of the landed men, the great increase of the value of land," exemption of "the nobility, gentry, etc., from taxes, enlarging their yearly estates, etc." Marx notes acidly that only the usurers would lose — "these worst enemies of the nation, who had caused the nobility and the yeomanry more damage than an invading army from France could have done."

§14 The Venetian and Genoese Credit Associations

Marx now turns to the truly productive institutional forms: "The credit associations set up in the twelfth and fourteenth centuries in Venice and Genoa arose from the need of the sea trade and the wholesale trade based on it to emancipate themselves from the rule of old-fashioned usury and from the monopolizing of money-dealing." He warns, however, against idealizing these institutions as purely public-spirited: "If the banks proper that were founded in these urban republics were at the same time institutions for public credit, from which the state received advances against taxes anticipated, it should not be forgotten that the merchants who formed these associations were themselves the most prominent people in those states and were equally interested in emancipating both their government and themselves from usury, while at the same time subordinating the state more securely to themselves." The banks were thus simultaneously instruments of commercial emancipation and instruments of class power.

Marx then supplies a famous observation on the political framing of banks, tied to the foundation of the Bank of England in 1694: "When the Bank of England was to be founded, the Tories objected that banks were republican institutions. Flourishing banks existed in Venice, Genoa, Amsterdam and Hamburg. But who ever heard of a Bank of France or Spain?" The point is that banking emerged where republican commercial oligarchies could control it, and that the Bank of England was an extension of a Whig financial interest.

§15 Amsterdam and Hamburg: Deposit Banks, Not Credit Institutions

Marx now distinguishes true credit institutions from mere deposit banks: "The Bank of Amsterdam (1609) was not a milestone in the development of the modern credit system, any more than that of Hamburg (1619). It was simply a bank for deposits. The cheques that the bank issued were in actual fact simply receipts for the coined and uncoined precious metal deposited with it and circulated only with the endorsement of their recipients." By contrast, in Holland "commercial credit and dealing in money did develop along with trade and manufacture, and by the course of development itself, interest-bearing capital became subordinate to industrial and commercial capital." The proof is the low level of the Dutch interest rate. "In the seventeenth century, however, Holland served as the model country of economic development, just as England does today. The monopoly of old-fashioned usury, based on poverty, was thrown overboard there automatically."

§16 The Seventeenth-Century Struggle Against Usury: Sir Josiah Child

Marx then documents the ideological struggle for a compulsory reduction in the interest rate: "Right through the eighteenth century we hear the cry for a compulsory reduction in the interest rate, with reference being made to Holland, and legislation proceeds in the same direction; the aim being to subordinate interest-bearing capital to commercial and industrial capital, instead of vice versa." The leading spokesman was Sir Josiah Child, "the father of normal English private banking." Marx compares his polemic against usurers with the attack of "off-the-peg tailors Moses and Son" on the monopoly of the bespoke tailors. He notes Child's multiple faces: "the father of English stock-jobbing" and "as autocrat of the East India Company, he defends its monopoly in the name of free trade." Child's Traites sur le commerce is quoted against Thomas Manley's Interest of Money Mistaken:

"As the champion of the timid and trembling band of usurers he erects his main batteries at that point which I have declared to be the weakest ... he denies point-blank that the low rate of interest is the cause of wealth and vows that it is merely its effect."

Child then articulates the dialectical relationship of cause and effect between low interest and wealth:

"If it is commerce that enriches a country, and if a lowering of interest increases commerce, then a lowering of interest or a restriction of usury is doubtless a fruitful primary cause of the wealth of a nation. It is not at all absurd to say that the same thing may be simultaneously a cause under certain circumstances, and an effect under others." And again: "The egg is the cause of the hen, and the hen is the cause of the egg. The lowering of interest may cause an increase of wealth, and the increase of wealth may cause a still greater reduction of interest." And: "I am the defender of industry and my opponent defends laziness and sloth."

Marx comments that "this violent struggle against usury, the demand for the subjection of interest-bearing capital to industrial capital, is simply the prelude to the organic creations that these conditions of capitalist production produce in the form of the modern banking system, which on the one hand robs usurer's capital of its monopoly, since it concentrates all dormant money reserves together and places them on the money market, while on the other hand restricting the monopoly of the precious metals themselves by creating credit money." This is a precise dialectical formulation: the modern banking system both socializes idle money (thereby undermining usury's local monopoly) and displaces the precious metals from the center of the circulation by creating credit money. It is a specifically capitalist social relation that breaks down the pre-capitalist walls of money monopoly.

§17 The Bank of England and the Founding Generation

Marx then traces the ideological milieu around the founding of the Bank of England. "The Scot William Paterson, founder of the Bank of England and the Bank of Scotland, is in every way Law the First." The comparison to John Law is significant: both Paterson and Law believed in the productive power of credit and public paper, and Law's experiment in France — his bank of 1716, made a state bank in 1718, ending in the inevitable collapse of 1720 — is given in a long footnote as the collapse of the very ideology of credit-as-wealth. Marx's footnote is precise: "The English banker and economist John Law ostensibly believed that the state could increase public wealth simply by inconvertible note issue. In 1716 he founded a bank in Paris, which two years later was made into a state bank based on this principle. Law's operations gave rise to a previously unheard-of degree of speculation, ending in 1720 with the inevitable collapse of his bank." The point is to document the genealogy of credit illusions.

Marx documents the opposition of the old money-lenders to the Bank of England. "Against the Bank of England, 'all goldsmiths and pawnbrokers set up a howl of rage' (Macaulay, History of England, IV [London, 1855], p. 499)." And further: "During the first ten years the Bank had to struggle with great difficulties; great foreign feuds; its notes were only accepted far below their nominal value ... the goldsmiths' (in whose hands the trade in precious metals served as the basis of a primitive banking business) 'were jealous of the Bank, because their business was diminished, their discounts were lowered, their transactions with the government had passed to their opponents' (J. Francis, op. cit., p. 73)." The old usurers, in their goldsmith form, are displaced by a socialized banking institution; the new institution breaks the old monopoly.

§18 The Credit Bank Project of 1683

Marx then turns to an earlier, less successful institutional experiment. "Even before the foundation of the Bank of England, a plan for a National Bank of Credit had already been drawn up in 1683, its purpose being, among other things: 'that tradesmen, when they have a considerable quantity of goods, may, by the help of this bank, deposit their goods, by raising a credit on their own dead stock, employ their servants, and increase their trade, till they get a good market instead of selling them at a loss.'" The bank was established in Devonshire House on Bishopsgate Street, and lent to industrialists and merchants, on security of three-quarters of the value of commodities deposited, in the form of bills of exchange. "In order to give these bills currency, a number of people in each branch of business combined together to form a company, from which anyone possessing this bank's bills was supposed to receive commodities for them with the same ease as if he offered cash payment. The bank did not do a flourishing business. The machinery was too complicated, and the risk involved in the depreciation of commodities too great." Marx thus documents the experimental failures that cleared the ground for the Bank of England's more centralized scheme.

§19 The Modern Credit System's Real Content: Subjugation of Interest-Bearing Capital

Marx now extracts the real content of the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century writings: "If we concentrate on the real content of these writings, which were the theoretical accompaniment to the formation of the modern credit system in England and helped to foster it, we find nothing in them but the demand for the subjugation of interest-bearing capital and loanable means of production in general to the capitalist mode of production, as one of its preconditions." The word "preconditions" is significant. The credit system is not a secondary institution; it is one of the constitutive preconditions of the capitalist mode of production.

§20 The Saint-Simonians and the Confusion of Travailleur with Industrialist

Marx now turns to the socialist "credit fantasies" of the Saint-Simonians — a discussion he himself apparently intended to revise. He begins by noting that "if we just look at the phrases used, the way they coincide with the banking and credit illusions of the Saint-Simonians is often astonishing, right down to the very words." He then makes a sharp economic-theoretical criticism of Saint-Simonian terminology: "Just as for the Physiocrats the cultivateur does not mean the actual worker on the land, but rather the big farmer, so Saint-Simon's travailleur is not the worker but rather the industrial and commercial capitalist, and this usage is still current with his disciples." He quotes from Religion saint-simonienne. Économie politique et politique: "A travailleur [worker] needs helpers, supporters, ouvriers [labourers — Marx emphasizes the word in the French quotation]; he looks for intelligent, adept and devoted ones; he puts them to work, and their labour is productive."

Marx then notes Saint-Simon's own development: "It should in no way be forgotten that it was only in his last work, Le Nouveau Christianisme, that Saint-Simon directly emerged as a spokesman for the working class and declared its emancipation to be the final goal of his endeavours. All his earlier writings are in fact simply a glorification of modern bourgeois society against feudal society, or of the industrialists and bankers against the marshals and law-mongers of the Napoleonic era. How different from the contemporary writings of Owen!" Marx's footnote — inserted by Engels from source manuscripts — clarifies that "in working over the manuscript again, Marx would undoubtedly have modified this passage substantially. It was inspired by the role of the former Saint-Simonians under the Second Empire in France, where, by historical irony, the world-shattering credit fantasies of this school were precisely realized, as Marx writes, in a swindle of previously unheard-of dimensions. Marx subsequently spoke only with admiration of the genius and encyclopedic mind of Saint-Simon. If in his earlier writings Saint-Simon ignored the opposition between the bourgeoisie and the proletariat, which in France was only just arising, and if he included the section of the bourgeoisie active in production in the travailleurs, this was Fourier's conception also ... If Owen saw further ahead here, this was because he lived in a different environment, in the midst of the industrial revolution and the class antagonism that was already acutely coming to a head." (The footnote is signed "F. E." for Friedrich Engels.) The methodological point stands nonetheless: terminology that conflates the industrial capitalist with the worker cannot grasp the wage relation.

§21 The Crédit Mobilier

The institutional realization of Saint-Simonian credit ideas was the Crédit Mobilier founded by Emile and Isaac Pereire, both ex-Saint-Simonians. Marx's characterization is withering in its historical precision: "a form that incidentally could come to such prominence only in a country like France, where neither the credit system nor large-scale industry was developed to the modern level. In England and America this kind of thing would have been impossible." He quotes the Saint-Simonian manifesto Doctrine de Saint-Simon. Exposition. Première année, 1828/29: "It is easy to understand how the banker can make cheaper advances than the capitalist and private usurer. And so it is also possible for this banker 'to provide the industrialist with his tools more cheaply, i.e. at lower interest, than the landlords and capitalists could do, as these could more easily be mistaken in their choice of borrower.'" Marx's note explains: "The Crédit Mobilier's operations, ostensibly in support of industrial projects and protected by the Bonapartist government, were based as Marx points out here on unsound principles and provided the framework for a variety of palpably fraudulent speculations. In 1856 and 1857 Marx wrote several articles for the New York Daily Tribune exposing the operations of the Crédit Mobilier, which finally went bankrupt in 1867."

But the Saint-Simonian manifesto itself, in a footnote Marx quotes with some relish, gives away the flaw in the credit-banking scheme: "The advantage that was supposed to follow from the intervention of the banker between the idle capitalist and the travailleurs is often outweighed and even destroyed by the opportunity that our disorganized society offers for egoism to hold sway, in the various forms of fraud and charlatanry; the bankers often intervene between travailleurs and idle capitalists simply to exploit both sides to the detriment of society." Marx's gloss is exact: "Travailleur here stands for capitaliste industriel."

§22 The Sources of Banking Resources

Marx now makes a decisive analytical correction of the Saint-Simonian conception of the banking system. "It is wrong, incidentally, to view the resources that the modern banking system has at its disposal simply as the resources of idle capitalists. In the first place, these resources include the portion of capital that industrialists and merchants keep temporarily unoccupied in the money form, as a money reserve or as capital still to be invested, i.e. idle capital, but not the capital of the idle; secondly, that portion of everyone's revenues and savings that is permanently or temporarily set aside for accumulation. And both of these are essential to the character of the banking system." This is an important theoretical distinction. The banking system's power resides not in the accumulated hoards of rentiers but in the idle capital of active industrialists and merchants (capital temporarily in money form between productive cycles) plus the savings of all classes. The character of the banking system is essentially circulatory — it pools the fluid capital of production itself.

§23 The Two Ultimate Presuppositions of Credit

Marx now states the two presuppositions on which the credit system rests. "It must never be forgotten, however, firstly that money in the form of precious metal remains the foundation from which the credit system can never break free, by the very nature of the case. Secondly, that the credit system presupposes the monopoly possession of the social means of production (in the form of capital and landed property) on the part of private individuals, that it is itself on the one hand an immanent form of the capitalist mode of production and on the other hand a driving force of its development into its highest and last possible form." The first presupposition — that credit ultimately rests on precious-metal money — sets an absolute limit to any purely credit-based expansion; the second — that credit rests on the private monopolization of the means of production — establishes credit as a constitutively capitalist institution, not a neutral technology.

§24 The Banking System as Social Book-Keeping

Marx now states, in an 1844-style formulation repeated from earlier drafts (the reference is to the Some Thoughts of the Interests of England of 1697), the great insight into the banking system's social character: "The banking system, by its organization and centralization, is the most artificial and elaborate product brought into existence by the capitalist mode of production. Hence the tremendous power an institution such as the Bank of England has over trade and industry, even though their actual movement remains completely outside its orbit and it behaves quite passively towards them. Such a bank, however, supplies the form of a general book-keeping and distribution of the means of production on a social scale, even if only the form."

This passage contains one of the chapter's most theoretically consequential formulations. Marx connects the social character of capital to its mediations: "We have seen that the average profit of the individual capitalist, or of any particular capital, is determined not by the surplus labour that this capital appropriates first-hand, but rather by the total surplus labour that the total capital appropriates, from which each particular capital simply draws its dividends as a proportional part of the total capital. This social character of capital is mediated and completely realized only by the full development of the credit and banking system."

The banking system, then, is the form of realization of capital's social character. And beyond that: "On the other hand this also goes further. It places all available and even potential capital that is not already actively committed at the disposal of the industrial and commercial capitalists, so that neither the lender nor the user of this capital are its owners or producers. It thereby abolishes the private character of capital and thus inherently bears within it, though only inherently, the abolition of capital itself." This is the dialectical pivot on which the chapter's final synthesis rests: the banking system, by socializing capital functionally while leaving its private ownership formally intact, creates a contradiction that points inherently toward a supersession of capital — but only inherently, only as a possibility, only in connection with other large-scale organic revolutions. Marx is scrupulous about refusing to read into the credit system an automatic transcending of capitalism.

§25 The Two-Sided Character of Credit

Marx now states the ambivalence of credit in explicit terms. "Through the banking system, the distribution of capital is removed from the hands of the private capitalists and usurers and becomes a special business, a social function. Banking and credit, however, thereby also become the most powerful means for driving capitalist production beyond its own barriers and one of the most effective vehicles for crises and swindling." This is the formula for credit as both expansive and crisis-generating. The same institution that mobilizes capital for production generates over-production, speculation, and collapse. The mutual implication is not incidental.

Marx further identifies the banking system as the operative demystifier of money's social character: "The banking system further shows, by substituting various forms of circulating credit for money, that the latter is in actual fact nothing but a special expression of the social character of labour and its products, which however, as antithetical to the basis of private production, must always present itself in the last instance as a thing, as a particular commodity alongside other commodities." Money's "thinghood" is thus revealed as an appearance of the social character of labour, an appearance that is however objectively necessary under private production. Credit, by functionally displacing money in circulation, exposes this.

§26 Credit as a Lever of Transition — with Caveats

Marx now returns to the theme of transition: "Finally, there can be no doubt that the credit system will serve as a powerful lever in the course of transition from the capitalist mode of production to the mode of production of associated labour; however, only as one element in connection with other large-scale organic revolutions in the mode of production itself." He immediately qualifies: "On the other hand, illusions about the miraculous power of the credit and banking system, in the socialist sense, arise from complete ignorance about the capitalist mode of production and about the credit system as one of its forms."

And then the resolute statement of the impossibility of credit without capitalism: "As soon as the means of production cease to be transformed into capital (which also means the abolition of private property in land), credit as such no longer has any meaning, something incidentally that even the Saint-Simonians have realized. As long as the capitalist mode of production persists, however, interest-bearing capital persists as one of its forms, and in fact forms the basis of its credit system." This gives us the chapter's most decisive formulation of the relation between interest-bearing capital and credit: interest-bearing capital is the basis of the credit system — credit is not merely a technical arrangement but a form of interest-bearing capital.

§27 Proudhon and the Absurdity of Crédit Gratuit

The critique turns finally to Proudhon: "Only that same sensationalist writer who wanted commodity production to continue while money was abolished (Proudhon) could dream up the enormity of a crédit gratuit [interest-free credit], the ostensible realization of the pious wish arising from the petty-bourgeois standpoint." The footnote refers to The Poverty of Philosophy and A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy. The critic's target is a basic incoherence: to retain commodity production while abolishing interest is to retain the social relations that generate interest while wishing them away. Proudhon's plan to have a bank supply interest-free credit to all producers would leave the wage-labour relation intact; or rather, it would collapse because interest is not a free-standing evil but a form of surplus-value.

§28 The Saint-Simonian Theory of Credit as Transferred Property

Marx enlists the Saint-Simonian text itself to state the relation of credit to property. "The purpose of credit is that in a society where some people possess tools for industry without the ability or the will to use them, while other industrious people possess no instruments of labour, these instruments can be transferred in the easiest possible manner from the hands of the former, their owners, to others who know how to use them. Let us note that according to this definition credit is a result of the manner and form in which property is constituted." Marx's gloss: "Thus credit disappears together with this constitution of property." The Saint-Simonians therefore themselves recognized that credit presupposes the specific property relations of capitalism and would be meaningless without them.

The Saint-Simonians then describe the passive function of banks: banks "consider themselves destined to follow the movement initiated by transactions outside their own domain, but not to provide the impulse for these themselves; in other words, the banks play the role of capitalists for the travailleurs to whom they advance capital." But the Saint-Simonians wanted more: "The Crédit Mobilier is latent already in the idea that the banks should take over this leadership themselves, and should excel 'by the number and usefulness of the firms they control and the works they have promoted.'"

§29 Constantin Pecqueur

Marx then introduces Constantin Pecqueur as "essentially always a Saint-Simonian, even if far more radical." Pecqueur demanded that the credit institution "govern the entire movement of national production," and the Saint-Simonians called this "système général des banques." Pecqueur's own warning is quoted: "Just try to create a national credit institution which will advance resources to people of talent and merit, but no property, without binding these borrowers together compulsorily in a close solidarity in production and consumption, but rather, on the contrary, in such a way that they themselves determine what they exchange and produce. In this way you will only achieve what the private banks already do achieve, anarchy, a disproportion between production and consumption, the sudden ruin of some and the sudden enrichment of others; so that your institution will never do more than produce a sum of benefit equally balanced by a sum of misfortune borne by others ... you will simply have provided the wage-labourers whom you assist with the means to compete with one another, just like their capitalist masters do now" (C. Pecqueur, Théorie nouvelle d'économie sociale et politique, Paris, 1842, pp. 433, 434). Pecqueur thus himself recognizes the coerced solidarity required if credit is to function as a socially organized alternative to competitive capitalism — and he rejects the alternative of merely subsidizing competition.

§30 Interest-Bearing Capital as the "Form of Capital Par Excellence"

Marx now returns to the question posed at the outset: why does interest-bearing capital appear to be the fundamental form of capital? "We have seen how merchant's capital and interest-bearing capital are the oldest forms of capital. But it lies in the very nature of the matter that interest-bearing capital should appear to the popular mind as the form of capital par excellence. In merchant's capital we have a mediating activity, whether this is considered as fraud, labour or whatever. In interest-bearing capital, on the other hand, the self-reproducing character of capital, self-valorizing value, the production of surplus-value, appears as a purely occult quality."

The phrase "purely occult quality" is precise. Because the interest-bearing capitalist performs no mediating activity — neither the merchant's buying and selling nor the industrialist's organization of production — the self-expansion of value appears as an intrinsic power of money. Hence: "even a section of political economists, particularly in countries where industrial capital is not yet fully developed, as in France, cling to interest-bearing capital as the basic form and see ground-rent, for example, simply as another form of this, in so far as here too it is the form of a loan that prevails. In this way the internal articulation of the capitalist mode of production is completely misconstrued, and it is quite overlooked that both land and capital are only hired out to capitalists."

§31 Loan of Capital in Kind and the Distinction of Immediate Producers

Marx then refines the analysis. "Instead of money, means of production can of course be loaned in kind, in the shape of machines, business premises, etc. But in this case these represent a certain sum of money, and if, apart from the interest, a portion is paid for wear and tear, this arises from the use-value, the specific natural form, of these capital elements. The distinguishing thing here again is whether they are loaned to the immediate producers, which presupposes the non-existence of the capitalist mode of production, at least in the sphere in which this kind of thing takes place, or whether they are loaned to industrial capitalists, which presupposes precisely that the basis is the capitalist mode of production." The class position of the borrower determines the character of the loan.

Marx then dismisses the confusion of housing rent with capital hire. "It is still more irrelevant and senseless to drag in the renting of houses, etc. for individual consumption. It is plain enough that the working class is swindled in this form too, and to an enormous extent; but it is equally exploited by the petty trader who supplies the workers with means of subsistence. This is a secondary exploitation, which proceeds alongside the original exploitation that takes place directly within the production process itself. The distinction between selling and lending here is completely immaterial and formal, and, as already shown, appears fundamental only for those who are in complete ignorance of the real context." Rent of a dwelling is not interest on capital — it is a species of secondary exploitation, on the same level as the petty trader's markup on the worker's means of subsistence.

§32 Usury as Conservative and Mode-Destroying

The chapter's general recapitulation now begins. "Usury, just like trade, exploits a given mode of production but does not create it; both relate to the mode of production from outside." This is the summary thesis stated at the outset and restated here. "Usury seeks directly to maintain this mode of production, so as constantly to exploit it anew; it is conservative, and simply makes the mode of production more wretched. The less the elements of production enter as commodities into the production process and emerge from it as commodities, the more does their establishment at a given place by means of money appear as a special act. The less important the role circulation plays in social reproduction, the more usury flourishes."

The inverted proportion is exact. The less commodity circulation dominates production, the more usury flourishes. "To say that monetary wealth develops as a special kind of wealth means, as far as usurer's capital is concerned, that this possesses all its claims in the form of monetary claims. It develops in a country all the more, the more the bulk of production is confined to services in kind, etc., i.e. to use-values."

§33 The Dual Role of Usury in Forming Industrial Capital

The chapter's historical synthesis is then stated: "Usury, by its double effect, is a powerful lever in forming the preconditions for industrial capital. Firstly, it always forms an autonomous monetary wealth alongside the class of merchants, while secondly it appropriates the conditions of labour, by ruining the owners of the old conditions of labour." Thus usury's contribution to capitalism is twofold: creating liquid capital and expropriating producers. Both preconditions of industrial capital, but not the sufficient conditions.

§34 The Luther Quotations and the Medieval-Versus-Modern Rate

The chapter now closes with a series of supporting historical documents. The first is Gilbert's History and Principles of Banking on the medieval pattern: "In the Middle Ages the population was purely agricultural. Under such a government as was the feudal system there can be but little traffic, and hence but little profit. Hence the laws against usury were justified in the Middle Ages." Gilbert goes on to give the statutory history in England: interest limited to 10 per cent under Henry VIII, 8 per cent under James I, 6 per cent under Charles II, 5 per cent under Queen Anne. The reason for statutory caps is that "in those times, the lenders ... had, in fact, though not a legal, yet an actual monopoly, and hence it was necessary that they, like other monopolists, should be placed under restraint." The key dialectical point: "In our times, it is the rate of profit which regulates the rate of interest. In those times, it was the rate of interest which regulated the rate of profit." This is a precise statement of the inversion of causality between pre-capitalist and capitalist exchange. Under usury, high interest feeds into high merchant's profit; under capitalism, the general rate of profit regulates interest.

Second, Marx gives the Lutheran passage from An die Pfarrherrn wider den Wucher zu predigen (1540), quoted by Gilbart (or rather, from Luther's Werke, Wittenberg, 1589, part 6, p. 312). Luther's complaint about the rates current at the Leipzig fairs is colorful and quantitative: "I have heard it said that 10 gulden are now taken at each Leipzig fair in the year, that is 30 gulden on every 100; some add the Neuenburg fair, thus making 40 gulden on every 100." Luther's arithmetic of the ruination is vivid:

"If someone who has 100 florins at Leipzig takes 40 per year, this means he gobbles up a peasant or a burgher. If he has 1,000 florins, he takes 400 each year and gobbles up a knight or a rich nobleman. If he has 10,000 florins, he takes 4,000 per year and gobbles up a rich count. If he has 100,000, as must be the case with the large dealers, he takes 40,000 per year and has gobbled up a great rich prince. If he has 1,000,000, he takes 400,000 and gobbles up a great king. And to do this he does not suffer any danger, either to his body or his goods, does not work, but sits by his stove and bakes apples; in this way a mean robber could sit at home and gobble up the whole world in ten years."

Luther's second passage Marx adduces marks the ideological crisis of usury in the sixteenth century: "Fifteen years ago I wrote against usury, when it had already spread so widely that I could not hope for any improvement. Since that time it has become so arrogant that it is no longer content to be classed as vice, sin, or shame, but has itself praised as a pure virtue and honour. What will deliver us now that shame has become honour, and vice virtue?" The dialectic of moral evaluation tracks the dialectic of class power: as usury consolidates, its apologetic voice displaces its moral condemnation.

§35 Hardcastle's Bankers and the Venetian Example

Marx then quotes Hardcastle's Banks and Bankers (2nd edn, London, 1843, pp. 19, 20) on the social origins of banking: "Jews, Lombards, usurers and extortioners were our first bankers, our primitive traffickers in money, their character little short of infamous ... They were joined by London goldsmiths. As a body ... our primitive bankers ... were a very bad set, they were gripping usurers, iron-hearted extortioners." This is the moral-historical register in which the emergent credit system was remembered by the nineteenth-century bankers themselves.

Next he quotes Augier again on the spread of banking after Venice: "The example provided by Venice (the formation of a bank) was thus quickly imitated; all coastal cities, and all cities everywhere which had made a name for themselves by their independence and their trade, founded their first banks. The return of their ships, which was often long delayed, led unavoidably to the custom of giving credit, which was strengthened still further in the wake of the discovery of America and the trade there. (This is an important point.) The chartering of ships made large advances necessary, as was already true in antiquity in the case of Athens and Greece. In 1308 the Hansa city of Bruges possessed an insurance company." The parenthetical "(This is an important point.)" is Marx's own insertion. Its logic is that the insecurity and temporal extension of maritime trade — ships returning late — generated credit relations as a functional necessity, and the discovery of America extended this further still. Maritime trade is thus identified as a principal historical driver of credit institutions.

§36 Sir Dudley North and the Confession of Consumption-Lending

Marx then quotes Sir Dudley North's Discourses upon Trade (London, 1691, pp. 6–7): "The moneys employed at interest in this nation, are not near the tenth part, disposed to trading people, wherewith to manage their trades; but are for the most part lent for the supplying of luxury, and to support the expense of persons, who though great owners of lands, yet spend faster than their lands bring in; and being loath to sell, choose rather to mortgage their estates." North's confession, from the late seventeenth century, is that even in England before the modern credit system, most money lent was for consumption by the wealthy — that is, usury of the first kind, the lending to extravagant magnates that Marx identified as one of the two characteristic pre-capitalist forms. The empirical confirmation is important: even in the country that would become the home of industrial capitalism, the dominant form of interest-bearing capital up to 1690 was the usurer's lending to rentiers on mortgage.

§37 The Polish Bill-of-Exchange Usury

Marx then gives the Polish example from J. G. Busch's Theoretisch-praktische Darstellung der Handlung, etc. (3rd edn, Hamburg, 1808, vol. ii, pp. 232, 233): "Warsaw had a large business in bills of exchange, but one that was principally based on and oriented towards the lending of its bankers. In order to obtain money, which they could lend to the extravagant magnates at 8 per cent and more, these sought and obtained open exchange credit abroad, i.e. a credit that did not have any commodity trade as its basis, but which the foreign drawee would continue to accept as long as the remittances from these exchange dealings continued to return. They paid heavily for this with the bankruptcy of men like Tepper and other respected Warsaw bankers." The Polish example demonstrates the exploitation of the bill of exchange mechanism — originally a credit instrument based on commodity trade — as a pure vehicle for consumption usury. The moment the credit-worthy basis vanished (as the returns failed to return), the structure collapsed. This is a case study of a functional misuse: the credit mechanism is not intrinsically productive; it is retrofitted to consumption-lending and produces bankruptcy.

§38 The Advantages of the Church in Prohibiting Interest

The chapter concludes with a remarkable passage from Busch concerning the Church's material interest in its own prohibition of interest. "Taking interest had been banned by the Church, but not selling property to extricate oneself from need. It was not even forbidden to transfer property to the money-lender for a definite period, until repayment, so that the money-lender not only found his security in this, but could also enjoy compensation for the money he had lent in having the use of this property." The Church itself, "or the communities and pia corpora associated with it, drew great advantage from this, especially in the time of the crusades. This brought a very great part of the national wealth into mortmain, especially since the Jews were barred from practising usury in this way, it being impossible to conceal the possession of such fixed liens ... Without the ban on interest, the Churches and monasteries could never have got so rich."

This is the chapter's final historical irony. The Church's moral condemnation of interest, rather than restraining usury, functioned as a competitive advantage for the Church itself. The prohibition drove its Jewish competitors out of the mortgage market by exposing them to the confessional disqualification, while the Church was able to accumulate liens and property in mortmain — through the formal structure of a loan secured on land without appearing to violate its own ban on interest. The passage completes the demonstration that morality is systematically subordinate to the material relations of the modes of production in which it operates.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS:

The chapter yields a set of fundamental principles about the relation between capital, history, and social form that should be borne in mind long after the specific historical data fade.

First principle: Form is not function. Marx's most sustained methodological lesson is that a social form receives its meaning from the mode of production in which it functions and from the class position of the person through which it operates. Interest, a loan of money, the bill of exchange, the mortgage — these are names that recur across radically different social systems. Their content is determined by the relation of production in which they are embedded. To compare the Roman centurion's 40 per cent with the modern interest rate is not to compare analogous facts but to equivocate. From this follows Marx's critique of Adam Smith's comparison of English and Indian rates of interest, and above all his critique of Carey's use of the fall in the interest rate as evidence for the worker's improvement under capitalism.

Second principle: Usury is parasitical, not generative. Usury exploits the existing mode of production; it does not create it. Its characteristic action is the concentration of monetary wealth through the exhaustion of small producers and the ruination of feudal proprietors, without transforming the technique or the social relations of production. It "clings on like a parasite and impoverishes," it "sucks dry," it "cripples the productive forces instead of developing them." Only where "the other conditions for the capitalist mode of production are present" does the dissolving labor of usury become part of the formation of the new mode of production. This is the crucial guard against any teleological reading of economic history in which decaying usury automatically gives rise to capitalism.

Third principle: The double expropriation. Usury accomplishes the expropriation of the producer from his conditions of labour and the formation of an independent monetary wealth; both are necessary preconditions of industrial capitalism; but neither is sufficient, and the second can occur without the first, producing instead a slave economy (as in Rome) or a "harsher" version of the pre-existing exploitation.

Fourth principle: The modern credit system is a reaction against usury, not its abolition. The subordination of interest-bearing capital to industrial and commercial capital does not eliminate usury — it frees it in some domains (pawnshop, small producer, consumer credit) while disciplining it in others. And the credit system that effects this subordination itself rests on two presuppositions: the continued foundation of credit in precious-metal money, and the private monopoly of the social means of production. There is no credit without capitalism, and the dissolution of the property form would dissolve credit as a meaningful category.

Fifth principle: The two-sidedness of credit. Credit is simultaneously the highest form of the social character of capital — the form under which capital's social character is mediated and fully realized, the most artificial and elaborate product of the capitalist mode — and one of the most powerful instruments for driving production beyond its own limits and hence for generating crises and swindling. This ambivalence is not a contingent defect but constitutive. Any promise of a credit-based transcendence of capitalism (Saint-Simonian, Proudhonian) misconstrues credit as a neutral technology, whereas in fact credit is the functional expression of the monopoly of the social means of production in private hands.

Sixth principle: The radical independence of social form from social personnel. The usurer who becomes a landowner or slaveowner, the Catholic hierarchy built from the best brains irrespective of birth, the capitalist system that recruits the able from every class — these are all versions of the same lesson: a dominant class strengthens its rule not by exclusivity but by recruitment. The progressive-sounding openness of accession to the dominant class is itself a mechanism of domination.

Seventh principle: The functional asymmetry between lending for production and lending for consumption. Interest-bearing capital presupposes a borrower who will use the loan to valorize — to appropriate unpaid labour. Where the borrower is a small producer just struggling to reproduce, or an extravagant magnate spending faster than his lands yield, the economic logic is different: capital has its mode of exploitation without its mode of production. This asymmetry explains why the same "interest" can be an instrument of capitalist development or of parasitical decay.

The broader implications for Marx's project as a whole are considerable. The chapter provides the historical counterpart to the abstract theory of the division of surplus-value in the preceding parts of Volume III. It demonstrates that the differentiation of surplus-value into profit of enterprise, interest, and rent is not a historically invariant structure of human economy but a specific form of the capitalist mode of production, whose possibility rests on the expropriation of the producer and whose pre-capitalist analogues are functions of entirely different relations. It also develops the theme — essential to the critique of political economy — that the surface forms of economic life systematically veil the underlying relations. Interest-bearing capital, because it appears as the self-expansion of money alone, is the form of capital that most thoroughly "fetishizes" the social relation of exploitation; hence its predominance in the popular imagination and in apologetic theory.

Finally, the chapter's treatment of credit anticipates the later analysis of finance capital and of crises. The banking system is described as the most powerful means of pushing capitalist production beyond its own limits and as one of the most effective vehicles for crises. Marx's conception of credit is thus dialectical: credit socializes production without socializing ownership; it mediates the realization of the social character of capital precisely in the form of a vast private-credit network; and thereby it inherently — only inherently, only abstractly, only as a tendency mediated by other transformations — bears within it the possibility of the abolition of capital itself.

The fundamental lesson is thus the inseparability of economic form, class relation, and historical development. Usury is not simply an early or crude form of banking. It is the mode of exploitation that corresponds to the dominance of petty production, the mode that decomposes without constructing, the mode that concentrates claims to wealth without developing the productive forces. Where the conditions exist for its revolutionary effect, it serves as one of the means of the formation of capitalist production; where they do not, it merely multiplies the pathos of the pre-capitalist world. To imagine that credit freed from the wage relation could liberate the producer is thus to mistake an instrument of capitalization for a means of emancipation — the deepest error of both the Saint-Simonian banking utopia and the Proudhonian petty-bourgeois socialism, and the error against which Marx's Chapter 36 sets the most systematic and historically grounded warning.
