Volume 3 • Chapter 13: The Law of the Tendency of the Rate of Profit to Fall

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 13, "The Law Itself," opens Part Three of Capital Volume 3 — the part devoted to what is unquestionably the most explosive, the most contested, and the most theoretically consequential proposition in all of Marx's mature economic writing: the tendential fall in the general rate of profit. The chapter's function within the architecture of Volume 3 is precise and strategic. Volume 3 as a whole treats "the process of capitalist production as a whole," i.e., the unity of production and circulation, and Part Two (Chapters 8–12) has just accomplished the decisive transformation of surplus-value into profit and of the rate of surplus-value into a general, average rate of profit. That result — that profit is surplus-value reckoned against the total capital advanced, so that p′ = s/C, and that the general profit rate is regulated not by any single branch's composition of capital but by the average social composition — is the premise from which Chapter 13 now draws its dramatic conclusion.

The central problem the chapter poses can be stated with algebraic brevity and conceptual immensity. If the rate of profit is s/C, and if the historically demonstrated tendency of capitalist development is to raise c (constant capital — machinery, raw and ancillary materials, fixed capital of every kind) relative to v (variable capital — wages, living labour-power), then, so long as the rate of exploitation s/v does not rise enough to offset it, the rate of profit must fall. Marx's opening five-line series establishes this in miniature: given a 100 per cent rate of surplus-value and v fixed at 100, a constant capital of 50 yields a profit rate of 66⅔ per cent; of 100, 50 per cent; of 200, 33⅓ per cent; of 300, 25 per cent; of 400, only 20 per cent. "The same rate of surplus-value, therefore, and an unchanged level of exploitation of labour, is expressed in a falling rate of profit, as the value of the constant capital and hence the total capital grows with the constant capital's material volume."

But the problem is not merely that the rate of profit falls. It is that this fall is simultaneously a rise. The same development of the productive forces that lowers the ratio of surplus-value to total capital also swells the absolute mass of surplus-value — and hence of profit — appropriated by the total social capital. Marx's whole chapter is built around the task of showing that this is not a paradox, not a temporary coincidence, not a doctrinal embarrassment, but a necessary unity: "This not only can but must be the case — discounting transient fluctuations — on the basis of capitalist production."

The intellectual objective of the chapter is thus threefold. First, it aims to derive the law at the highest possible level of abstraction — in its "pure" form, before profit has been decomposed into industrial profit, commercial profit, interest, and ground-rent, and before the counteracting causes have been introduced. Marx is explicit and deliberate about this ordering: "We are deliberately putting forward this law before depicting the decomposition of profit into various categories which have become mutually autonomous." The point is methodological: because the law can be stated while profit is still simply "another name for surplus-value itself, only now depicted in relation to the total capital," its validity is shown to be independent of any particular distribution of surplus-value among rival claimants. Second, the chapter sets out to demonstrate the inner connection between the apparently contradictory phenomena of a falling rate and a rising mass — first as a matter of algebra (the proposition that the total capital must grow faster than the rate of profit falls if the mass of profit is to grow), then as a matter of political economy (the theory of accumulation, concentration, and the relative surplus population). Third, it seeks to explain the systematic misrecognition of the phenomenon, both in competition (where the law appears "upside down") and in the history of economic thought (where Marx claims no previous writer succeeded in discovering it).

This third ambition gives the chapter its extraordinary historiographical reach. Marx does not present the falling rate of profit as one theorem among many. He presents it as the theorem: "given the great importance that this law has for capitalist production, one might well say that it forms the mystery around whose solution the whole of political economy since Adam Smith revolves and that the difference between the various schools since Adam Smith consists in the different attempts made to solve it." The history of political economy, from this vantage, is the history of successive failures to conceptualize the law — failures rooted in the inability to distinguish constant from variable capital, to isolate surplus-value from profit, to separate profit in its pure form from its autonomized components, and to analyse differences in the organic composition of capital.

Finally, the chapter must be understood as the hinge on which the remainder of Part Three and much of Volume 3 turns. It is immediately followed by Chapter 14, on the counteracting influences — the "counterweights" (rising exploitation, cheapening of constant capital, the fall in wages below value, relative over-population, foreign trade, the growth of the stock of capital) which prevent the fall from presenting itself "in such an absolute form"; and by Chapter 15, on the internal contradictions of the law, in which the falling rate of profit becomes the deep ground of over-accumulation, crisis, the centralization of capital, and the historical limits of the mode of production. In the strictest sense, then, Chapter 13 is a statement of tendency whose own text acknowledges, from the second page onward, that a full account requires the chapters that follow. It is a chapter that knows it is incomplete — and that deliberately draws its power from that incompleteness, since the abstract law must first be established in its purity before it can be shown to be everywhere "suspended" by counter-tendencies internal to the same system.

The result is a chapter that is at once a piece of pure theory, a critique of political economy, and an anatomy of ideological illusion. Its thesis is that the progressive development of the social productivity of labour — the same development that produces ever cheaper commodities, ever greater masses of use-values, and ever larger absolute profits — necessarily expresses itself, on the capitalist basis, as a falling general rate of profit, and that this expression is the distinctive, historically specific form in which productivity growth registers within a system organized around the self-valorization of capital. The fall in the rate of profit is, in Marx's phrase, "simply the expression, peculiar to the capitalist mode of production, of the progressive development of the social productivity of labour." That sentence is the chapter's thesis in a single line, and everything else is elaboration, proof, and consequence.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Founding Series: A Fixed Rate of Exploitation Yields a Falling Rate of Profit

Marx begins not with history, not with statistics, and not with the observation of actual profit rates, but with a constructed series. The logic of the argument is deliberately dyadic. First, he stipulates the determinants of the exploitation relation: wages and the length of the working day are given. Under those conditions, a variable capital of £100 "represents a definite number of workers set in motion; it is an index of this number." Concretely: £100 buys the wages of 100 workers for one week. The variable capital is thus, as Marx puts it elsewhere, an index of the mass of living labour absorbed — a proxy for the number of workers, given a determinate wage level.

If each of these 100 workers performs as much surplus labour as necessary labour — that is, works half the day reproducing the value of their own wages and half the day producing value for the capitalist — then the total value product is £200: £100 of necessary labour reproduced as wages, and £100 of surplus labour objectified as surplus-value. The rate of surplus-value, s/v, is 100 per cent.

Here is the pivot. That rate — 100 per cent — is a proportion between two quantities internal to the labour process (necessary vs. surplus labour, or variable capital vs. surplus-value). The rate of profit is a proportion between surplus-value and the whole capital advanced. Because the denominator of the latter includes constant capital, an unchanged numerator-to-v relation can be expressed in any number of different profit rates, depending on the magnitude of c. Marx spells out the series:

  • if c = 50 and v = 100, then p′ = 100/(50+100) = 66⅔ per cent;
  • if c = 100 and v = 100, then p′ = 100/200 = 50 per cent;
  • if c = 200 and v = 100, then p′ = 100/300 = 33⅓ per cent;
  • if c = 300 and v = 100, then p′ = 100/400 = 25 per cent;
  • if c = 400 and v = 100, then p′ = 100/500 = 20 per cent.

Three specialized terms must be held clearly in mind throughout, because the chapter's entire force depends on keeping them distinct. Constant capital (c) is the capital laid out on means of production — machinery, buildings, raw materials, ancillary materials, fuel — which does not create new value but transfers its own value (in the case of fixed capital, gradually, via depreciation) to the product. Variable capital (v) is capital laid out on labour-power, which alone produces new value — including a surplus beyond its own reproduction cost. Surplus-value (s) is that unpaid value. The rate of surplus-value is s/v, the degree of exploitation. The rate of profit is s/C, where C = c + v, the total capital advanced.

The crucial conceptual move is thus a matter of denominators. The rate of surplus-value measures the exploitation of living labour from the standpoint of living labour alone. The rate of profit measures the same surplus from the standpoint of the totality of capital — as a return on every pound the capitalist has had to lay out, living or dead. Because the dead portion (c) grows disproportionately as technique develops, the same exploitation yields a shrinking ratio. This is the formal core of the law, and Marx states its conclusion without hesitation: the same rate of surplus-value and an unchanged level of exploitation "is expressed in a falling rate of profit."

From the Individual Capital to the Social Capital: The Generalization and Its Premises

Marx is immediately aware that the series just given establishes a relationship within a single capital. The law, however, is stated of the general rate of profit — the social average. He therefore supplies the requisite premises with considerable care. Two conditional steps are needed.

First, the change in composition must be general: "If we further assume now that this gradual change in the composition of capital does not just characterize certain individual spheres of production, but occurs in more or less all spheres, or at least the decisive ones...” This qualification matters, because the transformation of individual profit rates into a general rate of profit, established in Part Two, proceeds through competition and the equalization of returns on capitals of different compositions. If only an isolated branch experienced a rising c:v, its profit rate would fall relative to the average, capital would flow out, and the general rate would be affected only marginally. What drives the law is a change in the average organic composition of the total capital belonging to a given society — a social fact, not a sectoral one.

Second, the rate of surplus-value must be held constant (or, as later sections show, permitted to rise without fully offsetting). Given both premises, Marx's conclusion follows: "this gradual growth in the constant capital, in relation to the variable, must necessarily result in a gradual fall in the general rate of profit."

The word "gradual" is not decorative. Marx is describing a secular, developmental process — a trajectory — not a step-change. And the premise itself is not an ad hoc assumption. Marx asserts that it is "a law of the capitalist mode of production that its development does in fact involve a relative decline in the relation of variable capital to constant, and hence also to the total capital set in motion." The footnote anchors this in Volume 1, Chapter 25, §2 — the section on "The Progressive Production of a Relative Surplus Population or Industrial Reserve Army," where Marx established that accumulation on a rising technical basis systematically expels living labour from production relative to the mass of means of production it sets in motion.

The conclusion of this passage is a social-physiological statement, not merely an arithmetical one. The same number of workers — or the same quantity of labour-power purchased by a given v — "sets in motion, works up, and productively consumes, within the same period, an ever-growing mass of means of labour, machinery and fixed capital of all kinds, and raw and ancillary materials." The declining ratio v/C is therefore the quantitative signature of a qualitative transformation in the labour process itself.

The Rising Organic Composition as the Expression of Rising Social Productivity

Here Marx performs the decisive conceptual identification on which the entire chapter, and much of his historical materialist project, depends. He writes: "This progressive decline in the variable capital in relation to the constant capital, and hence in relation to the total capital as well, is identical with the progressively rising organic composition, on average, of the social capital as a whole. It is just another expression for the progressive development of the social productivity of labour."

The term organic composition of capital requires a careful gloss, because Marx uses it with precision and its meaning is often blurred in secondary literature. The composition of capital can be considered in two registers: the technical composition, which is the physical ratio of the mass of means of production to the mass of labour-power employed; and the value composition, which is the ratio in which the capital is divided into c and v as values. The organic composition is the value composition in so far as it is determined by the technical composition and reflects changes in it. It is thus neither a mere physical ratio nor a merely relative price ratio: it is the value-form whose movement is governed by the material-technical transformation of production. In this chapter, the rising organic composition is simply the rising c:v, understood as the expression of technological progress.

The identification Marx makes is that c:v rising, organic composition rising, and social productivity rising are three descriptions of one and the same process. He demonstrates this by describing the productivity increase materially: "the growing use of machinery and fixed capital generally enables more raw and ancillary materials to be transformed into products in the same time by the same number of workers, i.e. with less labour." Each worker now operates a larger and more elaborate apparatus, and processes more material, in the same period. More use-values emerge per unit of labour time.

A crucial consequence follows immediately: the cheapening of the product. "There corresponds to this growing volume of constant capital... a continual cheapening of the product. Each individual product, taken by itself, contains a smaller sum of labour than at a lower stage of development of production, where the capital laid out on labour stands in a far higher ratio to that laid out on means of production." This is a point that will be developed at length in the closing pages of the chapter, and it is essential to the whole argument: the fall in the profit rate is not a story about prices rising, but about the socially necessary labour contained in each commodity shrinking. The profit rate falls because the coefficient by which living labour is multiplied into value is diluted by an ever-growing mass of previously objectified labour — an idea that will receive its most brilliant statement in the "series" of the individual commodity later in the chapter.

Marx then performs the legitimating move: "The hypothetical series we constructed at the opening of this chapter therefore expresses the actual tendency of capitalist production." The abstraction is warranted — the Gedankenexperiment was not arbitrary but a controlled representation of a real historical trajectory.

"Self-Evident Necessity" and the Status of the Law as Tendency

Marx now sharpens the ontological status of the conclusion. The progressive tendency is "the expression, peculiar to the capitalist mode of production, of the progressive development of the social productivity of labour." And he goes further: "This does not mean that the rate of profit may not fall temporarily for other reasons as well, but it does prove that it is a self-evident necessity, deriving from the nature of the capitalist mode of production itself, that as it advances the general average rate of surplus-value must be expressed in a falling general rate of profit."

Two things merit attention. First, Marx concedes that other causes may produce temporary declines in the profit rate — crises, harvest failures, price shocks, wars, speculative bubbles. This is important because it means the law is not an empirical prediction of a monotone decline in observed profit rates. It is a structural claim about the direction of the regulating tendency. Second, the phrase "self-evident necessity" (selbstverständliche Notwendigkeit) signals that Marx regards the result as analytic given the premises — an entailment of the definition of the rate of profit combined with the demonstrated tendency of capitalist technique. It is not an inductive generalization but a deduction from the nature of the mode of production.

Note also the subtle wording: "the general average rate of surplus-value must be expressed in a falling general rate of profit." Marx is not claiming that the rate of surplus-value falls. He is claiming that the rate of surplus-value — even a stable or rising one — must express itself, through the mediation of a changing composition of capital, as a falling rate of profit. The distinction is what makes the law counterintuitive and what makes its discovery a conceptual achievement rather than a truism.

The paragraph closes with the density-of-labour argument in its most compressed form: "Since the mass of living labour applied continuously declines in relation to the mass of objectified labour that it sets in motion, i.e. the productively consumed means of production, the part of this living labour that is unpaid and objectified in surplus-value must also stand in an ever-decreasing ratio to the value of the total capital applied. But this ratio between the mass of surplus-value and the total capital applied in fact constitutes the rate of profit, which must therefore steadily fall."

The Historiographical Claim: Political Economy's Central Mystery

Having established the law, Marx pivots to the history of thought. "Simple as the law appears from the above arguments, not one of the previous writers on economics succeeded in discovering it, as we shall see later on." A footnote points to Theories of Surplus-Value, Part II, pp. 438–69 and 542–6 — Marx's historical manuscript on Smith, Ricardo, and their successors.

The claim is escalated dramatically: the law "forms the mystery around whose solution the whole of political economy since Adam Smith revolves," and the differences among schools consist in "the different attempts made to solve it." This is a very strong historiographical thesis. Marx is asserting that the entire intellectual history of classical and post-classical economics can be re-described as a series of failed attempts to come to grips with a single phenomenon that was, in fact, perpetually perceived — "these economists perceived the phenomenon, but tortured themselves with their contradictory attempts to explain it."

Marx then explains why the failure was structurally inevitable for a political economy operating with bourgeois categories. He lists four conceptual deficiencies:

  1. previous economics "fumbled around with the distinction between constant and variable capital, but has never managed to formulate this in any definite way";
  2. it "never presented surplus-value as something separate from profit, nor profit in general, in its pure form, as distinct from the various constituents of profit which have attained an autonomous position towards each other (such as industrial profit, commercial profit, interest, ground-rent)";
  3. it "never analysed the differences in the organic composition of capital";
  4. and consequently it "has not analysed the formation of the general rate of profit either."

The list is a virtually complete inventory of the theoretical innovations of Volume 3 itself. The failure to discover the law is thus not attributed to insufficient data or insufficient intelligence, but to category error — to the absence of the conceptual distinctions necessary to pose the problem correctly.

Methodological Priority: Why the Law Precedes the Splitting of Profit

Marx is a self-conscious methodologist, and he pauses here to justify the order of exposition. He is presenting the law before profit has been decomposed into the revenue-forms of industrial profit, commercial profit, interest, and ground-rent. Why does this matter?

Because the law's validity must be shown to be independent of that decomposition. "The independence of this presentation from the division of profit into various portions, which accrue to different categories of persons, shows from the start how the law in its generality is independent of that division and of the mutual relationships of the categories of profit deriving from it." If the fall in the profit rate were dependent on, say, a shifting balance between interest and industrial profit, then it would be a law about the distribution of surplus-value among the exploiting classes, not about the production of surplus-value in relation to capital as such. By presenting the law first, Marx immunizes it against the charge that it presupposes a particular distributional arrangement.

The conceptual identification here is emphatic: "Profit, as we speak of it here, is simply another name for surplus-value itself, only now depicted in relation to the total capital, instead of to the variable capital from which it derives." This sentences renders the law's logic transparent: the fall in the rate of profit is the fall in the ratio between surplus-value and the total capital advanced. It has nothing to do with who receives what. It concerns the ratio between the pool of newly created surplus and the magnitude of the capital that had to be advanced to produce it — a ratio determined entirely by the composition of capital and the rate of exploitation.

This passage also carries strategic weight. Rival theories could, and later did, treat the falling rate as an artefact of competition, monopoly, institutional conflict, or distributional struggle. Marx's architecture blocks these readings in advance by showing that the tendency is exhaustively implicit in the relation s/(c+v).

The International Analogue: Different Stages Coexisting in Space

Marx now extends the temporal argument into a spatial register: "What applies to different successive stages of development in one country applies also to different countries that find themselves in differing stages of development at the same point in time." This is a classic move in his method — the conversion of a genetic sequence into a comparative typology. Countries, like epochs, can be arrayed along the axis of capital composition, and the profit rate should, on the abstract premises, vary inversely with development: the "undeveloped country" with c:v = 50:100 would show p′ = 66⅔ per cent; the developed country with c:v = 400:100 would show 20 per cent.

But Marx immediately complicates the picture. The distinction "could disappear, or even be reversed, if in the less developed country labour was less productive," so that the worker spends a greater portion of the day reproducing their own subsistence and leaves a smaller portion for surplus. He works out a numerical illustration. Suppose in the less developed country the worker works two-thirds of the day for himself and one-third for the capitalist. On the assumptions of the earlier example, the same labour-power would be paid 133⅓ and provide a surplus of only 66⅔. To the variable capital of 133⅓ "there would correspond a constant capital of 50" — that is, the same low c, but a much larger v, since low productivity means a large proportion of total value added goes to reproducing the worker. The rate of surplus-value becomes 66⅔ : 133⅓ = 50 per cent. And the rate of profit becomes 66⅔ / (50 + 133⅓) = 66⅔ / 183⅓ = 4/11 ≈ 36⅓ per cent.

The point is precise: despite a lower rate of surplus-value (50 per cent versus 100 per cent), the underdeveloped country exhibits a higher rate of profit (36⅓ per cent versus 20 per cent) — because its very low constant capital compensates for its low exploitation rate. The argument demonstrates that the international comparison does not run in a single dimension. A high rate of exploitation can coexist with a low rate of profit, and vice versa. This is a serious analytical result, and it should be defended against the naive reading that "developed countries have low profit rates because of the tendency" — the cross-sectional pattern involves two variables moving at once.

The Interest-Rate Fallacy and the Case of the Ryot: An Anticipated Caveat

Marx now inserts an "anticipated" caveat — a foreshadowing of later chapters on interest-bearing capital and ground-rent — in order to block a likely misunderstanding. "Since we have not investigated up till now the various components into which profit is divided, so that these do not exist for us as yet, the following point is anticipated here simply for the sake of avoiding any misunderstandings."

The mistake he is preempting is the substitution of the rate of interest for the rate of profit in comparative national analysis. This is a real error, and it has a determinate structural source. In countries where capitalist production is developed, the rate of interest "simply expresses an aliquot part of the surplus-value or profit produced" — a fraction of the surplus, filtered through the credit system. But in countries where wage-labour is not yet formally subsumed under capital — Marx's example is India, where "the ryot operates as an independent peasant farmer, and his production is not yet subsumed under capital" — the money-lender's interest "includes both the entire profit and more than the profit." It may extort not only the peasant's entire surplus labour but "even — to put it in capitalist terms — a part of his wages." In other words, in such contexts, what appears as "interest" is not the price of loanable capital at all but a relation of usury and rent, whose magnitude is determined by the power of the money-lender over a rentier class, not by the productivity of capital.

Marx supplies the footnote definition here: for the concepts of "formal" and "real subsumption" he refers the reader to "Results of the Immediate Process of Production," published as an appendix to the Pelican edition of Capital Volume 1. Formal subsumption is the relation in which wage-labour is incorporated into a capitalist labour process as it previously existed — the capitalist takes over an existing craft or agrarian technique and simply extracts surplus through extended hours and supervision. Real subsumption is the relation in which production is transformed by capital itself — machinery, large-scale cooperation, factory discipline, scientific technique — so that the labour process becomes specifically capitalist in its material form. This distinction is directly relevant: the fall in the rate of profit presupposes real subsumption, because it presupposes a systematic transformation of technique, not merely a change in the legal form of exploitation. And countries where only formal subsumption obtains are precisely those where the borrowed category "interest" is most misleading.

The further caveat is that in such countries "the rate of interest is predominantly determined by factors such as the level of advances by money-lenders to the big landowners who are the recipients of ground-rent, which have nothing at all to do with profit but rather express the extent to which the money-lender himself appropriates this ground-rent." The rate charged is thus a function of the rentier income stream and the moneylender's ability to intercept it — not of capitalist profitability. To measure the "national rate of profit" by the "national rate of interest" in such a context is therefore doubly erroneous: the category is wrong, and the object being measured is not profit at all.

Intensity, Length of the Working Day, and the Limits of Cross-Country Comparison

Marx continues the international analysis by introducing a second-order complication that cuts in the opposite direction from the underdevelopment case. Between countries of differing organic composition, the rate of surplus-value — one of the two determinants of the profit rate — may be higher in the country with the shorter normal working day. He gives two reasons.

First, intensity. If the English working day of 10 hours equals an Austrian working day of 14 hours "on account of its higher intensity," then, given the same proportional division between necessary and surplus labour, five hours of surplus labour in England may represent "a higher value on the world market" than seven hours in Austria. The point is that the hours of the working day are not homogeneous units of social labour: an hour of labour of higher intensity produces more value in the same clock time, because intensity is itself a mode of increasing the quantity of labour embodied per unit time (the point developed at length in Volume 1, Chapter 10, on the working day, and Chapter 12 on relative surplus-value). Marx's formulation anticipates the modern concept of differential productivity and the labour-theoretic treatment of intensive magnitude.

Second, the division of the day. "A greater part of the working day in England may form surplus labour than in Austria" — that is, the same length of day can be divided differently, depending on the value of labour-power (wages), which in turn depends on the productivity of the branches producing subsistence goods. A more productive economy can cheapen the means of subsistence, thereby shortening necessary labour time and extending surplus labour time, even with a shorter working day.

The analytic structure here is subtle. The rate of profit depends on two fundamental magnitudes: the organic composition and the rate of surplus-value. Advanced countries tend to have higher composition (which depresses the profit rate) and higher rates of surplus-value via intensity and cheapened wage-goods (which raises it). The net cross-sectional outcome is therefore underdetermined, and depends on the relative strengths of the two forces. Marx is here laying the groundwork for the theory of unequal development and for the subsequent treatment of foreign trade in Chapter 14, where trade is identified as a counteracting influence that can raise the profit rate in advanced countries at the expense of less advanced ones.

The Compact Restatement and the 80c + 20v / 20c + 80v Contrast

Marx now restates the law in its most intuitive, "physical" form: "taking any particular quantity of average social capital, e.g. a capital of 100, an ever greater portion of this is represented by means of labour and an ever lesser portion by living labour." Note the phrase "represented by" — the composition is a value composition, but it is a representation of the physical ratio of means of production to labourers.

Because the mass of living labour added falls relative to the value of the means of production, so does the unpaid labour, and the portion of value in which it is represented, relative to the total capital advanced. Marx offers an alternative formulation: "an ever smaller aliquot part of the total capital laid out is converted into living labour, and hence the total capital absorbs ever less surplus labour in relation to its size, even though the ratio between the unpaid and paid parts of the labour applied may at the same time be growing."

The concession in the last clause is crucial. Even as the capital absorbs less surplus labour relative to its size, the proportion of unpaid to paid labour within the laboured mass may be growing. Both movements are real. Their interaction is the "double-edged" nature of the law, developed in the following pages.

Two concrete illustrations follow. First, developed capital of 100 = 80c + 20v, where v represents 20 workers. Rate of surplus-value 100 per cent, workers work half the day for themselves. Second, less-developed capital of 100 = 20c + 80v, where v represents 80 workers. These 80 workers, however, need two-thirds of the working day for themselves, working only one-third for the capitalist — a rate of surplus-value of 50 per cent. Taking everything else equal, "the workers in the first case produce a value of 40, in the second case a value of 120." The value added by the labour in the second case is £120 because 80 workers are employed (at £80 necessary labour value, plus £40 surplus). In the first case, only 20 workers are employed, producing £20 necessary plus £20 surplus.

Hence: - First capital: 80c + 20v + 20s = 120, rate of profit 20 per cent; - Second capital: 20c + 80v + 40s = 140, rate of profit 40 per cent.

The rate of profit is twice as large in the second case — even though the rate of surplus-value there is half as large (50 per cent versus 100 per cent). Why? "Because a capital of the same size appropriates in the first case the surplus labour of only 20 workers, as against that of 80 workers in the second case." The determining variable, given a fixed capital of 100, is how many workers it can buy; and since the value composition is inverse to the physical composition, the low-composition capital commands four times the labour.

This is a devastating illustration for naive readings of the law. It shows that a country or sector with a lower rate of exploitation may have a higher profit rate, and that the profit rate is not the same as the exploitation rate. It also clarifies what kind of variable determines the profit rate: the mass of living labour employed per unit of capital, which is inversely related to the composition.

Relative, Not Absolute: Why the Decline Does Not Imply Fewer Workers

Marx is careful to block a common misreading. The law "of a progressive fall in the rate of profit, or the relative decline in the surplus labour appropriated in comparison with the mass of objectified labour that the living labour sets in motion, in no way prevents the absolute mass of labour set in motion and exploited by the social capital from growing." The fall is relative, not absolute. Even if the number of workers exploited never increases, the mass of surplus-value could stay the same — and since capital continues to accumulate, the number will normally increase.

Consider a fixed working population — say 2 million — with the length and intensity of the working day given, and wages given. Then the total labour of these 2 million workers always produces the same magnitude of value, and the same surplus-value. But "as the mass of constant (fixed and circulating) capital set in motion by this labour grows, so there is a fall in the ratio between this magnitude and the value of the constant capital." The ratio falls, and with it the profit rate, "even though capital still commands the same mass of living labour as before and absorbs the same mass of surplus labour."

The explanatory point is precise and should be underlined: "If the ratio changes, this is not because the mass of living labour falls but rather because the mass of already objectified labour that it sets in motion rises." This distinction separates Marx's law from any version of the argument that says "capitalists exploit fewer workers, hence profit falls." The workers may be exactly as numerous as before — indeed, they may be increasing. The rate falls because the denominator grows. This is a purely structural movement.

Two Million Workers, Three Million Workers: The Doubly-Moving Magnitudes

Marx now admits both movements simultaneously. If the working population rises from 2 to 3 million, and variable capital rises correspondingly from 2 to 3 million, while constant capital rises from 4 to 15 million, then — with working day and rate of surplus-value constant — the mass of surplus labour and surplus-value still rises by 50 per cent, from 2 to 3 million.

The two cases: - I. 4c + 2v + 2s; C = 6, p′ = 2/6 = 33⅓ per cent; - II. 15c + 3v + 3s; C = 18, p′ = 3/18 = 16⅔ per cent.

The mass of surplus-value has risen by half, while the profit rate has fallen by half (33⅓ → 16⅔). This is the chapter's first fully explicit statement of the "double-edged law." Marx then draws out the crucial identification: "profit is nothing more than the surplus-value reckoned in terms of the social capital, and the mass of profit, therefore, its absolute magnitude, is the same as the absolute magnitude of surplus-value, considering it on a social scale." Thematic identity: mass of profit = mass of surplus-value at the level of social capital. The absolute mass of profit grows by 50 per cent despite "the enormous decline in the ratio between this mass of profit and the total capital advanced."

The conclusion is sweeping and is stated with the hallmark of a law: "The number of workers employed by capital, i.e. the absolute mass of labour it sets in motion, and hence the absolute mass of surplus labour it absorbs, the mass of surplus-value it produces, and the absolute mass of profit it produces, can therefore grow, and progressively so, despite the progressive fall in the rate of profit. This not only can but must be the case — discounting transient fluctuations — on the basis of capitalist production."

The modal shift from "can" to "must" is the chapter's central philosophical moment. The coexistence of a falling rate with a rising mass is not an accidental accommodation, nor an apologetic concession to optimism. It is structurally necessary, given accumulation.

Accumulation, the Surplus Population, and "Misery Produces Population"

Marx now argues from the nature of the capitalist production process as such. "The capitalist production process is essentially, and at the same time, a process of accumulation." Several linked claims follow.

First, as productivity rises, the mass of value that must simply be reproduced and maintained grows — "even if the labour-power applied remains constant." The reproduction requirement itself expands with the scale of the means of production, because a larger and more technically elaborate stock of capital must be maintained in working order.

Second, the use-value dimension: as productivity develops, "the mass of use-values produced grows still more, and the means of production form a portion of these." Crucially, the additional labour required to convert this additional wealth into capital "does not depend on the value of these means of production (including means of subsistence), since the worker is not concerned in the labour process with the value of the means of production but rather with their use-value." This is a technically important point. The transformation of produced wealth back into capital — into new means of production and new means of subsistence for workers — is a material operation. It requires labour that is applied to materials and machines as use-values, not labour measured against their values. This means that a growth in the physical scale of accumulation does not require a proportionately larger value of labour-power; the labour-power's task is to work materials, not to "embody" a value quantity.

Third, accumulation and concentration are "simply a material means for increasing productivity." Larger-scale production permits more advanced technique, more machinery, more scientific application.

Fourth — and this is the transition into the theory of surplus population — the growth of the means of production "entails a growth in the working population, the creation of a surplus population that corresponds to the surplus capital or even exceeds its overall requirements, thus leading to an over-population of workers." Note: the surplus population is entailed by the growth of capital. It is not a natural excess. It is the specifically capitalist form of over-population.

Marx then describes the "double effect" of a momentary excess of surplus capital over the working population it commands. On the one hand, "it will gradually increase the working population by raising wages, hence attenuating the destructive influences that decimate the offspring of the workers and making marriage easier." This is a fascinating passage — it is Marx's version of what a modern reader would call the demographic response to rising living standards: higher wages reduce infant and child mortality and facilitate family formation, so the labour supply grows through natural increase. On the other hand, "by using methods that create relative surplus-value (introduction and improvement of machinery), it produces far more quickly an artificial and relative over-population, which in turn is the forcing house for a really rapid increase in the number of people — since, under capitalist production, misery produces population."

The last phrase is a pregnant one. As a positive law of population under capitalism, Marx is asserting the opposite of Malthusian naturalism: population growth is produced by the specific conditions of capitalist accumulation — including the poverty and insecurity of the workforce — and not by some natural law of reproduction. And this is why the system can continue to have an abundant labour supply even as the relative demand for labour falls.

The conclusion is explicit: "It thus follows from the very nature of the capitalist accumulation process... that the increased mass of means of production designed to be turned into capital finds a correspondingly increased and even excessive working population available for exploitation." As accumulation advances, "the mass of surplus labour that can be and is appropriated must grow, and with it too the absolute mass of profit appropriated by the social capital." But — and this is the crucial dialectical hinge — "the same laws of production and accumulation mean that the value of the constant capital increases along with its mass, and progressively more quickly than that of the variable portion of capital which is converted into living labour." So the same laws produce both the growing absolute mass of profit and the falling rate of profit. They are not two results of two causes; they are two results of one cause.

Concentration, Expropriation, and the Growing Armies of Workers

Marx then notes, parenthetically, that he is abstracting from the fact that "the same amount of value represents a progressively rising mass of use-values and satisfactions" — i.e., that the value-based analysis understates the growth in the material wealth produced. This matters because it shows that he is being conservative, not inflationary, with respect to the growth of social wealth.

He next turns to the scale conditions of capitalist development. Increasingly large labour processes require "increasingly large dimensions and increasingly large advances of capital for each individual establishment." The result is the growing concentration of capitals — accompanied, note carefully, by "a growing number of capitalists," though "in lesser degree." Marx is here distinguishing concentration (the growth in size of individual capitals) from centralization (the merging of capitals, treated elsewhere, notably in Volume 1, Chapter 25, §2 and in Chapter 15 of this volume). Both are at work: capitals grow in size, and their number also grows, though slower. The growth in the number of capitalists is compatible with — indeed, initially a consequence of — accumulation's sweep.

Hand in hand with concentration, "in a relationship of reciprocity, goes progressive expropriation of the more or less immediate producers." The reference is to the long history of primitive accumulation — enclosures, dispossession of peasants, the separation of the producer from the means of production. That process is not merely a historical precondition but an ongoing feature of capitalist development.

The consequence: individual capitalists command "increasingly large armies of workers (no matter how much the variable capital may fall in relation to the constant capital)," so that the mass of surplus-value and hence profit which they appropriate grows, "along with and despite the fall in the rate of profit." Marx gives a crucial gloss: "The reasons that concentrate massive armies of workers under the command of individual capitalists are precisely the same reasons as also swell the amount of fixed capital employed, as well as the raw and ancillary materials, in a growing proportion as compared with the mass of living labour applied." The very processes that allow individual capitalists to employ more labour in absolute terms also ensure that the proportion of living labour to dead labour falls. The law and its counter-tendencies spring from a single root.

The Rising Rate of Surplus-Value as a Reinforcing Case

Marx's next move is to note a reinforcing case. With a given working population, if the rate of surplus-value grows — whether by prolongation or intensification of the working day, or by reductions in the value of wages as a result of developing productivity — "then the mass of surplus-value and hence the absolute mass of profit must also grow, despite the relative lessening of variable capital in relation to constant." In other words, the rise in exploitation makes the coexistence of a falling rate and a rising mass even more robust. But this does not overthrow the law; it merely raises the numerator while c raises the denominator. Nor does it "offset" the law in any systematic way: even if the exploitation rate rises, so long as it does not rise faster than the composition, the rate of profit necessarily falls.

The "Double-Edged Law" Posed as a Problem

Marx now consolidates the entire argument so far into a formal challenge. The results are:

  1. The same development of the productivity of social labour is expressed in the relative decline of variable capital as a proportion of the total capital.
  2. The accelerated accumulation that follows from this reacts back to become the starting point for a further development of productivity and a further relative decline in the variable capital.
  3. This same development expresses itself, "leaving aside temporary fluctuations," in the progressive increase in the total labour-power applied, and in the progressive growth in the absolute mass of surplus-value and therefore profit.

The question Marx then asks is rhetorical and architectonic: "How, then, should we present this double-edged law of a decline in the profit rate coupled with a simultaneous increase in the absolute mass of profit, arising from the same reasons?" And he specifies the constraint: this law is based on the fact that, under the given conditions, the mass of surplus labour and hence surplus-value that is appropriated grows, and that, viewing the total capital as a whole, or the individual capital as simply a piece of the total capital, profit and surplus-value are identical quantities.

The formal problem is this: if you measure the profit rate against a fixed capital of 100, the mass of profit on that 100 also falls, in absolute terms. So within the frame of a fixed-magnitude benchmark, the fall in the rate is a fall in the mass. How can there simultaneously be a rise in the mass of profit "on a social scale"? The apparent contradiction must be resolved by seeing that the benchmark and the whole move differently.

The Aliquot-Part Argument: Relative Fall as Absolute Fall — and the Tautology

Marx takes an aliquot part of the capital — say 100 — as the basis for reckoning the profit rate. This 100 represents the average composition of the total capital, e.g., 80c + 20v. Recall from Part Two that the average rate of profit is determined not by any one particular composition of capital but by its average social composition. The 100 is thus the "representative capital"; the profit rate on it is the general rate.

Now the crucial analytical move. As the variable portion declines relative to the constant, "the profit rate falls if the level of exploitation of labour remains constant, or even if it rises; hence the relative magnitude of surplus-value falls, i.e. its relationship to the value of the total capital of 100 that is advanced." So far, the argument is relative. But Marx immediately sharpens it: "But it is not only this relative magnitude that falls. The amount of surplus-value or profit absorbed by the total capital of 100 also falls in absolute terms."

He gives three cases, at a rate of surplus-value of 100 per cent: - 60c + 40v produces a mass of surplus-value and profit of 40 (p′ = 40%); - 70c + 30v produces a mass of profit of 30 (p′ = 30%); - 80c + 20v produces a mass of profit of 20 (p′ = 20%).

A reader must dwell on this. On a capital of 100, if the composition is 60:40, the profit is 40; if 70:30, the profit is 30; if 80:20, the profit is 20. The mass of profit itself falls — not just the ratio. Why? "Whatever aliquot part of the social capital we take as the standard for measuring surplus-value, i.e. whatever part of the capital of average social composition — and this is the case with any calculation of profit — a relative fall in surplus-value is always identical with an absolute fall."

Marx then states the tautological structure explicitly. "Since the size of the capital against which we measure the surplus-value is given as 100, a fall in the ratio of surplus-value to this magnitude, which itself remains constant, can only be another expression for the decline in the absolute magnitude of surplus-value and profit. This is in fact a tautology." The point: because the benchmarking capital is fixed at 100, any fall in the rate is by construction a fall in the mass on that benchmark. There is nothing paradoxical about it — it is definitional. "But the reason for this decline, as has been shown, lies in the nature of development of the capitalist process of production." The tautology is about the form of measurement; the substantive content is the falling mass of living labour absorbed per unit of capital.

The Resolution: The Total Capital Must Grow Faster Than the Rate Falls

Now the actual resolution of the apparent paradox. "On the other hand, however, the same reasons that produce an absolute decline in surplus-value and hence profit on a given capital, thus also in the rate of profit as reckoned as a percentage, bring about a growth in the absolute mass of the surplus-value and profit appropriated by the social capital (i.e. by the totality of capitalists). How are we to explain this, what is it dependent on, or what conditions are involved in this apparent contradiction?"

The answer turns on the variability of the total capital. "If any aliquot part of the social capital, say 100, and hence any capital of 100 of average social composition, is a given magnitude, so that as far as it is concerned the decline in the rate of profit coincides with a decline in the absolute amount of profit — precisely because the capital on which this is measured is a constant magnitude, then the magnitude of the total social capital, on the other hand, just like that of the capital to be found in the hands of any individual capitalist, is a variable magnitude, and it must vary in inverse proportion to the decline in its variable portion if it is to fulfil the conditions we have presupposed."

In other words: the unit of measurement is fixed at 100, but the number of such units into which the social capital is divisible changes over time. The apparent contradiction resolves once we realize that the falling rate and the rising mass are measured against different objects of comparison — one against the fixed unit, the other against the growing aggregate.

The numerical example: at composition 60c + 40v, the profit on 100 is 40, so the rate is 40 per cent. Suppose at this composition the total capital was 1,000,000 — then the total surplus-value and profit is 400,000. Now let composition become 80c + 20v, so that the profit on each 100 is 20, with exploitation unchanged. Nonetheless, "the surplus-value or profit grows in its absolute mass... despite this decline in the rate of profit or the decline in the production of surplus-value by each capital of 100, and this growth might be from 400,000 to 440,000."

What does this require? The new total capital must be such that 20 per cent of it equals 440,000. That is 2,200,000. The text prints "2,220,000," but the surrounding glosses — that this represents a rise "to 220 per cent of its initial value," and that the new composition yields 20 per cent on each 100 — imply that the correct figure is 2,200,000. The discrepancy appears to be a transposition in the printed text (or a slip in Marx's manuscript); the internal logic points unambiguously to 2,200,000. Either way, the thrust is unchanged: the capital has grown by 120 per cent, while the rate of profit has fallen by 50 per cent.

Marx then contrasts this with the case where the composition did not rise: "Had it grown by less than this, it would have produced less surplus-value or profit than the capital of 1,000,000 did previously, although at its earlier composition this would only have had to grow from 1,000,000 to 1,100,000 in order for its surplus-value to rise from 400,000 to 440,000." Check: at 40 per cent, 1,100,000 × 0.40 = 440,000. So at the old composition, a mere 10 per cent increase in capital would have raised profit by 10 per cent; at the new composition, achieving the same 440,000 requires a 120 per cent increase. The rising composition multiplies the capital required for any given absolute profit.

The result: "Here we can see asserting itself the law we developed earlier, according to which the relative decline in the variable capital, and thus the development of the social productivity of labour, means that an ever greater amount of total capital is required in order to set the same quantity of labour-power in motion and to absorb the same amount of surplus labour."

This "law of capital requirement" is the crucial corollary of the falling rate of profit, and it produces a significant structural consequence: "In the same proportion as capitalist production develops, therefore, there also develops the possibility of a relative surplus working population, not because the productivity of social labour declines but rather because it increases, i.e. not from an absolute disproportion between labour and means of subsistence, or the means of producing these means of subsistence, but rather from a disproportion arising from the capitalist exploitation of labour, the disproportion between the progressive growth of capital and the relative decline in its need for a growing population."

The surplus population is generated by rising productivity, not by scarcity. Its source is the disproportion between the growth of capital and the diminishing relative need for labour that capital's own development creates.

The Arithmetic of Compensation: From 40 Per Cent to 20 and to 8

Marx then formalizes the general rule. "A fall of 50 per cent in the rate of profit is a fall of a half." If the mass of profit is to remain the same, the capital must double. "In general, if the mass of profit is to remain the same with a declining rate of profit, the multiplier that indicates the growth in the total capital must be the same as the divisor that indicates the fall in the profit rate." If the rate falls from 40 per cent to 20 per cent, the total capital must rise in the ratio 20:40 (i.e., doubled) for the profit to stay constant. If the profit rate fell from 40 per cent to 8 per cent, the capital would have to grow in the ratio 8:40, i.e., five times. A capital of 1,000,000 at 40 per cent produces 400,000; a capital of 5,000,000 at 8 per cent also produces 400,000. "This is necessary if the resultant is to remain the same."

But the rule is only the condition for keeping profit constant. "If it is to grow, on the other hand, the capital must grow in a higher ratio than that in which the profit rate falls." This is the pivotal implication for the theory of accumulation. To have an absolutely larger mass of profit while the rate falls, capital must grow more rapidly than the rate falls — i.e., the percentage growth in total capital must exceed the percentage fall in the rate.

And still further: "In other words, if the variable component of the total capital is not just to remain the same in absolute terms, but rather to grow, even though its percentage falls as a proportion of the total capital, then the total capital must grow in a higher ratio than that at which the percentage of variable capital falls. It must grow so much that in its new composition it requires not only the former amount of variable capital, but still more than this, for the purchase of labour-power." So to employ an absolutely larger mass of workers while the v/C ratio falls, the total capital must grow even faster. "If the variable part of a capital of 100 falls from 40 to 20, the total capital must rise to more than 200 if it is to deploy a variable capital of more than 40." Check: at composition 20v/100, to have v = 40 the total capital must be 200; to exceed 40, the total must exceed 200.

Even if the exploited mass of working population remains constant and only the length and intensity of the working day increase, "the mass of capital applied must still rise," because the rising composition means more capital is required to employ the same labour-power, whatever the level of exploitation.

The Law of Capital Requirement, and the "Permanent Apparent Surplus Population"

Marx consolidates the argument in one of the chapter's most consequential formulations: "Thus the same development in the social productivity of labour is expressed, with the advance of the capitalist mode of production, on the one hand in a progressive tendency for the rate of profit to fall and on the other in a constant growth in the absolute mass of the surplus-value or profit appropriated; so that, by and large, the relative decline in the variable capital and profit goes together with an absolute increase in both. This two-fold effect, as explained, can be expressed only in a growth in the total capital that takes place more rapidly than the fall in the rate of profit."

Note: "a growth in the total capital that takes place more rapidly than the fall in the rate of profit." This is a necessary condition for the two movements to coexist. If the total capital grew more slowly than the rate fell, the mass of profit would shrink — which would mean a full-blown crisis of accumulation. The chapter thus implicitly identifies the precarious condition for stable accumulation: capital must grow faster than its own rate of return declines.

Marx then translates this into a general rule about the scale of capital required: "In order to apply an absolutely greater variable capital at a higher composition, or with a relatively steeper increase in the constant capital, the total capital must grow not only in the same proportion as this higher composition, but still faster than it." He concludes: "It follows from this that the more the capitalist mode of production is developed, the more an ever greater amount of capital is needed to employ the same amount of labour-power (and this is still more the case if the amount of labour-power is growing). The rising productivity of labour thus necessarily gives rise, on the capitalist basis, to a permanent apparent surplus working population."

This phrase — "permanent apparent surplus working population" — fuses the theory of accumulation with the theory of the industrial reserve army. The reserve army is not a temporary market phenomenon; it is a structural property of the mode of production, produced by rising productivity, and it grows, so to speak, with the system. The word "apparent" (scheinbare) does not qualify away the surplus population; it signals that the surplus is a capitalist form — the population is not surplus "in itself" (there is always work to be done in the sense of potential needs to be met) but is surplus relative to capital's capacity to profitably employ it.

The final concrete illustration: "If the variable capital forms only a sixth of the total capital instead of a half, as formerly, then in order to employ the same amount of labour-power, the total capital must be tripled; but if it is to employ double the labour-power, this capital must be increased six-fold." Check: at v/C = 1/2, C = 2v. At v/C = 1/6, C = 6v. To deploy the same v, total capital must go from 2v to 6v — tripled. To deploy 2v, C = 12v, requiring a six-fold increase of the original 2v.

Ricardo's Consolation and the "Deliberate Miscalculation"

Marx here turns to the critics. "Previous economists, not knowing how to explain the law of the falling rate of profit, invoked the rising mass of profit, the growth in its absolute amount, whether for the individual capitalist or for the social capital as a whole, as a kind of consolation, but this was also based on mere commonplaces and imagined possibilities."

The accusation is precise: the classical economists' answer to the falling rate is to point to the rising mass — "consolation." Marx's reply is that this is a tautology. "It is no more than a tautology to say that the mass of profit is determined by two factors, firstly by the rate of profit and secondly by the mass of capital applied at this rate." The point is that the classical formulation adds nothing to the phenomenon; it merely restates the definition of the mass of profit. "The fact that the mass of profit may possibly grow, therefore, despite a simultaneous fall in the rate of profit, is only an expression of this tautology and does not get us a single step further, since it is equally possible for the capital to grow without the mass of profit growing, and, indeed, the capital might even grow while the mass of profit falls. 25 per cent on 100 gives 25, 5 per cent on 400 gives only 20."

This is the crucial quantitative correction. The consolation argument treats the mass of profit as automatically rising when capital grows. But this is not so: if the rate falls faster than the capital grows, the mass falls. Marx's target here is the classical assumption that accumulation always raises the absolute profit. His own position is the more analytic one: accumulation raises the mass only under the condition stated (capital growing faster than the rate falls).

Footnote 35 provides the Ricardian illustration. Ricardo writes: "We should also expect that, however the rate of the profits of stock might diminish in consequence of the accumulation of capital on the land and the rise of wages, yet the aggregate amount of profits would increase." He gives a numerical example: with repeated accumulations of £100,000, and the rate of profit falling 20, 19, 18, 17 per cent, the aggregate would be "always progressive" — greater at £200,000 than at £100,000; still greater at £300,000, and so on, "increasing, though at a diminishing rate, with every increase of capital." But Ricardo then concedes: "This progression, however, is only true for a certain time... but after capital has accumulated to a large amount, and profits have fallen, the further accumulation diminishes the aggregate of profits." His example: at £1,000,000 and 7 per cent, aggregate profit is £70,000; add £100,000 and the rate falls to 6 per cent, giving £66,000 — a decline of £4,000, even though the stock has risen to £1,100,000.

Marx's editorial comment in the footnote is pitiless: "In point of fact, what is assumed here is that the capital grows from 1,000,000 to 1,100,000, i.e. by 10 per cent, while the rate of profit falls from 7 per cent to 6 per cent, i.e. by 14⅔ per cent. Hinc illae lacrimae!" — "Hence those tears!" (Terence, The Maid of Andros, Act 1, Scene 1). The comment identifies the exact condition: capital grew by 10 per cent, but the profit rate fell by ~14.3 per cent — faster than capital grew — so the mass fell. The "tears" are the lament of capital over its own self-undermining dynamic. Marx's own analysis, in contrast, has explained the condition: the mass of profit grows only if the capital grows faster than the rate falls — a condition that the development of the organic composition makes progressively harder to satisfy.

Marx then adds: "But if the same reasons that make the profit rate fall also promote accumulation, i.e. the formation of additional capital, and if all additional capital also sets additional labour in motion and produces additional surplus-value; if on the other hand the very fact of the fall in the rate of profit means that the constant capital and with it the total amount of the former capital has grown, then the entire process ceases to be a mystery." The apparent paradox is fully resolved by the identity of cause and effect: the falling rate and the growing mass are both consequences of rising productivity operating through accumulation. And he adds the ominous forecast: "We shall see later on how resort was made to deliberate miscalculation, in an attempt to swindle away the possibility of an increase in the mass of profit together with a decline in the profit rate." A footnote refers to Theories of Surplus-Value, Part II, pp. 438–66 and 542–6. This is a preview of Marx's exposure of apologetic manipulations in the history of economic thought.

The Law Inverted in Competition: Cheapening, Undercutting, and the Big Capitalist

Marx now shifts register entirely, from theory to the phenomenology of competition. "We have seen how it is that the same reasons that produce a tendential fall in the general rate of profit also bring about an accelerated accumulation of capital and hence a growth in the absolute magnitude or total mass of the surplus labour (surplus-value, profit) appropriated by it. Just as everything is expressed upside down in competition, and hence in the consciousness of its agents, so too is this law — I mean this inner and necessary connection between two apparently contradictory phenomena."

The concept of inverted appearance in competition is central to Marx's critique of political economy and to his theory of ideology. In the actual competitive struggle, the structural connection between the falling rate and the rising mass does not present itself as such. Instead, agents perceive the fall in the profit rate as the result of their own strategic choices — price cuts, expansion, efficiency measures. Marx's project is to show that these strategic choices are themselves the form of appearance of the underlying law.

"It is evident that, on the figures given above, a capitalist controlling a large capital will make more profit in absolute terms than a smaller capitalist making apparently high profits." This is where the law becomes strategically visible: bigger capital commands more surplus in absolute terms despite a lower rate. "The most superficial examination of competition also shows that, under certain conditions, if the bigger capitalist wants to make more room for himself on the market and expel the smaller capitalists, as in times of crisis, he makes practical use of this advantage and deliberately lowers his profit rate in order to drive the smaller ones from the field." This is a devastating empirical grounding: the falling rate, which appears as a curse, becomes in the hands of large capitals a weapon of competition.

Commercial capital "in particular, which we shall discuss in more detail later, also exhibits phenomena that allow the fall in profit to be seen as a result of the expansion of business and hence of the capital concerned." Marx reserves the fuller analysis for a later portion of Volume 3 (the chapters on merchant's capital, interest-bearing capital, and the division of profit on the basis of average profit). Similar superficial considerations arise "from comparing the rates of profit that are made in particular branches of business, according to whether these are subject to the regime of free competition or to monopoly."

Roscher, the "More Clever and More Humane," and Profit Upon Alienation

Marx then brings in his favourite exemplar of vulgar economy. "The entire shallow conception that thrives in the heads of the agents of competition can be found in our Roscher, namely his assertion that this reduction in the rate of profit is 'more clever and more humane'." The footnote identifies Wilhelm Roscher (1817–94), a German vulgar economist and founder of the "historical school," and cites his Die Grundlagen der Nationalökonomie (3rd edn, 1858, p. 192).

The phrase "more clever and more humane" is a parody of the apologetic mode. For Roscher, the fall in the profit rate is a matter of capitalist calculation and benevolence: it is "more clever" to earn a lower rate on a larger mass, and "more humane" because — on this view — it is a concession to workers or consumers. Marx's sarcasm is aimed at the whole approach that treats a systemic tendency as an intentional choice. "Here the decline in the rate of profit appears as a result of the increase of capital and the capitalists' consequent calculation that a lower rate of profit will enable them to tuck away a greater mass of profit."

The error, for Marx, is conceptual: "All this (with the exception of Adam Smith, on whom more later) is based on a complete misconception of what the general rate of profit actually is and on the crude idea that prices are determined by adding a more or less arbitrary quota of profit onto the commodity's actual value." This passage names the core vulgar conception: profit upon alienation. Prices are conceived as value plus a surcharge. But profit is not a surcharge; it is the form of surplus-value, determined by the relation between living labour and total capital. To treat it as a surcharge is to abolish the distinction between rate and mass and to substitute voluntary pricing for determinate ratios.

Marx concludes the passage: "Crude as these notions are, they are a necessary product of the upside-down way that the immanent laws of capitalist production present themselves within competition." This is a crucial theoretical move. The vulgar conception is not simply an intellectual failure; it is the necessary reflection in consciousness of the inverted way the laws actually appear. In this sense, Marx is offering a theory of the genesis of ideological error, not merely a refutation of it.

The Commodity-Price Restatement and the Anatomy of the Individual Commodity

Marx now restates the law in a new register: "The law that the fall in the rate of profit occasioned by the development of productivity is accompanied by an increase in the mass of profit is also expressed in this way: the fall in the price of commodities produced by capital is accompanied by a relative rise in the amount of profit contained in them and realized by their sale." Note carefully: the price of the individual commodity falls, but the amount of profit contained in it rises relative to the value that goes to wages and materials. This is an aspect of the individual commodity in which the whole law is inscribed.

The mechanism is laid out. As productivity rises and composition rises, "each aliquot part of the total product, each individual commodity or each specific group of commodities absorbs less living labour and also contains less objectified labour, both in terms of the depreciation of the fixed capital applied and in terms of the raw and ancillary materials that are consumed." The commodity contains less living labour (because productivity has multiplied output per worker), and it also contains less objectified labour per unit (because the fixed capital's depreciation is spread over more units, and the raw material consumption per unit is reduced by efficiency gains). "Each individual commodity therefore contains a smaller sum of labour objectified in means of production and labour newly added in the course of production. The price of the individual commodity therefore falls."

Yet the profit on the individual commodity may still increase "if the rate of absolute or relative surplus-value rises. It contains less newly added labour, but the unpaid portion of this labour grows in proportion to the paid part." This is the "relative rise in the amount of profit contained" in the sentence quoted above: the ratio s/v may rise even as the absolute mass of newly added value falls.

But — and Marx immediately limits the concession — "this is only true within certain definite limits. With the enormous decrease, in the course of the advance of production, of the absolute amount of living labour newly added to the individual commodity, the unpaid labour it contains also undergoes an absolute decline, no matter how much it may have grown in relation to the paid portion." In other words, once the absolute amount of newly added labour falls far enough, the proportional rise in the unpaid share cannot prevent the absolute unpaid value in the individual commodity from shrinking. "The profit on each individual commodity becomes very much reduced as labour productivity develops, despite the rise in the rate of surplus-value; and this reduction, just like the fall in the rate of profit, is slowed down only by the cheapening of the elements of constant capital and the other circumstances adduced in Part One of this volume, which increase the rate of profit with a given or even falling rate of surplus-value." The reference to Part One (Chapters 5–7 of Volume 3 — the effect of the economy in the use of constant capital, price fluctuations of raw materials, and related factors) is important: even before the counteracting influences of Chapter 14, Part One has already established mechanisms that raise the profit rate, and these serve to "slow down" the fall.

Marx then interprets the fall in individual commodity prices. "If there is a fall in the price of the individual commodities whose sum makes up capital's total product, this means nothing more than that a given quantity of labour is realized in a greater mass of commodities, so that each individual commodity contains less labour than before. This is the case even if one part of the constant capital, e.g. raw material, rises in price." The last clause matters because it answers a potential objection: even a rise in raw material prices does not negate the general tendency, because the labour embodied per unit has fallen. Indeed, the rise in raw material prices is itself a way in which the composition of capital shifts, with the same result.

The two reasons the rate of profit falls despite a higher rate of surplus-value are now given explicitly:

  1. "because even a greater unpaid portion of the smaller total sum of newly added labour is less than a smaller aliquot unpaid portion of the greater total sum was" — algebraic point: if total newly added labour falls from, say, 100 to 50, then even a 100 per cent unpaid share (50) is less than a 40 per cent unpaid share of 100 (40... hmm, actually if the unpaid share rises from 40 per cent of 100 to 100 per cent of 50, the unpaid portion goes from 40 to 50 — that's an increase. So Marx's example must involve a smaller fall in the rate of surplus-value — i.e., the unpaid portion of the smaller total is less than the unpaid portion of the greater total. The condition is: if the total newly added labour falls by more than the (reciprocal) increase in the rate of surplus-value, the absolute unpaid mass falls. That is, the fall in the mass outweighs the rise in the rate.)
  2. "because the higher composition of capital is expressed, in the case of the individual commodity, in the fact that the whole portion of this commodity's value that represents newly added labour falls in comparison with the portion of value that represents raw materials, ancillary materials, and wear and tear of the fixed capital."

Marx then spells out this second point as the value-form of the composition change: "This change in the proportion between the various components of the individual commodity's price, the decline in the portion of price that represents newly added living labour, and the increase in the portions of price that represent previously objectified labour — this is the form the decline of the variable capital as against the constant takes in the price of the individual commodity. Just as this decline is absolute for a given amount of capital, e.g. 100, so it is also absolute for each individual commodity as an aliquot part of the capital reproduced."

This is one of the most elegant analytic identifications in the chapter: the falling rate of profit, the changing composition of capital, and the changing anatomy of the individual commodity's price are three interchangeable descriptions of the same movement. The fall in the share of newly added value in the individual commodity's price is the falling share of v in C, seen from the side of the commodity.

The Turnover Problem: Engels's Interpolation and the Three Cases

Now Marx (or rather Engels, whose initials "F.E." mark the passage) introduces a complication that threatens the simple identification of the profit rate on the individual commodity with the actual profit rate on the total capital. The passage is in parentheses, marked "F.E." — Friedrich Engels — who edited and prepared Volume 3 from Marx's manuscript.

The point: The rate of profit is calculated on the total capital applied, for a specific period of time, in practice a year. The proportion between surplus-value realized in a year and the total capital, computed as a percentage, is the rate of profit. This is not necessarily identical with the ratio of profit to cost price of commodities, since the latter is based on the turnover period of the capital. The two coincide only if the capital turns over exactly once in the year.

To make this precise: if we calculate the profit on the cost price of commodities, we obtain p/k, where p is the profit realized in the course of the year and k is the sum of the cost prices of the commodities produced and sold in the same period. It is readily apparent that this profit rate p/k can only coincide with the actual profit rate p/C (mass of profit divided by the total capital) when k = C, i.e., when the capital turns over exactly once in the year.

Three cases for an industrial capital illustrate the distinction:

Case I. A capital of £8,000 produces and sells 5,000 items of a certain commodity each year, at 30 shillings per item, so that its annual turnover is £7,500. On each item it makes a profit of 10 shillings, a total of £2,500 per year. Each item contains a capital advance of 20 shillings and a profit of 10 shillings, so that the profit rate on each item is 10/20 = 50 per cent. In the sum of £7,500 turned over, £5,000 is capital advance and £2,500 is profit; the rate of profit on the turnover, p/k, is similarly 50 per cent. Reckoned on the basis of the total capital, however, p/C = 2,500/8,000 = 31¼ per cent. Here the turnover is less than the total capital (the capital has not completed a full cycle), so the profit rate on the cost price is higher than the actual rate.

Case II. The capital increases to £10,000. As a result of increased labour productivity, it produces 10,000 items each year at a cost price of 20 shillings. It sells these with 4 shillings profit on each, i.e., at 24 shillings per item. The price of the annual product is £12,000, of which £10,000 is capital advance and £2,000 is profit. p/k = 4/20 = 20 per cent reckoned per item, or 2,000/10,000 = 20 per cent reckoned on the annual turnover — in both cases 20 per cent. Since the total capital equals the sum of the cost prices (£10,000), the actual profit rate p/C is also 20 per cent. The three coincide. Note: this case also illustrates the law directly — capital has grown from £8,000 to £10,000 (25 per cent), but the profit rate on the individual commodity has fallen from 50 per cent to 20 per cent. Since the capital doubled its output, the mass of profit on the accumulation basis is stable.

Case III. The capital grows to £15,000, and productivity continues to rise, so that it produces annually 30,000 items at a cost price of 13 shillings each, selling these with 2 shillings profit, i.e., at 15 shillings. The annual turnover is 30,000 × 15 shillings = £22,500, of which £19,500 is capital advance and £3,000 is profit. p/k = 3,000/19,500 = 2/13 = 15 5/13 per cent. p/C = 3,000/15,000 = 20 per cent. Here the total capital is less than the sum turned over, so the profit rate on the cost price is less than the actual rate.

The conclusion: "We see, therefore, that only in case II, where the capital value turned over is the same as the total capital, is the rate of profit on each item of the commodity or on the sum turned over the same as the profit rate calculated on the total capital. In case I, where the sum turned over is less than the total capital, the profit rate calculated on the cost price of the commodity is higher; in case III, where the total capital is less than the sum turned over, this profit rate is less than the actual rate of profit, calculated on the total capital. This is a general rule."

Engels adds a practical remark on how the turnover is estimated in commercial practice: "It is assumed that the capital has turned over once as soon as the sum of commodity prices realized reaches the sum of the total capital applied. But the capital can have completed a whole cycle only if the sum of the cost prices of the commodities realized equals the sum of the total capital." This is the "general rule" of turnover calculation. The point of the whole digression, in the context of the chapter, is to underline that the profit rate cannot be read off the individual commodity's price; it must be computed on the total capital advanced and for an appropriate period.

Why the Individual Commodity Can Never Yield the Rate of Profit

Marx pulls the methodological moral: "We see here once again how important it is in capitalist production not to view the individual commodity or the commodity product of some particular period of time in isolation, as a simple commodity; it must rather be viewed as the product of the capital advanced, and in relation to the total capital that produces this commodity."

This is a decisive rejection of what might be called "commodity-level" profit-rate reasoning. The rate of profit is a capital-level magnitude. The price of the individual commodity is produced by the total capital — including the portion that is not consumed and does not reappear in the commodity. "Even though the rate of profit cannot just be calculated by measuring the mass of surplus-value produced and realized against the portion of capital consumed which reappears in the commodity, but one must rather measure it against this portion plus the portion of capital which is admittedly not consumed, but is still applied in production and continues to serve there, the mass of profit can nevertheless only be equal to the mass of profit or surplus-value actually contained in the commodities and destined to be realized by their sale."

The passage distinguishes two magnitudes: (1) the total capital applied (including fixed capital not consumed in the period) — the correct denominator of the profit rate; and (2) the mass of profit, which equals the surplus-value actually contained in the commodities and realized on sale. The point is that the rate and the mass are computed on different bases — the rate on the total capital applied, the mass on the commodities realized. This is precisely why the apparent contradiction (falling rate with rising mass) is a matter of which measure one is using at what scale, not of two incompatible facts.

The Surface Appearance and the Two Illusions of the Capitalist

Marx returns to the productivity of the individual commodity. "If industrial productivity increases, the price of the individual commodity falls. Less labour is contained in it, both paid and unpaid." He gives a vivid example: "The same labour may produce three times the product, for instance, in which case two-thirds less labour is needed for each individual item. Since profit can only be a portion of the labour contained in the individual commodity, the profit on each individual commodity must decrease, and this is true within certain limits even if the rate of surplus-value rises."

The crucial limiting condition again: the profit on the total product does not fall below the original mass of profit "as long as the capital continues to employ the same mass of workers as before at the same level of exploitation. (This can even be the case if fewer workers are employed at a higher level of exploitation.)" The reasoning is elegant: "in the same ratio as the profit on the individual commodity falls, the number of products rises. The mass of profit remains the same, even though it is differently distributed over the sum of commodities." And this redistribution "in no way changes the distribution between worker and capitalist of the quantity of value created by the newly added labour." The proportionality between paid and unpaid labour is unaffected by the fall in commodity price.

Marx then states the key condition for the mass of profit to rise with the same labour: it can only do so "if the unpaid surplus labour grows, or, with the level of exploitation of labour remaining the same, if the number of workers increases. Both of these factors may operate simultaneously."

Then a concession and demarcation: "Viewed abstractly, the rate of profit might remain the same despite a fall in the price of the individual commodity as a result of increased productivity" — for example, if the increase in productivity affected all the ingredients of the commodity uniformly and simultaneously, so their total price fell in the same proportion as productivity increased, while the ratio between the ingredients stayed the same. "The rate of profit could even rise, if a rise in the rate of surplus-value was coupled with a significant reduction in the value of the elements of constant capital, and fixed capital in particular." These are abstract possibilities, but "in practice, however, the rate of profit will fall in the long run, as we have already seen."

The methodological moral: "In no case does the fall in the price of the individual commodity, taken by itself, permit any conclusion as to the rate of profit. It all depends on the size of the total capital involved in its production." Marx illustrates with the yard of material. Suppose the price of one yard of material falls from 3 shillings to 1⅔ shillings. Before the fall, 1⅔ shillings went on constant capital, ⅔ shilling on wages, and ⅔ shilling was profit. After the fall, 1 shilling went on constant capital, ⅙ shilling on wages, and ⅙ shilling was profit. (The figures must sum to the price: 1⅔ + ⅔ + ⅔ = 3; 1 + ⅙ + ⅙ = 1⅓... but the price is 1⅔, so the pairing must be 1 shilling on constant, ⅓ on wages, ⅓ on profit — the printed OCR is confused. What matters is the logic, not the exact figures, which the printed text garbles. Marx's point holds: even given the division of the yard's price into c, v, and s, "we still do not know whether the rate of profit has remained the same or not. This will depend on whether and by how much the total capital advanced has grown and how many yards more it produces in a given time.")

The argument is thus complete: the profit rate is a global ratio, determining the return on the whole capital, and it cannot be inferred from the anatomy of the individual commodity.

The Final Beat: Falling Rates with Rising Prices, and the Adjustment Process

Marx's final paragraphs assemble the results into a unified statement of the "surface appearance" of the law. The phenomenon — falling prices of individual commodities, rising number of commodities, falling profit per unit and falling rate on the total sum, but rising total mass of profit — "simply appears on the surface as a fall in the amount of profit on the individual commodity, a fall in its price, and a growth in the mass of profit on the increased total number of commodities produced by the total social capital or the total capital of the individual capitalist. The matter is then conceived as if the capitalist voluntarily made less profit on the individual commodity, but compensated himself by the greater number of commodities which he now produces. This conception rests on the notion of profit upon alienation, which is derived from the viewpoint of commercial capital." The footnote identifies this notion with Sir James Steuart, whose conception Marx criticizes in Theories of Surplus-Value, Part I, pp. 41–3. (The reference back to Steuart, the eighteenth-century writer on "profit upon alienation," ties the contemporary apologetics — Roscher — to a much older error.)

Marx reminds the reader that the analysis of the individual commodity earlier in Capital (Volume 1, Parts Four and Seven) showed that the growing mass of commodities and the cheapening of the individual commodity do not in themselves affect the proportion of paid and unpaid labour — except insofar as the commodities enter into the determination of the value of labour-power. This is a crucial qualification: the fall in the price of wage-goods does eventually affect wages, and therefore the rate of surplus-value — which is why the composition and the exploitation rate are not independent in the long run. But the direct effect of cheapening commodities on the profit rate is nil, because profit is determined by capital-level ratios.

Then the two "imaginings" of the individual capitalist. "Since everything presents a false appearance in competition, in fact an upside-down one, it is possible for the individual capitalist to imagine: (1) that he reduces his profit on the individual commodity by cutting its price, but makes a bigger profit on account of the greater quantity of commodities that he sells; (2) that he fixes the price of the individual commodity and then determines the price of the total product by multiplication, whereas the original process is one of division (see Volume 1, Chapter 12, pp. 433–4), and this multiplication comes in only at second hand and is correct only on the premise of that division."

The second point is a mathematical methodology observation of broad significance. The value of the total product is divided among its components (c + v + s) — this is the primary operation, determined by the labour embodied and the relation of exploitation. The capitalist then multiplies the price of the individual commodity by the quantity to obtain the aggregate. Multiplication is a derived operation, valid only on the premise of the underlying division. The capitalist's mental arithmetic runs the process backwards and thereby mystifies it: he appears to make the total price by setting the unit price, whereas in fact the total value precedes and determines the unit price.

Marx then delivers the diagnosis: "In point of fact, the vulgar economist does nothing more than translate the peculiar notions of the competition-enslaved capitalist into an ostensibly more theoretical and generalized language, and attempt to demonstrate the validity of these notions." The circle is closed: the categories of vulgar economy are the theoretical expression of the everyday consciousness of the capitalist, which is itself the inverted reflection of the laws of the system.

The penultimate sentence restates the equivalence: "In actual fact, the fall in commodity prices and the rise in the mass of profit on the increased mass of cheapened commodities is simply another expression of the law of the falling profit rate in the context of a simultaneously rising mass of profit."

The final paragraph steps methodologically outside the main line. "An investigation of how far a falling rate of profit can coincide with rising prices would be no more pertinent here than the earlier point elaborated in Volume 1, pp. 433–4, in connection with relative surplus-value." This is a gesture of scope-limitation — Marx will not pursue the question of falling rates with rising prices (which arises in inflation, in raw-material price shocks, in wartime, etc.) because it belongs elsewhere. He then sketches the relevant dynamics: "The capitalist who employs improved but not yet universally used methods of production sells below the market price, but above his individual price of production; his profit rate thus rises, until competition cancels this out; in the course of this period of adjustment, the second requirement is fulfilled, i.e. growth in the capital laid out; and according to the level of this growth, the capitalist will then be in a position to employ a portion of the workers employed earlier, perhaps all of them or even a greater number, under the new conditions, and thus to produce the same amount of profit or even a larger amount."

The passage connects the falling-rate law to the theory of super-profit and differential profit (developed in Volume 3, Chapters 10 and beyond). The early adopter of a superior technique captures a temporary super-profit until the technique diffuses. This is the mechanism by which productivity grows — individual capitalists seeking super-profits drive the general composition up, lowering the general rate over time. The result of the whole process: the capitalist who gains by the innovation ends up, after its diffusion, able to employ the same or a greater number of workers under the new conditions, producing the same or a larger absolute profit. The law of the falling rate and the theory of the rising mass are thus, at the end, identical: they are the two sides of the process by which capital undermines its own rate of return in the very act of expanding its productive power.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter yields a set of fundamental principles that extend far beyond its immediate algebraic content.

First principle: the categorical distinction between rate and mass. The chapter's entire force depends on recognizing that "rate of profit" and "mass of profit" are magnitudes of different logical types. The rate is a ratio — a return per unit of capital advanced; the mass is an absolute quantity — a total sum. Because they are different types, they can and must move in opposite directions under determinate conditions. This is not a paradox but a structural implication of any system in which productivity raises the ratio of dead to living labour. The lesson applies far beyond Marx: any analysis of returns on capital that conflates rate and mass will mis-specify the problem.

Second principle: the law is a law of tendency, not an empirical uniformity. Marx explicitly concedes that "the rate of profit may not fall temporarily for other reasons as well." The law describes a directive tendency internal to the relations of production, which may be suspended, reversed, or masked by counteracting causes. The methodological status of the law is therefore akin to that of a thermodynamic tendency — a directional pressure on a system, not a prediction about every observable outcome. To read the law as an empirical claim that observed profit rates must decline monotonically is to commit a category error that the chapter itself precludes.

Third principle: the law is a form of appearance of the development of the productive forces. The most philosophically significant formulation in the chapter is that the falling rate of profit is "simply the expression, peculiar to the capitalist mode of production, of the progressive development of the social productivity of labour." The same process — rising productivity — would, under a different set of social relations, register as an unqualified increase in wealth and free time. Under capitalist relations, it registers as a falling rate of return on capital. The form is not a mere illusion imposed from outside; it is the necessary way the process appears within a system organized around the self-valorization of value. This is a textbook example of Marx's method: the form of appearance is real, socially efficacious, and historically specific, and criticizing it does not mean denying it.

Fourth principle: the precarious condition of accumulation. The chapter establishes that the mass of profit can grow despite the falling rate only if the total capital grows faster than the rate falls. This is a stringent, non-automatic condition. It means that accumulation has a built-in dynamic fragility: as the composition rises, the capital required to sustain any given absolute profit grows, and the condition that profit grow becomes progressively harder to satisfy. In the limit, the falling rate becomes a barrier to accumulation — the implicit crisis-theoretic content that Chapter 15 will make explicit.

Fifth principle: the genesis of the relative surplus population. The chapter's treatment of the "permanent apparent surplus working population" is a crucial methodological contribution. The reserve army is not produced by a Malthusian mechanism of natural reproduction exceeding subsistence; it is produced by rising productivity itself. "Misery produces population" — poverty is not a natural condition but a structural product of capitalist accumulation. This connects the abstract theory of the falling rate to the concrete historical theory of the labour market and class composition.

Sixth principle: the epistemology of competition. The chapter provides what is arguably Marx's most compact statement of his theory of ideological inversion. In competition, everything appears "upside down." The structural law is perceived in the form of an intentional calculation: the capitalist chooses a lower rate to capture a larger mass; the capitalist "fixes" the unit price and derives the total; the capitalist is "more clever and more humane" (Roscher). These appearances are not simply errors; they are "a necessary product of the upside-down way that the immanent laws of capitalist production present themselves within competition." The critique of political economy is therefore not merely a refutation of false propositions but a genealogy of the categories of bourgeois thought.

Seventh principle: the methodological priority of structural over distributional analysis. By presenting the law before the decomposition of profit into industrial profit, commercial profit, interest, and ground-rent, Marx demonstrates the law's independence from distribution. The profit rate falls, on his account, regardless of how surplus-value is subsequently divided among the appropriating classes. This is a powerful argumentative strategy with broad application: it shows that some structural tendencies operate at a level of abstraction beneath any particular distributional arrangement, and that the critic who tries to explain such tendencies by reference to distribution has confused the derivative with the primary.

The broader implications of the chapter extend in several directions.

For the history of economic thought: the chapter identifies the falling rate of profit as the pivot of classical and post-classical political economy. The Ricardian consolation (which Marx quotes at length and dissects numerically — capital up 10 per cent, rate down 14⅔ per cent, hence mass falls — "Hinc illae lacrimae!") is shown to be a partial, tautological answer that confuses the condition for the mass of profit growing with an automatic tendency. In this, Marx situates his own work as the completion — and critique — of the classical project.

For the theory of crises: the chapter is the indispensable premise for the analysis of over-accumulation and crisis in Chapter 15. If the mass of profit cannot grow unless capital grows faster than the rate falls, then a system in which the rate is persistently declining becomes progressively more prone to crises of over-production, in which capital cannot be valorized at the required scale. The tendency to over-capacity, over-investment, and falling returns — and the drive to compensate through speculation, credit expansion, and the opening of new markets — have their theoretical ground in the relationship articulated here.

For the analysis of imperialism and uneven development: the international passages provide an analytical basis for the theory of unequal exchange and unequal development. Advanced economies have high compositions (which depress their profit rates ceteris paribus) but also higher rates of surplus-value (through intensity and cheapened wage-goods), which may offset the tendency. Less advanced economies have low compositions but lower productivity, which may yield either higher or lower rates depending on the configuration of the two variables. The chapter thus establishes the theoretical vocabulary for subsequent analyses of international exploitation and unequal development.

For the critique of vulgar economy: the analysis of Roscher, the "profit upon alienation" tradition, and the two illusions of the capitalist provides a template for Marx's critique of the economics of his time and — by extension — of the foundational moves of much modern economics: the treatment of profit as a markup, the analysis of the firm as an intentional pricing agent, the substitution of the individual commodity as the unit of analysis for the capital as a whole. Marx's insistence that the total capital is the correct unit of accounting, and that multiplication is a derived operation premised on the prior division of value, remains a live methodological challenge.

For the philosophical understanding of the mode of production: the chapter makes vivid what Marx means by the historical specificity of capitalist production. Productivity growth is not a neutral metric of human progress; under capitalist relations it takes the form of a falling rate of return on capital, a permanent surplus population, an intensifying struggle over distribution, and periodic crises. The same material development would, under differently organized relations, appear in an entirely different form. The falling rate of profit is, in this sense, the sign under which capitalism's own productive achievements become the ground of its internal contradictions.

Read in its proper context — between the completed theory of the average rate of profit in Part Two and the counteracting causes of Chapter 14 and the internal contradictions of Chapter 15 — Chapter 13 is a chapter of pure theoretical construction that deliberately brackets the qualifications it will later require. Its function is to establish the law in its purity, to demonstrate its derivation from the most fundamental categories of the capitalist mode of production, and to set the framework within which the subsequent analysis of counteracting causes, crises, and historical transformation will be conducted. It is, in the most literal sense, the statement of the law itself — the pivot on which the rest of Volume 3 turns, and the theoretical discovery on which Marx stakes his claim to having solved the central puzzle of political economy since Adam Smith.
