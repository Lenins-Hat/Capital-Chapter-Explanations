Volume 3 • Chapter 46: Building Site Rent. Rent in Mining. Price of Land

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 46 of Capital, Volume 3, closes the long investigation of ground-rent by extending its logic beyond the agricultural field where Marx had developed it, and by dissecting the peculiar form in which landed property appears in the market: the price of land. The chapter's governing thesis is stated in its very first sentence: "Wherever rent exists, differential rent always appears and always follows the same laws as it does in agriculture." The analytical objective is therefore one of generalization — to demonstrate that the mechanism in which a monopolizable natural force (a waterfall, a rich mine, a fishing ground, a well-situated building site) generates a surplus profit for the capitalist who exploits it, and which is then seized by the owner of that natural object "in the form of rent," is a single law operating across all spheres of production.

Three subsidiary objectives organize the chapter. First, Marx characterizes the specific features of non-agricultural rent — above all the preponderance of location in building-site differential rent, the complete passivity of the landowner who merely "exploits advances in social development," and the prevalence of monopoly price and the "most shameless exploitation of poverty." Second, he establishes the critical distinction between house-rent (interest plus amortization on the capital invested in a building) and land-rent proper, and shows through the testimony of the London speculative builder Edward Capps that the real object of speculation is not the houses but the ground-rent. Third, and most fundamentally, he exposes the ideological illusion of the price of land: because rent is capitalized and sold as a commodity, the buyer's claim to rent appears as the return on his equivalent rather than as a tribute on unpaid labour — a mystification Marx compares directly to the slaveowner's belief that his property in a slave is produced by purchase rather than by the institution of slavery.

The chapter thus functions within the broader theoretical framework as the moment where rent's irrational form of appearance (the ratio of a money sum to a piece of land) is systematically decomposed into its rational determinants, and where the historical and moral verdict on landed property — that private property in the earth will appear "just as absurd as the private property of one man in other men" — is delivered.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Generalization of Differential Rent Beyond Agriculture

Marx opens by asserting an identity rather than an analogy. Wherever a natural force can be monopolized and confers on the industrialist who uses it a surplus profit — the excess of the individual capitalist's profit over the general rate of profit — the person who legally owns that natural object, "by virtue of his title to a portion of the earth," diverts this surplus profit from the functioning capital as rent. The examples enumerated (waterfall, rich mine, fishing ground, well-situated building site) are not decorative; they establish that rent is not a peculiarity of farming but a juridical-economic mechanism attaching to any natural monopoly. The essential causal chain is: monopoly of a natural force → superior productivity relative to competitors → surplus profit → appropriation by the title-holder → rent.

Turning to building land specifically, Marx invokes Adam Smith's treatment (Book I, Chapter XI, sections 2 and 3), according to which the rent of building land is governed by agricultural rent proper as its basis. Yet Marx immediately emphasizes three differentiating characteristics. The first is the preponderant influence of location on differential rent — "very important, for example, in the case of vineyards, and building land in big towns." This is a nuance that makes building-site differential rent far more volatile and socially determined than the fertility-based differential rent of farmland: two identical plots a mile apart in a growing city command radically different rents purely because of their position relative to markets, transport, and population.

The second characteristic is the palpable and complete passivity of the owner, whose entire activity "consists simply in exploiting advances in social development (particularly in the case of mines), towards which he does not contribute and in which he risks nothing, unlike the industrial capitalist." This is a pointed contrast: the industrial capitalist bears risk, organizes production, and advances capital; the landowner merely holds title and waits for society's collective development to raise his rent. The third characteristic is the prevalence of monopoly price "in many cases, and particularly the most shameless exploitation of poverty," crystallized in Marx's famous aside that "poverty is a more fruitful source for house-rent than the mines of Potosi were for Spain." Here the exploitation of the poor for housing — the fact that the necessity of shelter allows landlords to extract rents far above any cost-based calculation — is presented as a more productive engine of wealth than the legendary silver mines of colonial Spain.

Landed Property, the Earth as Habitat, and the Growth of Building Rent

Marx pushes the argument into a moral-historical register: the tremendous power that landed property acquires when it is combined with industrial capital in the same hands enables capital "practically to exclude workers engaged in a struggle over wages from the very earth itself as their habitat." The phrase "as their habitat" is crucial — it identifies housing and living space, not merely farmland, as an object of class struggle. "One section of society here demands a tribute from the other for the very right to live on the earth," a formulation that universalizes the rent relation to the fundamental precondition of human existence. Landed property in general, he adds, "involves the right of the proprietors to exploit the earth's surface, the bowels of the earth, the air and thereby the maintenance and development of life."

He then enumerates the causes of rising building rent. Population growth and its consequent "growing need for housing" is one factor, but "not the only factor." The second is the development of fixed capital that is "either incorporated into the earth or strikes root in it" — industrial buildings, railways, factories, docks, all of which "rest on it." This is an analytically important point: as capital fixes itself into the land, it raises the value of the land as site and foundation, converting improvements the landowner did not finance into a higher rent. Here Marx warns that "it is impossible even with Carey's good intentions to confuse house-rent ... with rent of land pure and simple." The "Carey" reference is a nuance/counter-argument: the American economist Henry Carey had attempted to harmonize landlord and tenant interests by conflating them, but Marx insists that house-rent contains two distinct components — interest and amortization on the capital invested in the house, and land-rent proper — and that in England especially "the landowner and the speculative builder are completely different persons," which makes the distinction empirically visible.

Marx then isolates two elements that landed property exploits: first, "the exploitation of the earth for the purpose of reproduction or extraction" (agriculture, mining); second, "the space that is required as an element for any production and any human activity." "On both counts landed property demands its tribute." Thus the demand for building land raises the value of land as space and foundation, while simultaneously the demand grows for "those elements of the earth's physical constitution that serve as building material" (sand, stone, clay, timber).

The Speculative Builder and the Real Object of Speculation

To document this empirically, Marx refers back to Volume 2, Chapter 12, and the testimony of the major London speculative builder Edward Capps before the Bank Acts Committee of 1857 (evidence no. 5435). Capps's testimony is deployed as hard evidence: a man who wishes to rise "can hardly expect to rise by following out a fair trade"; he must add speculative building "not on a small scale," because "the builder makes very little profit out of the buildings themselves; he makes the principal part of the profit out of the improved ground-rents." Marx then reconstructs Capps's illustrative arithmetic: the builder takes a piece of ground for £300 a year; by laying it out carefully and erecting certain descriptions of buildings, he raises the annual income to £400 or £450; his profit is the increased ground-rent of £100 or £150 a year, not the profit of the buildings, "which ... in many instances, he scarcely looks at at all." The formula embedded here is:

Speculative profit = New ground-rent − Original ground-rent = £450 − £300 = £150.

This demonstrates that ground-rent, not construction, is the true object of urban speculation. Marx then adds a decisive temporal dimension: after the leasehold expires — "ninety-nine years at the most" — the land "together with all the buildings on it, and with a ground-rent that has in the meantime generally doubled, tripled or more, reverts from the speculative builder or his heir to the original ultimate landowner." The leasehold system is thus revealed as a mechanism by which all the accumulated social development embodied in the built environment ultimately flows back to the original title-holder, who has done nothing but wait.

Rent of Mines and the Marginal Mine

"Rent of mines is determined just as is agricultural rent." Marx supports this with a long quotation from Adam Smith concerning coal mines "of which the produce is barely sufficient to pay the labour, and replace, together with its ordinary profits, the stock employed in working them." Such mines "afford some profit to the undertaker of the work, but no rent to the landlord." They can be worked advantageously "by nobody but the landlord, who, being himself undertaker of the work, gets the ordinary profit of the capital which he employs in it. Many coalmines in Scotland are wrought in this manner." This is the mining analogue of no-rent land at the margin: where a mine yields only average profit, no surplus exists to be converted into rent, and the landlord must operate it himself if it is to be worked at all — because any rent charged would push the operator below the ordinary profit rate and make the venture unviable.

Monopoly Price: Does It Create Rent, or Does Rent Create It?

Marx then introduces a crucial conceptual distinction. "It is necessary to distinguish whether the rent flows from an independent monopoly price for the products or the land itself, or whether the products are sold at a monopoly price because there is a rent."

Definition of monopoly price: "any price determined simply by the desire and ability of the buyer to pay, independently of the price of the product as determined by price of production and value." This is a demand-determined price, unmoored from the labour-value and cost-of-production anchors.

Case one — monopoly price creates rent. A vineyard producing wine of exceptional quality "but ... only in a relatively small quantity" bears a monopoly price. The wine-grower realizes a "substantial surplus profit" determined "purely and simply by the wealth and the preference of fashionable wine-drinkers." This surplus profit, "which in this case flows from a monopoly price, is transformed into rent and accrues in this form to the landowner by virtue of his title to the portion of the earth endowed with these special properties." Here the causal arrow runs: scarcity of a natural endowment → monopoly price → surplus profit → rent.

Case two — rent creates the monopoly price. Conversely, "the rent would create the monopoly price if corn were sold not only above its price of production but also above its value, as a result of the barrier that landed property opposes against the rent-free investment of capital on untilled land." This is the mechanism of absolute rent: landed property as a barrier prevents capital from being invested rent-free, so the price must rise above value to cover the rent, and the price is thereby set by the rent.

Capitalized Rent, the Price of Land, and the Slave Analogy

Marx now arrives at the chapter's central ideological critique. The fact "that it is only the title a number of people have to property in the earth that enables them to appropriate a part of society's surplus labour as tribute" is concealed by the fact that "the capitalized rent, i.e. precisely this capitalized tribute, appears as the price of land, which can be bought and sold just like any other item of trade." Capitalization means dividing the annual rent by the interest rate (in the simplest case, price of land = rent ÷ rate of interest), converting a perpetual tribute into a capital sum.

For the buyer, therefore, his claim to rent "does not appear as something obtained for nothing, without labour, risk or the entrepreneurial spirit of capital, but rather as the return for his equivalent." Rent appears to him "simply [as] interest on the capital with which he has purchased the land."

Marx then deploys his most striking analogy: "In exactly the same way, it appears to the slaveowner who has bought a Negro slave that his property in the Negro is created not by the institution of slavery as such but rather by the purchase and sale of this commodity. But the purchase does not produce the title; it simply transfers it. The title must be there before it can be bought." No sale, nor any series of sales, "can create this title." The title was "entirely created by the relations of production." This is the analytical core: exchange conceals appropriation. The market transaction of buying land appears to legitimize rent, but the legitimacy is borrowed from pre-existing property relations rooted in the social process of production.

And then the historical verdict: once these relations "have reached the point where they have to be sloughed off," the material and economically justified source of the title disappears, "and with it all transactions based on it." "From the standpoint of a higher socio-economic formation, the private property of particular individuals in the earth will appear just as absurd as the private property of one man in other men." Even an entire society, or all societies taken together, "are not the owners of the earth. They are simply its possessors, its beneficiaries, and have to bequeath it in an improved state to succeeding generations, as boni patresfamilias" (good heads of the household) — a phrase invoking the Roman legal ideal of the conscientious steward.

A footnote establishes scope conditions for what follows: in analyzing the price of land, Marx sets aside "all fluctuations due to competition, all speculation in land, and even petty landownership where the earth forms the major instrument of the producers."

The Determinants of the Price of Land, I: Rise Without Rent Increase

Marx organizes the analysis into numbered cases. Case I: the price of land may rise without an increase in rent. Two sub-mechanisms are given. (1) A fall in the rate of interest, "which means that rent is sold more dearly, and so capitalized rent, the price of land, increases." This follows directly from the capitalization formula: if rent R is constant and the interest rate i falls, the capitalized price P = R / i rises. (2) "A growth in the interest on the capital incorporated into the land" — i.e., interest attributable to improvements or fixed capital sunk into the land, which is itself a component of the price paid and can rise independently of pure rent.

The Determinants of the Price of Land, II: Rise Because Rent Rises

Case II: the price of land may rise because the rent increases. This occurs when "the price of the product of the land rises, in which case the rate of differential rent always rises." Marx here supplies a precise definition: "By the rate of differential rent we mean the ratio between the part of surplus-value that is transformed into rent, and the capital advanced to produce the agricultural product." He explicitly distinguishes this from the ratio between surplus product and total product, because "the total product does not include all the capital advanced, i.e. it does not include the fixed capital, which continues to exist alongside the product." The implication: on lands bearing differential rent, "a growing portion of the product is transformed into excess surplus product." On the worst land, "it is the rise in price of the product of the land that creates rent for the first time and hence creates the price of land" — the marginal-land mechanism, where previously no differential surplus existed.

Rent Growth With Constant or Falling Product Price

Marx then demonstrates that rent can grow "without any rise in the price of the agricultural product," which "can remain constant or even decline." If the price is constant, rent may grow because new lands of better quality are cultivated alongside equal-sized additional capital investments on older lands, "sufficient only to meet the increased demand, so that the governing market price remains unchanged." In this case, "the price of the older lands does not rise, but the price of land newly taken up rises above that of the old."

Alternatively, rent rises "because with the relative yield remaining the same, and the market price too, the amount of capital exploiting the land grows." The arithmetic is given: "even if the rent remains the same in relation to the capital advanced, it might double in amount, say, because the capital itself has doubled." Since the price has not fallen, "the second capital investment yields a surplus profit just as much as the first, which is similarly transformed into rent once the term of the tenancy expires." The underlying equations:

If capital C yields rent x at rate r = x / C, then doubling capital to 2C yields rent 2x with r unchanged. Expressed per acre: "The same acre that previously brought in £2 rent now brings in £4."

The Polemic Against the Uneven-Yield Doctrine, and the Rodbertus Critique

Marx here attacks a rival contention: that successive capital investments on the same land can produce rent "only in so far as their yield is uneven, and hence a differential rent arises." He reduces this to absurdity: it would imply that "if two capitals of £1,000 each are invested on two fields of equal productivity only one of them can yield rent, even when these two fields belong to the better class of land which does yield a differential rent." The fallacy lies in treating investment across different pieces of land under a different law from successive investment on the same piece of land, "although we have precisely derived differential rent from an identical law in both cases, from the growth in productivity of capital investment both on the same field and on different fields."

The overlooked modification is decisive: "when successive capital investments are applied to land in different locations, they come up against the barrier of landed property, which is not the case with successive capital investments on the same land. This is the reason for the opposing tendencies by which these different forms of investment in practice set barriers to one another." With constant capital composition and rate of surplus-value, the rate of profit is unaltered, so twice the capital yields twice the profit and twice the rent. Additionally, "the rental — the total rent of a country — grows with the amount of capital invested without a necessary rise in the price of the individual unit of land," even alongside "a fall in the rent on individual holdings."

A substantial footnote credits Rodbertus with having developed this point, but levels two errors: first, Rodbertus "sees the growth in profit as always expressing a growth in capital, so that the ratio remains the same as the mass of profit rises," which Marx calls wrong because "the profit rate may still rise as the composition of capital changes, through a fall in the proportionate value of the constant part of capital as compared with the variable." Second, Rodbertus wrongly treats "the proportion of money rent on a piece of land of a definite size, 1 acre for example," as if this "had been the general premise of classical economics." Marx corrects: classical economics "always treated the rate of rent ... in relation to the product" for natural-form rent, and "in relation to the capital advanced" for money rent, "since these are in fact the rational expressions."

The Irrational Form of Appearance: Rent per Square Foot

Marx now confronts the absurdity directly: "The proportion of one part of the surplus-value, the money rent ... to the land is as it stands absurd and irrational; for it is incommensurable quantities that are measured against one another" — a use-value (square feet of land) against a value (surplus-value). Its real meaning: "the ownership of these square feet of land enables the landowner to seize a certain amount of unpaid labour, which capital has realized by rooting in the soil like a pig in potatoes." He likens the surface expression to "the ratio of a £5 note to the diameter of the earth."

Yet "these irrational forms ... do not bother the practical bearers of these relationships in their everyday dealings; since they are accustomed to operating within these forms, it does not strike them as anything worth thinking about." In estranged forms of appearance, people feel "as much at home as a fish in water." He invokes Hegel: "what the common human understanding finds irrational is in fact rational, and what it finds rational is irrational" (Encyclopaedia, Part One, para. 231). Because a rise in the amount of rent is expressed identically to a rise in the rate of rent, "hence the embarrassment when the conditions that would explain the one case are absent in the other."

Price of Land Rising While Product Price Falls

Marx then argues the price of land "can rise even if the price of its product declines." Either differential rent may have increased "by a further differentiation, and with it the price of the better lands," or increased labour productivity lowers the product price while increased production more than compensates. He supplies a numerical example: assume 1 quarter costs 60s. If 2 quarters are produced on the same acre with the same capital instead of 1, and the cost falls to 40s., then 2 quarters fetch 80s., "so that the value of the product of the same capital on the same acre would have risen by a third, even though the price per qr had fallen by a third." The algebra:

Price per unit: 60s → 40s (a fall of 1/3). Total value: 1 × 60s = 60s → 2 × 40s = 80s (a rise of 1/3).

He notes this is possible "in only two ways": either poor land is withdrawn from competition while the price of better land rises (uneven effect of general improvement), or "the same price of production on the worst land ... is expressed in a larger amount of product, on account of increased labour productivity," so that while the product "still represents the same value as before," the price of its aliquot parts has fallen while "their number has increased." Critically: "If the same capital is applied, this is impossible; for in that case the same value is always expressed in any portion of the product." It becomes possible only when extra capital is invested "for gypsum, guano, etc., i.e. for improvements whose effects extend over several years," provided "the price of the individual quarter, even though it falls, does not fall in the same ratio as the number of quarters grows."

Case III: Mutual Interference of Conditions

Case III summarizes: these conditions "may partly compete with one another, partly exclude one another, and may only take effect in alternation." The decisive conclusions: "a rise in the price of land does not necessarily mean a rise in rent, and ... a rise in rent, which always brings with it a rise in the price of land, does not invariably mean an increase in its products." A footnote (citing Passy) references an actual case of falling land prices combined with rising rent.

The Earth as Instrument of Production vs. the Machine as Depreciating Capital

The chapter closes with a substantive theoretical argument against the Edinburgh Review, which, countering Richard Jones, had invoked the superficial notion that "there is a limit to the amount of capital which can be invested in a spatially limited field" — the reductio that "the whole of England cannot be fed by cultivating Soho Square." Marx responds that if this is seen as "a particular disadvantage of agriculture, precisely the opposite is the case." Successive capital investments can bear fruit "just because the earth itself functions as an instrument of production, which is not the case with a factory, where it functions only as the foundation, the site, the spatial basis of operations." In a factory, space has practical limits — "building upwards also has its definite practical limits" — and beyond them expansion must go outward. Crucially, "the fixed capital invested in machines, etc. is not improved by use; on the contrary, it depreciates," and when productivity develops rapidly, "the whole of the old machinery must be replaced by a more advantageous kind, and it is therefore lost." The earth, by contrast, "continuously improves, as long as it is treated correctly." The final sentence states the double-edged conclusion: "The advantage of the earth, that successive capital investments can have their benefit without the earlier ones being lost, at the same time implies the possibility of a difference in yield between these successive capital investments."

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The fundamental principle revealed by this chapter is that rent is a juridical claim on surplus value, not a productive contribution, and that it arises wherever a natural force can be monopolized. Differential rent, absolute rent, building rent, and mine rent are not separate species but one mechanism — the diversion of surplus profit to a title-holder — differentiated only by the natural or social basis of the monopoly and by the market form (competition vs. monopoly price) through which it is realized.

A second systemic law is the asymmetry between appearance and essence in the price of land. Capitalized rent presents a tribute as an equivalent, an appropriation as an investment, a pre-existing title as a purchase. Marx's slave analogy and his analysis of the irrational ratio of money to square footage both demonstrate that economic categories routinely invert their own foundations — and that the practical actors of the system, "like fish in water," do not find this contradictory. This is a general lesson about ideology: the everyday forms of economic life have a self-justifying grammar.

The third law is the asymmetry between land and machinery as recipients of capital. Because the earth improves with correct treatment while fixed capital depreciates, agriculture uniquely permits successive investments to accumulate benefit — which also makes it uniquely susceptible to differential yields and hence to rent. The Edinburgh Review's objection is thus inverted: what is treated as agriculture's limit is in fact its peculiar advantage.

The broader implications are both practical and historical. Practically, the chapter explains urban housing crises, leasehold reversion, land speculation, and the structural passivity of the rentier as built-in features of landed property under capitalism. Theoretically, it completes Marx's demolition of the view that rent compensates a productive service. Philosophically, it delivers the most radical claim in the volume: that the appropriation of the earth by private individuals will one day appear "just as absurd as the private property of one man in other men," and that even all nations together are merely "possessors and beneficiaries" of the earth, bound to hand it on improved — the bonus paterfamilias of humanity.
