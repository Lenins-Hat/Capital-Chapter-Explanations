Volume 3 • Chapter 32: Money Capital and Real Capital: III

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 32 closes the three-chapter sequence (Chs. 30–32) in which Marx dismantles the "currency school" doctrine — most famously embodied in Lord Overstone (Samuel Jones Loyd) and the Bank Act of 1844 — that money capital and real (industrial) capital are functionally identical, that the money market and the capital market are one and the same, and that a rising rate of interest simply means a scarcity of capital as such. Marx's central thesis in this conclusion is the following: the accumulation of loanable money capital is a distinct and partly autonomous phenomenon from the genuine accumulation of industrial capital, and the two can diverge in either direction — money capital can overflow while real accumulation stalls (a "plethora" of loan capital appearing precisely when the barriers of capitalist valorization have been hit), and, conversely, the demand for loan capital can explode at the moment of crisis even while the mass of real capital sits idle, unsaleable, and devalued. Loan capital is therefore not a transparent mirror of productive capital; it is a specific social form with its own laws of motion, its own fictions, and its own capacity to intensify and transmit crisis.

The problem the chapter addresses directly is the theoretical ambiguity of the interest rate. Overstone and his allies argue that the rate of interest is determined by the supply and demand of "capital" — a term they slip between meaning money, loan capital, and real productive capital at will, using the equivocation to present the money-lender as the only capitalist and his capital as the only capital. Marx, by contrast, insists that the demand for loan capital is not the same as the demand for productive capital, and that the same money can serve many times over as loan capital, generating fictitious titles to value that can expand far beyond the underlying base of metal. The interest rate is thus not a barometer of real accumulation but a highly distorted signal, whose movement across the industrial cycle (low in the recovery phase, rising in prosperity, peaking in the crisis) has to be explained by the peculiar mechanics of the credit system, not by a simplistic "scarcity of capital."

Intellectually, this chapter is the culmination of Marx's effort in Part V of Volume 3 to give a rigorous account of interest-bearing capital (zinstragendes Kapital) as a separate form of capital — a form in which capital appears as a commodity whose use-value is the capacity to produce profit — and thereby to show that the distribution of surplus value between interest and profit of enterprise reflects not natural facts but specific social relations of property, mediated by the credit system. The chapter also contains Marx's most concentrated treatment of fictitious capital: money capital that exists only as a claim, as a title to value, not as metal or as actual productive resources. The famous "£5,000 in deposits" colloquy with banker Twells (footnote 10) and the observation that "the same quantity of actual money can therefore represent very different quantities of money capital" capture this point with unusual precision.

Finally, the chapter supplies the diagnosis of monetary crisis that Marx had promised: crises in which the value of commodities is "sacrificed in order to ensure the fantastic and autonomous existence of this value in money." The paragraph beginning "It is the foundation of capitalist production that money confronts commodities as an autonomous form of value" is one of Marx's most philosophically loaded passages, articulating the necessary character of monetary crisis under capitalism — the way in which the split between exchange-value and use-value, latent in the commodity form, becomes actual and catastrophic when credit evaporates. The chapter thus fits into the broader theoretical framework in two directions: backwards, as the resolution of the interest/money-market debates of the 1840s–1850s, and forwards, as the foundation for Volume 3's later treatment of crisis.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Claim: Massiveness of Money Capital Is Not Itself Reproductive Capital

The chapter opens by restating a conclusion reached in the preceding two chapters and giving it an initial formulation: "The massive nature of the sum of money which has to be transformed back into capital in this way is the result of the massive scale of the reproduction process; but considered for itself, as money capital for loan, it is not itself a sum of reproductive capital." This is the anvil on which the entire chapter is hammered. Marx is drawing a distinction between cause and form. The scale of the money pool in the loan market is a symptom of the scale of reproduction — large reproduction requires large monetary turnover, hence large sums find their way into banks and money markets. But the money itself, as loan capital, is not reproductive capital. It is not machinery, raw material, or labor-power; it is a sum of value in money form, temporarily available for lending. The importance of this distinction becomes apparent when Overstone and others treat any abundance of money capital as evidence of abundant real capital; Marx insists that the money form is a separate moment in the circuit of capital, capable of swelling out of proportion to the productive base it nominally represents.

Revenue that Appears as Accumulation of Money Capital

Marx immediately identifies what he considers the "most important thing." The expansion of that portion of revenue destined for consumption — the portion that, in the aggregate, simply feeds workers, capitalists, and rentiers — presents itself first of all as an accumulation of money capital. This is a deceptive appearance, and Marx unpacks it step by step. First: the portion of the annual product designed for consumption is in no way capital. A loaf of bread is a use-value to be eaten, not a machine to be operated. Second: a part of this annual product does replace capital — specifically, it replaces the constant capital of the producers of means of consumption (the seed, tools, machinery, etc. needed to bake more bread next year). But in so far as it really is transformed into capital, it exists in kind as the natural form of the revenue of the producers of this constant capital. That is, the wheat sold to the miller is revenue for the farmer, even though it becomes constant capital for the miller. The money that mediates this transaction — the money representing revenue and serving simply to mediate consumption — is regularly transformed, for a certain period, into loanable money capital. It sits in a bank between the sale of the bread and the payment of wages or the purchase of seed.

Marx now identifies the dual aspect of this money: in so far as it represents wages, it is at the same time the money form of variable capital; and in so far as it replaces the constant capital of the producers of means of consumption, it is the money form that their constant capital temporarily assumes, serving to purchase the elements of their constant capital that must be replaced in kind. Crucially: "Neither in one form nor the other does it in itself represent accumulation, even though its volume grows with the scale of the reproduction process. But it temporarily performs the function of money for loan, i.e. of money capital." This is the first great displacement effect Marx wants to establish. Money does not have to be new accumulation to function as loan capital; money that is simply passing through the system, mediating existing reproduction, becomes loan capital for the interval. The loan market thereby swells with resources that are not net new capital at all.

From this follows the chapter's central critical lemma: "the accumulation of money capital must always reflect a greater accumulation of capital than is actually taking place, in so far as the expansion of individual consumption, because mediated by money, appears as an accumulation of money capital, since it supplies the money form for genuine accumulation, for money that initiates new capital investments." The illusion is structural, not accidental. Because consumption is mediated by money, and because money passes through the loan market, expansion of consumption looks from the loan market like expansion of capital.

The Intermediary Function of the Banker

Marx then sharpens the point in institutional terms. The accumulation of loanable money capital partly represents "nothing more than the fact that all money into which industrial capital is transformed in the course of its circuit assumes the form, not of money that the reproductive agents advance but of money that they borrow." In a developed credit system, the money that productive agents need to complete their circuit is advanced by someone else — it is borrowed. On the basis of commercial credit, "one party lends another the money that he needs in the reproduction process." But this takes the institutional form that the banker, to whom one section of reproductive agents lends money, lends it in turn to the other section — so that "the banker appear[s] as the benefactor; while at the same time disposal over this capital passes entirely to the bankers as intermediaries." Here Marx is describing the emergence of what would later be called the deposit multiplier and the intermediation function of banking, though in his own terms: the banker's position as the meeting point of lender-depositors and borrower-industrialists gives him decisive power over the disposal of social capital, even though the money is not his.

Three Special Forms of Money-Capital Accumulation

Marx now enumerates special forms in which money capital accumulates, forms that further detach loan-capital accumulation from genuine accumulation.

First special form: release of capital via falling prices. "Capital may be set free, for example, by a fall in the price of the elements of production, raw materials, etc." If the industrialist cannot immediately expand production (say, because his plant is already fully utilized or the market for his product won't absorb more), the money that formerly would have gone to buy raw materials at the higher price is now ejected from his circuit as superfluous and becomes loanable money capital. The capital is released from real functioning and pushed into the loan market.

Second special form: interruptions to business. "Capital is released in the money form, particularly in the case of the merchant, as soon as there are any interruptions to business." If the merchant completes a series of deals and there is a gap before the next series, the money realized represents for him a hoard, superfluous capital. Simultaneously and immediately, it represents an accumulation of loan capital.

Marx now draws the decisive analytical conclusion from the contrast between these two cases: "In the first case, the accumulation of money capital expresses the repetition of the reproduction process under more favourable conditions, the genuine release of a portion of capital previously tied up, enabling the reproduction process to be expanded with the same monetary means. In the second case, on the other hand, there is simply an interruption in the flow of transactions. But in both cases, money is transformed into loanable money capital, representing an accumulation of it, and has the same effect on the money market and the rate of interest, even though in the one case the genuine accumulation process is promoted, while in the other case it is inhibited." The italicized emphasis is Marx's: the same money-market effect (a lowering of the rate of interest) can accompany either an expansion or a contraction of real accumulation. The rate of interest, therefore, is not a reliable index of real economic activity.

Third special form: withdrawal of the wealth-holders. "Finally, accumulation of money capital is effected by various people who have feathered their nests and withdrawn from the reproduction process." These are the retired capitalists, the rentiers, the recipients of fixed interest and dividends — people who receive shares of surplus value and hold them as claims, without participating in production. "The greater the profits made in the course of the industrial cycle, the more of these people there are." The conclusion Marx draws is subtle: "In this case the accumulation of capital for loan expresses on the one hand a genuine accumulation (in its relative volume); on the other hand simply the degree to which industrial capitalists are transformed into simple money capitalists." This is a moment of class differentiation: productive capitalists become, in part, mere moneyed proprietors, their capital shedding its productive function and taking on the pure form of rentier wealth — a transformation that swells loan capital without adding anything to the productive base.

Profit Destined for Re-Investment and Its Two Obstacles

Marx now shifts to the other portion of profit — that not destined for consumption as revenue. This portion is transformed into money capital "only if it cannot be directly used to expand business in the sphere of production in which the profit was made." Two reasons:

(a) Satiation: the sphere is saturated with capital. There is no profitable outlet left within the existing branch. (b) Threshold of scale: "in order to function as capital, the accumulation must first have attained a certain volume, according to the due proportions for investment of new capital in this particular business." In many lines of production, a new plant requires a minimum capital outlay. Accumulated profit below that threshold has to sit idle temporarily and can only be parked as loan capital.

In either case the money is first transformed into money capital for loan and "serves to expand production in other spheres." Taking all circumstances equal, the amount of profit destined for transformation back into capital will depend on the amount of profit made, hence on the expansion of reproduction itself. But Marx now makes his plethora argument: "if this new accumulation comes up against difficulties of application, against a lack of spheres of investment, i.e. if branches of production are saturated and loan capital is over-supplied, this plethora of loanable money capital proves nothing more than the barriers of capitalist production." This is a decisive theoretical point. The apparent abundance of capital — a "plethora" — is not over-abundance relative to needs but over-abundance relative to the rate of return that capital demands. The "resulting credit swindling demonstrates that there is no positive obstacle to the use of this excess capital. But there is an obstacle set up by its own laws of valorization, by the barriers within which capital can valorize itself as capital." Capital can only be used if it can yield profit at the prevailing rate; when it cannot, it lies idle or is pushed into speculative ventures. Critically: "A plethora of money capital as such does not necessarily signify overproduction, or even a lack of spheres of employment for capital."

Loan Capital Accumulation vs. Genuine Transformation into Capital

Marx then gives a compact restatement: "The accumulation of loan capital simply means that money is precipitated as loanable money. This process is very different from a genuine transformation into capital; it is simply the accumulation of money in a form in which it can be transformed into capital." Once again the modal qualification ("can be") is critical. Precipitation as loan capital is a mere formal precondition of investment, not investment itself. Furthermore, "this accumulation can express elements that are very different from genuine accumulation." In a period of expanding genuine accumulation, the swelling of money capital can be: - In part the result of that accumulation; - In part the accompaniment of factors quite different from it; - In part even the result of blockages in genuine accumulation.

This three-fold taxonomy is essential. The same statistical phenomenon — a growing pool of loan capital — can have three entirely different causes with entirely different implications. That is why the loan market can run ahead of or behind the industrial cycle: "The very fact that the accumulation of loan capital is augmented by these elements that are independent of genuine accumulation, even if they accompany it, must lead to a regular plethora of money capital at certain phases of the cycle, and this plethora develops as the credit system improves." The phrase "must lead" is unambiguous: the phenomenon is systematic, not contingent. As credit improves, the amplification grows.

"At the same time as this, there develops the need to pursue the production process beyond its capitalist barriers: too much trade, too much production, too much credit. This must also happen always in forms that bring about a reaction." The "reaction" is crisis. So the same mechanisms that make loan capital swell beyond real accumulation also drive production to overshoot, and the overshoot has its own inherent corrective — the crash.

The Savings of Others as the Capitalist's Capital

Marx turns briefly to "the accumulation of money capital from ground-rent, wages, etc." and declines to treat it in detail; but he makes one sharply polemical point. "As capitalist production and its division of labour progress, the job of genuine saving and abstinence (by hoarders), in so far as this supplies elements of accumulation, is left to those who receive the minimum of such elements, and often enough lose what they have saved, as workers do when banks collapse." This is a bitter reference to the "abstinence" theory of capital (Senior) and a class-level observation: the rhetoric of saving and abstinence is applied to those with the least to save. Meanwhile, "For the industrial capitalist does not 'save' his capital but rather disposes of the savings of others in proportion to the size of this capital; while the money capitalist makes the savings of other people into his capital, and the credit that the reproductive capitalists give one another, and that the public give them, he makes into his own source of private enrichment."

This culminates in one of the chapter's two most famous declarations: "The final illusion of the capitalist system, that capital is the offspring of a person's own work and savings, is thereby demolished. Not only does profit consist in the appropriation of other people's labour, but the capital with which this labour of others is set in motion and exploited consists of other people's property, which the money capitalist puts at the disposal of the industrial capitalist and for which he in turn exploits him." The critique here is twofold: not only is profit (the return on capital) derived from the exploitation of alien labor, but the capital itself (the principal) is alien property. Property and labor are thereby distinguished from one another entirely — the capitalist class does not work, yet disposes of the wealth produced by others.

Credit Capital: How Often the Same Money Serves as Loan Capital

Marx now takes up "credit capital" directly. "How often the same piece of money can serve as loan capital depends entirely" on three factors:

(1) The frequency of its realization of commodity values in sale or payment — "i.e. transfers capital, as well as how often it realizes revenue." How often it comes into someone else's hands as realized value, "whether that of capital or of revenue, hence depends evidently on the scale and volume of real transactions."

(2) Economy in payments and the development and organization of the credit system. The more payments cancel each other out (clearing), the less metal actually needs to circulate — so a single unit of money accomplishes many more transactions. This is an explicit development of Marx's earlier insight that credit and clearing substitute for money circulation.

(3) The linkage and speed of action of credits — "so that if the money is precipitated at one point as a deposit, it is immediately sent out again as a loan." The velocity of loan capital, so to speak, multiplies the effect of any given deposit.

These three factors together mean that the nominal quantity of loan capital in an economy can be manifold more than the quantity of money in existence. Marx is describing, in 19th-century form, what we would now call the velocity of money and the credit multiplier.

Fictitious Capital: Loan Capital as Mere Title to Value

Now comes the most philosophically rich passage on fictitious capital in Marx's corpus. Even granting that loan capital exists in the form of actual money — gold or silver — "a large portion of this money capital is still necessarily always merely fictitious, i.e. a title to value, just like value tokens." Marx's reasoning: "In as much as money functions in the circuit of capital, it certainly forms money capital at one point, but it is not transformed into loanable money capital; it is rather exchanged for the elements of productive capital, or paid out as a means of circulation when revenue is realized, so that it cannot be transformed into loan capital for its possessor." Money performing its function in the circuit cannot simultaneously serve as someone else's loan. "In so far as it is transformed into loan capital and the same money repeatedly represents loan capital, it is still clear that it only exists at one point as metal money; at all other points it exists simply in the form of a claim to capital."

This is the key: what appears as a multiplicity of money capitals is in fact one sum of metal plus many claims against it. The accumulation of these claims "arises from a genuine accumulation... and yet the accumulation of these claims or titles as such is still different both from the genuine accumulation from which it arises and from the future accumulation (the new production process) which is mediated by the lending of money." Three distinct things: (i) genuine accumulation; (ii) the accumulation of paper titles, itself a separate phenomenon; (iii) the future accumulation these titles finance. A confusion of the three is the source of the currency-school error.

Footnote 9 — which belongs integratedly here — brings in the Twells/Overstone evidence. The banker Twells testifies: "As a banker, do you deal in capital or in money? — We deal in money." "How are the deposits paid into your bank? — In money." "How are they paid out? — In money." "Then can they be called anything else but money? — No." The naive identification is exposed. Overstone's "persistent confusion between 'capital' and 'money'" (already noted in Ch. 26) is then catalogued: his "value of money" is conflated with interest, and his 4140 remark "The use of the word 'capital' is very dangerous" reveals the unease. His 4148 statement — bullion export diminishes the quantity of money, "and a diminution of the quantity of money in this country must of course create a pressure upon the money-market generally" — expresses the currency-school axiom. And Marx wryly notes: "since in the meantime it has been incontrovertibly demonstrated even for him that the quantity of money in circulation does not determine prices, it is now the reduction in money as means of circulation that is supposed to raise its value as interest-bearing capital, and hence the rate of interest." The theoretical tourniquet is exposed: having conceded that quantity doesn't set commodity prices, Overstone postulates that quantity does set interest.

Loan Capital as Money, Then Claim to Money

Marx continues the fictitious-capital analysis. "Prima facie, loan capital always exists in the form of money" — but only at the moment of loan, and only in the hands of the borrower: "later as a claim to money, since the money in which it originally existed is now in the hands of the borrower in the actual money form. For the lender, it has been transformed into a claim to money, into an ownership title." Formally the lender has capital; substantially he holds a claim. From this, the crucial consequence: "The same quantity of actual money can therefore represent very different quantities of money capital."

"Mere money, whether it represents realized capital or realized revenue, becomes loan capital by the simple act of lending it out, by its transformation into a deposit, if we consider the general form in the developed credit system. The deposit is money capital for the depositor. But in the hands of the banker it may only be potential money capital, lying idle in his safe instead of in that of its owner." This introduces footnote 10, Marx's most extended concrete illustration of the double-counting of money capital. Banker Twells gives an example: "If I begin business with £10,000, I buy with £5,000 commodities and put them into warehouse. I deposit the other £5,000 with a banker, to draw upon it and use it as I require. I consider it still £10,000 capital to me, though £5,000 is in the shape of deposits or money."

The subsequent colloquy — reproduced by Marx as evidence — proceeds:

  • (4531) "You have parted with your £5,000 of notes to somebody else? — Yes."
  • (4532) "Then he has £5,000 of deposits? — Yes."
  • (4533) "And you have £5,000 of deposits left? — Exactly."
  • (4534) "He has £5,000 in money, and you have £5,000 in money? — Yes."
  • (4535) "But it is nothing but money at last? — No."

Here the £5,000 has doubled as money capital. Marx's resolution: A can dispose of his deposit "as if he still had it" — so far as he is concerned, it functions as potential money. But whenever he draws on it, "he destroys his deposit pro tanto." If his money has since been lent again, he is paid with "the money someone else has deposited." If A pays B by cheque, and both cheques are cleared between banks, "the money that A deposited has performed money functions twice: firstly, in the hands of the person who received the money that A had deposited; secondly, in the hands of A himself. In the second function, there is an adjustment of claims... without the intervention of money. Here the deposit has a double effect as money, i.e. first as actual money and subsequently as a claim to money. Mere claims to money can only take the place of money in the balancing of claims."

Growth of the Money-Capitalist Class and the Fictitious Securities Market

Marx now traces the social consequences: "As material wealth increases, the class of money capitalists grows. On the one hand there is an increase in the number and wealth of the retired capitalists, the rentiers; and secondly the credit system must be further developed, which means an increase in the number of bankers, money-lenders, financiers, etc." Alongside this, "the volume of interest-bearing paper, government paper, shares, etc. also expands." The result is a dual growth: on the demand side, the "jobbers who speculate in this paper play a major role in the money market."

Marx then addresses a possible objection. If all purchases and sales of paper were simply "the expression of genuine capital investment," one could argue they have no effect on the demand for loan capital — A sells paper, B buys it; the money position nets out. But Marx points out even then, "in view of the fact that the paper certainly exists, while the capital that it originally represented does not (at least not as money capital), a new demand for money capital of this kind is always created to that extent." The mere existence of claims against capital creates a demand for capital to service those claims. And "at all events it is then money capital, which was previously at B's disposition, and is now at A's."

Chapman's Testimony: All Convertible Securities Affect the Rate

Footnote and interlude: B.A. 1857, No. 4886. Question: is it correct to say the rate of discount is fixed by "the quantity of capital on the market, which is applicable to the discount of mercantile bills, as distinguished from other classes of securities?" Chapman answers: "No, I think that the question of interest is affected by all convertible securities of a current character; it would be wrong to limit it simply to the discount of bills, because it would be absurd to say that when there is a great demand for money upon (the deposit of) Consols, or even upon Exchequer bills, as has ruled very much of late, at a rate much higher than the commercial rate, our commercial world is not affected by it; it is very materially affected by it." He gives the arbitrage logic: "I can hardly expect a man to let me have money at 5 per cent upon commercial bills, if he can lend his money at the same moment at 6 per cent upon Consols... a man can hardly expect me to discount bills at 5½ per cent, if I can lend my money at 6 per cent." Then in 4892 he explains who actually drives the money market: "We do not talk of investors who buy their £2,000, or £5,000, or £10,000, as affecting the money-market materially... I allude to people, who deal in hundreds of thousands of pounds, who are what are called jobbers, who take large portions of loans, or make purchases on the market, and have to hold that stock till the public take it off their hands at a profit; these men, therefore, want money." A concentrated dealer class, not the dispersed public, sets the marginal demand.

Concentrated Money Markets and the Brood of Gamblers

Marx generalizes: "With the development of the credit system, large and concentrated money markets are created, as in London, which are at the same time the major seats of dealings in these securities. The bankers put the public's money capital at the disposal of this gang of dealers on a massive scale, and so the brood of gamblers multiplies." The Governor of the Bank of England, cited before the House of Lords Secret Committee (C.D. 1848, printed 1857, no. 219), confirms: "Money upon the Stock Exchange is, generally speaking, cheaper than it is elsewhere." This is because the stock exchange enjoys privileged access to loan capital, subsidized by the banking system's hold over public deposits.

The Average Rate of Interest Is Determined by the Average Rate of Profit

Marx now returns to a point established earlier (Ch. 22, footnoted here): "the average interest over a period of several years is determined, other things being equal, by the average rate of profit, and not by profit of enterprise, which is no more than profit minus interest." The distinction between profit and profit of enterprise is crucial: the total profit yielded by capital is split, via the money-market mechanism, into interest and profit of enterprise. Average interest tracks average profit (the whole), since both components are claims on the same total. The variation of commercial interest over the industrial cycle — "the interest charged by money-lenders for discounting and loans within the world of trade" — shows a phase in which interest rises above its minimum and reaches the average medium level (then exceeds it), "this movement being the result of a rise in profits."

Two Critical Observations on Interest and Profit

Marx flags "two things... to be noted here."

(1) Prolonged high interest as prima facie evidence of high profit — but not high profit of enterprise. If the interest rate remains high for a long period (particularly in a country like England with a long-standing average rate expressed in long-term loans — "what we might call private interest"), "this is prima facie evidence that the rate of profit during this period is also high, but it does not necessarily prove that the rate of profit of enterprise is high." The distinction vanishes for capitalists working predominantly with their own capital — "they realize the high rate of profit, since they pay their interest to themselves." But for the borrowing capitalist, "this high rate of profit, after deducting the high rate of interest, leaves only a low rate of profit of enterprise. The latter may contract, while the high rate of profit continues." This is possible because "enterprises once embarked upon must be continued." Once a factory is built, the capital is sunk; the capitalist must go on paying interest even if the residual profit thins. And here is the speculative hazard: "In this phase, operations are conducted largely with credit capital (other people's); and the high rate of profit may in places be speculative and prospective. It is possible to pay interest at a high rate with a high rate of profit but a declining profit of enterprise. It can be paid — and this is partly the case in periods of speculation — not out of profits but out of the borrowed capital itself, and this situation can last a good while." The payment of interest out of capital rather than out of profit — the Ponzi structure of speculative finance — is identified here as a phase of the cycle, not an aberration.

(2) Demand for money capital ≠ demand for industrial capital. "To say that the demand for money capital and hence the interest rate rises because the profit rate is high is not the same as saying that the demand for industrial capital rises and that this is why the interest rate is high." The two could diverge sharply — in crisis, demand for loan capital peaks while demand for industrial capital collapses: "In times of crisis the demand for loan capital, and with it the interest rate, reaches its maximum; the rate of profit as good as disappears, and with it the demand for industrial capital. In times such as these everyone borrows simply to pay, to settle commitments already entered into." Demand here is for means of payment, not for means of production.

Conversely, in the revival phase — "the period when business revives after the crisis" — loan capital is demanded "in order to buy, and to transform the money capital into productive or commercial capital. And then it is demanded either by the industrial capitalist or by the merchant. The industrial capitalist invests it in means of production and labour-power." So the same high interest rate can express totally different economic states.

Wages and the Interest Rate

Marx now addresses a specific confusion. "The rising demand for labour-power can never be in itself a reason for a rising rate of interest, in so far as this is determined by the profit rate. Higher wages are never a cause of higher profit, even though, taking particular phases of the industrial cycle, they may be one of its results." The point: if wages rise, they reduce the profit rate (assuming the working day and intensity are given, higher wages eat into surplus value). They cannot by themselves push the interest rate up via the profit channel — because that channel runs the wrong way.

But: "The demand for labour-power may increase because the exploitation of labour is proceeding under particularly favourable conditions, but the rising demand for labour-power and hence for variable capital does not in and of itself increase profits but rather reduces them in proportion. Yet the demand for variable capital may increase, and thus also the demand for money capital, while this in turn increases the rate of interest. The market price of labour-power then rises above its average, a more than average number of workers come to be employed, while at the same time the rate of interest rises, because the demand for money capital rises in these conditions." So there can be a correlation between rising wages and rising interest — but the causal arrow goes through the demand for money capital, not through the profit rate. "An increased demand for labour-power raises the price of this commodity just like any other, without raising profits, which depend principally on the relative cheapness of this particular commodity. At the same time, however, under the conditions we have assumed, this demand raises the rate of interest, by increasing the demand for money capital."

Marx draws out the consequence with an analytic counterfactual: "If the money capitalist, instead of lending out money, should transform himself into an industrialist, the fact that he has to pay more for labour would not in and of itself increase his profit but would rather lead to a proportionate reduction in it." The role of lender or the role of industrialist does not alter the underlying logic: higher wages mean lower profit. "The combination of circumstances may still be such that his profit rises, but this is never because he pays more for labour." Finally: "If wages rise for whatever reason, in otherwise unfavourable conjunctures, the rise in wages causes the profit rate to fall, although the rate of interest rises to the extent that the wage rise increases the demand for money capital." This is a foundational statement of the non-identity of interest and profit rate: they can move in opposite directions.

Overstone's "Demand for Capital" Dissected

Marx now zeroes in on Overstone. "Apart from labour, what Overstone calls the 'demand for capital' consists simply of the demand for commodities. The demand for commodities raises their price, whether this demand rises above the average, or the supply falls below its average. If the industrial capitalist or merchant now has to pay £150, for example, for the same quantity of commodities for which he formerly paid £100, he would have to borrow £150 instead of £100 and would therefore have to pay £7½, at a 5 per cent rate of interest, instead of £5. The amount of interest he has to pay rises, because of the rise in the amount of capital borrowed." Note carefully: the rate of interest is unchanged at 5%; the sum of interest rises only because the sum borrowed rises. Overstone's "demand for capital" is thus either the demand for commodities (a price effect, not a capital effect) or the demand for labor (which, as just shown, pressures labor costs upward but not profit rates).

Marx states his primary polemical target precisely: "Mr Overstone's entire aim is to present the interests of loan capital and industrial capital as identical, while his Bank Act is precisely calculated to exploit the difference between these interests for the benefit of money capital." This is one of the chapter's most characteristic dialectical formulations: Overstone's theory (loan capital = industrial capital) is a mask for his practice (the 1844 Bank Act, with its separation of Issue and Banking Departments and its rigid limits on note issue, tightens the money market precisely in moments when industrial capital most needs relief). The user-ideology is at odds with the user's own institutional design.

Commodity Supply, Speculation, and the Rate of Interest

Marx now refines the analysis of how commodity-market conditions affect the loan market.

Case 1: supply has fallen below average. "It is possible that the demand for commodities... absorbs no more money capital than before. The same sum, and perhaps a smaller one, has to be paid for their overall value, but a smaller quantity of use-values is received for this sum." So the quantity of use-values falls but the sum of value (money) may be the same or lower — hence "the demand for loanable money capital remains the same, i.e. the interest rate will not rise, even though the demand for commodities has risen in relation to their supply and the commodities' price has therefore risen as well. The rate of interest can only be affected when the total demand for loan capital grows, and under the above assumptions this is not the case." This is a precise analytical wedge: price rises of commodities do not by themselves raise the rate of interest; only the demand for loan capital does.

Case 2: supply fallen below average with speculation. "As is the case with a harvest failure in corn, cotton, etc., while the demand for loan capital grows on the speculation that prices will rise still higher, the most immediate means to make them rise being to withdraw a part of the supply temporarily from the market. In order to pay for the commodities bought without selling them, gold is obtained by way of commercial 'bill of exchange operations'. In this case, the demand for loan capital grows, and the rate of interest may rise as a result of this attempt to block the supply of commodities to the market artificially. The higher rate of interest then expresses an artificial reduction in the supply of commodity capital." The interest-rate rise here expresses deliberate stock-hiding, not scarcity of real capital.

Case 3: supply has increased, price below average. "The demand for loan capital may remain the same or even fall, because more commodities are to be had with the same sum of money. There could also be a speculative formation of stocks, partly for use at a favourable moment for the purpose of production, partly in expectation of a later rise in price. In this case, the demand for loan capital could grow, so that the higher rate of interest would be an expression of capital investment in the formation of excessive stocks of the elements of productive capital."

Marx then states the methodological frame for all of this: "All we are considering here is the demand for loan capital as it is influenced by the demand and supply for commodity capital. We have already explained earlier how the changing condition of the reproduction process in the various phases of the industrial cycle affects the supply of loan capital." Then the critical attack: "The trivial statement that the market rate of interest is determined by the supply and demand for (loan) capital is cunningly conflated by Overstone with his own assumption in which loan capital is identical with capital in general, and he seeks in this way to transform the money-lender into the only capitalist and his capital into the only capital." This is the ideological function of a seemingly neutral formula: the tautology (interest = supply-demand of loan capital) is inflated to a metaphysical identification (loan capital = capital), and the money-lender is thereby enthroned as the unique capitalist.

Demand for Means of Payment in Times of Pressure

"In times of pressure, the demand for loan capital is a demand for means of payment and nothing more than this; in no way is it a demand for money as means of purchase. The interest rate can then rise very high, irrespective of whether real capital — productive and commodity capital — is abundant or scarce." Here is the theoretical core of Marx's account of the panic: what lenders are being asked for is not means by which goods may be bought, but means by which debts may be paid. Real capital may be abundant; still the panic drives interest to usurious heights.

"The demand for means of payment is simply a demand for convertibility into money, in so far as the merchants and producers are able to offer good security; it is a demand for money capital, in so far as this is not the case, i.e. in so far as an advance of means of payment gives them not only the money form, but also the equivalent that they lack for payments, in whatever form this might be." Distinction drawn: (a) holders of sound securities want only the liquid form; (b) holders of unsound positions want real resources to cover losses.

Both Sides of the Crisis Controversy Are Right and Wrong

"This is the point at which both sides in the present controversy in the theory of crises are simultaneously right and wrong." Marx gives the dialectical verdict:

  • Those who say there is simply a lack of means of payment are right if they mean the owners of bona fide securities; but "they are fools who believe it the duty of a bank, and within its power, to transform every bankrupt swindler into a solvent capitalist by means of paper tokens."
  • Those who say there is simply a lack of capital are right in a tautological sense (inconvertible capital is present on a massive scale due to over-importing and overproduction), and yet in a substantive sense wrong: "or else they are just referring to those credit-jobbers who actually are put in a position where they can no longer obtain other people's capital to operate with and then demand that the bank should not only help them pay for the capital lost but also enable them to continue their swindling."

The dialectical point: each side seizes one aspect — lack of the money form vs. lack of usable capital — and forgets the other. Both relate to a moment in the crisis in which the formal and the real aspects of capital come apart and confront each other as enemies.

The Foundation: Money as Autonomous Form of Value

Marx now rises to the most general plane. "It is the foundation of capitalist production that money confronts commodities as an autonomous form of value, or that exchange-value must obtain an autonomous form in money, and this is possible only if one particular commodity becomes the material in whose value all other commodities are measured, this thereby becoming the universal commodity, the commodity par excellence, in contrast to all other commodities." This is the categorial derivation of money from the commodity-form, restated at the concrete level of crisis.

"This must show itself in two ways, particularly in developed capitalist countries, which replace money to a large extent either by credit operations or by credit money."

First: "In times of pressure, when credit contracts or dries up altogether, money suddenly confronts commodities absolutely as the only means of payment and the true existence of value. Hence the general devaluation of commodities and the difficulty or even impossibility of transforming them into money, i.e. into their own purely fantastic form." The commodity's "own purely fantastic form" is money — the value-stuff of the commodity, detached from its useful body. In crisis, that detachment becomes a chasm: goods pile up unsold because no one has money.

Second: "credit money is itself only money in so far as it absolutely represents real money to the sum of its nominal value. With the drain of gold, its convertibility into money becomes problematic, i.e. its identity with actual gold. Hence we get forcible measures, putting up the rate of interest, etc. in order to guarantee the conditions of this convertibility." Credit money has a structural dependence on metal — not because metal is magically necessary for domestic exchange, but because the credit system's promises to convert are honored only as long as the underlying metal reserve holds. "This can be more or less intensified by erroneous legislation based on incorrect theories of money and enforced on the nation in the interest of money-dealers such as Overstone and company. But the basis for it is provided by the basis of the mode of production itself." The 1844 Bank Act intensifies the crisis, but it does not create the crisis's monetary form; the monetary form is grounded in the capitalist relation itself.

The Sacrifice of Commodities to Money

"A devaluation of credit money (not to speak of a complete loss of its monetary character, which is in any case purely imaginary) would destroy all the existing relationships. The value of commodities is thus sacrificed in order to ensure the fantastic and autonomous existence of this value in money. In any event, a money value is only guaranteed as long as money itself is guaranteed. This is why many millions' worth of commodities have to be sacrificed for a few millions in money." This is one of Marx's most compressed formulas for crisis: the form of value (money) is preserved by destroying the substance of value (commodities). Warehouses of goods are dumped, factories are closed, and the metal stock is preserved — the sacrifice of wealth in its material form to wealth in its money form.

"This is unavoidable in capitalist production, and forms one of its particular charms." The irony is cutting: the author identifies with grim satisfaction a structural feature that bourgeois political economy presents as either an anomaly to be legislated away (currency school) or a benign "oversight" (laissez-faire). "In former modes of production, this does not happen, because given the narrow basis on which these move, neither credit nor credit money is able to develop. As long as the social character of labour appears as the monetary existence of the commodity and hence as a thing outside actual production, monetary crises, independent of real crises or as an intensification of them, are unavoidable." The condition — "the social character of labour appears as the monetary existence of the commodity" — is the fetish form of the commodity: labor is social only indirectly, through the market, and the market's universal equivalent (money) is the surface on which the sociality of labor is tested. Where that test fails, monetary crisis is inevitable.

The Bank's Power to Alleviate or Intensify

"It is evident on the other hand that, as long as a bank's credit is not undermined, it can alleviate the panic in such cases by increasing its credit money, whereas it increases this panic by contracting credit." This is a crucial practical corollary. A solvent bank, confronting a panic, can issue more credit money (banknotes, drafts, acceptances) and relieve the pressure of payment — as long as its credit is not itself in question. If it contracts instead, it deepens the panic. This point is aimed at the 1844 Act, which forces the Bank of England to contract precisely when the panic demands expansion. "The entire history of modern industry shows that metal would be required only to settle international trade and its temporary imbalances, if production at home were organized." This is perhaps the single most radical practical implication of Marx's analysis — an anticipation, in germ, of the argument for planned production and managed money that would be developed by later Marxist and social-democratic writers. "The suspension of cash payments by the so-called national banks, which is resorted to as the sole expedient in all extreme cases, shows that even now no metal money is needed at home." The Bank Act's own suspension clauses, invoked in every crisis, refute the Act's theory.

The Balance of Payments vs. the Balance of Trade

Marx now addresses an international-relations point — the distinction between the balance of payments and the balance of trade. "It would be ridiculous to say of two individuals that they both have an unfavourable balance of payments in their dealings with one another. If they are each mutually debtor and creditor, it is clear that when their claims do not balance, one of them must be debtor to the other for the remainder. With nations, this is in no way the case. And this fact is recognized by all economists in the statement that the balance of payments may be favourable or unfavourable for a country, even though the balance of trade must ultimately balance out." The crucial difference: between two individuals, a defined settlement can occur; between nations, settlement dates, capital flows, and gold transfers create a persistent asymmetry.

"The balance of payments is distinct from the balance of trade in that it is that balance of trade which must be settled at a particular date. The effect of crises, then, is to compress the difference between the balance of payments and the balance of trade into a short period of time; and the specific conditions that develop in nations affected by a crisis, and hence by the arrival of this date of payment, already involve a contraction of this kind in the settlement period." Crisis means a sudden synchronization of all payment dates across nations.

The Chain of Crisis Transmission

Marx provides a step-by-step list of crisis transmission internationally:

  1. Shipment of precious metals abroad — gold flows out to close trade gaps.
  2. Forced selling of goods sent on consignment — foreign merchants holding goods sell them at any price.
  3. Export of commodities in order to sell them off cheaply or obtain money advances on them at home — a nation ships goods abroad and monetizes them via credit at home.
  4. Recall of credit — lenders call in loans across national borders.
  5. Fall in security values — stock and bond markets devalue.
  6. Forced selling of foreign securities — capital is repatriated by dumping foreign assets.
  7. Attraction of foreign capital to invest in these devalued securities — foreign buyers pick up bargains, reversing gold flows.
  8. Bankruptcy, which settles a whole series of claims — final resolution by cancellation.

"In this connection, metal is often still sent to the country where the crisis has broken out, because drafts on it are uncertain and payment in metal more secure." When credit is uncertain, gold is the ultimate settlement instrument. "Added to this is the fact that, in relation to Asia, all capitalist countries are generally debtors simultaneously, either directly or indirectly. Once these various factors exert their full effect on the other countries involved, these too experience an export of gold or silver, i.e. their payments fall due, and the same phenomenon is repeated." The Asiatic trade balance (Europe's chronic deficit with Asia, especially in silver) makes the crisis general and transnational.

Commercial Credit and the Price of Commodities

Marx now makes a technical point about commercial credit. "In the case of commercial credit, interest, as the difference between the credit price and the cash price, is involved in the price of a commodity only in so far as bills of exchange have a longer term than usual. In other cases not. And this is explained by the fact that each person takes this credit from one direction and extends it in another." In other words, short-term trade credit typically nets out at each business's level — the businessman receives and extends credit on roughly the same terms, so its cost washes out. Engels's famous editorial note appears here: "(This does not tally with my own experience. — F. E.)" — an illuminating instance of Marx's collaborator registering disagreement with the master, and a reminder that Volume 3 was assembled from manuscripts. Marx continues: "But in so far as discounting is involved here in this way, it is not governed by this commercial credit, but rather by the money market." The banker's discount rate, not the bilateral credit terms of trade, determines the cost — and that rate is set by the money market, i.e. the aggregate supply and demand for loan capital.

The Cotton Example (1844–1845)

Marx now provides an empirical reductio ad absurdum. "If the demand and supply of money capital, which determines the rate of interest, were identical with the demand and supply of real capital, as Overstone maintains, then according to whether we considered various different commodities or the same commodity at different stages (raw material, semi-finished goods, finished product), interest would have to be both low and high at the same time."

He then supplies the evidence:

Bank of England interest rate: - 1844: 4 per cent (January–September), 2½–3 per cent (November–December). - 1845: 2½, 2¾, 3 per cent (January–October), then between 3 and 5 per cent (remaining months).

Prices: - Average price of fair Orleans cotton: 6⅛ d. in 1844, 4¾ d. in 1845.

Stocks: - Liverpool cotton stock, 3 March 1844: 627,042 bales. - Liverpool cotton stock, 3 March 1845: 773,800 bales.

The analytical inference: Cotton price was low in 1845 — which, on the "loan capital = real capital" thesis, should have meant low interest. It was indeed low for most of the year. But on the same thesis, judged by the price of yarn, interest should have been high, because yarn prices were relatively high and profits absolutely so. The same commodity at different stages of its circuit implies opposite interest rates. The evidence is decisive.

The yarn computation: - Cotton at 4 d. per lb. in 1845. - Spinning cost: 4 d. (good secunda mule twist No. 40). - Total cost to spinner: 8 d. per lb. - Selling price, September–October 1845: 10½ d. or 11 d. per lb. - Margin: 2½ to 3 d. per lb. on a total cost of 8 d. — a very substantial profit.

(Evidence of Wylie is cited but the transcript is just indicated by Marx.)

Thus: real capital conditions (cotton cheap, yarn profitable) would have implied low interest on the raw-material stage and high interest on the finished-product stage — an incoherent result. In fact the money-market rate was determined by factors independent of the physical commodity's stage. The "identity" thesis collapses.

The Whole Question Resolved

Marx now undertakes a hypothetical reconstruction. "The demand and supply for loan capital would be identical with the demand and supply for capital in general (although this last phrase is absurd; for the industrialist or merchant, commodities are a form of his capital, but he never demands capital as such, but always a particular commodity, buying and paying for it as a commodity, corn or cotton, irrespective of the role it has to fulfil in the circuit of his capital), if there were no money-lenders and instead of them the lending capitalist owned machines, raw material, etc. and lent these out or hired them, as houses are rented now, to industrial capitalists who were themselves the owners of a section of these objects."

So the counterfactual: imagine a world without money-lenders, in which lending capitalists owned machines and raw materials in kind and hired them out. "In conditions such as these, the supply of loan capital would be identical with the supply of elements of production for the industrial capitalist, and of commodities for the merchant." Even then — and this is the crucial point — "the division of profit between lender and borrower would then be completely dependent, in the first place, on the ratio in which this capital is borrowed and in which it is the property of the person using it." The split between interest and profit of enterprise would still be a matter of property relations, not of technical scarcity. The thought experiment emphasizes that the form of lending (money vs. machines) does not eliminate the property relation involved.

Weguelin's "Unemployed Capital"

Marx turns to the testimony of Weguelin (B.A. 1857). Weguelin holds the rate of interest is determined by "the amount of unemployed capital" (252), and that it is "but an indication of a large amount of capital seeking employment" (271). Later this is called "floating capital" (485), by which Weguelin understands: "the Bank of England notes and other kinds of circulation in the country, for instance, the country banks circulation and the amount of coin which is in the country... I include in floating capital the reserves of the bankers' (502, 503)" — and even "gold bullion" (503). Marx flags the circularity: what determines interest is the "amount of unemployed capital," but "unemployed capital" is defined as the very money whose availability is at issue. Weguelin also says the Bank of England exerts great influence when "we" (the Bank of England) "are holders of the greater portion of the unemployed capital" (1198) — contradicting Overstone's insistence that the Bank "is no place for capital." Weguelin concludes: "I think the rate of discount is governed by the amount of unemployed capital which there is in the country. The amount of unemployed capital is represented by the reserve of the Bank of England, which is practically a reserve of bullion. When, therefore, the bullion is drawn upon, it diminishes the amount of unemployed capital in the country and consequently raises the value of that which remains" (1258). So on Weguelin's own account, the rate of interest is determined by the size of the Bank's bullion reserve — a purely monetary quantity.

John Stuart Mill and the Reserve

Mill's testimony (2102): "The Bank is obliged to depend for the solvency of its Banking Department upon what it can do to replenish the reserve in that department; and therefore as soon as it finds that there is any drain in progress, it is obliged to look to the safety of its reserve, and to commence contracting its discounts or selling securities." Mill describes the Bank's institutional compulsion: as soon as gold drains, the Bank must contract its credit. But this is precisely the opposite of what a panic demands (per Marx earlier: "as long as a bank's credit is not undermined, it can alleviate the panic... by increasing its credit money"). The 1844 Act institutionalizes a pro-cyclical contraction — a machine for intensifying panics.

The Banking Department and the Metal Reserve

The chapter's final movement clarifies the 1844 Act's institutional structure. "Taking the Banking Department by itself, the reserve is a reserve for deposits only. According to the Overstones, the Banking Department should simply act as a banker, without regard to the 'automatic' note issue." The Act's theory separates Issue (tin box of notes, gold certificate) and Banking (commercial deposits), allegedly confining the Reserve to the Banking side and letting the Issue side operate "automatically." But in practice: "in times of real pressure, the Bank of England keeps a very sharp eye on the metal reserve, independently of the reserve of the Banking Department, which consists simply of notes; and it must do so, if it does not want to go broke. For to the same extent that the metal reserve disappears, so too does the reserve of banknotes, and no one should know this better than Mr Overstone, who so wisely established this very device in his 1844 Bank Act."

The final irony: the very legislator who claimed that the Banking Department need only attend to its own reserve, and that the money in circulation is "automatic," finds himself in a panic forced to attend precisely to the metal reserve, because if his bullion drains the notes are no longer convertible — and it is this convertibility that the Act was specifically designed to enforce. The "automatic" mechanism collapses into discretionary panic-management whenever its own conditions are threatened, and the separation of Issue from Banking turns out to be a fiction that intensifies rather than mitigates the crisis. This is Marx's final, exacting verdict on the 1844 Bank Act in this chapter.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

First systemic law: the categorial heterogeneity of loan capital and industrial capital. The central discovery of the chapter is that loanable money capital is not a transparent expression of real accumulation but a distinct social form with its own dynamics. Money capital can be swelled by revenue being parked in banks, by capital released via falling raw-material prices, by interrupted business producing hoards, by the withdrawal of rentiers, by the "savings of others" placed at the disposal of industrialists and bankers, and by the multiplication of credit claims against the same metal. Because each of these channels can expand independently of real accumulation, the loan market regularly runs ahead of real production, and the resulting "plethora" is a systematic phenomenon, not a market failure to be legislated away.

Second systemic law: the multiplicative nature of credit capital. How often the same piece of money serves as loan capital depends on (1) the volume of real transactions it mediates, (2) clearing and credit organization, and (3) the speed of circulation of deposits into loans. This means the nominal quantity of money capital vastly exceeds the physical stock of money, so monetary statistics cannot be treated as proportions of the productive base. The Twells example (£5,000 doubled) and the "claim to money" analysis establish that a large portion of loan capital is necessarily fictitious — not fraudulent, but structurally a claim that can only be honored in the aggregate if the real economy expands in step.

Third systemic law: interest and profit do not move in lockstep. Marx systematically shows: (a) high interest can coexist with falling profit of enterprise; (b) interest can rise because of rising wages, which reduce the profit rate; (c) in crisis, interest peaks while profit collapses; (d) in revival, interest falls while profit recovers. The same "high rate of interest" can express fundamentally different economic states. Interest, therefore, is not a barometer of real capital scarcity but a signal of the money-market's specific conjunctural conditions.

Fourth systemic law: the necessary possibility of monetary crisis. "It is the foundation of capitalist production that money confronts commodities as an autonomous form of value" — the split between value's money form and value's commodity substance is not an accident of institutional design but is written into the commodity form itself. Where labor is only indirectly social (via the market), the possibility of monetary crisis is constitutive. Crises emerge not as failures of the system but as the system working itself out: commodities are sacrificed for money precisely when the systemic guarantee of money's value breaks down. This is why "many millions' worth of commodities have to be sacrificed for a few millions in money" — a formula whose applicability to 19th-century panics remains, mutatis mutandis, an interpretative schema for financial crises ever since.

Fifth systemic law: the ideological function of theoretical confusion. Overstone's identification of loan capital with capital is not naïve error but performs an ideological function: it transforms the money-lender into the sole capitalist and mystifies the property relation involved in lending. Marx's polemic against the 1844 Bank Act is at once theoretical and practical — the Act's designers confuse money and capital in theory, and design a machine that intensifies monetary crises in practice. This concern for the class function of theory runs throughout the chapter.

Broader theoretical implications. Chapter 32 is the culmination of Marx's account of the specificity of financial capital — a category that later Marxist theorists (Hilferding, Lenin, Bukharin) would develop into the theory of finance capital and imperialism. The chapter provides: a mechanism for the multiplication of claims (the credit multiplier), a theory of fictitious capital (the securities market divorced from the capital it nominally represents), an analysis of the concentration of money markets (London's Stock Exchange), and an account of speculative dynamics (jobbers, speculators, the payment of interest out of borrowed capital). It also points forward to the theory of crisis in Volume 3's later chapters, providing the monetary mechanism by which a "real" crisis of profitability is transmitted and intensified through the credit system, and providing the grounds for distinguishing monetary crisis from crisis of real accumulation, while insisting on their interdependence.

Broader practical implications. Marx's conclusions sustain several claims about economic policy that go beyond his own era. First: metallic money is not necessary for domestic circulation in a developed economy — an insight behind modern fiat currency and central banking. Second: a solvent bank facing a panic should expand credit, not contract it; otherwise it deepens the crisis. Third: rigid monetary rules (like the 1844 Act) that force pro-cyclical contraction will always be suspended in extremis, revealing their theoretical incoherence in practice. Fourth: the profit of enterprise and the rate of interest are distinct categories, and industrial policy that ignores the difference will find the two moving in opposite directions.

Philosophical implications. The most profound philosophical payoff concerns the role of appearance in capitalist economy. The appearance that capital grows through saving and abstinence is demolished by the demonstration that the industrial capitalist "disposes of the savings of others," the money capitalist "makes the savings of other people into his capital," and the credit that reproductive capitalists give one another is transformed into "his own source of private enrichment." The final illusion of the system — that capital is the offspring of work and thrift — is shown to rest on appropriation and property, not labor and virtue. The chapter thus closes the great arc of Marx's analysis in Part V by grounding the distribution of surplus value between interest and profit of enterprise in the same exploitation that is the source of surplus value itself — completing the demonstration that the appearance of a natural, harmonious economy conceals a structure in which the wealth of the few is constituted by the labor and losses of the many.

The chapter ends on a note of grim but rigorous closure: the metal reserve of the Bank of England — the ultimate fetish of the currency school — turns out to be nothing more than the material form on which the whole edifice of credit rests, and to which, at the moment of crisis, commodities (real wealth, use-values, human labor embodied in goods) must be sacrificed. Marx does not offer a remedy in this chapter; he offers a diagnosis. The 1844 Bank Act will produce, in every crisis, the paradoxical result of intensifying precisely the panic it was meant to prevent — and this paradox is not a flaw of the Act but the truth of the capitalist relation, appearing at the surface of the money market in forms that all the currency-school verbiage cannot disguise.
