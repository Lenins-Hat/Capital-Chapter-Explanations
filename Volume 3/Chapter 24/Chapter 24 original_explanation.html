Volume 3 • Chapter 24: Externalization of the Relations of Capital in Interest-Bearing Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 24 — "Interest-Bearing Capital as the Superficial Form of the Capital Relation" — is the opening salvo of the section in Capital, Volume 3 that dissects how the total social surplus-value is divided into interest and profit of enterprise, and eventually the credit system that governs this division. Its central thesis is unambiguous and deliberately scandalous in form: in interest-bearing capital, designated by the formula M–M′, the capital relation reaches "its most superficial and fetishized form." Everything that actually produces surplus-value — the purchase and sale of commodities, the employment of labour-power, the extraction of surplus labour in production — is silently erased, leaving only a diptych of extremes: money on one side, more money on the other. The pivot of the argument is that capital itself now wears no marks of its own origin. The social relationship between capitalist and wage-labourer is transmuted into the relationship of a thing (money) to itself, as though money were as naturally and inherently interest-bearing as "a pear tree is pear-bearing."

The intellectual objective of the chapter is twofold. First, it exposes and anatomizes the fetish form of capital in its purest expression — an analysis that completes, at the level of capital-in-general, the fetishism of the commodity form announced in Volume 1. Second, it performs a powerful ideological critique: it shows that this fetishized form is not a mere optical illusion but a functional representation (a Vorstellung) that serves the apologetic interests of "vulgar economics," which can now present capital "as an independent source of wealth, of value creation." The bulk of the chapter — roughly two-thirds of its pages — is a polemic against specific historical expressions of this delusion: Martin Luther's medieval polemic against usury, Dr Richard Price's compound-interest fantasies, William Pitt's "sinking fund" (a state-financing scheme that rests on the idea of paying off debt by accumulating it), Josiah Child's claims about 10% over 70 years, The Economist's declaration that all wealth "has long ago become the interest of capital," and Adam Müller's "Romantic" drivel about compound interest accruing to the worker. Marx uses these episodes not just as rhetorical targets but as a case study in the pernicious theoretical consequences of taking appearance for the essence of capital.

Philosophically and structurally, the chapter is the hinge-point between the "internal" analysis of capital (value, surplus-value, the profit rate, as developed in the earlier parts of Volume 3) and the "external" or surface analysis (the division of profit, credit, fictitious capital, the money market). It also anticipates the chapters on the money-dealing capital, credit, and the banking system, and it disciplines the reader: the correct theory of interest must never lose sight of its origin in surplus labour, and must remain anchored in the two great real limits — the devaluation of existing capital through rising productivity and the falling rate of profit — because against these, Dr Price's fantasy of geometric growth is exposed as mathematically impossible, not merely socially unjust. This fusion — of fetish analysis, financial polemic, and political-economy critique — makes the chapter both a culminating moment of the critique of political economy and a foundational text for any critique of financial fetishism.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Starting Point: Why Interest-Bearing Capital is the Most Fetishized Form

Marx begins by ranking the fetish. In commercial capital, whose circuit is M–C–M′, at least the general form of the capitalist movement is preserved: money buys commodities, commodities are sold for more money. Even though the whole operation occurs in the sphere of circulation, and the profit "appears as merely profit upon alienation" (i.e. it looks like a pure markup on the selling price, as if profit arose from buying cheap and selling dear), the process still exhibits "the unity of opposing phases" — purchase and sale — and therefore "presents itself as the product of a social relation, not the product of a mere thing." In other words, commercial capital cannot fully hide the fact that the increase depends on transactions among persons. Interest-bearing capital obliterates even this. In M–M′ the "two opposite procedures" of buying and selling are gone. What remains is an empty syllogism: money in, more money out. There is no commodity, no sale, no labour process — only money and more money. The social relation has collapsed into the relation of a thing to itself.

The Numerical Demonstration: Capital as Relation of Quantities

To make this concrete, Marx deploys a deceptively simple arithmetic example. A capitalist lends £1,000 at 5% for one year. The value of that £1,000 as capital is:

C + C·i (or C(1 + i)) = 1,000 + (1,000 × 1/20) = £1,050.

Since 5% = 1/20, the surplus accruing to the loan is £50. Marx draws a crucial conceptual point from this trivial calculation: "capital is not a simple quantity. It is a relation of quantities." The £1,000 is now a ratio between the principal as "a given value" and itself as "a principal that has produced a surplus-value." Money in itself is a magnitude; money as capital is a self-referential magnitude with a built-in increment. This is why the same formal appearance holds for every active capitalist — those operating with their own capital and those operating with borrowed capital alike: the value of capital now spontaneously presents itself as a quantity that produces another quantity. And this self-referential quantity relation is precisely what is sold when money is hired out.

M–M′ as the "Meaningless Abbreviation" of the General Formula

Marx returns to the original starting point — the general formula M–C–M′ — and shrinks it to its two extremes, M–M′, where M′ = M + ΔM. This is "the original and general formula for capital reduced to a meaningless abbreviation." The word meaningless is not metaphorical: the entire middle term C (the actual production and circulation process, the purchase of labour-power and means of production, the extraction of surplus labour) has been excised. What remains is "capital in its finished form" — the result of the whole reproductive ensemble — presented as if it were produced by the money form on its own. Here is the birth of the fetish in its definitive form: capital appears as a mysterious, self-creating source of interest, unmediated by production and circulation. It is no longer the outcome of a process but a property of a thing. "The thing (money, commodity, value) is now already capital simply as a thing." This is the exact structural parallel Marx will draw again and again: the result of the overall reproduction process appears as a property devolving on a thing in itself, just as in commodity fetishism the social relations of producers appeared as properties of things.

An important dialectical move follows: because money is "commodities in their ever-exchangeable form," the possessor's mere choice — to spend the money as money or "hire it out as capital" — seems to determine whether valorization occurs. This makes the fetish appear voluntary and thing-like simultaneously: as if the same thing (money) carries a hidden capacity that the owner may "release" at will. The consequence is that "the social relation is consummated in the relationship of a thing, money, to itself." Where the actual transformation of money into capital should be, we have "only the form of this devoid of content."

Money's Use-Value: The Parallel with Labour-Power

Marx then raises the analysis to the level of the theory of value. In the case of labour-power, the use-value is precisely that it creates value, and more value than it contains. Here the same is claimed for money: "the use-value of money is that of creating value, a greater value than is contained in itself." Money as such is treated as "potentially self-valorizing value," and it is as such that it is lent. This constitutes "the form of sale for this particular commodity" — money-capital. The parallel is deliberate and damning: what was in the case of labour-power an anthropological-social fact of the human metabolism with nature (living labour is the source of value) is transposed onto money, and the transposition is entirely false. From this false use-value Marx extracts the famous botanical metaphor: value-creating capacity becomes a property of money as naturally as bearing pears is a property of a pear tree. This is the "hidden quality" (qualitas occulta) of the scholastics — a term Marx reintroduces later when attacking Price — i.e., a mysterious power attributed to a thing without any real causal basis.

The Further Distortion: Interest as Original, Profit as Accessory

The fetish completes itself in a second inversion. In actuality, interest is simply a part of profit, itself one part of the surplus-value extorted from the worker by the functioning capitalist. But in the M–M′ form, the inversion of the parts is total: interest appears as "the specific fruit of capital, the original thing," while profit — now renamed profit of enterprise — appears as "a mere accessory and trimming added in the reproduction process." This is the specifically ideological effect of interest-bearing capital: not just that profit looks like it comes from capital, but that the merely reflexive species of revenue (interest, earned by lending) is taken to be the primary one, and the laborious, socially entangled species (profit of enterprise, earned by running production) is treated as derivative. Marx labels this the point at which "the fetish character of capital and the representation of this capital fetish is now complete." He then gives his most dialectical formulation of the whole chapter: in M–M′ we have "the irrational form of capital… the misrepresentation and objectification of the relations of production, in its highest power." The interest-bearing form is "the simple form of capital, in which it is taken as logically anterior to its own reproduction process." The adjective anterior is critical: the fetish doesn't merely invert the sequence — it precedes the process that supposedly produces it, turning the effect into the cause.

The Payoff for Vulgar Economics

This is where the ideological function becomes explicit. For vulgar economics — the apologetic branch of political economy that treats capital as an independent source of wealth — "this form is of course a godsend." It is a form in which "the source of profit is no longer recognizable" and in which "the result of the capitalist production process — separate from the process itself — obtains an autonomous existence." Vulgar economics finds in M–M′ a theory of capital that requires no labour, no exploitation, no social relation; only a thing and a rate. Marx will hammer this point home with historical examples ranging from sixteenth-century sermons to The Economist of 1851.

Money Capital as a Commodity with a Fixed Price

A short but important aside clarifies why money capital specifically is the seat of the pure fetish. Marx writes: "It is only in money capital that capital becomes a commodity, whose self-valorizing quality has a fixed price as expressed in the prevailing rate of interest." Other forms of interest-bearing capital (he says) are derived from this form and presuppose it — money capital is therefore the immediate and canonical embodiment of the fetish. The self-valorizing capacity of capital becomes a price, the interest rate. This is the exact point at which capital becomes fully reified: its dynamic social power now has a quotable market price, discoverable in the financial pages, adjustable by supply and demand, and thus apparently as natural and thing-like as the price of a barrel of oil.

Why Money Capital is the Pure Fetish Form: Two Structural Reasons

Marx then gives a rigorous structural diagnosis of why money capital is uniquely suited to hide the real relations. Two reasons:

First, money is the form in which "all capital's determinations are dissolved and its real elements are invisible." Money obliterates the use-value distinctions among commodities, and therefore also obliterates the distinctions between different industrial capitals and the material conditions of their production. Money is "the form in which value — and here capital — exists as autonomous exchange-value." In the reproduction process of capital, the money form is "an evanescent moment, a moment of mere transition" — you get money, but you immediately convert it into constant and variable capital. But on the money market, capital "always exists in this form." The evanescent moment is frozen into permanence. This is a decisive dialectical insight: it is not that money is inherently a better vehicle for capital (money is only one phase of the circuit), but that the money market abstracts one phase of the circuit and treats it as if it were the whole. The fetish is thus an artifact of a particular sphere of appearance — the market — whose conditions of visibility suppress the conditions of production.

Second, the surplus-value that capital creates — itself in the form of money — "appears to accrue to it as such." Marx gives the famous botanical-linguistic pun: like the growth of trees, the generation of money (tokos, the Greek word for interest, literally "what has been born") appears to be a property of capital in its money form. The etymology itself betrays the fetish: interest is called a "birth" precisely because the money-capital is imagined to have given birth to its own increase. Marx will repeat this etymological point later in the Luther discussion.

The Movement of Capital Abbreviated: The Wine-in-the-Cellar Analogy

Marx then condenses the argument into one of his most famous images: in interest-bearing capital the movement of capital is abbreviated. The mediating process is omitted, "and a capital of 1,000 is characterized as a thing that in itself is 1,000 and in a certain period is transformed into 1,100, just as wine in the cellar improves its use-value after a given period of time." The analogy is precisely chosen to be false in a revealing way: wine does improve with time via a real physical-chemical process independent of social relations; capital does not grow with time via a real natural process (or, at least, not in the way the fetish claims). The wine analogy is exactly the kind of reasoning that the fetish invites, and its absurdity is its own refutation. "Capital is now a thing, but the thing is capital. The money's body is now by love possessed." The Goethe quotation (Faust, Part I, Auerbach's Cellar) reinforces the mystical-erotic cast of the fetish: money is not just said to breed, it is possessed — as if by a demonic love.

Marx presses the point: interest accrues "no matter whether it is asleep or awake, at home or abroad, by day and by night." It has become an autonomous self-validating force — the realization of "the hoarder's most fervent wish," i.e., the dream of value that grows without labour, risk, or social entanglement. This is the mythological-ideological core of interest: it fully realizes the abstract greed of hoarding while pretending to have overcome hoarding's futility (the hoarder's stagnant treasure). Interest, in the fetishized form, is the perfect unholy marriage of avarice and growth.

Luther's Naïve Polemic Against Usury: The First Historical Figure

Marx now shifts into a historical-ideological register, beginning with Martin Luther in An die Pfarrherrn wider den Wucher zu predigen (Wittenberg, 1540). The passage is long and worth unpacking, because Marx uses it to expose exactly what the fetish looks like to a pre-capitalist moralist. Luther grants that interest may be demanded if the failure to repay causes the lender real damages — a missed payment or a missed profitable bargain (Luther's example: the missed purchase of a garden). Luther labels these duplex interesse, damni emergentis et lucri cessantis — a double damage, both the loss caused and the profit missed — a scholastic distinction Marx reproduces in Latin to mark the legal-theological form of justification. But Luther then attacks the generalization of this justification: "they rush in and charge double on each 100 guilders … just as if the 100 guilders had had these two losses grown on to it naturally [Marx's emphasis]." Here Luther has intuitively hit the fetish precisely: he is indignant at the idea that losses can be treated as grown-on the money, like a natural property. Someone is charging "for a supposed loss which in fact no one caused you, and which you can neither prove nor reckon." Luther names this non verum sed phantasticum interesse — "not actual damages, but imagined damages."

Marx highlights Luther's formulation ex contingente necessarium — "making something out of a thing that is not, and making what is uncertain into something completely sure." This is exactly the operation of the capital fetish: it transforms contingent social outcomes into necessary natural properties of money. Luther's peroration — "usury of this kind would devour the whole world in a few years" and his sarcasm against men who scheme "to be lazy and idle and live in luxury on the labour of other people, without care, danger or loss," who would "sit by my stove and let my 100 guilders gather wealth for me in the country, and yet keep it in my pocket" — is included because it captures, in pre-capitalist moral-theological language, exactly the hoarder's fantasy into which mature interest-bearing capital has transformed itself. Marx's point is not that Luther is right in the confessional sense (he explicitly calls him "naïve"), but that the usury polemic is the transparent moral articulation of what the fetish-classical political economy will later disguise as a natural law of capital. Where Luther denounces, vulgar economics celebrates.

Dr Price and the Fantasy of Compound Interest: The Second Historical Figure

The central polemical episode of the chapter is Marx's attack on Dr Richard Price (1723–1791), whose writings on the national debt (An Appeal to the Public on the Subject of the National Debt, London, 1772, and Observations on Reversionary Payments, London, 1772) became the theoretical foundation of British government fiscal policy under William Pitt the Younger. Price's conception of capital is precisely the M–M′ fetish in its purest, most speculative-ideological form: "value that reproduces itself and increases in reproduction, by virtue of its innate property as ever persisting and growing value." Marx identifies Price's assumption of an "innate property" with the scholastics' hidden quality (qualitas occulta) — i.e., with pre-scientific magic dressed in the language of natural philosophy. Price's "amazing fancies" are said to "leave far behind the fantasies of the alchemists." Alchemy at least tried to transmute metals through material processes; Price claims that money grows by an innate spiritual-in-financial force with no process at all. Marx's contempt is measured in that verb: Price's alchemy exceeds the alchemists'.

The famous Price quotations are quoted by Marx at length. In the Appeal, Price writes: "Money bearing compound interest increases at first slowly. But, the rate of increase being continually accelerated, it becomes in some time so rapid, as to mock all the powers of the imagination. One penny, put out at our Saviour's birth to 5 per cent compound interest, would, before this time, have increased to a greater sum, than would be contained in a hundred and fifty millions of earths, all solid gold. But if put out to simple interest, it would, in the same time, have amounted to no more than seven shillings and four pence half-penny." In the Observations, Price flies "still higher": a shilling at 6% compound interest from the birth of Christ "would have increased to a greater sum than the whole solar system could hold, supposing it a sphere equal in diameter to the diameter of Saturn's orbit." Price's punchline is fiscal: "A state need never therefore be under any difficulties; for with the smallest savings it may in as little time as its interest can require pay off the largest debts" (pp. xiii, xiv). Marx's own conclusion is savage and laconic: "What a charming theoretical introduction for the English national debt!"

The Mathematical Refutation: Price's Formula and Its Hidden Preconditions

Marx now states the formula that underlies Price's mystification. Price "viewed capital as a self-acting automaton, without regard to the conditions of reproduction and labour, as a mere number that increases by itself." Hence the formula:

s = c(1 + i)ⁿ

where: - s = the sum of capital + compound interest (the final amount), - c = the capital advanced (the initial sum), - i = the rate of interest, expressed in aliquot parts of 100 (e.g., 0.05 for 5%), - n = the number of years for which the process is continued.

The formula itself is trivially correct as arithmetic — the issue is its use as a theory of capital. Price's error is not mathematical; it is conceptual: he treats the interest rate as if it were independent of, and unaffected by, the processes of production and reproduction that alone can generate the surplus out of which interest is paid. He presupposes a rate of profit that never falls, an accumulation process that never encounters its real limits, and a reproduction of capital that costs nothing. He is dazzled, Marx says, "by the incredible figures that arise from geometric progression," and forgets that interest is a deduction from profit, so that a 5% interest rate presupposes at least a profit rate of 5% — and in fact, for regular payment, considerably more.

Marx now delivers the two fundamental real-economic limits to the Price fantasy, both drawn from the earlier argument of Volume 3:

Limit 1 — The devaluation of existing capital through rising social productivity. Value is determined not by the labour-time originally taken by production, but "by the labour-time that their reproduction takes, and this steadily decreases as the social productivity of labour develops." Hence a substantial part of existing capital is constantly being devalued during the reproduction process. As productivity rises, the accumulated stock of capital does not present itself as the patient growth of simple accumulation, but "as the result of a relatively short reproduction period." The longer the horizon, the less sense Price's geometric expansion makes — because the very substance on which the compounding is supposed to operate is being continually eroded by the cheapening of its reproduction. (Footnote 81 points to Mill, Carey, and Roscher, whom Marx will criticize further; a second footnote comments on Mill as the "fountainhead of economic theory for the British Labour movement," distinguishing Mill's attempt to harmonize capital and proletariat from pure vulgar apologetics.)

Limit 2 — The falling rate of profit. As shown in Part Three of Volume 3, the profit rate declines in proportion to growing accumulation and the accompanying rise in the productivity of social labour, expressed in the relative decrease of variable capital vis-à-vis constant capital. (Variable capital is the portion laid out on labour-power, the source of surplus-value; constant capital is machinery, raw materials, etc., which transmits but does not create value.) If one worker sets in motion ten times as much constant capital, then surplus labour-time would have to grow tenfold to keep the profit rate the same — but this is manifestly impossible: "very soon the total labour-time, or even the full twenty-four hours of the day, would not be sufficient, even if it were entirely appropriated by capital." Price's progression, therefore, "depends on the idea that the rate of profit does not decline, as does every iota of this 'all-engrossing capital at compound interest.'" Note the dialectic: the same accumulation that appears to Price as self-expanding wealth generates the fall in the rate of profit that destroys his dream.

Pitt's Sinking Fund: The Fiscal Enactment of the Fetish

Marx then documents how this mystification became government policy. In 1786 the House of Commons resolved to raise £1,000,000 for public purposes. Pitt, "believing" Price, thought nothing better than to tax the people in order to "accumulate" the sum raised and thereby "spirit away" the national debt "by the mystery of compound interest." The bill (Act 26 George III, Chapter XXXI) provided for accumulation of £250,000 annually "until, with the expired annuities, the fund should have grown to £4,000,000 annually." In a 1792 speech, Pitt named "accumulation" as "the most widespread and enduring cause" of England's commercial supremacy — appealing to Adam Smith. Marx's cutting conclusion: "By way of Dr Price, therefore, Pitt transformed Adam Smith's theory of accumulation into the enrichment of a nation by accumulation of debts, and thus arrived at the comforting progress towards an infinity of loans — loans to pay loans with." This is the crucial practical implication of the theoretical fetish: a state can delude itself into thinking it can grow out of debt by compounding the debt itself, because the interest-bearing-capital illusion suggests that money grows by being loaned, not by being productively invested. Adam Smith's sane theory of accumulation — laying aside a portion of profit to increase the principal productively — is inverted into a policy of state debt enrichment, where the "accumulation" is merely financial and the surplus needed to pay it comes from taxation of the real economy.

Josiah Child and The Economist: The Fetish in Bourgeois Common Sense

Two more historical citations round out the argument. Josiah Child (1630–1699) — "the father of modern banking" — is quoted as claiming that "£100 put out at 10 per cent for seventy years, at compound interest would produce £102,400" (from Traité sur le commerce, written 1669, French edition 1754). This is historically important as a pre-Price example of the same fetish, showing it to be a continuous ideological phenomenon, not a personal idiosyncrasy of Price's.

More significantly, Marx cites The Economist (19 July 1851): "Capital, with compound interest on every portion of capital saved, is so all-engrossing that all the wealth in the world from which income is derived, has long ago become the interest of capital… All rent is now the payment of interest on capital previously invested in the land." Marx's comment is scathing: this shows how "Dr Price's conception has unwittingly been taken over by modern economics." Where Price was naïve and mystical, The Economist propagates the same fetish as sober commercial fact. In doing so, it reaches the logical end: all past and future surplus labour is already the property of capital by right; everything else is a mere first instalment toward capital's "all-engrossing" appetite. Marx's vocabulary here is intentionally mythic — "Moloch" — the ancient deity to whom children were sacrificed. This is one of the most economically-pregnant and ideologically-loaded passages in Volume 3: the fetish of interest-bearing capital, once generalized, becomes a claim to universal and legally-justified ownership of all future labour. This is the theoretical matrix of later critiques of financialization and of "rentier" capital.

Adam Müller and the "Romantic" Inversion: The Fetish in Poetic-Mystical Form

Marx then turns to Adam Müller (Elemente der Staatskunst, quoted as "op. cit., III, pp. 147-9"), a German Romantic conservative whom Marx treats with open contempt. Müller's passage purports to critique Price — but actually reproduces the fetish in poetic dressing. Müller argues that Price's "tremendous growth of compound interest" presupposes "an undivided or uninterrupted and uniform arrangement for several centuries," that once capital is broken up among branches "the entire accumulation of forces begins afresh," that the average worker's "career" is 20-25 years ("Nature has distributed over a career of some twenty to twenty-five years the progression of force that is the average lot of each individual worker (!)"), after which the worker "must now transfer the capital obtained through compound interest on labour to a new worker, in most cases dividing it among several workers or children," and so on. Marx's verdict is ferocious: "It would be impossible to drivel out a more hair-raising absurdity than this in so few lines." He identifies the fatal confusions: (a) the confusion of worker with capitalist — Müller pretends that the laborer, not the owner of capital, is the recipient of compound interest; (b) the confusion of the value of labour-power with interest on capital — the wage is treated as if it were interest accruing to the labourer's "capital" (his labouring capacity), and the labourer is treated as a capitalist who hires himself out. This is one step further into apologetics than even Price: it not only presents capital as self-valorizing, it presents the wage relation itself as a capital relation, so that exploitation vanishes entirely.

Marx diagnoses Müller's method in a passage that is centrally important for the chapter's theoretical stakes: "Its content is formed out of everyday prejudices, skimmed from the most superficial appearance of things. This false and trivial content is then supposedly 'elevated' and rendered poetic by a mystifying mode of expression." This is Marx's general theory of Romantic anti-capitalism: the Romantics are the literary counterparts of the vulgar economists. They sense something is wrong, but because they only grasp appearances — and grasp them with poetic flourish rather than analytic rigor — their critique reinforces the fetish it purports to attack. The "Romantic" here is a species of ideology-critique that cannot possibly be scientific, because its content is drawn from the very surface it ought to question.

Marx's Concession and the Two Real Limits to the Compound-Interest Analogy

Now Marx makes a concession that is itself instructive. He writes: "The accumulation process of capital may be conceived as an accumulation of compound interest, in so far as the part of profit (surplus-value) that is transformed back into capital — i.e., which serves to absorb new labour — may be called interest." Here Marx grants the analogy a strictly limited validity: if by "interest" we mean the reinvested portion of surplus-value, then capital accumulation does bear a formal resemblance to compound interest. This dialectical generosity sharpens the subsequent refutation, because Marx then gives two substantive grounds on which even this limited analogy collapses when it is taken as a law of growth:

Ground 1 — Devaluation of existing capital. Already stated above in the Price discussion, but formalized here as a first limit: because value is determined by reproduction labour-time and not by originally expended labour-time, and because productivity steadily rises, a large part of existing capital is permanently being devalued. Under high productivity, "all existing capital, instead of appearing as the result of a long process of capital accumulation, appears as the result of a relatively short reproduction period." In other words, the accumulated stock is not the patient product of centuries of compounding; it is the cheap reproduction of a currently existing productive apparatus, whose cost is systematically driven down by technical progress. A compound-interest model misses this entirely.

Ground 2 — Falling rate of profit. Marx here restates the argument of Part Three as a direct mathematical refutation of Price's formula: as accumulation grows and the productivity of labour rises, constant capital grows relative to variable capital, and the profit rate therefore falls. Consequently, to keep the profit rate constant, the surplus labour-time must rise in proportion to the constant capital each worker sets in motion — which is physically impossible once we hit the twenty-four-hour day. Since surplus-value is nothing other than surplus labour-time, it is subject to a qualitative limit: the length of the working day, the number of workers, the current development of the productive forces, the available population. Price's formula treats this limit as merely quantitative — as if by definition capital could always grow further, given time. This passage sets up Marx's sharpest formulation of the contrast:

"The identity of surplus-value and surplus labour sets a qualitative limit to the accumulation of capital… But if surplus-value is conceived in the irrational form of interest, the limit is only quantitative, and beggars all fantasy."

The philosophical significance is enormous. Interest — as a distinct category of revenue pretending to natural growth — is precisely that representation of surplus-value in which the qualitative limit (the finite human working day; the finite number of working days that can be simultaneously exploited) is suppressed, so that the mind is free to imagine unlimited quantitative growth. Marx's final salvo on this point cites Hodgskin (footnote 82, from Labour Defended Against the Claims of Capital, p. 23): "It is clear, that no labour, no productive power, no ingenuity, and no art, can answer the overwhelming demands of compound interest. But all saving is made from the revenue of the capitalist, so that actually these demands are constantly made and as constantly the productive power of labour refuses to satisfy them. A sort of balance is, therefore, constantly struck." Hodgskin's formulation is important because it makes the argument in the register of class struggle: the demands of compound interest are unlimited, and the productive power of labour is limited, and the class relation is the mechanism by which the gap is continuously reconciled — through devaluation, crisis, and intensified exploitation.

The Consummated Fetish and the Materialist Denouement

Marx closes the chapter with a summary statement that both restates the fetish and dissolves it. Interest-bearing capital "displays the conception of the capital fetish in its consummate form": the idea that the accumulated product of labour, "in the fixed form of money at that," has "the power of producing surplus-value in geometric progression by way of an inherent secret quality, as a pure automaton." This is the form in which The Economist believes that the world's wealth has "long since discounted" itself to capital, "as belonging to it by right." Here the fetish becomes a claim of right. The accumulated dead labour is now imaginatively pregnant "with a portion of present or future living surplus labour."

Marx's dialectical riposte is a single, dense sentence that dissolves the fetish: "We know however that in actual fact the preservation and thus also the reproduction of the value of products of past labour is only the result of their contact with living labour; and secondly, that the command that the products of past labour exercise over living surplus labour lasts only as long as the capital relation, the specific social relation in which past labour confronts living labour as independent and superior."

Two doctrines are packed into this: 1. Dead labour's value is preserved only through contact with living labour. Constant capital and money are not self-valorizing; their value would decay or become worthless were it not continually re-appropriated in the living production process. The pear tree's "hidden quality" is exposed as the reified expression of a labor process. 2. The domination of past labour over living labour is not natural but historical. It rests on "the capital relation, the specific social relation" — which is contingent, fought-over, and therefore revocable. The fetish's "geometric progression" is only the ideologically frozen form of a relation of class domination.

The chapter thus concludes in the mode of the Critique: it has shown that the most "natural" and "obvious" of all capital-forms is in fact the most socially produced and the most reversible. The appearance of capital as a self-breeding thing is the exact measure of the distance between capitalist production as it presents itself and capitalist production as it is.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

1. The fetish is structural, not optical. The chapter's deepest lesson is that the M–M′ fetish is not a mere error of perception, nor a quirk of vulgar apologists, but a necessary product of the money-market form of capital. Because interest-bearing capital always exists in the money form — and because in the money form all real differences between commodities, capitals, and production conditions have been dissolved — the appearance of self-valorizing money arises of itself from the surface of the credit system. This is a genuinely dialectical insight: the fetish is not a mistake to be corrected by education alone; it must be explained, traced back to the specific abstraction (the freezing of the money phase) that generates it. This anticipates and grounds later theories of financial fetishism, money illusion, and the ideological power of "capital markets."

2. The inversion of cause and effect is the core of ideology. The pivotal formulation — capital appears "as logically anterior to its own reproduction process" — identifies what is philosophically at stake. The effect (surplus-value) is treated as the cause (a property of money). This is the same structure Marx identified in commodity fetishism (a social relation appearing as a natural property of a thing), but raised to a higher power because it is now value itself being fetishized. The chapter demonstrates that ideology is not merely false consciousness; it is inverted causality, and to break its spell you must reinstate the process the appearance has suppressed: production, hire of labour-power, extraction of surplus labour.

3. Compound interest as a limit-illusion. Marx's refutation of Price, Pitt, Child, and The Economist carries an astonishingly modern payload. The critique of a state that believes it can "grow out of" debt by compounding it; the critique of a rentier class whose claims rest on the fantasy of infinite geometric growth; the critique of a market discourse that treats the entire world's wealth as always-already the property of capital — these are structurally the arguments any serious political economy must deploy against speculative finance. The key insight is not that compound interest is bad because it is exploitative; it is that it is impossible apart from the two real constraints: the devaluation of existing capital through rising productivity and the falling rate of profit. These two limits, derived rigorously from the theory of value and the theory of accumulation developed earlier in Volume 3, are the pivot of Marx's rejection of the infinite-growth view of capital.

4. The qualitative limit as the deepest critique. Perhaps the single most important philosophical move in the chapter is the claim that surplus-value is surplus labour, and that this identity imposes a qualitative, not merely quantitative, limit on capital's expansion. Interest's misrepresentation consists precisely of converting that qualitative limit into a mere quantitative one. This opens the door to two of Marx's signature conclusions for later readers: (a) capital's tendency to overreach its own material basis (the working day, the number of exploitable workers) is what generates crises and the permanent "balance" imposed by class struggle; (b) the whole ideological mythology of infinite wealth rests on the abstraction from labour-time that the interest form performs. The identity surplus-value = surplus labour is therefore one of Marx's most powerful tools, both analytically and ideologically.

5. The critique of Romanticism as a general method. The treatment of Müller is not merely comic relief. Marx identifies a genuine epistemic trap: content "made out of everyday prejudices, skimmed from the most superficial appearance of things," then "elevated and rendered poetic by a mystifying mode of expression." This is the signature of a whole genre — not only nineteenth-century German Romantics, but the "poetic" nostalgia for pre-financial capital, social-credit mystifications, and the later aestheticization of crisis. Marx's method warns against allowing moral outrage at speculation to substitute for rigorous analysis of the production process; indeed, Müller's Romanticism reinforces the fetish by transferring the "fertility" of capital to the labourer-as-owner-of-labour-power.

6. Broader implications. Bankability of the argument: the chapter founds a critique of interest as socially generated but privately appropriated, and it prefigures the theory of fictitious capital and the credit system developed in later chapters. Practically, it shows that any project of emancipation must undo not only the material but the ideological apparatus of interest-bearing capital: the appearance of money that breeds money is what enables the whole edifice to command "the products of past labour over living surplus labour." Theoretically, the chapter ties together the fetishism of the commodity, the theory of surplus-value, the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, and the criticism of apologetic economics into a single, unified critical programme. Finally, philosophically, it demonstrates the central Marxist dialectical principle: the appearance that is most taken for granted — a money that simply "grows" — is precisely the appearance most thoroughly produced by a specific, historically contingent, and therefore changeable social relation. As Marx puts it in the closing sentence: capital's command lasts "only as long as the capital relation." That "only as long as" is the entire political stake of the chapter, and of the critique of political economy as a whole.
