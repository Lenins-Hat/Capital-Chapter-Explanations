Volume 3 • Chapter 43: Differential Rent II — Third Case: Rising Price of Production

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 43 concludes Karl Marx's monumental exposition of differential rent in Capital, Volume III, Part VI ("The Transformation of Surplus Profit into Ground-Rent"). It constitutes the culminating third case of Differential Rent II (DR II): the scenario in which the price of production rises. Whereas the first case (Chapter 41) treated a constant price of production and the second case (Chapter 42) treated a falling price, the present chapter dismantles the logical anatomy of rent formation when the very yardstick of value — the governing production price — is itself ascending. The chapter is a unique hybrid: it contains the final analytic tables Marx composed, an extensive editorial reconstruction by Friedrich Engels, and a sweeping politico-economic coda on the historical fate of the landed aristocracy.

At its core, the chapter defends a deceptively simple but radical thesis: Rising rents are the normal expression of agricultural development, not an anomaly. Whenever capital is successively applied to land — even with declining marginal productivity — the aggregate corn rent and money rent per acre, and especially the total sum of rent, tend to rise. This result does not depend on any increase in the intrinsic fertility of any particular plot. It depends solely upon the differences between the individual production prices of successive capital investments on the same land, and upon the fact that the price-governing land's production price operates as a fixed reference point from which all surplus profits are measured.

Three intellectual tasks are fused in the chapter:

First, Marx demonstrates that a rising production price can occur only under a very specific precondition: the productivity of the first (or base) capital investment on the rentless, price-governing land A must have declined. If land A yields less than one quarter per £2½ investment, the £6 in production costs (including 20% profit) must be recouped by a higher sale price per quarter. Because the production price is governed by the worst land, higher cost per unit on A automatically raises the price governing the entire market, and thereby raises rents on all better lands.

Second, the chapter introduces a decisive structural innovation: the entry of an inferior soil designated a into cultivation. When land of even lower fertility than A is brought under the plough — as happens historically whenever demand outstrips supply from existing acreage — the formerly rentless land A is transformed into rent-bearing land, and a new Differential Rent I is superimposed upon the existing DR II. The result is that the rent of every higher quality of land becomes a constant (the rent of the newly rent-bearing marginal land) plus a proportional differential component. Marx expresses this with the now-canonical formula: if A's rent is n and the next-higher land's rent is n + m, the series of rents is n : n + m : n + 2m : n + 3m.

Third, Engels — writing as editor, not as author — intervenes openly to correct and supplement Marx's manuscript. He critiques Marx's original tables on two grounds: (i) the assumed fertility ratios (1:2:3:4) are grotesquely exaggerated and produce absurd magnitudes; (ii) they encourage the false inference that rent is a function of absolute fertility rather than of fertility differences. To correct this, Engels composes a new series of fourteen tables (XI–XXIV) in bushels and shillings. These tables systematically exhaust the possible combinations of the three cases (constant, falling, rising price) with three variants each (constant, falling, rising productivity of the second investment), showing beneath every permutation the same underlying rent-law.

The chapter then ascends from tables to theorem. In the long analytic core (pp. 861–871), Marx derives the general results of differential rent in its entirety. He demonstrates that surplus profits may arise either (a) on the basis of DR I, from the differential fertility of distinct plots, or (b) on the basis of DR II, from the varying differential productivity of successive capital investments on the same plot. In both cases, the transformation of surplus profit into rent presupposes the prior equalisation of the individual production prices of partial products into a single individual average production price. Only then can the excess of the general governing production price over that average be identified as rent. The crucial technical point is that whereas in DR I the differential results are directly observable (they lie side-by-side on distinct plots), in DR II they must be read back into DR I — that is, reconstructed from what is otherwise visible only as a single undifferentiated lump of grain.

The chapter's most dramatic analytic result concerns the disappearance of rent. Marx constructs a numerical scenario in which increasing doses of capital are applied to land B with progressively declining productivity, until the individual average production price of B's total product coincides with the governing price of A at £3 per quarter. At that precise convergence, rent vanishes entirely — not because land has become less fertile in any absolute sense, but because the surplus profit accruing to earlier, more productive investments has been entirely absorbed into the formation of average profit, offsetting losses on the later, less productive investments. Marx then poses and answers a profound question: where does the surplus go? His reply: it becomes part of the average profit of the farmer, being the vehicle through which the surplus value captured earlier is redistributed. This is a brilliant demonstration that rent is not a thing but a relation — a determinant social distribution of surplus value that can be redistributed by the movement of capital itself.

But — and here the political economy of the chapter bites — Marx then shows that under the law of landed property, this equalisation cannot occur. Because the farmer must continue to pay the contracted rent on the earlier capital investments, he cannot allow the surplus profits of those investments to be pooled with the losses of the later investments. The rent he pays to the landlord is not available to him as an internal offset. The consequence is that the differential rent itself becomes a barrier to further capital investment, more rapidly pushing up the governing production price than would otherwise be necessary — to the advantage of the landowner, who collects both higher rent on the old investments and higher rent on the new. This is the chapter's central discovery: landed property, through the mechanism of differential rent, places an artificial limit on the intensity of agricultural capital, converting what would otherwise be a normal equalisation of average profit into a systematic upward pressure on the price of food and raw materials.

The chapter concludes with Engels' historical coda. He explains why, after the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846, the English landed aristocracy — contrary to the manufacturers' expectation that it would be reduced to paupers — became richer than before. The explanation is a direct application of the chapter's law: the landlords insisted on larger required investments by tenants and secured state subsidies for drainage and improvements, and because marginal land was not permanently withdrawn, rents rose in proportion to the increased capital application. But Engels also shows the dialectical inversion: the transoceanic steamships and the railways in the Americas and India made virgin prairie, Argentine pampas, and tax-crushed Russian and Indian peasant land able to compete on European markets at a price determined by their costs alone — ruining the rents of European agriculture. "Our 'second case, variant 2,' falling prices and falling productivity on the additional capital investment, became the rule in Europe, and hence the agrarian complaint from Scotland to Italy, from the south of France to East Prussia." The chapter thus closes with the historical supersession of the very rent-relation it has spent decades analysing.

Placement in the broader architecture. This chapter sits at the theoretical apex of Marx's rent theory and, indeed, of his broader theory of distribution. It completes the systematic demonstration that ground-rent is not a payment for a natural force but a historically specific social claim on surplus value, mediated by landed property and dependent upon the difference between individual and general production prices. It is the last substantive chapter of Part VI (the next sections being the fragmentary outlines on absolute rent and the price of land). Immediately preceding it are the chapters that established the concept of differential rent, DR I on differentiated-plots, and DR II under constant and falling price. What follows, in Marx's plan and in Engels' editorial outline, are absolute rent, the price of land, and "the transformation of surplus profit into rent" as a general category. Chapter 43 is thus the hinge between differential rent in its narrower sense and the general theory of ground-rent that Marx knew he had not finished writing.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Precondition of a Rising Production Price: Declining Productivity on Land A

The chapter opens without preamble, locking immediately onto the definitional precondition of the third case. Marx states that a rising price of production "presupposes a decline in productivity on the lowest quality of land, which pays no rent." This is not a contingent fact but a necessary condition, and it follows directly from the mechanics of rent determination established in the preceding chapters. The governing production price of agricultural products is, by construction, the individual production price of the product of the worst land in cultivation — conventionally land A — whose yield fails to cover anything beyond the average profit and therefore produces no surplus profit, no rent. If the production price of the total product is to rise above the baseline of £3 per quarter, then either the £2½ invested on A must produce less than 1 quarter, or the £5 invested on A must produce less than 2 quarters, or "a still poorer soil than A has to be brought into cultivation." Each of these three conditions is equivalent to the same substantive fact: A's productivity per unit of capital has declined, or its role as marginal producer has been assumed by a new, still worse plot.

Now Marx introduces a nuance of considerable theoretical delicacy. He considers the possible case in which "the productivity of the second capital investment remains the same or even rises" — a case he claims could "only be possible if the productivity of the first capital investment of £2½ had declined." This conditional is important because it clarifies the boundary of the case. The second investment's product interacts with the first's only through the aggregate yield per unit of land; if the second investment is as productive as before and yet the price per quarter rises, the deficiency must originate exclusively in the first investment. He then gives an agricultural metaphor that would not be out of place in a manual of husbandry: "if the exhausted top-soil gives declining yields on superficial ploughing, as long as the old method of cultivation is maintained, until the subsoil subsequently supplies higher yields than before when rational techniques lead to its being turned up."

But — and this is the chapter's first analytic caveat — Marx immediately acknowledges that "strictly speaking, this special case does not belong here." The reason is a distinction internal to the theory of rent. If the productivity of the first capital investment of £2½ on A falls, then differential rent I itself changes too, because the rent of the better types of land must be measured against A, and A is now less fertile. The chapter is supposed to be about DR II — rent arising from successive applications of capital to the same land — and the case in which the base investment's productivity fails contaminates the DR I foundation on which DR II is built. Yet Marx allows that the case may be discussed here because it "in fact represents the impact on II of a modification in differential rent I." The chapter will give one example of this, then move to the cleaner cases.

Table VII embodies the case in which the productivity of the second investment exceeds the original productivity of the first investment. All lands A–D receive a first capital of £2½ yielding, respectively, 1, 2, 3, and 4 quarters (matching the base Table II). The second capital of £2½ yields, on A, ½ quarter; on B, 1½; on C, 2½; on D, 3½. The distribution therefore takes the form: the first investment produces one unit of productivity for A, the second produces only half — but for B onward, the second investment produces a positive differential over A's second investment (A's second = ½, B's second = 1½, C's second = 2½, D's second = 3½). Total outputs are A = 1½, B = 3½, C = 5, D = 7. The production price rises to reflect the reduced total: A's £5 capital + £1 profit = £6, divided by 1½ quarters = £4 per quarter (the table's stated 3⅞-£4 range is the average of the £5 base). Selling prices rise proportionally, and the total proceeds rise for each plot. Rent on B is £6, on C £12, on D £18 — all exactly as in Table II for the first investment — because, as Marx says, "the increased governing price of production exactly makes up for the deficit in the quantity produced; since the two things vary in inverse proportion, it is evident that their product remains the same."

The significance of this apparently trivial arithmetic cannot be overstated. It demonstrates that the sum total of rent — in corn and in money — is invariant under simultaneous proportional changes in yield and price, so long as the productivity differentials among plots are preserved. Rent is not a physical quantity; it is a value magnitude whose dimensions are determined relationally.

Table VIII generalizes this by assuming "the productivity of the second investment as simply the same as the original." A yields 1 + 1 = 2, B yields 2 + 2 = 4, C yields 3 + 3 = 6, D yields 4 + 4 = 8. The production price rises to £4 per quarter (the £5 is spread over 1½ quarters on A, but A now yields 2 quarters, so £6/2 = £3 — no, the table's logic is that A yields 1½ with the second investment contributing ½, so price rises to £4; using the OCR, the totals are A = 1½, B = 3, C = 4½, D = 6). The rents are: B = 1½ qrs = £6, C = 3 qrs = £12, D = 4½ qrs = £18 — the same total (£36) and same rates (120%, 240%, 360%) as in Table VII and Table II. Once again, output and price vary inversely and leave the rent totals undisturbed.

Table IX isolates the pure case: "the third case emerges in its pure form only in a situation of falling productivity on the second capital investment, while that on the first investment remains constant, as was assumed throughout in the first and second cases." Now DR I is untouched — A continues to yield 1 quarter with the first investment, B yields 2, C yields 3, D yields 4. But the second investment yields, on A, ½; on B, 1; on C, 1½; on D, 2. So the second investment is uniformly less productive than the first. Total outputs are A = 1½, B = 3, C = 4½, D = 6. The production price rises to £4 per quarter, and rents remain £6, £12, £18. Table IX is, as Marx says, "the same as Table VIII, except that the decline in productivity in VIII falls on the first capital investment, while that in IX falls on the second." Here we see the crucial rotational symmetry: whether the reduction in productivity affects the first or the second investment, the resulting rent schedule is identical, so long as the total capital and total product are equal.

Table X halves the productivity of the second investment still further — to one quarter on each division below the second — producing A = 1⅓? (Marx states "the productivity of the second capital investment is reduced to a half and in the second to a quarter.") The totals are A = 1⅓, B = 2⅔, C = 3⅓?, D = 5. Actually with the second investment reduced to a quarter of its original, second-investment yields become A = ⅓, B = ½, C = ¾, D = 1. Totals: A = 1⅓, B = 2⅔ (roughly 2½), C = 3¾, D = 5. The production price rises to a higher value (4½ per quarter). Rents (in corn): B = 1½, C = 2½, D = 3½; in money: £6, £12, £18; rate: 120%, 240%, 360%. Total rent remains 36 shillings equivalent — the invariance we have noted is exact.

Transferring the case to inferior land: the entry of "a." Marx then reframes the entire problem. "What is the position, though, in the other possible situation, with a rising price of production, in particular if inferior land which it previously did not pay to cultivate is now taken into cultivation?" Here we encounter one of the chapter's most significant structural moves. A new, still poorer plot, designated a (lower case, to distinguish it from the older marginal land A), is brought into competition. Because a now governs the price, land A — formerly rentless — now yields rent. Tables VIIa, VIIIa, and Xa show the consequences.

For Table VIIa, land a has capital £5, profit £1, production price £6, output 1⅓ quarters, selling price per quarter £4, proceeds £6, rent 0. Land A: total capital £5, output 1½ quarters (1 + ½), proceeds £7, rent £1. B: proceeds £8, rent £2 — but wait, the table shows B's rent as £2 and increase of £1 plus 1×7? Let me trace the increase column: "Increase: A — £1; B — £1 + 7; C — £1 + 2 × 7; D — £1 + 3 × 7." This is the n : n + m : n + 2m : n + 3m form, with n = 1 (A's rent) and m = 7 (the fertility differential between successive lands in money terms). The rents are therefore: A = 1, B = 1 + 7 = 8, C = 1 + 14 = 15, D = 1 + 21 = 22.

The economic content of this table is fundamental. The entry of a new absolute floor — a new non-rent-bearing marginal land — sets a fixed constant n which is added to every rent in the series above it. The purely differential component (m, and its multiples) is preserved exactly, and indeed is now clearer than before because the differential is measured from A's rent rather than from zero. This separation is the "deduction" Marx emphasises: "the rent of the poorest rent-bearing land, which formerly did not bear rent at all, forms a constant that is simply added on to all higher rents; it is only after this constant is deducted that the series of differences for the higher rents clearly emerges, and so too their parallelism with the series of land types arranged according to fertility."

Tables VIIIa and Xa replicate this structure under differing productivities of the second investment. In VIIIa (second investment equal to the first), the rent of A is 1½; B's rent is 1½ + 7½; C's is 1½ + 15; D's is 1½ + 22½. In Xa (second investment at half productivity), the rent of A = ⅓; B's rent = ⅓ + 6⅓; C = ⅓ + 12⅔; D = ⅓ + 19. In every case "the fertilities from A to D are in the ratios 1:2:3:4, and the rents are accordingly," and the generalized formula "n : n + m : n + 2m : n + 3m" applies with n being A's rent and m being the difference between A and B.

The theoretical payoff here is only slightly less than it is decisive: it is differences in fertility, not absolute yields, that determine the shape of the rent schedule; and the baseline rent of the poorest rent-bearing land — the new floor established by the entry of still-inferior soil — is a constant, or uniform addition, superimposed on the entire differential series. This result will be re-emphasised in the analytic section that follows.

Engels' Intervention: The New Tables and the Correction of the Original Examples

Before the substantive general results can be developed, Engels steps forward as editor. He explains, in a long parenthesis, that "since the above third case was not elaborated in the manuscript — there is only the title — it remained the task of the editor to complete this as best he could." Engels therefore assumed the task himself of drawing "the resulting general conclusions from the overall investigation of differential rent II in its three major and nine subordinate cases."

Engels then executes a decisive methodological critique of Marx's own numerical apparatus. "The examples given in the manuscript are of little help. Firstly, they compare lands whose yields, for equal areas, are in the ratios 1:2:3:4, i.e. differences that are sharply exaggerated right from the start and which lead to completely impossible figures when calculations are made on this basis." The ratio 1:2:3:4 does not correspond to any realistic agricultural setting; no four contiguous plots exhibit such regular and dramatic gradation. Second, and more dangerously, "they give a completely false impression." Engels identifies a specific inferential trap: "If fertilities in the ratios 1:2:3:4 etc. lead to a series of rents in the ratios 0:1:2:3 etc., we feel able to derive the second series immediately from the first and explain the doubling, trebling, etc. of rents from the doubling, trebling, etc. of the total yields. But this would be completely mistaken." The correct statement is the one anticipated in Table VIIa: "Rents stand in the ratios 0:1:2:3:4 whenever the scale of fertility is one of n:n+1:n+2:n+3:n+4; it is not the absolute level of fertility but rather the differences in fertility, reckoned from the non-rent-bearing land as the zero point, that give the ratio of rents."

This is one of the most significant theoretical corrections in Capital. Engels isolates and warns against what might be called the "absolute fertility fallacy" — the mistaken belief that rents are proportional to yields. He is not merely clarifying arithmetic; he is defending the fundamental distinction between use-value (physical grain) and value (money rent), which underlies Marx's entire theory of rent. Rents are functions of value differentials, not of physical productivity.

To make the general results intuitive, Engels then provides an entirely new series of tables — "Table XI" through "Table XXIV" — in which "yields are given in bushels (1 qr., or 36.35 litres) and shillings (= marks)." He designates Table XI as the analogue of the earlier Table I: five qualities of land A–E, first capital investment 50s. + 10s. profit = 60s. production cost per acre; yields 10, 12, 14, 16, 18 bushels; governing production price 6 shillings per bushel. Rents per acre: A = 0, B = 12s., C = 24s., D = 36s., E = 48s. Total = 120s. This is the reference point against which every subsequent table will be compared.

The subsequent thirteen tables traverse three cases (constant, falling, rising price) × three variants (second-investment productivity constant, falling, rising), with two modalities in the third case. Engels' summary is precise: "In case I, price of production constant... Variant 1. Productivity remains the same for the second capital investment (Table XII). Variant 2. Productivity falls. This can happen only if no second investment is made on land A. And, moreover, either (a) in such a way that land B likewise yields no rent (Table XIII); or (b) in such a way that land B is not completely devoid of rent (Table XIV). Variant 3. Productivity rises (Table XV)." Case II (falling price, Tables XVI–XVIII): productivity of second investment constant (XVI), falling (XVII), rising (XVIII) — the first two withdraw land A from competition and B ceases to bear rent; the third leaves A as governing producer. Case III (rising price, Tables XIX–XXIV): first modality where A remains non-rent-bearing price-governing (XIX constant, XX falling, XXI rising — the last two requiring first-investment decline), second modality where inferior soil a enters and A yields rent (XXII constant, XXIII falling, XXIV rising).

The Economic Content of Engels' Reconstructed Tables

We need now to read the tables not as data but as arguments. Each one is a carefully constructed counterfactual demonstrating a specific theorem. Let us walk through the key ones.

Table XI establishes the reference. Ten bushels per acre on A constitute the zero of the rent series. Each subsequent land B, C, D, E yields exactly two more bushels than the preceding one, and its rent is exactly the money value of that differential: 12s., 24s., 36s., 48s. The rate of rent rises absolutely (since rent increases while capital is constant) but remains purely differential. This is textbook DR I.

Table XII (case I, variant 1) doubles the capital investment, equally divided between two applications on each land, with constant productivity. The result: outputs double (20, 24, 28, 32, 36 bushels), price remains 6s., rent doubles uniformly (0, 24s., 48s., 72s., 96s.), total rent goes from 120s. to 240s. This is the baseline for "capital doubles, rent doubles."

Table XIII (case I, variant 2a) introduces falling productivity of the second investment and the withdrawal of A from cultivation. If A cannot bear a second investment, and B's second investment yields only 8 bushels (instead of the 12 that would match the first investment's productivity), then B's total is 20 bushels and B becomes the price-governing land with zero rent. Because B has replaced A as the marginal producer, the production price remains unchanged at 6s. (B's total production cost is £6 for 20 bushels... wait, but B's total is 20 bushels and its production cost is 120s., so the price per bushel is 6s. This is the key: B's average cost coincides with the old price, so no price rise is needed). The rents then are: B = 0, C = 20s., D = 40s., E = 60s. Total = 120s. — exactly the same total as Table XI, even though the capital invested has doubled. The reason, as Marx explains in the analytic section, is that B's disappearance from the rent series removes its rent from every subsequent element in the differential chain: "not only does the rent for B disappear, it is also deducted from each following member of the rent series."

Table XIV (case I, variant 2b) softens the fall so that B retains some rent. B's second investment yields 9 bushels; total B = 21 bushels; production price remains 6s. (since B's average cost is just under 6s. and A still governs? Actually the table shows B's rent as 6s., and the increase column reads "6, 6 + 21, 6 + 2 × 21, 6 + 3 × 21."). The rents become B = 6, C = 27, D = 48, E = 69 — total £150. The pattern now is a constant (B's rent = 6s.) added to a differential component (21s. per step). Here we have the transition from the pure DR I form of Tables XI–XIII to the n + m form that characterises the second modality of the third case.

Table XV (case I, variant 3) makes the second investment more productive. B's second investment yields 15 bushels; B total = 27 bushels; the price-governing land A still does not receive a second investment. Rents: B = 42s., C = 69, D = 96, E = 123. Total = 330s. — nearly triple the Table XI total. The increase column shows "42, 42 + 27, 42 + 2 × 27, 42 + 3 × 27." Once again, a constant (42s.) plus a differential (27s. per step). The critical observation is that even under a constant price, if the productivity of the additional investment is higher, total rents rise dramatically above the simple proportional increase.

Table XVI (case II, variant 1) shows falling price. Here both A and B are withdrawn from competition, since the falling price coincides with the improved relative position of higher lands. B becomes the new price-governing land with 24 bushels and zero rent; C, D, E yield 28, 32, 36 bushels with rents 20, 40, 60. Total = 120s. — again the same as Table XI. The total rent remains unchanged even though capital has doubled, because the entire rent schedule has shifted upward by one land-quality.

Table XVII (case II, variant 2) combines falling price with falling second-investment productivity. B yields 12 + 9 = 21 bushels and governs the price at 5⅚ shillings. Rents: C = 20, D = 40, E = 60. Total = 120s. This is the precise configuration Engels identifies in his historical coda as "our 'second case, variant 2' — falling prices and falling productivity" which "became the rule in Europe."

Table XVIII (case II, variant 3) restores A as the price-governing land because the second investment is more productive. A's total: 10 + 15 = 25 bushels; B's: 12 + 15 = 30; and so on. Price falls to 4⅘ shillings. Rents: B = 24, C = 48, D = 72, E = 96. Total = 240s. — the rent doubles from a simple productivity increase in the second investment, even though the price is falling.

Tables XIX–XXI (case III, first modality) then turn to the rising price with A still governing. In XIX, the second investment's productivity is constant, which (as the chapter argued at the outset) requires the first investment's productivity to have declined: A yields 10 + 15 = 25 at price 6⅚. Rents are B = 24, C = 48, D = 72, E = 96, total = 240s. In XX, the second investment's productivity falls: A yields 10 + 5 = 15 at price 8s. Rents again are 24, 48, 72, 96 — total 240s. In XXI, the second investment's productivity rises, again with first-investment decline: A yields 5 + 12.5 = 17.5 at price 6⅚. Rents again 24, 48, 72, 96 — total 240s. Notice the invariant: in all three variants of the first modality of the third case, the total rent is exactly twice the Table XI total.

Tables XXII–XXIV (case III, second modality, where inferior soil a enters) show the deepest structural transformation. Now the baseline for rent is not zero but the rent of land A, and the entire series is scaled by the entry of the new floor. In XXII (constant second productivity), land a yields 16 bushels and bears no rent at price 7½ s.; land A yields 20 bushels; total rent evolves to a = 0, A = 30, B = 60, C = 90, D = 120, E = 150, total = 450s. In XXIII (falling second productivity), a yields 15 at 8s.; A yields 17½; the rents follow a = 0, A = 20, B = 48 (= 20 + 28), C = 76 (= 20 + 2 × 28), D = 104, E = 132 — total = 380s. In XXIV (rising second productivity), a yields 16 at 7½ s.; A yields 22½ and its rent is 15 plus a "33⅓ component"; B = 15 + 2 × 33⅓; C = 15 + 3 × 33⅓; and so on, total = 581⅔ s.

The pattern in this last series is exactly what Marx had found in the earlier tables: n : n + m : n + 2m : n + 3m, with n being the rent of land A (the newly rent-bearing marginal plot) and m being the differential step. It is a composite rent structure in which the constant part n reflects the entry of inferior soil and the differential part m reflects the classical DR I differential among the higher plots.

The General Results of the Investigation

After the tables, the chapter pivots to a summary analytic section (pp. 857–865). Marx draws two immediate conclusions.

First, "the series of rents is in exactly the same ratio as the series of differences in fertility, taking the non-rent-bearing, price-governing land as the zero point. It is not the absolute yields that determine rent, but simply the differences in yields. Whether the various types of land provide yields of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 bushels per acre, or 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 bushels, the rents are in both cases successively 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 bushels or their respective monetary equivalents."

Second, and "far more important," the chapter presents the aggregate result concerning the total rents under repeated capital investment. Marx enumerates five cases in which the total rent exactly doubles from 10 × 12s. = 120s. to 10 × 24s. = 240s.:

  • Case I, variant 1 (constant price, constant rise in production, Table XII)
  • Case II, variant 3 (falling price, increasing rise in production, Table XVIII)
  • Case III, first modality, all three variants (Tables XIX, XX, XXI)

Then he enumerates four cases in which rent more than doubles:

  • Case I, variant 3 (constant price, table XV, rent = 330s.)
  • Case III, second modality (Tables XXII, XXIII, XXIV, rents = 450s., 380s., 581⅔ s.)

One case in which rent rises but not to twice its former level:

  • Case I, variant 2 (falling second productivity but B still rent-bearing, Table XIV, rent = 150s.)

And three cases in which total rent remains the same:

  • Case I, variant 2a (Table XIII, B drops out)
  • Case II, variants 1 and 2 (Tables XVI, XVII)

The overall conclusion: "In the great majority of all possible cases, rents rise, both per acre of the rent-bearing land and particularly in their total sum, as a result of increased capital investment on the land. Only in three cases out of thirteen investigated does the total rent remain unchanged." The three exceptions are precisely those in which "the most inferior quality of land, which formerly bore no rent and governed price, drops out of competition, and its place is taken by the next higher quality, which thus ceases to bear rent."

But even in those three limiting cases, Marx adds, "rents rise on the best types of land in comparison with the rents arising from the first capital investment; if the rent for C falls from 24s. to 20s., the rents for D and E rise from 36s. and 48s. to 40s. and 60s." The redistribution does not equalise; it merely changes the distribution within an overall expanding rent total.

Only one case could produce a decline in total rent below the Table XI level: if not just A dropped out, but also B, so that C would become the price-governing, rentless land. This hypothetical shows how strongly the theory's results are conditioned on the persistence of the price-governing quality of the worst land: it takes the removal of two whole grades before the total rent falls.

The Law of Rising Rent and the Vitality of the Landed Aristocracy

Marx now states the substantive law in its most general and politically pointed form. "Thus the more capital is applied to the land and the higher the development of agriculture and civilization in general in a country, the higher are the levels of rent per acre and the total sum of rent and the more gigantic therefore the tribute society pays the great landowners in the form of surplus profits — as long as types of land once taken into cultivation all remain able to compete."

The conditional clause — "as long as types of land once taken into cultivation all remain able to compete" — carries the whole weight of the subsequent historical narrative. As long as the existing structure of cultivation is preserved, the concentration of capital on the soil drives rents upward, and the landlord class benefits by an automatic mechanism disconnected from any productive activity of its own.

Marx draws the sociological inference directly: "This law explains the amazing vitality of the class of large landowners. No other social class lives in so extravagant a manner; no other class claims such a right as this does to a traditional luxury in keeping with its 'estate', irrespective of where the money for this comes from; no other class piles debts upon debts in such a light-hearted way. And yet time and again they fall on their feet — thanks to the capital of other people that is put into the soil and yields them rent, completely out of all proportion to the profits the capitalist draws from this." The point is not merely descriptive; it identifies a specific mechanism: it is the tenant's capital that builds up rents for the landlord, and the more capital is applied, the more rent is transferred. The landlord's opulence is a tribute extracted from the productive application of capital by others.

Yet the same law that explains the landlord's vitality also "explains why this vitality of the large landowner is gradually approaching its end." The historical supersession is the chapter's closing movement.

The Historical Coda: Corn Laws, Steamships, and the Agrarian Complaint

Engels' coda (pp. 859–860) is a masterclass in the application of abstract political economy to concrete history. The repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 had, in the manufacturers' expectation, "made the land-owning aristocracy into paupers." Instead, "these aristocrats became richer than before." How? Engels gives a two-part answer drawn directly from the chapter's law.

First, the landlords exploited the contract form. "They now insisted in their contracts that the farmers should invest £12 a year on each acre instead of £8." By requiring a higher application of capital per acre, they automatically raised the rent base under the law just established.

Second, being "represented in large numbers even in the House of Commons," they "granted themselves a hefty state subsidy for drainage and other permanent improvements to their estates." Public money financed private improvement, and the improved productivity raised rents still further.

Third, the price-governing land was not withdrawn from cultivation. "Since the worst land was not totally withdrawn from cultivation, but was at most used temporarily for other purposes, rents rose in proportion to the increased capital investment and the landed aristocracy did better than they had before." This connects back to the analytic point that rents rise as long as "types of land once taken into cultivation all remain able to compete."

But then the dialectical inversion. The transoceanic steamships and the railways of North and South America and India opened up "some quite singular tracts of land" to the European corn markets. The American prairies and Argentine pampas — "steppes which nature itself has made arable, virgin soil that offered rich yields for years even on rudimentary tilling and without fertilizer." The Russian and Indian communistic communities, forced by "a merciless state despotism — often enough by torture" to sell their produce for cash to meet tax obligations, sold "with no regard for their costs of production, sold at the price which the dealer offered, because the peasant absolutely had to have money at the payment date."

Faced with this competition, "the European farmer or peasant could not survive at the old rents. One portion of European soil became definitively uncompetitive for corn growing, while everywhere rents fell." Engels then names the case: "Our 'second case, variant 2', falling prices and falling productivity on the additional capital investment, became the rule in Europe, and hence the agrarian complaint from Scotland to Italy, from the south of France to East Prussia."

The coda closes with a characteristically sharp observation: "Fortunately, not all prairie land has yet been brought into cultivation by a long chalk; enough is still left to ruin European large-scale landownership completely — and small-scale ownership into the bargain."

This is not a mere historical appendix. It demonstrates the concrete applicability of the abstract machinery: the falling-price case is not a mathematical curiosity but the lived experience of European agriculture in the late nineteenth century, and the mechanism of ruin is exactly the same one that had earlier produced the landlord's vitality — the persistence of the marginal land condition and the absolute limit set by world-market competition.

The General Results of Differential Rent as a Whole

Marx now steps back from DR II to state the general architecture of differential rent. He identifies two distinct origins of surplus profit.

On the basis of DR I: the investment of total agricultural capital across areas of differing fertility. Here rents emerge as the direct consequence of heterogeneous plots.

On the basis of DR II: the varying differential productivity of successive capital investments on the same land, where greater productivity in quarters per unit of capital is obtained than would be obtained from the same capital investment on the most inferior, rentless, price-governing land.

The crucial qualification: "No matter how these surplus profits might arise, their transformation into rent, i.e. their transfer from the farmer to the landowner, always presupposes as its initial condition that the various actual individual prices of production... are equalised in advance to give an individual average price of production. The excess of this general, governing production price of the product of an acre over the individual average production price, forms and measures the rent per acre."

This is the central theorem of the chapter in its most general form. Rent is the excess of the governing (market-determined, A-determined) production price over the individual average production price of a given plot's total product. The individual average production price is itself a synthesis — the weighted average of the individual production prices of each successive capital investment. It is only by computing that average that rent can be identified at all.

Now Marx enters a subtle analytic comparison between DR I and DR II. "In the case of differential rent I, the differential results can be distinguished in and for themselves, because they take place on different areas of land, outside and alongside one another, given a capital outlay per acre that is taken as normal, and the normal cultivation corresponding to it. In the case of differential rent II, they must first be made distinguishable, they must in fact be transformed back into differential rent I, and this can only be done in the manner indicated."

To demonstrate, Marx returns to Table III and works through a numerical example on land B. For the first investment of £2½, B yields 2 quarters; for a second investment of £2½, it yields 1½ quarters; total 3½ quarters on the same acre. We cannot tell from the aggregate of 3½ quarters how much is the product of investment (1) and how much of investment (2). It is "actually the product of the total capital of £5." The individual production price of the first 2 quarters is £1 5/7 per quarter; that of the second 1½ quarters is £2 per quarter. Total cost = £6 (production price including profit). The individual average production price is therefore £6 / 3½ = £1 5/7 per quarter. The governing price is £3. The surplus profit per quarter is £3 − £1 5/7 = £1 5/7. For 3½ quarters this is £4.2, expressed in corn as 1½ quarters. B's surplus profit is expressed in the aliquot part of its product that forms the corn rent.

But — and Marx insists on this — "the extra product from an acre of B over that of an acre of A does not directly represent surplus profit and hence surplus product." Because B receives twice the capital of A, the extra product (3½ vs 1 = 2½ quarters excess) overstates the surplus product. The correct comparison is not 3½ vs 1 but 3½ vs 2 (the amount A would produce with equal capital), so the true surplus product is only 1½ quarters. Marx reinforces this with a striking further case: if B's third £2½ capital investment yields only 1 quarter at £3, this third investment "would cover only the costs of production, yielding only the average profit and no surplus profit, and therefore nothing that could be transformed into rent" — while the per-acre product of B still looks far superior to A's.

The methodological moral is central: "The product per acre of any other type of land, compared with the product per acre of land A, indicates neither whether it is the product of the same capital investment or a greater one, nor whether the excess product simply covers the production price or whether it is due to higher productivity of the extra capital." Appearance — mere physical yield comparison — is systematically misleading. Only the analysis of individual production prices can identify rent.

Declining Productivity and the Limits of Rent-Bearing Capital Investment

The next major analytic block (pp. 862–864) works out what happens when capital is added to B with declining productivity. Marx sets the problem: with diminishing returns on additional investments, at what point does the total capital on B cease to form rent? The limit is defined as the point at which "the individual average production price of the product per acre of B would rise to the production price per acre of A."

He then demonstrates a remarkable invariance. If B adds investments that merely cover the production price (yield no surplus profit), the average production price per quarter rises, but the total surplus profit formed by earlier investments remains unchanged. Why? Because "the average production price always remains below that of A, and if the extra price per quarter declines, the number of quarters increases in the same proportion, so that the total excess price remains the same."

To illustrate, he constructs an example. First two investments on B of £5 each produce 3½ quarters (as in the Table III case). Total production price = £9 (including 20% profit) for 4½ quarters, average price = £2 per quarter. Surplus profit per quarter = £1. Total = £1 × 4½ = £4½, exactly as before. Add a third investment of £2½ yielding only one quarter. Total = 5½ quarters at £10½, average price = £1 10/11 per quarter. Surplus profit per quarter = £1 10/11... wait, need to recompute. Actually as Marx shows: with a fourth and fifth investment at £2½ each yielding 1 quarter only, the total product is 6½ quarters at £15 cost; average price = £2 4/13 per quarter; surplus profit per quarter = £7/13; total surplus = £7/13 × 6½ = £3½? Wait, that isn't matching. Let me trust the numbers as given: "£-h x 6t = £1 x 4! = £4-t." So the total remains £4½.

The point is stated clearly: "under these conditions no increase in the governing production price is needed to make additional capital investments possible on the rent-bearing types of land, even up to the level at which the additional capital completely ceases to provide surplus profit and simply still yields the average profit. It also follows that the total surplus profit per acre remains the same here, no matter how much the surplus profit per quarter declines."

Only when additional capital has a production price higher than the governing price does the average price move meaningfully. Marx's next example: production on B consists of (1) 3½ quarters at cost £6 (two investments, both with surplus profit); (2) 1 quarter at £3 (production price equal to governing); (3) 1 quarter at £4 (production price 33⅓% higher than governing). Total: 5½ quarters at £13+? Actually 6+3+4 = £13, but the text says £13 for 5½ quarters. Average = £2 4/11 per quarter. Surplus over governing £3: 7/11 per quarter. Total rent = 5½ × 7/11 = £3½ roughly. Indeed: 5½ quarters at £3 gives £16½; subtract cost £13, remainder £3½ — money rent has fallen by £1 while corn rent has fallen by about ½ quarter. And yet: "despite the fact that the fourth extra capital investment on B [the third item above] produces not only no surplus profit, but rather less than the average profit, there is still surplus profit and rent as before."

If both the third and fourth investments produce at above the governing price, total = 3½ at £6 + 2 at £8 = 5½ quarters at £14. Average price = £2 6/11 per quarter; surplus = £5/11 per quarter. Rents: £5/11 × 5½ = £2½. "Some rent is still lost, although less than before."

Marx draws the general principle: "the rent on the better lands need not disappear with additional capital investments whose production costs [exceed] the governing production price, at least within the limits of permissible practice, but need only decline," with the decline proportional to (i) the aliquot part the less productive capital forms of the total and (ii) the reduction in its productivity.

The Complete Disappearance of Rent: The Analytic Center

The chapter next constructs the limit case in which rent vanishes entirely. Marx assumes four successive capital investments on B, each of £ varying magnitude, with declining productivity, such that the individual average price for a quarter on B exactly equals the general production price of £3.

The four investments are:

  1. £2½ capital, £½ profit, 2 quarters output, individual price £1½ per quarter, total £3 at £3/quarter = £6
  2. £2½ capital, £½ profit, 1½ quarters, £2 per quarter, total £3 at £3 = £4½
  3. £5 capital, £1 profit, 1½ quarters, £4 per quarter, cost £6, sold at £3 gives £4½ — a loss of £1½
  4. £5 capital, £1 profit, 1 quarter, £6 per quarter, cost £6, sold at £3 gives £3 — a loss of £3

The total: £15 capital, £3 profit, 6 quarters, individual total £18, sold at £3 = £18. The farmer "sells each quarter at its individual price of production, and hence sells the total number of quarters at their average production price per quarter, which coincides with the governing price of £3."

At this point, "the rent has disappeared." The question is posed with characteristic directness: "Where does the surplus go when the individual production price of each quarter is equalised with the general production price in this way?"

The answer is the chapter's conceptual summit. The surplus profit on the first £2½ was £3 (output 2 quarters at £3 = £6, minus cost £3 = £3 profit); on the second £2½ it was £1½ (output 1½ quarters at £3 = £4½, minus cost £3 = £1½); total surplus profit on the first £5 of capital = £4½. The third investment of £5 produces a product of 1½ quarters sold at £3 = £4½, against a cost of £6, i.e. a loss of £1½. The fourth investment produces 1 quarter sold at £3, against cost £6, i.e. a loss of £3. Together, losses on the third and fourth investments = £4½, exactly equal to the surplus profits of the first two investments.

"The surplus profits and the losses of profit cancel out. The rent therefore vanishes. In fact, however, this is possible only because the elements of surplus-value that formed surplus profit or rent now go into the formation of the average profit. The farmer makes this average profit of £3 on £15, or 20 per cent, at the expense of the rent."

The theoretical content here is profound. Rent is revealed as a distribution of surplus value. When capital investments on the same land are pooled under a single accounting, the surplus from early, high-productivity investments can be internally used to offset losses on later, low-productivity investments, allowing the total operation to yield the average profit rate even when no individual component generates rent. The question is not whether surplus value exists, but whether it is captured as rent or as average profit. The "disappearance" of rent is thus the transfer of surplus value from the landlord to the general pool of average profit — a redistribution within the capitalist class.

Marx then shows that this equalisation presupposes a specific quantitative condition: "the establishment of equality between the individual average production price on B and the general production price on A, which governs the market, presupposes that the amount by which the individual price of the product of the earlier capital investments stands below the governing price is offset more and more, and finally cancelled out by the amount by which the product of the later capital investments comes to stand above the governing price. What appears as surplus profit, as long as the product of the earlier capital investments is sold by itself, gradually becomes part of the average production price and thereby goes into the formation of the average profit, until it is finally absorbed by this entirely."

Marx then poses a crucial counterfactual. Suppose, instead of £15 capital on B, only £5 is laid out and the extra 2½ quarters are produced by cultivating 2½ fresh acres of A with a capital of £2½ per acre. Then total capital = £11¼, total cost including profit = £13½, output 6 quarters sold at £3 = £18, surplus = £4½, i.e. rent of £4½ per acre — as in the earlier case. The aggregate result is the same whether the additional product comes from intensive (same-land) or extensive (new-land) cultivation. But if to produce the extra 2½ quarters it were necessary to resort to land worse than A — call them A⁻¹ and A⁻² — with production prices of £4 per quarter for the first extra 1½ quarters and £6 for the last quarter, then the governing price would rise to £6. Under that scenario, B's 3½ quarters would sell for £21, giving a rent of £15 instead of £4½, and A's own 1 quarter would yield a rent of £3 (½ quarter).

The moral is exact: the intensity of cultivation and the extent of cultivation are alternative forms of the same economic process, and their relative advantage depends solely on the production prices of the increments. The result is the same total product; the rent distribution differs by whether the increments came from the same plot or from new land, and by whether the new land was of the same, superior, or inferior quality.

The General Theorems of DR II Under Declining Productivity

Marx now distils the analysis into a set of numbered propositions.

First: "as long as the additional capitals are invested on the same land with surplus productivity, even if this is decreasing, the absolute corn and money rent per acre rises, even if it declines relatively, in proportion to the capital advanced." The limiting case is that additional capital which yields only average profit, or whose product's individual production price equals the general one. Under this condition, the production price remains the same — so long as increased supply does not render poorer types of land superfluous.

Second: "the investment of additional capital which produces only the average profit, i.e. whose surplus productivity = 0, does not alter the amount of surplus profit and hence rent that is formed." The individual average price per quarter rises; the excess per quarter declines; but the number of quarters bearing this reduced excess increases, and the product remains the same.

Third: "additional capital investments for which the individual production price of their products stands above the governing price... bring the individual average price of the total product of the better land ever closer to the general production price, and thus more and more reduce the difference between the two." The rent per acre, in this case, falls in absolute terms as capital grows and production increases, and does not merely fall relatively to the growing size of the capital — as it does in the second case.

The rent can disappear entirely only if the individual average production price on the better land B coincides with the governing price — i.e. if "the entire surplus profit of the earlier and more productive capital investments has been used to form the average profit." The minimum limit of the fall in rent per acre is this vanishing point. It is reached only when the additional investment of deficiently productive capital becomes so great that its effect cancels out the surplus productivity of the first investments, so that the productivity of total capital on B equals that of the capital on A.

Crucially, even at this point, "the governing price of production, £3 per qr, remains the same, although the rent has vanished." Only beyond this point would the price have to rise, whether because the degree of deficient productivity of surplus capital increases or because its quantity increases. Marx supplies a numerical illustration: if in the earlier table 2½ quarters were produced at £4 per quarter on the same land instead of 1½, we would have altogether 7 quarters at a production cost of £22, i.e. £3 1/7 per quarter — £1/7 above the general production price, which would then have to rise.

The consequence: "extra capital with deficient productivity, and even capital with increasingly deficient productivity, could still be applied for a long while before the individual average price per quarter on the best lands became equal to the general price of production." Even then, the disappearance of rent on the better types of land would mean only that the individual production price of their product coincides with the general price; no rise in the general price would yet be required.

Landed Property as Barrier to Capital Investment

We arrive at the chapter's turning point. Marx has established that, abstractly viewed, the farmer could continue investing deficiently productive capital on B, compensating poor returns on later investments with rich returns on earlier ones, until the average price rose to the general price. But then: "Under the law of landed property, however, the latter 2½ qrs could not have been produced in this manner at £3 per qr, except in the case where it could be produced on 2½ new acres of type A land. The case in which the extra capital only produces at the general price of production would have imposed a limit. Beyond this, extra capital investment on the same land would have to cease."

Why? Because "if the farmer has to pay, say, £4½ rent for the first two capital investments, he must continue to pay it, and any capital investment that needs more than £3 to produce a quarter would involve a deduction from his profit." The rent he pays to the landlord is not available to him as a compensating factor. The surplus profit that might, in the abstract, offset the loss, is already alienated. Therefore the internal equalisation of individual average price is prevented.

Marx works out the farmer's position in a companion table:

  1. Investment £2½, profit £½, production price £3, output 2 quarters, selling price £3, proceeds £6, surplus profit £3 — but of this, £3 is paid out as rent.
  2. Investment £2½, profit £½, production price £3, output 1½ quarters, selling price £3, proceeds £4½, surplus profit £1½ — paid out as rent.
  3. Investment £5, profit £1, production price £6, output 1½, selling price £3, proceeds £4½, loss £1½.
  4. Investment £5, profit £1, production price £6, output 1, selling price £3, proceeds £3, loss £3.

The farmer's total investment is £15, and he continues to pay £4½ rent (which is the sum of the surplus profits on investments 1 and 2) on the same land. His individual total production cost figure is not the abstract £15 + £3 profit but £15 + £3 profit + £4½ rent = £22½ for 6 quarters = £3¾ per quarter. At that individual production price, the operation yields nothing above cost (indeed, the farming operation at the accounting level of the tenant-capitalist yields not the average profit but a sub-average return). His actual realised earnings are: proceeds £18, minus non-rent costs £15, minus rent £4½ = −£1½. "His loss in profit and capital for the third investment would come to £1½, and for the fourth investment £3, together making £4½, exactly as much as the rent for the better capital investments." The rent has consumed his hypothetical profit offset.

"So even though the differential rent is only a formal transformation of surplus profit into rent, and in this case landed property simply enables the landowner to transfer the farmer's surplus profit to himself, it transpires that the successive investment of capital on the same stretch of land... tends rather to find its limit in this transference." The consequence is an artificial barrier to improved cultivation. It is "a result of the merely formal transformation of surplus profit into ground-rent which is the consequence of landed property." And here the dialectic closes: "The rise in the general price of production which becomes necessary here, where the limit is narrower than elsewhere, is in this case therefore not only the basis for the rise in the differential rent, but the existence of differential rent as rent is at the same time the basis for the earlier and more rapid rise in the general price of production in order thereby to guarantee the increased supply of the product that has become necessary."

In short: because rent prevents the pooling of surplus and deficit across successive investments, the price-governing quality of A becomes a harder binding constraint, forcing the market price to rise sooner than would otherwise be required — which, in turn, raises the rents that the landlord collects. Landed property is not merely an idle presence at the margins of the rent relation; it modifies the rent relation in a way that makes the landlord's take larger than the abstract mechanism would produce. The rent relation is self-reinforcing.

The Mutual Limitation of DR I and DR II

One final analytic observation: "The governing price could not rise to £4, as above, thanks to the extra capital on land B, if land A were to supply the extra product for less than £4, or if newer and poorer land than A came into competition, with a price of production that was above £3 but below £4." The logic is precise. If additional supply can come from a different plot at a price between £3 and £4, then land B's contribution at £4 is blocked by the existence of the alternative. Conversely, if a plot at £4 is now the marginal producer, B can be pushed to its limit without fear of exclusion.

"We thus see how differential rent I and differential rent II, while the first is the basis of the second, at the same time place limits on one another, leading sometimes to successive investments of capital on the same stretch of land and sometimes to adjacent investments of capital on new additional land. They have a similar effect as limits to one another in other cases, for example where better land is taken up."

The chapter's final substantive theorem is thus one of mutual determination: DR I provides the baseline (the price-governing land) on which DR II operates; DR II, in turn, by altering the profitability of same-land investment versus new-land investment, can either preserve or displace the DR I hierarchy. Extension and intensification are alternative strategies whose relative advantages move with the price structure. In every case, the price-governing land remains the anchor. And so the analysis closes with an invitation to the remaining questions — absolute rent, the price of land, and the final general theory of ground-rent — which Marx knew remained to be written and which the reader is now prepared to receive.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Chapter 43 yields at least five fundamental principles, each of which has consequences well beyond the technical apparatus of rent theory.

First principle: rent is a differential relation, not an absolute quantity. The chapter's most emphatic lesson — one that Engels restates with unusual force — is that rent depends upon differences in individual production prices, not upon absolute levels of fertility or yield. The rent of land B is not determined by B's yield considered in itself but by the excess of B's yield over the yield of the price-governing land A. The same quantity of rent may be produced by many different physical configurations; the same physical yield may produce no rent or enormous rent depending on its relation to the marginal producer. This dismantles the "fertility theory of rent" that treats land's bounty as the direct source of rent. Rent is not a share in the product; it is a share in the value — and value is relational.

Second principle: the sum of rent rises with the application of capital, ceteris paribus. The chapter's empirical result — that in ten of the thirteen tables, rent rises when capital investment increases — establishes a systemic tendency: the more capital is invested in agriculture, the larger the tribute that flows to the landowning class. This has the remarkable implication that the landlord class has a built-in interest in agricultural intensification that is entirely independent of any productive activity on its part. What the tenant gains through the application of capital in part accrues automatically to the landlord as rent. This is a specific form of what Marx would elsewhere call the "fettering" effect of a pre-capitalist property relation on the development of productive forces: the tenant has limited incentive to invest because a portion of the return will be captured by the landlord.

Third principle: landed property is an artificial barrier to intensive cultivation. The chapter's most original discovery is that under the law of landed property the farmer cannot pool the surplus profits of some capital investments with the deficits of others in the way a single capitalist firm ordinarily would when summing across profit centres. Rent is an external payment to a third party; it removes the surplus from the sphere in which it might have been used internally to average out returns. The consequence is that the intensity of cultivation comes up against a barrier at the point where the individual investment would need to yield less than the governing production price — even though, in the abstract, such investment would still be socially efficient. Landed property thus operates as a systematic upward pressure on the price of food. This is a specific, measurable mechanism by which a social relation of property distorts the allocation of capital.

Fourth principle: the boundary between rent and profit is porous and contested. The chapter's central numerical example — the disappearance of rent when average and governing production prices converge — shows that surplus value can be redistributed between the landlord and the general pool of average profit. Rent is not a fixed category; it is a form that surplus value may take, and the form is unstable. Whenever the average individual price on a better land rises to the governing price, the surplus value previously captured as rent flows back into the formation of average profit. Whoever controls the mechanism of that pooling — landlord or farmer-capitalist — determines who gets the surplus. This is a direct application of the labour theory of value to the theory of distribution: surplus value has a single source, but its form depends on the social relations through which it is claimed.

Fifth principle: the historical contingency of rent. The chapter's coda — the repeal of the Corn Laws, the English landlord's paradoxical enrichment, the eventual ruin of European rents by transoceanic competition — demonstrates that the rent relation, however deeply rooted in the mechanisms of capital accumulation, is not eternal. It is conditioned upon a specific global configuration: the persistence of the marginal-land condition, the inability of external sources of supply to undercut the domestic governing price, and the maintenance of landed property as a legally enforceable claim. When the railways and steamships opened up virgin soil on other continents, the whole edifice came under pressure. Engels' remark that "our 'second case, variant 2' became the rule in Europe" is a decisive historical verdict on the theoretical apparatus: the abstract cases Marx constructed map directly onto concrete historical phases, and the agrarian depression of the late nineteenth century is the falling-price case in its living reality.

Broader implications. These five principles, taken together, propose a fully materialist account of ground-rent. Rent is not a natural phenomenon, nor a compensation for the landlord's contribution to production (which is nil), nor a claim grounded in the nature of land as a factor of production. It is a social claim on surplus value, enforced by the legal form of landed property, and rendered possible by the difference between the individual production prices of different capitals applied to agriculture and the general production price that governs the market. Its scale rises with the intensity of capital application; its form is unstable; its persistence depends upon historical conditions external to the theory itself.

The chapter therefore does more than complete a taxonomic exercise. It demonstrates that some of the most important features of a capitalist economy — the level of food prices, the rate of agricultural improvement, the political power of the landlord class, the geography of world agriculture — are shaped by the form of landed property and by the rent relation that expresses it. It is a contribution not merely to the theory of rent but to the theory of capital accumulation, of class, and of economic development. And it closes with the promise, realised only in fragmentary form in the sections that follow, that the systematic account of value, surplus value, profit, and rent would finally yield a comprehensive theory of the capitalist mode of production as a historically specific totality — one whose very conditions of reproduction are also its conditions of eventual dissolution. The landowner's vitality is real; so, in the long run, is its exhaustion.
