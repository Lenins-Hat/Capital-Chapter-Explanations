Volume 3 • Chapter 28: Means of Circulation and Credit

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem

This chapter is a sustained theoretical demolition. Marx sets out to demonstrate that the hallowed distinction drawn by the British banking school between "currency" and "capital" — a distinction that the entire practical and polemical apparatus of nineteenth-century English monetary debate turned upon — is conceptually empty. The chapter does not introduce a new theory of money; it applies, with surgical precision, a theory of money Marx had already developed (in Volume 1, Chapter 3, and in A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy) in order to dissolve a confusion that, on Marx's account, had muddled every important question of banking policy, monetary crisis theory, and the practical conduct of the Bank of England.

The immediate intellectual targets are Thomas Tooke (author of the Inquiry into the Currency Principle and the History of Prices), James Wilson (founder and first editor of The Economist, later Financial Secretary to the Treasury), and John Fullarton (author of On the Regulation of Currencies). These were the leading theorists of what we now call the Banking School, as against the Currency School (the "Currency Principle gentlemen" — the intellectual lineage of the 1844 Bank Act). Both schools were obsessed with the relationship between the note issue, the gold reserve, prices, and the balance of payments. Both treated the distinction between money functioning as circulating medium and money functioning as loanable capital as fundamental. Marx's argument is that this entire conceptual apparatus rests on a category error.

The Thesis Stated

Marx's thesis is compact and devastating: There is no distinction between "currency" and "capital" to be found in the functions of money at all. Money always functions as means of purchase or means of payment — as means of circulation in the broad sense — whether it serves to realize revenue or to transfer capital. What the Banking School theorists are actually observing, without understanding it, is the difference between two functions of money (means of purchase vs. means of payment), and between two spheres of the reproduction process in which money acts (revenue expenditure vs. capital transfer). Neither of these distinctions corresponds to a distinction between currency and capital. The confusion arises from three sources: (1) the conflation of functional characteristics, (2) the intrusion of questions about the total quantity of circulating money, and (3) the intrusion of questions about the relative proportions of circulating medium across the two spheres. Beyond this, the confusion is rooted socio-psychologically in the standpoint of the issuing banker, whose own banknotes cost him nothing to produce and whose "currency" therefore appears to him as something other than his "capital."

The chapter consequently performs three tasks simultaneously: it refutes Tooke's and Fullarton's specific doctrines; it demonstrates that the more sophisticated arguments these theorists make (particularly about the drain of gold and the independence of the demand for loans from the demand for circulation) rest on the same foundational error; and it clarifies — as a positive contribution — what actually happens to the circulation of money in prosperity and in crisis.

Intellectual Objective and Placement

The chapter sits within Volume 3, Part Five, "The Division of Profit into Interest and Profit of Enterprise." The whole of Part Five is concerned with interest-bearing capital, credit, and the banking system. By this point in the text, Marx has established that interest is a portion of profit, that money-dealing capital is a specialization of the functions of money, that the credit system centralizes and economizes the use of money, and that the mysteries of the "capital market" (capital appearing to bear fruit automatically as interest) arise from the fetishized forms taken by the social relations of production. Chapter 28 is where Marx brings this analysis directly into conflict with the dominant practical wisdom of the age — the wisdom embedded in the Bank Act of 1844, in parliamentary testimony from Bank of England governors, and in the theoretical treatises of the Banking School.

The chapter is also a case study in Marx's method: the method of dissolving a false conceptual distinction by showing that the phenomena it was meant to explain can be explained on the basis of a smaller and more coherent set of distinctions (means of purchase vs. means of payment; revenue circulation vs. capital circulation; domestic circulation vs. international circulation; the functions of the hoard). The critique of Tooke and Fullarton is not merely negative; it is the vehicle through which Marx sharpens his own account of the money market.

A crucial scholarly nuance: much of the chapter's central positive argument (the three cases of bank advances) was, as a footnote indicates, "incomprehensible in the original manuscript" and was "newly drafted by the editor" — i.e., by Friedrich Engels (marked "F. E.") in his work of preparing Volume 3 for publication. This means the reader is dealing with a text that is partly Marx and partly Marx-reconstructed-by-Engels. The editorial reconstruction is, however, strongly continuous with Chapter 26 (where the same point was "already dealt with in a different context," pp. 557–9), and it articulates points Marx makes elsewhere. It is important to register this not to diminish the chapter but to understand its texture: it is a palimpsestic argument in which the empirical engagement with 1840s parliamentary testimony is threaded through a theoretical reconstruction.

What the Reader Should Take Away

By the end of the chapter, the reader should be able to see that: (a) "currency vs. capital" is a distinction without a difference as far as the functions of money go; (b) the demand for "capital" in a crisis, when unmasked, is usually a demand for money in its function as means of payment — specifically, for the international means of payment (gold) when a drainage is underway, and for banknotes or book credit when it is not; (c) the fact that the note circulation of the Bank of England can decline while it performs enormous monetary accommodation is not evidence that the circulation is unaffected, but rather that the circulation's composition has shifted toward means-of-payment circulation that immediately refluxes and vanishes; (d) the genuine significance of the drain of gold is that what is demanded is money in its world money form — the absolute, metallic form of value — which is why capital in this crisis is not lacking but is present in superabundance in the form of unsaleable commodity capital; and (e) the banking school's "capital" is exposed as a banking-standpoint category (that which the banker must lend out of his own resources rather than out of his credit), not a category of political economy.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Tooke–Wilson Distinction and Its Source Material

The chapter opens by naming its quarry: the distinction between currency and capital "as made by Tooke, Wilson and others," together with the related distinctions between "means of circulation as money" and "means of circulation as money capital in general" — or as the English put it, as "interest-bearing or monied capital" — which, in Marx's sardonic phrasing, are "simply lumped together haphazardly." The chapter's programmatic sentence announces that this whole tangle "comes down to two things."

Before proceeding, Marx reproduces, in footnote 89, the crucial passage from Tooke's Inquiry into the Currency Principle in Tooke's own words (the earlier quotation in the text had been in German translation). Tooke divides the business of bankers into two branches, corresponding to Adam Smith's distinction between transactions among "dealers and dealers" and transactions between "dealers and consumers." The first branch is "to collect capital from those who have not immediate employment for it, and to distribute or transfer it to those who have"; the second is "to receive deposits of the incomes of their customers, and to pay out the amount, as it is wanted for expenditure by the latter in the objects of their consumption." Tooke concludes: the former is "a circulation of capital," the latter "of currency." He elaborates: the first is "the concentration of capital on the one hand and the distribution of it on the other"; the latter is "administering the circulation for local purposes of the district."

This is a definitional move of enormous practical consequence: if "currency" and "capital" name two genuinely distinct things, then policy questions about the one (e.g., note issues) can be separated from questions about the other (e.g., loanable funds). Marx will systematically deny that any such clean separation can be made.

Kinnear's Approach

Marx interposes that "Kinnear comes much closer to the correct conception," quoting from J. G. Kinnear's The Crisis and the Currency (London, 1847). Kinnear's argument runs: money is employed to perform two essentially distinct operations. As a medium of exchange between dealers and dealers, it is "the instrument by which transfers of capital are effected; that is, the exchange of a certain amount of capital in money for an equal amount of capital in commodities." But money employed in the payment of wages and in purchase and sale between dealers and consumers "is not capital, but income" — that portion of the community's incomes devoted to daily expenditure. It circulates in constant daily use, and this alone "can, with strict propriety, be termed currency." Kinnear then adds a distinction that will become the pivot of the chapter: "Advances of capital depend entirely on the will of the Bank and other possessors of capital, for borrowers are always to be found; but the amount of the currency depends on the wants of the community, among whom the money circulates, for the purposes of daily expenditure."

Kinnear is "closer to the correct conception" because he locates the distinction in the character of the incomes and capital being spent and transferred, not in some independent circulation domain. But note carefully: Kinnear still calls the income-side money "currency" and the capital-side "capital." Marx's task in the following pages is to show that even Kinnear's framing is a misdescription — though a much less misleading one than Tooke's — because the same physical money does both, and because "currency" and "capital" are not the right names for the two functions.

Footnote 89 also introduces James Wilson, whose biographical particulars Marx supplies to situate him in the debates: founder (1843) and first editor of The Economist, later a leading free-trade publicist, Financial Secretary to the Treasury 1853–58, and an opponent of the quantity theory and of the 1844 Bank Act. This signals that Marx is engaging a live, politically consequential school of thought, not a set of academic abstractions.

Marx's Reduction of Tooke's Distinction to Two Things

Marx now states what Tooke's distinction "comes down to." The means of circulation circulates in two ways:

First, as coin (money), insofar as it mediates the expenditure of revenue — commerce between individual consumers and retail traders, counting all merchants who sell to the consumer direct (individual consumers as distinct from productive consumers or producers). Here "money circulates in the function of coin, even though it constantly replaces capital." A certain portion of a country's money is always devoted to this function, even though the particular pieces comprising it are constantly changing.

Second, insofar as money mediates the transfer of capital — whether as means of purchase (means of circulation) or means of payment — it is capital.

Having laid out this two-part division, Marx immediately attacks the criterion: "Thus it is neither the function of means of purchase nor that of means of payment that distinguishes it from coin." Money can function as means of purchase between one dealer and another (if one buys from another with cash), and can function as means of payment between dealer and consumer (if credit is given, revenue being consumed first and paid for afterwards). So the functional criterion collapses.

The real difference, Marx argues, is not functional but relational: in the second case, the money "does not just replace capital for one party, the seller, but is also spent and advanced as capital by the other party, the buyer." The distinction, then, is "one between the money form of revenue and the money form of capital, not between currency and capital" — because a definite quantity of money circulates in both sets of transactions (dealer–dealer and dealer–consumer), so that it is "equally circulation in both functions."

Here is the first major conceptual move. The proper contrast is between money-as-form-of-revenue and money-as-form-of-capital, not between currency and capital. The reason this is decisive is that it implies that the same money, functioning identically as circulation, can represent revenue for one party and capital for another, or revenue at one moment and capital at another. A "currency vs. capital" distinction invites us to imagine two separate streams of money; Marx's distinction collapses the illusion into a single stream whose social significance differs on either side of each transaction.

The Three Modes of Confusion in Tooke's Conception

Marx then enumerates the three ways Tooke's conception "introduces confusion into the question":

  1. By confusing the functional characteristics — treating money as "currency" in one form and "capital" in another, as though these were distinct functions of money.
  2. By bringing in the question of the overall quantity of money circulating — in both functions taken together.
  3. By bringing in the question of the relative proportions of the circulating medium in the two functions — and hence in the two spheres of the reproduction process.

These three modes are not independent; they are three progressively deeper layers of the same underlying error. Marx will address each in turn, and the third will open onto the chapter's longest and most important positive analysis (the behavior of circulation in prosperity and crisis).

Point (1): The Confusion of Functional Characteristics

Marx restates the point sharply: insofar as money serves to realize revenue or to transfer capital, it functions "either in buying and selling or in payment, as means of purchase or payment, and in the broader sense of the terms as means of circulation." The further characteristic it may have in the accounts of its spender or receiver — that it represents capital or revenue — "alters absolutely nothing here." He demonstrates this in two ways.

First demonstration: the mobility of the same piece of money across spheres. Although the kinds of money circulating in the two spheres are different, "the same piece of money, for instance a £5 note, moves from one sphere to the other and performs both functions in turn; this is unavoidable simply because the retail trader can give his capital its money form only in the form of the coin that he receives from his buyers." This is a tightly argued point. The retailer's capital is embodied in commodities; to realize it he must sell; his buyers are consumers spending revenue; the money they pay is therefore, from the retailer's side, the money form of his capital, while from the consumers' side it is the money form of their revenue. One physical token, one circulation, two social meanings.

Marx then develops the concrete institutional mechanics. Small change "proper" has "the centre of gravity of its circulation in the realm of retail trade" — the retailer uses it to give change and receives it back. But the retailer also receives money in the metal that is a measure of value (sovereigns in England) and banknotes, particularly lower denominations (£5 and £10). These gold coins and notes, "as well as some surplus small change," he deposits each day or week in the bank, and he pays for his purchases with cheques on his bank deposit. But the same gold coins and notes "are just as constantly withdrawn from the bank by the public as a whole in their capacity as consumers, as the money form of their revenue," directly or indirectly (e.g., smaller coins by manufacturers for wage payment); they are "constantly flowing back to the retail traders, for whom they thus realize a part of their capital afresh and at the same time part of their revenue."

The last phrase is the analytic crux: the returning money realizes at the same time a part of the retailer's capital and a part of his revenue. This is, Marx says, "important and is completely overlooked by Tooke." He then connects this to the reproduction process: "Only in so far as money is laid out as money capital, at the beginning of the reproduction process (Volume 2, Part One), does capital value exist in its pure form. For the commodity produced contains not only capital but also surplus-value; it is not just inherently capital but capital that has already become such, capital together with the source of revenue incorporated in it." Consequently, "What the retail trader parts with in exchange for the money that flows back to him, i.e. his commodity, is thus for him capital plus profit, capital plus revenue."

Second demonstration: the false inference from the retailer's reflux. "Moreover, since the circulating money flows back to the retailer, it restores the money form of his capital." The return of money therefore cannot be read as a mere "currency" phenomenon; it is simultaneously the restoration of capital form and the realization of revenue.

Marx's conclusion for point (1) is emphatic: "It is completely mistaken, therefore, to transform the distinction between circulation as circulation of revenue and as circulation of capital into a distinction between currency and capital."

The Banker's Standpoint: Why the Currency/Capital Distinction Arises

Marx now offers a sociological diagnosis of the origin of Tooke's expression. It arises "through his simply taking the point of view of the banker issuing his own banknotes." The amount of the banker's notes continuously in the hands of the public (even if always different particular notes) and functioning as means of circulation "cost him nothing besides paper and printing costs." The notes are "circulating certificates of indebtedness (bills of exchange) made out in his own name," even if they bring him in money and thus serve as a means of valorizing his capital. "But they are different from capital, whether his own or borrowed." Hence a special distinction, for the banker, between currency and capital — "which has nothing to do with defining the concepts as such, let alone Tooke's suggested definitions."

This is a crucial methodological observation, and it recurs later ("the narrow-minded banker's conception of circulation"). The category error is motivated: the issuing banker experiences his own note issue as free (costing only paper), and therefore experiences any advance that must be made beyond that free note issue as an advance of something else — "capital." Marx insists that this subjective, accounting standpoint generates a pseudo-concept, which the theorists then mistake for an objective distinction in political economy. The critique is thus, in good Marxian fashion, a critique of fetishized appearance: a real experience at the level of the agent (the banker) generates a false category at the level of theory.

The Means of Payment / Means of Purchase Distinction as Internal to Money

Marx then makes a transitional step that sets up the deeper argument. Money's character as means of circulation is unaffected by whether it is the money form of revenue or of capital. But there is a tendency: if money appears as the money form of revenue, it functions "more as a means of circulation in the strict sense (coin, means of purchase)," because of the fragmentation of these purchases and because the majority of revenue spenders — the workers — "can buy relatively little on credit." Conversely, in the world of trade and commerce, where the circulating medium is the money form of capital, money functions "principally as means of payment," partly because of concentration and partly because of the prevailing credit system.

This tendency is real and important — it will be central to the crisis analysis later. But Marx immediately warns: "the distinction between money as means of payment and money as means of purchase (circulating medium) is a distinction within money itself, not a distinction between money and capital." The analogy he offers makes the point vivid: if more copper and silver circulate in the retail trade and more gold in the wholesale trade, this does not turn the distinction between silver/copper and gold into a distinction between currency and capital. The materials and the typical functions may correlate with the two spheres, but the spheres do not thereby become two kinds of value — currency and capital.

Point (2): The Quantity of Money Circulating in Both Functions

Marx disposes of the second source of confusion relatively briefly, because it can be settled by appeal to results already established. Insofar as money circulates, whether as means of purchase or means of payment, irrespective of sphere or of whether it realizes revenue or capital, "the laws developed earlier in considering simple commodity circulation (Volume 1, Chapter 3, 2, b) still apply for the quantity of money circulating."

Marx then restates those laws. The amount of money in circulation, the amount of currency, is determined by the same factors in both cases:

  • The velocity of circulation — the number of times the same function of means of purchase and payment is repeated by the same piece of money in a given period.
  • The mass of simultaneous sales and purchases, or payments.
  • The sum of the prices of the commodities circulating.
  • The balances of payments that have to be settled at the same time.

The conclusion is blunt: "Whether the money functioning in this way represents capital or revenue for those who pay it and receive it is absolutely without any bearing on the matter. Its quantity is determined simply by its function as means of purchase and payment."

This is the reductio of Tooke's position. Tooke's distinction would imply that two different quantities — the "currency" quantity and the "capital" quantity — obey different determining laws. Marx shows they cannot, because the determinants operate on money's function as means of purchase/payment, not on its social destination. The point is that the determinants (velocity, price-sum, simultaneity, payment balances) are properties of the circulation process as such, indifferent to whether the money will be booked as revenue or as capital.

Point (3): Relative Proportions of the Circulating Medium in the Two Spheres

Marx introduces the third confusion by acknowledging that the two spheres do have "an inner connection": the amount of revenue to be spent expresses the scale of consumption; the amount of capital circulating in production and trade expresses the scale and speed of the reproduction process. Nevertheless, "the same factors have different effects, and even work in opposite directions, on the quantity of money circulating in the two functions or spheres." This differential, Marx says, gives "a new occasion for Tooke's absurd distinction between currency and capital." And here Marx delivers a methodological rebuke that is important to register: "The fact that the Currency Principle gentlemen confuse two disparate things is in no way a reason for presenting this as a conceptual distinction." In other words, the existence of a real differential behavior does not license the reification of that differential into two distinct substances ("currency," "capital"). The differential is a differential within circulation.

Marx now turns to the substantive analysis, which is the positive core of the chapter.

Prosperity: The Expansion of Revenue Circulation

In "times of prosperity, of great expansion, when the reproduction process exhibits a great acceleration and energy," Marx enumerates the changes:

  • Workers are fully employed; in most cases there is even a rise in wages, which to some extent balances the fall below the average in other phases of the commercial cycle.
  • Capitalists' revenues grow significantly.
  • Consumption generally rises.
  • Commodity prices rise "just as regularly, at least in certain decisive branches of business."

The consequence for circulation: the quantity of money in circulation grows, "at least within certain limits, since the greater velocity of circulation places its own barriers on the growth in the quantity of the circulating medium." This is a subtle application of the quantity law: rising prices and rising transaction volume would by themselves demand more money, but the same prosperity raises velocity, which economizes on the quantity required. Hence growth in the quantity of money is bounded.

Marx then addresses a classic pitfall explicitly: "We must not however count this twice: once as money needed to circulate the variable capital, and then again as money needed to circulate the workers' revenue." The money paid to workers as wages is spent in retail trade and returns "with the same weekly regularity" to the banks in the deposits of the retail trader, after mediating all kinds of intermediate transactions in smaller circuits. This is a concrete illustration of the identity-of-money point made earlier: the wage-money is the money form of variable capital from the capitalist's side and the money form of revenue from the worker's side, but it is one stream of circulation, not two.

Consequently, in prosperity the reflux of money proceeds smoothly for the industrial capitalists, "and so their need for monetary accommodation is not increased by their having to pay more in wages, more money for the circulation of their variable capital." The overall result: "in periods of prosperity the mass of the circulating medium that serves for the expenditure of revenue experiences a decisive growth."

The Reflux Mechanism and Credit

Marx now contrasts this with the means of circulation needed for transfers of capital — transfers "simply between the capitalists themselves." A period of brisk business is simultaneously "a period of elastic and easy credit." The velocity of circulation between capitalist and capitalist "is regulated directly by credit," and the amount of circulating medium required to settle payments and make cash purchases "undergoes a relative decline." It "may expand in absolute terms, but it always decreases relatively, compared with the expansion of the reproduction process." Two mechanisms:

  1. A larger mass of payments is settled without any intervention of money (netting, book credit, clearing).
  2. Given the vigour of the process, there is quicker movement of the same quantities of money, as both means of purchase and payment.

"The same amount of money mediates the reflux of a greater number of individual capitals."

The overall picture in prosperity: monetary circulation appears "full," "although its division II (transfer of capital) is at least relatively contracted, while division I (expenditure of revenue) undergoes an absolute expansion." This constitutes Marx's two-sector model of circulation in boom: Division I (revenue expenditure) expands absolutely; Division II (capital transfer) contracts relatively. Note the "division I / division II" terminology, which Marx introduces here and uses consistently through the chapter.

Marx then develops the concept of reflux. The refluxes "express the transformation of commodity capital back into money, M–C–M′," as seen in the analysis of the reproduction process (Volume 2, Part One). Here we encounter the circuit of capital in its money-mediated form: M (money) is advanced, transformed into C (commodity, including means of production and labour-power), which is then transformed back into M′ (a larger money sum, containing surplus-value). Credit intervenes to make "the reflux in the money form independent of the point in time of the actual reflux." Each capitalist sells on credit: his commodity is alienated before it is transformed back into money for him. He buys on credit: the value of his commodity has been transformed back for him either into productive capital or into commodity capital even before the price falls due and is paid.

The consequence in prosperity: "the reflux takes place smoothly and easily. The retail trader is certain to pay the wholesaler, the latter the manufacturer, and he the importer of raw material, etc." But Marx adds the crucial warning about appearance vs. reality: "The appearance of rapid and assured refluxes always persists for a certain time after these are really at an end, by virtue of the credit that has already been given, since the credit refluxes stand in for real ones." The banks begin to scent danger "as soon as their clients deposit more bills of exchange with them than money." He refers to the evidence of the Liverpool bank director quoted earlier (p. 541). The signal of trouble is the substitution of credit claims for money in bank deposits.

Marx reinforces this with a quotation from his own earlier work: "In periods of expanding credit the velocity of currency increases faster than the prices of commodities, whereas in periods of contracting credit the velocity of currency declines faster than the prices of commodities" (A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, p. 105). This gives a precise asymmetry: velocity is more elastic than prices in the expansion, and contracts faster than prices in the contraction.

Crisis: The Reversal

"In periods of crisis, the opposite is the case." Marx enumerates the changes in each division:

  • Division I (revenue expenditure): circulation contracts, prices fall, wages fall, the number of workers employed is restricted, turnover declines.
  • Division II (capital transfer): "the need for monetary accommodation grows with the decline in credit," which Marx says will be examined in more detail immediately.

Marx states the result confidently: "There can be no doubt at all that with the decline in credit, which goes together with a stagnation in the reproduction process, the amount of currency required for no. I, the expenditure of revenue, declines, whereas that for no. II, the transfer of capital, increases." He then poses the critical question: "It remains to be investigated, however, how far this is identical with the assertions of Fullarton and others."

The Fullarton assertion is quoted: "A demand for capital on loan and a demand for additional circulation are quite distinct things, and not often found associated" (Fullarton, On the Regulation of Currencies, p. 82; the title of Chapter 5).

Fullarton's Thesis in Full

Footnote 90 reproduces Fullarton's argument at length, and it is worth reconstructing closely because Marx's critique is measured against its details. Fullarton writes:

  • It is "a great error" to imagine that the demand for pecuniary accommodation (that is, for the loan of capital) "is identical with a demand for additional means of circulation, or even that the two are frequently associated."
  • Each demand "originates in circumstances peculiarly affecting itself, and very distinct from each other."
  • When everything looks prosperous — wages high, prices rising, factories busy — "an additional supply of currency is usually required to perform the additional functions inseparable from the necessity of making larger and more numerous payments."
  • But it is "chiefly in a more advanced stage of the commercial cycle, when difficulties begin to present themselves, when markets are overstocked, and returns delayed," that interest rises and pressure comes upon the Bank for advances of capital.
  • It is true there is no medium through which the Bank is accustomed to advance capital except its promissory notes, and to refuse the notes is to refuse the accommodation. "But the accommodation once granted, everything adjusts itself in conformity with the necessities of the market; the loan remains, and the currency, if not wanted, finds its way back to the issuer."
  • Parliamentary Returns show that the securities in the hands of the Bank of England "fluctuate more frequently in an opposite direction to its circulation than in concert with it."
  • "No bank can enlarge its circulation, if that circulation be already adequate to the purposes to which a banknote currency is commonly applied; but that every addition to its advances, after that limit is passed, must be made from its capital, and supplied by the sale of some of its securities in reserve, or by abstinence from further investment in such securities."
  • The table compiled from Parliamentary Returns for 1833–1840 furnishes continued examples. On 3 January 1837, "when the resources of the Bank were strained to the uttermost to sustain credit and meet the difficulties of the money-market," advances on loan and discount reached £17,022,000, "an amount scarcely known since the war," while the aggregate issues "remain unmoved at so low a point as £17,076,000." Conversely, on 4 June 1833, circulation was £18,892,000 with private securities in hand "nearly, if not the very lowest on record for the last half-century," at £972,000.

The empirical pattern Fullarton is highlighting is real and important: securities (advances) and note circulation move inversely. When the Bank lends heavily in a crisis, its note circulation is low; when it lends little in a quiet period, its note circulation is high or high relative to advances. Fullarton uses this to argue that the note issue is determined by the needs of circulation (a fixed demand ceiling), and that advances beyond that ceiling must come from the Bank's "capital" (sale of securities), not from note creation. "Capital," for Fullarton, thus names the Bank's own resources that must be liquidated to make advances beyond the note-issue ceiling.

Footnote 90 also includes the evidence of Mr. Weguelin, Governor of the Bank of England, which Marx introduces to drive a wedge between a "demand for pecuniary accommodation" and a "demand for gold" (which Wilson, Tooke, and others call capital). Weguelin: "The discounting of bills to that extent" (one million daily for three successive days) "would not reduce the reserve" (of banknotes) "unless the public demanded a greater amount of active circulation. The notes issued on the discount of bills would be returned through the medium of the bankers and through deposits. Unless these transactions were for the purpose of exporting bullion, and unless there were an amount of internal panic which induced people to lock up their notes, and not to pay them into the hands of the bankers... the reserve would not be affected by the magnitude of the transactions." And: "The Bank may discount a million and a half a day, and that is done constantly, without its reserve being in the slightest degree affected, the notes coming back again as deposits, and no other alteration taking place than the mere transfer from one account to another" (Report on Bank Acts, 1857, Evidence, nos. 241, 500).

Marx draws the conclusion: "The notes therefore serve here merely as means of transferring credits."

Marx's Critique of Fullarton's Opposition

Marx now dismantles Fullarton's claim that "a demand for capital on loan and a demand for additional circulation are quite distinct things." Two things must be distinguished.

First, on the prosperity case. It is clear that in prosperity, when the quantity of circulating medium must grow, there is a growing demand for it. But, Marx argues, "if a manufacturer draws more out of his bank to deposit in gold or banknotes because he has to spend more capital in the money form, it is not his demand for capital that grows on this account but only his demand for this particular form of expending his capital." The demand relates "only to the technical form in which he puts his capital into circulation."

Marx illustrates: just as, according to the differential development of the credit system, the same variable capital, the same amount of wages, "requires a greater quantity of circulating medium in one country than in another" — in England, for example, more than in Scotland; in Germany more than in England. In agriculture, too, the same capital active in the reproduction process "requires different amounts of money in different seasons to perform its function." The point is that the demand for money here is a technical/form demand, not a demand for additional capital. The manufacturer is not asking for more capital; he is asking to hold the same capital in a different (money) form.

Second, and more importantly, Marx turns Fullarton's own contrast inside out. "It is in no way, as he claims, the strong demand for loans that distinguishes the period of stagnation from that of prosperity, but rather the ease with which this demand is satisfied in the time of prosperity and the difficulty of satisfying it once stagnation has set in." The demand for loans is strong in both; what changes is the supply side — the ease or difficulty of meeting it. And Marx identifies the deeper mechanism: "It is in fact precisely the tremendous development of the credit system during the period of prosperity, and also therefore the enormous rise in the demand for loan capital and the readiness with which this is made available in such periods, that leads to the shortage of credit in the period of stagnation." Thus the very elasticity of credit in prosperity is what generates the tightness in the downturn. Credit does not simply respond to conditions; it produces the conditions of its own restriction by over-expanding. "Thus it is not a difference in the demand for loans that distinguishes the two periods."

Marx then restates the genuine distinction: "As we have noted before, the two periods are distinguished in the first place by the fact that in the period of prosperity it is the demand for means of circulation between consumers and dealers that is dominant, while in the period of depression it is the demand for means of circulation between capitalists. In the period of stagnation the first declines while the second increases."

What Fullarton Means by "Capital"

Marx now performs the decisive conceptual unmasking. What "strikes Fullarton and others as decisively important" is the phenomenon that "at such times, while securities in the hands of the Bank of England increase, its note circulation declines, and vice versa." But the volume of securities "expresses the volume of monetary accommodation, of discounted bills of exchange, and of advances against marketable securities." Marx then reconstructs Fullarton's argument:

Fullarton says that securities in the hands of the Bank of England generally fluctuate in the opposite direction to its note circulation, and that this confirms the old-established principle of the private banks that no bank can increase its note issue beyond a certain definite amount, determined by the needs of its clientele. If it wants to make further advances above this amount, it must make these out of its own capital — either realize on securities or utilize deposits it would otherwise have invested.

"We see now what Fullarton means by capital." In his view, capital is involved when the bank can no longer make advances with its own banknotes — its own promises to pay, which cost it nothing. But with what does it make these advances? "With the proceeds from the sale of 'securities in reserve,' i.e. government paper, stocks and other interest-bearing paper." And what does it sell these securities for? "For money, gold or banknotes, in so far as the latter are legal tender, as those of the Bank of England are." What it advances, therefore, is "in all circumstances money." If it advances gold, obvious. If notes, "then these notes now represent capital, since the bank has parted with a real value in exchange, i.e. interest-bearing securities."

Marx then handles the special case of the private banks: the notes that accrue to them by the sale of securities can only be, in the main, either notes of the Bank of England or its own notes, "since others are only accepted with reticence in payment for securities." But if it is the Bank of England itself, then those of its own notes that it retains cost it capital, i.e. interest-bearing securities. Besides, it thereby withdraws its own notes from circulation. If it reissues them or issues new notes to the same amount, "these now represent capital." And they represent capital just as much when used for advances to capitalists as when used later, when the demand for monetary accommodation subsides, for new investments in securities. "In all these circumstances, the term capital is used here simply in the banking sense, where it means that the banker is forced to lend more than just his credit."

This is the chapter's central deflationary move. Fullarton's "capital" is not a category of political economy at all; it is an accounting category internal to the banking firm. "Capital" = the resources the banker must liquidate because he has exhausted his free credit-creation capacity. Marx's critique therefore replicates, at the level of monetary theory, his general critique of political economy: the practitioners of a sphere mistake the forms of their own practice for objective categories. The banker's "capital" is to the real capital of society as the merchant's "profit on alienation" was to surplus-value — an appearance generated by the specific standpoint of the agent.

The Drain of Gold

Marx now asks the operational question that the securities/circulation inverse pattern raises: "As is well known, the Bank of England makes all its advances in its own notes. If despite this, then, the Bank's note circulation normally decreases as the discounted bills and securities in its possession — i.e. the advances it has made — increase, what then becomes of the notes put into circulation, and how do they flow back to the Bank?"

He first disposes of one case: when the demand for monetary accommodation "arises from an unfavourable national balance of payments and hence mediates a drain of gold." Then "the matter is very simple. The banknotes are exchanged against gold at the Bank itself, in the Issue Department, and the gold is exported." It is as if the Bank paid gold directly, without the mediation of notes, as it does in discounting bills of exchange. "A rising demand of this kind — and in certain cases it reaches £7 to £10 million — naturally adds not a single £5 note to the country's domestic circulation."

Now comes a pivotal theoretical statement. If it is said that the Bank advances capital in this case and not currency, this has "a double meaning":

  1. That it does not advance credit but real value — a part of its own capital or the capital deposited with it.
  2. That it advances money not for domestic circulation but for international circulation, world money.

And for this purpose, "the money must always exist in its hoard form, its metallic embodiment; in the form in which it is not only the form of value but itself equal to the value whose money form it is." Even though this gold represents capital both for the Bank and for the exporting gold dealer (banking capital or commercial capital), "the demand does not arise for it as capital but rather as the absolute form of money capital. It arises in the very same moment as the foreign markets are flooded with unrealizable English commodity capital. What is demanded is not capital as capital but rather capital as money, in the form in which money is a commodity on the general world market; and this is its original form as precious metal."

Marx's conclusion on this point is a precise, dialectical refutation of both schools. "The drain of gold is not, as Fullarton, Tooke, etc. say, 'simply a question of capital.' It is rather a 'question of money,' even if in a specific function." And, crucially, the Currency School's error and the Banking School's error are mirror images: "The fact that it is not a question of domestic circulation, as the Currency Theory people maintain, in no way proves, as Fullarton and others believe, that it is just a 'question of capital.' It is 'a question of money,' in the form in which money is an international means of payment."

Marx then quotes Fullarton: "Whether that capital [the purchase price for the millions of quarters of foreign wheat imported into the home country after a harvest failure] is transmitted in merchandise or in specie, is a point which in no way affects the nature of the transaction" (Fullarton, p. 131). Marx concedes the transactional point but insists it is irrelevant to the monetary question: "But it has a very definite effect on whether there is a drain of gold or not. Capital is converted into the form of precious metal because it cannot be converted at all into the form of commodities, or not without a very major loss." And then the famous observation: "The anxiety that the modern banking system has when faced with a drain of gold goes beyond anything that the Monetary System ever dreamed of, even though for it precious metal was the only true wealth."

The Morris Testimony

To dramatize the point that gold is treated as "the only true wealth" in a crisis, Marx introduces the testimony of Morris, Governor of the Bank of England, before the parliamentary committee investigating the crisis of 1847–8:

  • Question 3846: "When I spoke of the depreciation of stocks and fixed capital, are you not aware that all property invested in stocks and produce of every description was depreciated in the same way; that raw cotton, raw silk, and unmanufactured wool were sent to the continent at the same depreciated price, and that sugar, coffee and tea were sacrificed as at forced sales? — It was inevitable that the country should make a considerable sacrifice for the purpose of meeting the efflux of bullion which had taken place in consequence of the large importation of food."
  • Question 3848: "Do not you think it would have been better to trench upon the £8 million lying in the coffers of the Bank, than to have endeavoured to get the gold back again at such a sacrifice? — No, I do not."

Marx's gloss: "Here gold is taken as the only true wealth." This is the empirical punchline of the theoretical argument: at the moment of crisis, the entire apparatus of commodity wealth (cotton, silk, wool, sugar, coffee, tea) is sacrificed in forced sales to preserve the metallic hoard. The "absolute form of value" — money as world money — asserts its dominance precisely when commodity capital is most abundant and least saleable. This is the concrete inverse of an ordinary crisis: not scarcity but superabundance of commodity capital, combined with a desperate scramble for money.

Tooke's Discovery on Exchange and Drain of Gold

Marx then turns to an empirical finding of Tooke's, quoted by Fullarton. Tooke's discovery: "with only one or two exceptions, and those admitting of satisfactory explanation, every remarkable fall of exchange, followed by a drain of gold, that has occurred during the last half-century, has been coincident throughout with a comparatively low state of the circulating medium, and vice versa" (Fullarton, p. 121). Marx reads this as showing that drains of gold "take place in most cases after a period of excitement and speculation, as 'the signal of a collapse already commenced... an indication of overstocked markets, of a cessation of the foreign demand for our productions, of delayed returns, and, as the necessary sequel of all these, of commercial discredit, manufactories shut up, artisans starving, and a general stagnation of industry and enterprise'" (p. 129).

Marx notes this is "also of course the best refutation of the Currency people's contention that 'a full circulation drives out bullion and a low circulation attracts it.'" The Currency School held that a full domestic circulation indicates excess issue, which spills abroad as gold; and a low domestic circulation attracts gold. Tooke's empirical finding reverses the causal story: the low circulation coincides with the gold drain, because the drain follows a collapse — the collapse reduces circulation and drives out gold simultaneously.

Marx acknowledges a point about the Bank's gold reserve: "although it is generally in periods of prosperity that the Bank of England has a strong gold reserve, this is always formed in the slack and stagnant period that follows the storm." So the reserve is built after the crisis, in the subsequent stagnation, not during prosperity. This is a sharp point against naive readings of the reserve's significance.

The Multiple Functions of the Hoard

Marx summarizes: all this wisdom about the drain of gold "amounts to saying that the demand for international means of circulation and payment is different from the demand for domestic means of circulation and payment" — which is why Fullarton can say, on p. 112, that "the existence of a drain does not necessarily imply any diminution of the internal demand for circulation." And the export of precious metals abroad "is not the same as putting notes or coin into circulation domestically."

Marx then refers back to earlier work: "I have already shown earlier that the movement of the hoard that is set aside as a reserve fund for international payments has in and of itself nothing to do with the movement of money as means of circulation" (see Volume 1, pp. 242–4). But he acknowledges a "certain complication": the different functions of the hoard — as a reserve fund of means of payment (for payments falling due at home), as a reserve fund of circulating medium, and as a reserve fund for world money — "are all imposed upon a single reserve fund." From this it follows that "in certain circumstances a drain of gold from the Bank domestically may be combined with a drain abroad." A further complication: the additional function "quite arbitrarily laid on this hoard," namely to serve as a guarantee for the convertibility of banknotes, in countries where the credit system and credit money are developed. And on top of all this: (1) the concentration of the national reserve fund in a single principal bank, and (2) its reduction to the minimum possible.

This produces Fullarton's complaint (p. 143): "One cannot contemplate the perfect silence and facility with which variations of the exchange usually pass off in continental countries, compared with the state of feverish disquiet and alarm always produced in England whenever the treasure at the Bank seems to be at all approaching to exhaustion, without being struck with the great advantage in this respect which a metallic currency possesses."

The theoretical weight of this passage is considerable. Marx here identifies the institutional basis of the "feverish disquiet": the artificial multiplication of functions on a single, miniaturized reserve fund. The panic is not a natural fact about money; it is an artifact of the banking system's structure — centrally concentrated reserves, minimal reserve ratios, and convertibility guarantees. This is one of the places in the chapter where Marx's critique of the Banking School's "narrow-minded banker's standpoint" converges with a structural critique of the credit system itself.

How Banks Expand Accommodation Without Expanding Note Issue

Returning from the drain of gold, Marx poses the general question: "if we leave aside for now the drain of gold, how then can a bank that issues banknotes, such as the Bank of England, increase the amount of monetary accommodation it provides without increasing its note issue?"

He first establishes a premise: all notes outside the walls of the bank, whether actually circulating or dormant in private hoards, "are in circulation as far as the bank itself is concerned, i.e. outside its possession." Thus if the bank expands its discounting and money-lending business, its advances against securities, "the banknotes issued must flow back to it again or otherwise the sum in circulation would increase, which is precisely not supposed to be the case." This sets up the question of the reflux, which can take place in two ways:

Reflux, first way: The bank pays notes to A against securities; A uses these to pay B for a bill of exchange that falls due; B deposits the notes again with the bank. "The circulation of these notes is at an end, but the loan remains." This is corroborated directly by Fullarton's own formula: "The loan remains, and the currency, if not wanted, finds its way back to the issuer" (p. 97). The notes advanced to A have returned to the bank; the bank is a creditor to A (or to whoever drew the bill that A discounted), while it is a debtor to B for the sum of value expressed in the notes, "and B thereby has at his disposal a corresponding part of the bank's capital."

Reflux, second way: A pays to B, and either B himself or C (to whom B pays the notes) uses the notes to pay bills due to the bank, directly or indirectly. In this case "the bank is paid with its own notes. And in this way the transaction is completed (until A's repayment to the bank)."

Marx then asks the loaded question: "How far, then, should the bank's advance to A be considered as an advance of capital, and how far as simply an advance of means of payment?"

The Three Cases of Bank Advances

Footnote 91 is decisive for understanding the textual history: "The passage now following was incomprehensible in the original manuscript and has been newly drafted by the editor, up to the end of the parentheses. This point has already been dealt with in a different context in Chapter 26 [pp. 557–9] — F. E." The reference is to Friedrich Engels. This means the tightly systematized three-case analysis that follows — the chapter's most analytically rigorous passage — was reconstructed by Engels from Marx's notes. It is continuous with Chapter 26, and its theoretical content is thoroughly Marxian, but the reader should register the editorial hand. The parenthetical passage ends with "F. E.," marking where Marx's recoverable text resumes.

First case: A obtains the sum advanced by the bank on his personal credit, without giving any security. "In this case he has received not only an advance of means of payment, but unquestionably also a new capital, which he can use as additional capital in his business, and valorize, until it has to be repaid." This is the only case of a genuine advance of capital.

Second case: A has given the bank securities as collateral — government bonds or stocks — and received on them a cash advance of, say, up to two-thirds of their present value. "In this case he has received means of payment that he needed, but no additional capital, for he has given into the bank's possession a greater capital value than he received from it." The greater capital value, on the one hand, was "of no use for his immediate need, as means of payment, since it was invested at interest in a specific form"; on the other hand, A had "his reasons for not transforming it directly into means of payment by selling it" — his securities functioned as reserve capital, and he let them continue to function as such. "There has therefore been a temporary mutual transfer of capital between A and the bank, so that A has not received any extra capital (on the contrary!), though he has received the means of payment he needed." For the bank, the transaction involves "the temporary tying-up of money capital in a loan, a transformation of money capital from one form to another, and this transformation is precisely the basic function of banking."

Third case: A has a bill of exchange discounted at the bank and receives the amount due for it in cash, after deduction of the discount. "In this case, he has sold a non-liquid form of money capital to the bank in exchange for the sum of value in liquid form; the bill of exchange that had not yet expired in return for ready cash." The bill is now the bank's property. It makes no difference that A, as last endorser, is responsible in default; he shares this responsibility with the other endorsers and with the drawer. "In this case, therefore, there is no advance at all, but simply an ordinary purchase and sale." A has nothing to repay; the bank reimburses itself by cashing the bill when it falls due. "Here, too, there has been a reciprocal transfer of capital between A and the bank, and this, moreover, is just like the purchase and sale of any other commodity; for this reason A does not receive any additional capital. What he needed and received was means of payment, and he receives this in that the bank transforms the one form of his money capital — the bill of exchange — into the other — money."

The conclusion is a striking reduction: "It is only in the first case, therefore, that there can be any talk of a genuine capital advance." In the second and third cases, it occurs "at most in the sense that every investment of capital implies an 'advance.'" In that sense, the bank advances A money capital; but for A this is money capital "at most in the sense that it is a part of his capital in general." And crucially: "he requires and uses it not especially as capital, but rather especially as means of payment. Otherwise every ordinary sale of commodities, by which means of payment are also obtained, would have to be seen as obtaining an advance of capital."

This passage is the conceptual engine of the chapter's refutation. It shows that what the Banking School called "advances of capital" are, in the overwhelming majority of bank operations, either (a) the exchange of one form of money capital for another (discounting), or (b) a temporary mutual transfer of capital against collateral. Genuine net capital advance is confined to unsecured personal credit. The empirical dominance of "capital advances" in bank statistics is thus thoroughly misleading as to what is actually happening.

Private Banks and Purely Metallic Circulation

Marx extends the analysis to private banks with rights of note issue: "the distinction is that, if their notes neither remain in local circulation nor return to the banks in the form of deposits or for payment of bills falling due, these notes then fall into the possession of people to whom they must pay gold or Bank of England notes in exchange for them. In this case the advance of their notes actually represents an advance of Bank of England notes, or, what is the same thing for them, of gold, i.e. a part of their banking capital." The same applies when the Bank of England itself, or any bank subject to a legal maximum on its note issue, has to sell securities to withdraw its own notes from circulation and reissue them in advances — "here its own notes represent a part of its mobilizable banking capital."

Then Marx makes a general point: "Even if circulation were purely metallic," there could be simultaneously (1) a drain of gold ("what is meant here is evidently a drain of gold in which at least one part goes abroad" — an editor's clarification) that empties the vaults, and (2) since the bank's principal requirement for gold is simply to make payments (to settle past transactions), its advances on securities could greatly increase, but return to it in the form of deposits or in repayment of bills falling due. So that "on the one hand its overall reserves would decrease with an increase in securities in the bank's portfolio, while on the other hand the same sum that it formerly had as an 'owner' would now be a sum for which it was in debt to its depositors, and finally the total quantity of circulating medium would decline."

Book Credit Without Notes

Marx now removes a simplifying assumption: "It has so far been assumed that the advances are made in notes and involve at least a temporary increase in the note issue, even if this immediately vanishes again. But this is not necessary."

Instead of paper notes, the bank can open a credit account for A, "so that A, as its debtor, becomes an imaginary depositor." A pays his creditors with cheques on the bank, and the recipient of these cheques pays them again to his banker, who exchanges them in the clearing house against the cheques drawn on him. "In this case there is no intervention of notes, and the entire transaction is confined to one in which the bank settles its own debt with a cheque drawn on itself, its actual compensation consisting in its claim against A. In this case the bank has advanced to A a part of its banking capital, in the form of a part of its own claim as a creditor."

This is the pure book-credit case, and it shows that the entire transaction can occur with zero note issue. The category of "currency" is simply inapplicable.

Securities Are Not Genuine Capital

Marx then broadens the point into a general theoretical statement about what "money capital" is in the banking context. "In so far as this demand for monetary accommodation is a demand for capital, it is simply a demand for money capital, capital from the standpoint of the banker; i.e. a demand for gold (in the case of a drain of gold abroad) or for notes on the national bank, which a private bank can obtain only by buying them with an equivalent, so that they represent capital for it. Or finally it might be a question of interest-bearing securities, government bonds, stocks, etc. that have to be sold if gold or notes are to be obtained."

He then delivers a decisive theoretical clarification — one that echoes the analysis of "fictitious capital" developed elsewhere in Volume 3:

  • Government bonds are "capital only for the person who has bought them, to whom they represent his purchase price, the capital he has invested in them. They are not capital in themselves, but simply creditor's claims."
  • Mortgages are "simply claims on future payments of ground-rent."
  • Stocks of other kinds are "simply property titles which give the holder a claim to future surplus-value."
  • "None of these things are genuine capital, they do not constitute any component of capital and are also in themselves not values."

Marx then observes that by similar transactions, "money that belongs to the bank can be transformed into deposits, so that the bank becomes a claimant for this money instead of its owner, and holds it under a different title. Important as this is for the bank itself, it in no way affects the amount of capital stored in the country, or even the money capital." And the theoretical payoff: "Capital figures here simply as money capital and, if it is not present in the actual money form, as a mere title to capital. This is very important, since the scarcity of, and pressing demand for, banking capital is confused with a restriction of actual capital, which in such cases, on the contrary, is present in excess in the form of means of production and products, and stifles the markets."

This is one of the most important passages in the chapter. It states the central contradiction of a crisis: the scarcity of banking capital (the appearance) coexists with the superabundance of real capital in the form of unsaleable means of production and products (the reality). The "denominator" of the crisis is not absence but excess — the excess that cannot be realized in money form.

Why Circulation Can Stay Stable or Decline

Marx now synthesizes the whole argument: "It is very simple to explain, therefore, how the amount of securities held by the bank as collateral increases, and the growing demand for monetary accommodation can be satisfied by the bank, at the same time as the total quantity of means of circulation remains the same or declines."

In periods of tight money, the total quantity is kept in check in two ways:

  1. By a drain of gold.
  2. By the demand for money simply as means of payment — where the notes issued flow back immediately or where the transaction takes place by way of book credit, without any mediation of notes.

In the latter case, "payments are effected simply by a credit transaction, the settlement of these payments being the sole object of the exercise." Marx then articulates a general law: "It is money's peculiar property that where it functions simply in settlement of payments (and in times of crisis an advance is obtained in order to pay, not to buy; to settle past transactions, not to start new ones), its actual circulation is simply a vanishing magnitude, even when this settlement does not take place entirely by credit operations, without any intervention of money; i.e. that when there is a great demand for monetary accommodation, a tremendous mass of these transactions can take place without any expansion in the circulation."

Here we have the conceptual key to the chapter's empirical puzzle. The Bank of England's note circulation can remain stable or decline, while the Bank performs enormous monetary accommodation, because that accommodation is dominated by means-of-payment transactions — advances made "in order to pay, not to buy" — whose circuit is a vanishing magnitude: notes are issued and immediately reflux. "The simple fact that the Bank of England's circulation remains stable or even declines, at the same time as it performs a great deal of monetary accommodation, is in no way prima facie proof, as Fullarton, Tooke and others assume (in consequence of their error in seeing monetary accommodation as identical with the receipt of capital on loan, of additional capital), that the circulation of money (banknotes) in its function as means of payment does not increase and expand."

Marx then formulates the resolution precisely: "Since the circulation of notes as means of purchase declines in times of business stagnation, where such a great deal of accommodation is required, their circulation as means of payment can increase while the total sum in circulation, the sum of notes functioning as means of purchase and of payment, still remains stable or even declines. Circulation of banknotes as means of payment, these notes immediately flowing back to the bank that issued them, is precisely not circulation in the eyes of these economists."

This sentence — "Circulation of banknotes as means of payment... is precisely not circulation in the eyes of these economists" — exposes the perceptual blindness at the root of the errors. The Banking School looks past the very circulation that is most active in a crisis.

Marx adds a refinement: "If circulation as means of payment increases to a higher degree than circulation as means of purchase declines, the total circulation will grow, even though the quantity of money functioning as means of purchase experiences a significant decline. And this actually does happen at certain points in the crisis, i.e. when there is a complete breakdown of credit, when not only are commodities and securities unsaleable, but it has also become impossible to get bills of exchange discounted, and nothing counts any more except money payment, or as the merchant says: cash." Hence Fullarton's "sudden, though only temporary, enlargement of the issues" at the close of 1825, even while the efflux of bullion is still going on, is not accidental.

Fullarton on Panic Hoarding

Marx quotes Fullarton's own account of this phenomenon:

"With respect again to those examples of eager competition for the possession of banknotes, which characterize seasons of panic and which may sometimes, as at the close of 1825, lead to a sudden, though only temporary, enlargement of the issues, even while the efflux of bullion is still going on, these, I apprehend, are not to be regarded as among the natural or necessary concomitants of a low exchange; the demand in such cases is not for circulation [read: circulation as a means of purchase], but for hoarding, a demand on the part of alarmed bankers and capitalists which arises generally in the last act of the crisis [hence, for a reserve of means of payment], after a long continuation of the drain, and is the precursor of its termination" (Fullarton, p. 130).

Marx's editorial bracketed glosses expose the sleight of hand. Fullarton concedes the empirical fact (a scramble for banknotes in panic) but reinterprets it as a demand "not for circulation but for hoarding." Marx has already shown that this is precisely the demand for money as means of payment — the demand for the "absolute form of value." Fullarton's move of labelling it "hoarding" allows him to preserve the fiction that circulation is not really expanding; but it is a semantic evasion, not an analysis.

Marx then refers back to his treatment of money as means of payment (Volume 1, Chapter 3, 3, b): "how, in the case of a violent interruption in the chain of payments, money reverts from its merely ideal form into the material and also absolute form of value vis-à-vis commodities. A few examples of this were given there, in notes [51 and 52]. This interruption itself is in part the effect, in part the cause, of the collapse of credit and the circumstances that accompany it: flooding of markets, devaluation of commodities, interruption of production, etc."

This is a dialectical statement of reciprocal causation: the interruption of the chain of payments is both cause and effect of the credit collapse. Money, which had functioned ideally (in credit bookkeeping), reverts to its material and "absolute" form.

Marx's conclusion on this whole line of argument: "It is clear, however, that Fullarton transforms the distinction between money as means of purchase and money as means of payment into a false distinction between currency and capital. And at the bottom of this again lies the narrow-minded banker's conception of circulation."

This sentence is the chapter's culminating verdict on the Banking School. Fullarton's error is now fully diagnosed: he has taken a real and important distinction within money (means of purchase vs. means of payment) and mapped it onto a false distinction between money and capital (currency vs. capital). The two errors are structurally identical to Tooke's, and they share the same sociological origin — the banker's standpoint.

The Final Question: Capital or Money as Means of Payment?

The chapter closes with the question that all the preceding analysis has been preparing: "It might still be asked what is lacking in such difficult times, capital, or money in its capacity as means of payment? And this is a well-known controversy."

Marx's answer has two components.

First, the drain-of-gold case: "In so far as the embarrassment is demonstrated by a drain of gold, it is clear that what is demanded is the international means of payment. But money in its capacity as international means of payment is gold in its metallic reality, as itself a valuable substance, an amount of value. It is also capital, but capital not as commodity capital but rather as money capital, capital not in the form of commodity but rather in the form of money (and moreover of money in the pre-eminent sense of the term, in which it exists on the general world commodity market). There is no opposition here between the demand for money as means of payment and the demand for capital. The opposition is rather between capital in its money form and in its commodity form; and the form in which it is required here, and can alone function, is its money form."

This is the definitive resolution of the "capital vs. money" controversy. The apparent opposition is dissolved into a real opposition: capital in money form vs. capital in commodity form. In the drain-of-gold case, what is demanded is capital in its money form, because money is the only form that functions on the world market. There is no separate "capital" demand standing apart from the "money" demand; capital is being demanded — in its money form.

Second, the general crisis case: "Apart from this demand for gold (or silver), it cannot be said that in such periods of crisis there is in any sense a lack of capital. Under extraordinary circumstances, such as a rise in grain prices, a cotton famine, etc., this can be the case; but these are in no way necessary or regular accompaniments of such periods; and the existence of such a lack of capital, therefore, cannot be immediately inferred from a demand for monetary accommodation. On the contrary. Markets are glutted, swamped with commodity capital. Hence it is in any case not a lack of commodity capital that gives rise to the difficulty. We shall return to this question later."

The chapter thus ends with a characteristic Marxian inversion. The crisis presents itself as a scarcity of capital; its reality is a superabundance of commodity capital that cannot be realized in money. The demand for "capital" is, in the final analysis, a demand for money in a specific function — and this is the thread Marx promises to pick up again ("We shall return to this question later"), linking the chapter forward into the remainder of the analysis of interest-bearing capital and the credit system.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principle: The Functional Unity of Money

The chapter's deepest lesson is methodological. It demonstrates, on a highly contested empirical terrain, that conceptual distinctions in political economy must be grounded in functions, not in the standpoints of agents or in accidental correlational patterns. Tooke and Fullarton observed real phenomena — the inverse movement of securities and note circulation, the scramble for money in a panic, the coincidence of low circulation with gold drains — and tried to capture them with a distinction between "currency" and "capital." Marx shows that these phenomena are fully explained by: (1) the two functions of money (means of purchase, means of payment); (2) the two spheres of reproduction (revenue expenditure, capital transfer); (3) the determinants of the quantity of money (velocity, price sum, simultaneity of transactions, payment balances); and (4) the institutional structure of the banking system (centralized reserves, convertible notes, credit chains). The distinction between "currency" and "capital" adds nothing and distorts everything.

This is an instance of a general Marxian principle: social categories must correspond to relations of production, not to subjective experiences or bookkeeping conventions. The banker's "capital" is the banker's own experience of having exhausted his free credit; it is not the social capital of the community. Marx makes this explicit in the passage on securities not being "genuine capital." The recurring warning — that the scarcity of banking capital is confused with a restriction of actual capital — is a template for the critique of all fetishized economic categories.

The Real Distinctions Marx Substitutes

Where Tooke and Fullarton put one false distinction (currency/capital), Marx puts four real ones:

  1. Means of purchase vs. means of payment — a distinction internal to money's function as means of circulation. In prosperity, means-of-purchase circulation (revenue side) dominates and expands absolutely; in crisis, means-of-payment circulation (capital side) dominates and expands.
  2. Circulation of revenue vs. circulation of capital — a distinction in the social character of the money, not in money itself. The same money performs both in turn.
  3. Domestic circulation vs. international circulation — the drain of gold is a question of money as world money; it is not simply a question of capital, nor is it a question of domestic circulation. Both the Currency School and the Banking School fail to see this correctly, from opposite sides.
  4. Capital in money form vs. capital in commodity form — the real opposition underlying the pseudo-controversy over whether what is lacking in a crisis is capital or money. In the drain case, money form is required; and this is a demand for capital, in a specific form. In the general crisis case, capital is present in excess in commodity form.

The Paradox of Crisis: Superabundant Capital, Desperate Scarcity of Money

The chapter's most provocative substantive finding is the coexistence of two apparently contradictory states in a crisis: markets are glutted and swamped with commodity capital (superabundance of real capital), while capital is desperately scarce in the form of means of payment (scarcity of money capital). This paradox is not an anomaly; it is the necessary form of crisis in a credit system. The chain of payments, having been built on credit, breaks down; money, which had functioned "ideally" in book credit, reverts to its material and absolute form. The very abundance of unsaleable commodities is what makes money scarce — because money can no longer be got by selling.

This finding anticipates and grounds the general theory of crisis. The Banking School's view that a crisis reflects a "lack of capital" (or a shortage of loanable funds) is the surface appearance; the deeper reality is a crisis of realization — the impossibility of converting commodity capital into money capital on the anticipated terms. And Marx is explicit: "it is in any case not a lack of commodity capital that gives rise to the difficulty."

The Critique of the Banking School's "Standpoint"

The chapter's social epistemology is as important as its economics. Tooke's and Fullarton's errors are not merely logical; they are situated. They arise "through his simply taking the point of view of the banker issuing his own banknotes"; they arise at bottom from "the narrow-minded banker's conception of circulation." Marx's critique shows how the specific material position of the theorist — the issuing banker for whom note creation is free and who experiences loan accommodation beyond that as a drain on "capital" — generates systematic distortions in theory. The financial theorist who universalizes his own accounting categories mistakes the forms of his practice for the forms of the economy.

This is a foreshadowing, in the monetary domain, of the general Marxian critique of political economy as the theory of the forms of appearance of capitalist relations. The task of critique is to identify the social relations that generate, veil, and reproduce those forms.

The Institutional Dimension: Panic as Artifact

A subtle but significant thread is Marx's treatment of the "feverish disquiet" of the English banking system versus the comparative calm of continental metallic systems. Marx does not naturalize this difference. He explains it by reference to: the multiplication of functions on a single reserve fund; the arbitrary requirement that the hoard guarantee note convertibility; the concentration of the national reserve in one bank; and its reduction to a minimum. The panic, in other words, is system-generated, not a fact of nature about money. Fullarton could see the symptom ("great advantage... which a metallic currency possesses") but not the structural cause. Marx's analysis thus contains a critique of the credit system's institutional fragility that goes well beyond the doctrinal dispute with which the chapter begins.

Ironies and Self-Corrections

One fascinating internal irony: Marx uses Fullarton's own evidence against Fullarton. He quotes Fullarton's confession that "the loan remains, and the currency, if not wanted, finds its way back to the issuer," and thereby shows that the phenomenon Fullarton uses to prove the "currency vs. capital" distinction is precisely the phenomenon that dissolves it. When the currency "finds its way back to the issuer," the same transactional event is simultaneously an advance of capital (the loan persists) and a circulation of currency (the note is issued). One event, two names — which is exactly Marx's point.

Similarly, Marx accepts Fullarton's and Tooke's empirical findings (the inverse movement of securities and circulation; Tooke's discovery about low circulation coinciding with drains of gold) while rejecting the theoretical framework that both theorists constructed to interpret them. The chapter is thus an object lesson in how correct empirical observations can coexist with wrong theoretical conclusions — and how the correction of theory requires reconstructing the conceptual space within which the observations are read.

The Textual Dimension: A Chapter at the Boundary of Two Hands

Finally, a scholarly lesson about the chapter as an artifact. The three-case analysis of bank advances — the conceptual heart of the chapter's positive argument — was, per footnote 91, reconstructed by Engels from an "incomprehensible" manuscript. The editorial marks ("F. E.") in the text demarcate Marx's recoverable material from Engels's reconstruction. This does not undermine the argument's Marxian character — it repeats points from Chapter 26 and is consistent with Marx's broader analyses — but it registers the chapter's unique status as a joint product of the two founders of historical materialism. The argument is Marx's; the presentation in this central passage is Engels's. That the reconstruction is so lucid, and so consequential for the chapter's overall structure, is a reminder of Engels's intellectual role in shaping Capital as we read it.

Broader Implications

For contemporary readers, the chapter's lessons remain sharp. First, the demand for money in a financial crisis is not the same as a demand for real resources; this is a point that recurs throughout modern financial-crisis analysis and bears directly on debates about liquidity provision, lender-of-last-resort policy, and the supposed distinction between "liquidity crises" and "solvency crises." Marx's demonstration that the scarcity of money capital can coexist with a superabundance of real capital is a foundational theoretical resource for those debates.

Second the chapter is a caution against the reification of accounting distinctions. The distinction between "currency" and "capital" was not merely theoretically mistaken; it had policy consequences. It was the distinction on which the Bank Act of 1844 and much subsequent practice rested, and its inadequacy was tested (and found wanting) in the crises of 1847–8 and 1857. Marx's argument shows that monetary-theoretical categories that derive their apparent rigor from the practice of a particular institution will fail when the institution's perspective is transcended by the phenomena.

Third, the chapter exemplifies a mode of theoretical criticism that is neither merely empirical nor merely conceptual. Marx does not refute Tooke and Fullarton by amassing counter-examples (he largely accepts their examples) nor by pure conceptual analysis (his analysis is always anchored in the concrete forms of banking practice). He refutes them by showing that their concepts do not carve reality at its joints — that what they call "currency" and "capital" are functions and relations that their own preferred examples demonstrate to be internally connected.

The chapter thus performs, in a small compass, the distinctive achievement of Marx's mature economic writing: the simultaneous critique of a doctrine, the rectification of empirical appearances, and the construction of a positive theoretical alternative — all pressed into the service of a single, rigorously maintained thesis: the money that circulates as currency and the money that constitutes capital are one and the same, and their distinction is a distinction, not within money, but within the social relations money mediates.
