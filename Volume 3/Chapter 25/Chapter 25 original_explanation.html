Volume 3 • Chapter 25: Credit and Fictitious Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 25, "Credit and Fictitious Capital," occupies a pivotal position in Volume III of Capital — specifically in Part V, "The Division of Profit into Interest and Profit of Enterprise." It is the entry point into Marx's treatment of the credit system, and its central thesis is both modest in its stated scope and explosive in its implications: credit under capitalism is not an exogenous convenience layered atop commodity production, but an organic outgrowth of the money-form itself — specifically of money's function as a means of payment — which then generates a vast, self-referential superstructure of paper claims (bills of exchange, banknotes, deposits, cheques) that can detach from the real transactions it purports to represent and become a mechanism for the creation of fictitious capital.

The chapter's central problem is the relationship between the "base" of real money (gold, banknotes) and the "enormous superstructure" of bills of exchange built upon it. Marx's intellectual objective is to characterize the credit system just far enough to show how it belongs intrinsically to the capitalist mode of production "in general" — not to provide a technical banking manual. He explicitly states this limitation at the outset, and the reader must hold this frame throughout: what follows is a conceptual anatomy, not a history of banking.

The chapter fits into the broader theoretical framework of Volume III in three decisive ways. First, it deepens the analysis of interest-bearing capital by showing that the loanable money capital handled by bankers is not simply a hoard of idle funds but a socially concentrated fund drawn from reserve funds, money-capitalists' deposits, the scattered savings of "all social classes," and revenues awaiting gradual consumption. Second, it sets up the argument — referenced here as awaiting development in Chapter 28 — that the quantity theory of money misconstrues the relationship between note issue, prices, and credit. Third, it provides the empirical-historical demonstration of the theory's danger: the English commercial crisis of 1845–47, in which the credit system that had lubricated a genuine boom transmuted into a machinery for swindling — commodities bought and sold not to realize value but merely to generate discountable bills.

The intellectual stakes are therefore enormous. The chapter is the hinge on which rests Marx's claim that crises under capitalism are not accidental failures of an otherwise sound system but structural potentials immanent to the unity of production and circulation. The "fictitious capital" of the title is not simply fraud (though fraud abounds in the evidence Marx marshals); it is the ordinary, systemic tendency of credit to create claims whose value rests on claims whose value rests on claims, until the whole edifice is tested against the narrow metallic base — and fails. This chapter supplies both the theoretical scaffolding and the documentary record for that argument.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Methodological Threshold: Why Credit Is Analyzed Only "In General"

Marx opens with a programmatic restriction: "It lies outside the scope of our plan to give a detailed analysis of the credit system and the instruments this creates (credit money, etc.)." This is not evasion but a deliberate delimitation. He will treat only commercial and bank credit — the credit that arises directly between producers and merchants, and the credit that banks extend as intermediaries. He explicitly excludes state credit (the credit of governments), stating that "the connection between the development of this and the development of state credit remains outside our discussion." The terminological precision matters: by isolating commercial and bank credit, Marx keeps the analysis anchored to the circuit of industrial capital, ensuring that the credit he describes is ultimately grounded in the production and circulation of commodities — not in sovereign borrowing, which obeys a distinct logic.

This framing determines everything that follows. The chapter will not survey banking institutions, nor legislate policy (except where the historical case demands it). Instead, it will identify the minimal necessary features of the credit system that characterize capitalist production as such. The reader should read every subsequent empirical citation — Leatham, Tooke, Coquelin, Gilbart, the parliamentary reports — as evidence marshaled to establish a structural point, not as independent historical research.

The Genesis of Credit in Money as Means of Payment

The theoretical foundation is announced by back-reference: Marx has "already shown (in Volume 1, Chapter 3, 3, b) how the function of money as means of payment develops out of simple commodity circulation, so that a relationship of creditor and debtor is formed." The essential move is that credit is not an invention; it is latent in the money-form. When money functions as a means of payment rather than as a means of purchase, the sale of a commodity and its payment are separated in time — and this temporal gap is the germ of the credit relation.

With the development of trade and of a mode of production that "produces only for circulation," this "spontaneous basis for the credit system is expanded, generalized and elaborated." Marx then states the decisive structural shift: "By and large, money now functions only as means of payment, i.e. commodities are not sold for money, but for a written promise to pay at a certain date." Here we encounter the first key term of the chapter: the bill of exchange — a written promise to pay at a certain date, which Marx adopts as the general name for "all these promises to pay."

The mechanics of the bill are crucial. Until they expire and fall due, "these bills themselves circulate as means of payment; and they form the actual commercial money." And when they "ultimately cancel each other out, by the balancing of debts and claims, they function absolutely as money, even though there is no final transformation into money proper." This is the analytical crux: credit instruments perform the money-function without ever becoming money. The theoretical architecture that follows is a layered dependence: "As these mutual advances by producers and merchants form the real basis of credit, so their instrument of circulation, the bill of exchange, forms the basis of credit money proper, banknotes, etc." The key negative claim is that credit money "is not based on monetary circulation, that of metallic or government paper money, but rather on the circulation of bills of exchange." The pyramid has, as it were, inverted: metallic money is no longer the base but a narrower, subordinate reserve at the bottom of a paper-driven system — precisely the "base" whose narrowing Leatham will later warn is so dangerous.

Leatham's Superstructure: The Arithmetic of the Bill Mountain

The banker W. Leatham, in Letters on the Currency (2nd edn, London, 1840), supplies the quantitative scaffolding. For 1839, the total of bills for the whole year is £528,493,842 (assuming foreign bills of exchange make up about a fifth of the total). The amount of bills out at one time in that year is £132,123,460. Bills of exchange constitute "one component part greater in amount than all the rest put together" — meaning that the mass of bills in existence exceeds banknotes and coin combined. The variables here — annual turnover of £528 million versus simultaneous stock of £132 million — illustrate the distinction between the flow of payments and the standing quantity of claims, a distinction essential to understanding why credit can be both economically vast and physically unbacked.

Leatham's warning deserves close reading: "This enormous superstructure of bills of exchange rests (!) upon the base formed by the amount of banknotes and gold, and when, by events, this base becomes too much narrowed, its solidity and very existence is endangered." Marx reproduces Leatham's exclamation mark, signaling that the "resting" is precisely what is questionable. The numbers Leatham gives are stark: if one estimates the entire currency (banknotes) plus the liabilities of the Bank and country bankers payable on demand, one finds a sum of £153 million which, by law, can be converted into gold — "and the amount of gold to meet this demand" only £14 million. The ratio of convertible claims to metallic reserve is thus roughly eleven to one. This is not a policy detail; it is the structural fragility of the system expressed numerically.

Leatham then identifies the mechanism by which fictitious paper is generated: "The bills of exchange are not ... placed under any control, except by preventing the abundance of money, excessive and low rates of interest or discount, which create a part of them, and encourage their great and dangerous expansion." This introduces the concept Marx will call accommodation paper — bills drawn not against a real transaction but to raise funds: "one bill of exchange is drawn to take up another running, in order to raise a fictitious capital, by creating so much currency." The distinction between real bona fide transactions ("actual bargain and sale") and purely financial paper is, Leatham admits, impossible to draw empirically: "It is impossible to decide what part arises out of real bona fide transactions ... or what part is fictitious." This epistemic ungovernability is not incidental — it is the heart of the chapter's case that credit cannot be regulated by mechanical rule.

Bosanquet and the Clearing House: The Economies of Endorsement

J. W. Bosanquet, in Metallic, Paper and Credit Currency (London, 1842), supplies complementary data on the efficiency of credit. "An average amount of payments to the extent of upwards of £3,000,000 is settled through the Clearing House ... every day of business in the year, and the daily amount of money required for the purpose is little more than £200,000." Here is a concrete ratio: roughly £3,000,000 of payments settled with £200,000 of actual money — a factor of about fifteen. (Engels' editorial footnote extends the series forward: "In 1889, the total turnover of the Clearing House amounted to £7,618½ million, which, in roughly 300 business days, averages £25½ million daily.") The function of the Clearing House — where London bankers exchange due bills and filed cheques — is to net out mutual claims, so that only the residual balance needs settlement in money. This is the institutional embodiment of the principle that credit instruments cancelling each other function absolutely as money.

Bosanquet then establishes the circulation of bills by endorsement. Bills "act undoubtedly as currency, independent of money," transferring property by endorsement. Assuming "upon an average there are two endorsements upon every bill in circulation, and ... each bill performs two payments before it becomes due," it follows that by endorsement alone, property changed hands in 1839 to the value of twice £528 million, or £1,056,000,000 — at a rate of more than £3,000,000 per day. Extending the calculation to deposits as well, Bosanquet concludes that "deposits and bills of exchange together, perform the functions of money, by transferring property from hand to hand without the aid of money, to an extent daily of not less than £18,000,000." Every variable here — endorsements, days, bills, deposits — compounds the same point: the volume of property transfers vastly exceeds the volume of money required to effect them. Marx accepts the calculation as illustrative while never losing sight of the fact that this efficiency is simultaneously a leverage — the mechanism by which a shrinking base can destabilize a swollen superstructure.

Tooke: Credit as Confidence and the Origin of Interest

Marx then introduces Tooke's definition of credit "in its most simple expression": "the confidence which, well, or ill-founded, leads a person to entrust another with a certain amount of capital, in money, or in goods computed at a value in money agreed upon, and in each case payable at the expiration of a fixed term." Two things are analytically important here. First, credit is grounded in confidence — a subjective, potentially ill-founded state. This prepares the way for the panic psychology that will dominate the crisis narrative (Gurney's testimony). Second, Tooke specifies two forms of credit: lending in money and lending in goods whose value is agreed in money terms (which constitutes a sale). In money-lending, whether "in banknotes, or in a cash credit, or in an order upon a correspondent, an addition for the use of the capital of so much upon every £100 is made to the amount to be repaid." That addition is the origin of interest, expressed as a rate per £100 (per centum). In goods-lending, the sum stipulated to be repaid "includes a consideration for the use of the capital and for the risk." Credit thus already contains the seed of the division between interest (payment for use) and profit of enterprise (the risk-bearing residual) — the very division the Part as a whole theorizes.

Tooke emphasizes the transferability of the obligations: written obligations of payment at fixed dates mostly accompany credits, and "the obligations or promissory notes after date being transferable, form the means by which the lenders, if they have occasion for the use of their capital ... before the expiration of the term of the bills they hold, are mostly enabled to borrow or to buy on lower terms, by having their own credit strengthened by the names on the bills in addition to their own." Here the endorsement (the "names on the bills") becomes collateral — a chain of personal liability that makes the paper more liquid than the underlying commitment of any single party. This is why a draft on a famous house (Jones Loyd, later in the evidence) circulates more readily than a private draft.

Coquelin: Credit as the Interlocking Network of Industrial Advances

Charles Coquelin (Revue des Deux Mondes, 1842) shifts the analysis from finance to the productive core. "In every country, the greater part of credit transactions take place within the orbit of industry." Coquelin sketches a vertical chain: the raw-material producer advances his product to the manufacturer "and receives from him a promise to pay on a certain date." The manufacturer, "after completing his share in the work, advances his product in turn to another manufacturer who is to process it further, on similar conditions, and in this way credit extends ever further, from one person to another, right through to the consumer." The wholesaler advances commodities to the retailer while himself receiving them from the manufacturer. "Everyone borrows with one hand and lends with the other, sometimes money, but far more frequently products."

This is the crucial substantive claim: credit is not primarily a monetary phenomenon but a mode of organizing production and distribution. "There is thus an incessant exchange of advances in industry, which combine and intersect each other in all directions. The development of credit is nothing more than the multiplication and growth of these mutual advances, and this is the true seat of its power." By locating credit's "true seat" in the factory-to-factory, merchant-to-merchant network rather than in the bank, Coquelin's evidence — which Marx adopts approvingly — grounds the entire chapter's subsequent discussion of bank credit. Bank credit supervenes upon industrial credit; it does not create it.

The Money Trade and the Emergence of the Banker as "General Manager of Money Capital"

Having established credit's productive seat, Marx turns to "the other aspect of the credit system": the development of the money trade, which "in capitalist production naturally keeps step with the development of trade in commodities." He back-references Chapter 19, where he showed that maintaining a reserve fund for businessmen, the technical operations of receiving and paying out money, international payments, "and hence the bullion trade as well, are concentrated in the hands of money-dealers." To this money-dealing is added the management of interest-bearing capital — money capital as the special function of the money-dealers. "The borrowing and lending of money becomes their special business."

The banker's position is defined with surgical precision: "They appear as middlemen between the real lender of money capital and its borrower." The business of banking "consists from this aspect in concentrating money capital for loan in large masses in the bank's hands, so that, instead of the individual lender of money, it is the bankers as representatives of all lenders of money who confront the industrial and commercial capitalists. They become the general managers of money capital." The symmetrical role: "they concentrate the borrowers vis-a-vis all the lenders, in so far as they borrow for the entire world of trade." The bank is thus double-sided: "a bank represents on the one hand the centralization of money capital, of the lenders, and on the other hand the centralization of the borrowers." And its profit is stated with the directness of classical political economy: "It makes its profit in general by borrowing at lower rates than those at which it lends." The rate spread is the bank's income — a formula whose simplicity conceals the whole later analysis of how the spread can be widened by the parasitic retention of interest-free balances (Gilbart).

The Sources of Loan Capital: Reserve Funds, Deposits, Small Sums, Revenues

Marx then enumerates how the loan capital at the banks' disposal accrues. First, they act as "the cashiers of the industrial capitalists," holding the money capital each producer and merchant keeps as a reserve fund or receives as payment. "These funds are thus transformed into money capital for loan. In this way the reserve fund of the business community is restricted to the necessary minimum, by being concentrated as a social fund, and one part of the money capital, which would otherwise be dormant in reserve, is loaned out and functions as interest-bearing capital." This is the socialization of the reserve fund — a collective economization that simultaneously frees capital for lending. Second, their loan capital is formed "from the deposits made by money capitalists, who hand over to them the job of loaning it out." With the development of the banking system, "and particularly once they pay interest on deposits, the money savings and the temporarily unoccupied money of all social classes are also deposited with them." Marx explicitly identifies a distinct function here: "Small sums which are incapable of functioning as money capital by themselves are combined into great masses and thus form a monetary power. This collection of small amounts, as a particular function of the banking system, must be distinguished from the banks' function as middlemen between actual money capitalists and borrowers." This is a nuance of considerable theoretical weight: the banker is not merely a broker between existing large lenders and borrowers; he is a transformer who manufactures loanable capital out of sums too small to have functioned as such. Finally, "revenues that are to be consumed only gradually are also deposited with the banks" — consumption funds temporarily entering the credit circuit as a further source of lendable money capital.

Forms of Lending and the Banknote as "Bill on the Banker"

Lending, Marx specifies (confining himself to "commercial credit proper"), is effected "by discounting bills — transforming them into money before their due date — and by advances of various kinds": direct advances on personal credit; loans against securities "such as interest-bearing paper, government paper and stocks of all sorts"; advances against bills of lading, dock warrants and other certified titles to ownership of goods; and overdrafts on deposits. The discount is thus defined as the purchase of a bill before maturity at less than face value — the deduction being the interest.

The credit the banker gives can take various forms: "bills and cheques on other banks, credit facilities of a similar kind, and finally, if the bank is authorized to issue notes, in its own banknotes." Marx defines the banknote with exactness: "A banknote is nothing more than a bill on the banker, payable at any time to its possessor and given by the banker in place of private drafts." He then explains why the banknote appears "especially striking and important to the layman": first, this kind of credit money "emerges from commercial circulation into general circulation and functions here as money"; second, "in most countries the major banks that issue notes are a peculiar mishmash between national banks and private banks and actually have the government's credit behind them, their notes being more or less legal tender"; and third, "it is evident here that what the banker is dealing in is credit itself, since the banknote merely represents a circulating token of credit." The layman's illusion is that the banknote is uniquely "credit," when in fact the banker "also deals in credit in every other form, even if he advances money deposited with him in cash." Hence the summary judgment: "In actual fact, banknotes are simply the small change of wholesale trade, and the deposit is always the main thing as far as the banks are concerned. The Scottish banks provide the best proof of this." The priority of the deposit over the note is one of the chapter's most consequential claims, since it turns attention from note issue (where the currency theorists concentrated) to the deposit multiplier.

An editorial note interrupts to say that "special credit institutions, like special forms of banks, need not be considered in any more detail for our present purpose." Marx then returns to Tooke, who distinguishes two branches of banking: collecting capital from those who lack immediate employment for it and distributing it to those who have, versus receiving deposits of customers' incomes and paying them out as needed for consumption. Tooke names the first "a circulation of capital" and the second "a circulation of currency," the first relating "to the concentration of capital on the one hand and the distribution of it on the other," the second "employed in administering the circulation for local purposes of the district." Marx notes: "We shall return to this passage in Chapter 28." The distinction is important because it separates the credit that funds production (capital) from the credit that merely circulates revenue.

Parliamentary Evidence: Banker's Bills as a "Privilege of Coining Money"

The notes to the chapter (drawn largely from Commercial Distress, 1847–8) supply vital empirical texture. In the 1840s, "twenty-one-day drafts of one bank on another were often accepted in lieu of banknotes when discounting bills of exchange in London" (J. Pease, country banker, nos. 4636 and 4645). It was customary for bankers to give customers such bills in payment "quite regularly, whenever money was tight." Recipients wanting banknotes had to discount the bills again — which "for the banks, this amounted to a privilege of coining money." Messrs Jones Loyd and Co. had paid in this way "from time immemorial" whenever money was scarce and the interest rate above 5 per cent. The customer was eager to get these banker's bills because a bill on Jones Loyd was easier to discount than his own — "they often changed hands twenty or thirty times." Here we see the mechanism by which a bank's name, added to a bill, functions as a superior form of money: the "names on the bills" (Tooke) confer liquidity, and each re-endorsement is a further use of the same paper before any final settlement.

Fullarton: Any Shape of Credit Can Function as Money

Fullarton supplies the general formulation: "There is scarcely any shape into which credit can be cast, in which it will not at times be called to perform the functions of money; and whether that shape be a banknote, or a bill of exchange, or a banker's cheque, the process is in every essential particular the same, and the result is the same." And: "Banknotes are the small change of credit." Marx adds a footnote identifying Fullarton (1780–1849) as "a notable opponent of the quantity theory of money; his ideas are dealt with in detail in Chapter 28." This signals that the chapter is laying a foundation for a polemic to come: Fullarton, the anti-quantity theorist, is here being recruited as a witness to the fungibility of credit forms — a fact the quantity theory cannot accommodate.

Gilbart: The Manufacture of Banking Capital Out of Other People's Money

The longest single block of evidence comes from J. W. Gilbart's The History and Principles of Banking (London, 1834). Gilbart divides a bank's trading capital into invested capital and borrowed banking capital, and identifies "three ways of raising a banking or borrowed capital": receiving deposits, issuing notes, and drawing bills. He then illustrates with three simple cases. If a person lends £100 for nothing and the bank lends it at 4 per cent, it gains £4 per year. If instead a person takes the bank's "promise to pay" (the standard formula of an English banknote) and returns it at year's end while paying 4 per cent for it, the bank again gains £4 — "just the same as though I had lent him 100 sovereigns." If a person in a country town brings the bank £100 on condition that twenty-one days later it pays the same amount to a person in London, "whatever interest I can make of the money during the twenty-one days, will be my profit." "This," Gilbart concludes, "is a fair representation of the operations of banking, and of the way in which a banking capital is created by means of deposits, notes, and bills." The formal principle: banking capital can be created by the very act of accepting deposits or issuing promises — a kind of capital not itself produced, but fabricated from credit relations.

Gilbart further specifies: "The profits of a banker are generally in proportion to the amount of his banking or borrowed capital." To determine the true profit, "the interest upon the invested capital should be deducted from the gross profit, and what remains is the banking profit." And then the crucial materialist point: "The advances of bankers to their customers are made with other people's money." This is developed in the remarkable passage on non-issuing bankers: "Precisely those bankers who do not issue notes, create a banking capital by the discounting of bills. They render their discounts subservient to the increase of their deposits. The London bankers will not discount except for those houses who have deposit accounts with them." Finally, the device of the compensating balance: "A party who has had bills discounted, and has paid interest on the whole amount, must leave some portion of that amount in the hands of the banker without interest. By this means the banker obtains more than the current rate of interest on the money actually advanced, and raises a banking capital to the amount of the balance left in his hands." Thus the banker's real yield exceeds the nominal discount rate — because he lends his own money (or rather, other people's) and gets back not only interest on the full amount but the free use of a retained fraction that he can lend again.

Gilbart on Deposit Banking, Transfer, and the Voluntary Supersession of Money

Gilbart then elaborates the transfer principle: "Banks of deposit serve to economize the use of the circulating medium. This is done upon the principle of transfer of titles." Banks "are enabled to settle a large amount of transactions with a small amount of money. The money thus liberated, is employed by the banker in making advances, by discount or otherwise, to his customers. Hence the principle of transfer gives additional efficiency to the deposit system." The vision reaches its limit in this passage: "It matters not whether the two parties, who have dealings with each other, keep their accounts with the same banker or with different bankers; for, as the bankers exchange their cheques with each other at the clearing house... The deposit system might thus, by means of transfers, be carried to such an extent as wholly to supersede the use of a metallic currency. Were every man to keep a deposit account at a bank, and make all his payments by cheques, money might be superseded, and cheques become the sole circulating medium." But Gilbart adds the crucial qualifier: "In this case, however, it must be supposed that the banker has the money in his hands, or the cheques would have no value." The qualifier preserves the structural tension between the credit superstructure and its base — the cheques are worthless unless there is money behind them, yet the whole efficiency of the system consists in not requiring that money to be present.

Gilbart describes the consolidation of local banking through branch banks (country banks with branches in smaller towns; London banks in the different districts) and agencies ("Each country banker employs a London agent to pay his notes or bills ... and to receive sums that may be lodged by parties residing in London for the use of parties residing in the country"). And he describes the mutual clearing of notes: "Each banker accepts the notes of others, but does not reissue them. In all larger cities they come together once or twice a week and exchange their notes. The balance is paid by a draft on London."

Gilbart on the Inseparability of Trade from Speculation

A passage of great interpretive importance follows: "It is the object of banking to give facilities to trade, and whatever gives facilities to trade gives facilities to speculation. Trade and speculation are in some cases so nearly allied, that it is impossible to say at what precise point trade ends and speculation begins... Wherever there are banks, capital is more readily obtained, and at a cheaper rate. The cheapness of capital gives facilities to speculation, just in the same way as the cheapness of beef and of beer gives facilities to gluttony and drunkenness." The simile is crude but precise: cheapness does not cause the appetite, but it facilitates its indulgence. Marx returns to this passage himself, stressing its recognition already in 1834.

Gilbart continues with an argument that the issue of notes and the discount of bills are not identical (a point against those who equated the two): "It is very possible for a banker to issue his own notes for all the bills he discounts, and yet nine-tenths of the bills in his possession shall represent real capital. For, although in the first instance, the banker's notes are given for the bill, yet these notes may not stay in circulation until the bill becomes due — the bill may have three months to run, the notes may return in three days." The logic: the note returned to the bank in three days cancels itself out as a claim against the bank, leaving the discounted bill as the bank's real asset. So the identity between note issue and fictitious capital formation is false — most notes return quickly, most bills represent real transactions. This is a nuance Marx accepts against the currency school: the danger lies not in the mechanics of note issue as such.

Gilbart then notes that "the overdrawing of a cash credit account is a regular matter of business; it is, in fact, the purpose for which the cash credit has been granted" — and cash credits are granted not only on personal security but "also upon the security of the Public Funds." Then: "Capital advanced, by way of loan, on the securities of merchandise, would produce the same effects as if advanced in the discounting of bills. If a party borrows £100 on the security of his merchandise, it is the same as though he had sold his merchandise for a £100 bill, and got it discounted with the banker. By obtaining this advance he is enabled to hold over this merchandise for a better market, and avoids a sacrifice which, otherwise, he might be induced to make, in order to raise the money for urgent purposes." This lombard advance — lending against commodities — is functionally equivalent to the discount of a bill and carries the same implication: credit allows a producer to withhold goods from the market, thereby (as Leatham implied and Marx will elsewhere develop) distorting the relation of supply and demand.

The Deposit Multiplier: The Currency Theory Reviewed

A passage cited from The Currency Theory Reviewed provides the chapter's sharpest image of the creation of credit money and the phenomenon of fictitious capital. "It is unquestionably true that the £1,000 which you deposit at A today may be reissued tomorrow, and form a deposit at B. The day after that, reissued from B, it may form a deposit at C ... and so on to infinitude; and that the same £1,000 in money may thus, by a succession of transfers, multiply itself into a sum of deposits absolutely indefinite." The variables: one initial £1,000 of money; a sequence of deposits A, B, C, ...; each step a redeposit. The consequence: "It is possible, therefore, that nine-tenths of all the deposits in the United Kingdom may have no existence beyond their record in the books of the bankers who are respectively accountable for them." The documentary evidence: "Thus in Scotland, for instance, currency (mostly paper money at that) has never exceeded £3 million, the deposits in the banks are estimated at £27 million" — a ratio of nine to one between deposits and currency. The passage then explains the reversibility of this multiplication: "Unless a run on the banks be made, the same £1,000 would, if sent back upon its travels, cancel with the same facility a sum equally indefinite. As the same £1,000 with which you cancel your debt to a tradesman today, may cancel his debt to the merchant tomorrow, the merchant's debt to the bank the day following, and so on without end; so the same £1,000 may pass from hand to hand, and bank to bank, and cancel any conceivable sum of deposits."

The passage is ambivalent — it presents the multiplicity of deposits as a natural and benign feature of the transfer system, but it also states plainly that the deposits have "no existence beyond their record in the books." This is precisely the line between legitimate credit (the same money performing successive cancellations) and fictitious capital (claims that appear to be wealth but are only claims). The condition of stability is the absence of a run; when a run occurs, the multiplication reverses, and claims contract to the money base.

Marx's Bridge from Gilbart to History

Marx inserts his own comment at this point: "We have seen how Gilbart was already aware in 1834 that 'whatever gives facilities to trade gives facilities to speculation...' The easier it is to obtain advances on unsold commodities, the more these advances are taken up and the greater is the temptation to manufacture commodities or dump those already manufactured on distant markets, simply to receive advances of money on them." This is the mechanism by which credit and swindling become indistinguishable: if a producer can get an advance on goods not yet sold, he has a motive to produce or dump goods purely to generate advances. The demand is not for the commodities but for the claims they engender. And: "As to how the entire business community in a country can be caught up in swindling of this kind, and where it ends up, we have a striking example in the history of English commerce between 1845 and 1847."

The 1845–47 Case: From Recovery to Mania

Engels' editorial narrative then takes over. The depression that began in 1837 eased at the end of 1842. In 1843 the Opium War "opened up China to English trade," and the new market "offered a new pretext for an expansion that was already in full swing, particularly in the cotton industry." The manufacturing optimism was boundless: "'How can we ever produce too much? We have 300 million people to clothe,' I was told at the time by a Manchester manufacturer." (The "I" is Marx's own voice, at one remove in Engels' reconstruction.)

The four sources of demand that Engels identifies as driving the boom are: the export demand, the new China market, the investment in railways (the passion for speculative building that diverted surplus-value), and the general availability of cheap credit. Capital invested in railways in 1846–47 reached £75 million (per Q1059, Commercial Distress, 1848–57). Stock was underwritten "to the limits of possibility, i.e. as far as there was money to cover the initial payments. As for the rest, a way would be found!" When further calls on railway shares came due, "recourse to credit was necessary, and the main business of the firm generally had to suffer." The banks' gold reserve was "of unheard-of dimensions," the bank rate low ("1½ to 2½ per cent in 1844, below 3 per cent until October 1845, then rising for a short period to 5 per cent (February 1846) before falling again to 3½ per cent in December 1846"), and share prices at record highs. The psychological mechanism is captured in a series of rhetorical questions: "Why let the splendid opportunity pass? Why not get into the swing of it? Why not send all that could be manufactured to foreign markets which were crying out for English goods? And why should the manufacturer himself not pocket the double profit from selling his yarn and cloth in the Far East and selling the return cargo in England?" This is the origin of "the system of mass consignments to India and China against advances, which developed very soon into a system of consignments simply for the sake of the advances," which "could lead only to a massive flooding of the markets and a crash."

The Harvest Failure and the Reversal: A Cascade in Numbers

The trigger was the harvest failure of 1846: England and Ireland needed enormous imports of provisions (corn and potatoes), but the supplier countries could be paid only to an "exceedingly small extent" in English industrial products. Therefore "precious metal had to be given in payment; at least £9 million in gold went abroad." Of this, "a full £7½ million ... came from the Bank of England's reserves, substantially impairing its freedom of action on the money market." Other banks, whose reserves were with the Bank of England "and in practice identical with its own," had to restrict accommodation. The cascade of rates is then transcribed as a sequence — a rate series documenting the panic: Bank rate in January 1847 at 3–3½ per cent; 7 per cent in April "when the first panic broke out"; a small respite in summer (6½, 6 per cent); a new and more violent panic when the new harvest was also bad; official minimum lending rate to 7 per cent in October and 10 per cent in November, "so that the great majority of bills could be discounted only at colossal and usurious rates of interest, if at all." The general stagnation of payments caused bankruptcies of a few leading firms and "very many medium and small ones"; the Bank itself was in danger of collapse "as a result of the clever Bank Act of 1844 and the restrictions this imposed" (cross-referenced to Chapter 34).

The denouement: the government suspended the Bank Act on 25 October, "bowing to a universal demand, and thereby released the Bank from the absurd legal fetters imposed on it." Freed to issue banknotes without obstacle — "and since the credit of these banknotes was actually guaranteed by the credit of the nation, and thus unimpaired, the monetary tightness was decisively eased." Many firms still collapsed, but "the peak of the crisis was over," and the Bank rate fell to 5 per cent in December. By 1848 a revival began, "breaking the edge of the revolutionary movements on the Continent in 1849" and leading to unprecedented prosperity in the 1850s, "only to be followed by the crash of 1857." The narrative thus completes a long-wave cycle — boom, crisis, revival, prosperity, crash — demonstrating that the crisis is not terminal but cyclical.

The Notes: Railway Swindle, Speculative Bills, East India Swindling, Panic

The five numbered notes supply the concrete texture of fictitious capital in operation. Note 1 documents the devaluation of securities between February and 23 October 1847: English government bonds fell by £93,824,217; dock and canal stock by £1,358,288; railway stock by £19,579,820 — a total of £114,762,325. The base of the collapse — the evaporation of paper wealth — is precisely the fictitious capital of the title.

Note 2 provides the mechanism of the "swindling in the East India trade, where bills were no longer drawn because commodities had been sold, but rather commodities sold in order to draw bills which could be discounted and converted into money." The Manchester Guardian of 24 November 1847 describes the round-robin: A instructs B to buy goods from C; B pays C in six-month drafts; B secures himself with six-month drafts on A; once the goods are shipped, A draws six-month drafts on D against the mailed bill of lading. "The shipper and the co-signee were thus both put in possession of funds — months before they actually paid for the goods; and, very commonly, these bills were renewed at maturity, on pretence of affording time for the returns in a 'long trade.'" The fatal dynamic: "Unfortunately losses by such a trade, instead of leading to its contraction, led directly to its increase. The poorer men became, the greater need they had to purchase, in order to make up, by new advances, the capital they had lost on the past adventures. Purchases thus became, not a question of supply and demand, but the most important part of the finance operations of a firm labouring under difficulties." The same logic held abroad: Indian houses bought sugar, indigo, silk, or cotton "not because the prices advised from London by the last overland mail promised a profit on the prices current in India, but because former drafts upon the London house would soon fall due, and must be provided for." The technique: purchase a cargo with bills at ten months' date, transmit the shipping documents by overland mail, and within two months pawn the goods "on the high seas, or perhaps not yet passed the mouth of the Hoogly ... in Lombard Street — putting the London house in funds eight months before the drafts against those goods fell due." All this functioned "as long as bill-brokers had abundance of money 'at call,' to advance on bills of lading and dock warrants, and to discount, without limit, the bills of India houses drawn upon the eminent firms in Mincing Lane."

Engels appends a remarkable footnote noting that this fraudulent procedure "remained in vogue as long as goods had to sail to and from India round the Cape." With the Suez Canal and steamships, the long journey time (the foundation of the fictitious capital) disappears; and with the telegraph, "the state of the Indian market [is made] known to the English businessman the same day, and the state of the English market to the Indian dealer, such a method has become completely impossible." This is a striking instance of technological change altering the conditions of fictitious capital — a historical-materialist nuance rare in Marx's own text and contributed by his editor.

Note 3 documents the Bank of England's contraction of credit to the Royal Bank of Liverpool in April 1847 and its effects. Payments into Liverpool had become "much more in bills than in cash"; merchants who previously brought cash could bring only bills received for produce. The bills were "of various dates, and of various descriptions; a considerable number of them were bankers' bills, of three months' date, the large bulk being cotton bills." The bills had been accepted by London bankers and merchants "in every trade that we could mention — the Brazilian, the American, the Canadian, the West Indian." Parties in the interior "remitted to the merchants bills on London bankers, or bills on various parties in London, or bills upon anybody." The Bank's announcement caused "a reduction of the maturity terms of bills drawn against sales of foreign products, frequently extending to over three months."

Also in Note 3 comes the railway account. In April 1847 "almost all mercantile houses had begun to starve their business more or less ... by taking part of their commercial capital for railways." Loans on railway shares at 8 per cent had been made by private individuals, bankers, and fire-offices. Heavy lending by commercial houses to railways "induced them to lean too much upon banks by the discount of paper." Yet on the question of whether the railway calls caused the money-market pressure in April and October, the testimony is nuanced: "up to April, and up, perhaps, to the summer, they had increased the power of bankers in some respects rather than diminished it; for the expenditure had not been nearly so rapid as the calls; the consequence was, that most of the banks had rather a large amount of railway money in their hands in the beginning of the year." In summer that melted away; "between the 22nd of April and the 31st of December the railway balances in our hands were reduced one-third; and the railway calls have also had this effect ... throughout the Kingdom; they have been gradually draining the deposits of bankers." Samuel Gurney (of Overend, Gurney and Co.) even claimed the railways "did not increase the value of money" because "there was a condensation of small sums into large masses, and those large masses were used in our market; so that, upon the whole, the effect was to throw more money into the money-market of the City than to take it out." This is a nuance Marx wishes the reader to register: the causal picture is complex, the timing of calls versus expenditures matters, and the bankers themselves testified in partial contradiction to each other.

A. Hodgson, director of the Liverpool Joint-Stock Bank, then demonstrates the collapse of the distinction between a banker's "reserve" and illiquid bills: "It has been our habit to keep at least nine-tenths of all our deposits, and all money we have of other persons, in our bill case, in bills that are falling due from day to day ... so much so, that during the time of the run, the bills falling due were almost equal to the amount of the run upon us day by day." The reserve fund exists only in the form of bills maturing daily — which is safe only if the bills are actually paid.

The "speculative bills" testimony (R. Gardner, cotton manufacturer) describes how the bills were generated: "Produce brokers: a person buys cotton, and places it in the hands of a broker, and draws upon that broker, and gets the bills discounted." They were taken to the Liverpool banks and discounted. Gardner is explicit that "if it had not been for the accommodation thus granted, and principally by the Liverpool banks, cotton would never have been so high last year as it was by 1½d. or 2d. a pound." Hodgson confirms the practice extended to "all kinds of colonial produce, but to cotton most especially," and — crucially — that bankers did not discourage it: "We do not; we consider it a very legitimate description of paper, when kept in moderation. This description of paper is frequently renewed."

Charles Turner's testimony (head of a leading Liverpool East India house) gives vivid evidence of advances "before it was shipped, and in some cases before it was manufactured." He describes buying bills in Calcutta to the extent of £6,000–7,000, the proceeds going down to the Mauritius "to help in the growth of sugar"; when the shipments came forward, above half the bills were protested because the sugar had been mortgaged to third parties "before it was shipped, in fact almost before it was boiled." He then describes the machinery of the Indian trade in 1847: buyers can draw on a London house and get the bill discounted to pay manufacturers cash, because "discounts now are cheap." Since returns from India take at least twelve months, a man with £10,000–15,000 opens a credit with a London house (paying the house one per cent), the understanding being that "the bills are to be renewed till the proceeds come home." The bills are discounted at Liverpool, Manchester, or London, and "many of them lie in the Scotch banks." Question 786 describes a transaction creating £600,000 of bills upon a single underlying operation — Manchester and Calcutta houses with reciprocal credits of £200,000 each against a London house, where the proceeds of bills sold in Calcutta were to buy other bills to remit to London to take up the first bills drawn from Glasgow. Q971 explains that a Calcutta house buying a cargo for England can draw bills on its London correspondent, send the bills of lading home, "and they have eight months' use of the money before their correspondents are called upon to pay."

Note 4 rehearses Mr Lister's testimony before the House of Lords' secret committee of 1848 (evidence published only in 1857). The causes of the crisis: "a man transferred property from business into railways and was still anxious to carry on the same extent of business. He probably first thought that he could sell the railway shares at a profit and replace the money in his business. Perhaps he found that could not be done, and he then got credit in his business where formerly he paid in cash. There was an extension of credit from that circumstance." The losses fell on "bills upon all kinds of produce, corn and cotton and sugar, all foreign produce of all descriptions. There was scarcely anything perhaps with the exception of oil, that did not go down." The structural description of produce bills: a broker accepts only with "a good margin as to the value"; the original bill drawn abroad falls due before the produce arrives; if the merchant lacks capital, he must pledge the produce with the broker, whereupon "a new species of bill is immediately drawn by the merchant in Liverpool upon the broker, on the security of that produce"; and "it is the business of the banker to ascertain from the broker whether he has the produce, and to what extent he has advanced upon it." Lister also notes the epistemic opacity of international bills: "A man buys a bill abroad on England, and sends it to a house in England; we cannot tell whether that bill is drawn prudently or imprudently, whether it is drawn for produce or for wind." Finally, the cause of the losses was "a very large import, and there not being an equal consumption to take it off. It appears that consumption fell off a great deal." And: "In October produce was almost unsaleable."

The panic psychology is captured in Gurney's testimony: "When a panic exists a man does not ask himself what he can get for his bank-notes, or whether he shall lose 1 or 2 per cent by selling his exchequer bills, or 3 per cent. If he is under the influence of alarm he does not care for the profit or loss, but makes himself safe and allows the rest of the world to do as they please." This sauve-qui-peut — every man for himself — is the subjective form of the objective dissolution of the credit system.

Note 5, the coda, returns to the theme of mutual over-trading. Mr Alexander, a merchant in the East India trade, testified in 1857: "At the present moment, if I lay out 6s. in Manchester, I get 5s. back in India; if I lay out 6s. in India, I get 5s. back in London." Marx's gloss: "So that the Indian market was satiated by England and the English market similarly by India. And this was the case in 1857, scarcely ten years after the bitter experience of 1847!" The chapter thus closes with a demonstration that the credit mechanism persists and reproduces its crisis — a single decade suffices for the "bitter experience" to be repeated, evidencing the structural (not accidental) character of the phenomenon.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

What fundamental principles does the chapter establish? The first is the derivation of credit from the money-form itself. Because commodities are routinely sold not for money but for a promise to pay at a future date, the creditor-debtor relation is built into the simplest acts of commodity circulation; credit is therefore not a late invention but a development of money as means of payment. The second is the pyramid structure of the credit system: mutual advances among producers and merchants constitute the "real basis," the bill of exchange is the "basis of credit money proper," and banknotes and deposits are erected on the circulation of bills, themselves resting on a narrow metallic base. The third, and the chapter's title-theme, is fictitious capital: the systemic production of claims (bills, banknotes, deposits, cheques) whose aggregate vastly exceeds the underlying money, and which, so long as no run occurs, perform the functions of money perfectly. The fourth principle is the ungovernability of the distinction between legitimate and fictitious paper — Leatham's confession that "it is impossible to decide what part arises out of real bona fide transactions ... or what part is fictitious" — which undermines any mechanical regulatory solution. The fifth is the cyclical logic of the boom-crisis-revival sequence: cheap credit facilitates speculation (Gilbart's principle), speculation generates fictitious paper, the conversion of bills into claims against money produces a general demand for the base, the base proves too narrow (Leatham's "superstructure" warning), and the whole system undergoes a forced contraction until the suspension of the Bank Act restores confidence by making the credit of the nation stand behind the notes.

The practical implications for the analysis of capitalism are profound. First, the crisis emerging from this chapter is immanent, not accidental — its conditions lie in the normal functioning of credit, not in the folly of individuals. The Manchester manufacturer's question — "How can we ever produce too much? We have 300 million people to clothe" — illustrates the optimism that credit makes possible and the delusion it sustains. Second, the chapter reframes the standard policy debates: the Currency School's obsession with note issue is misplaced, because the deposit and the endorsement chain (not the note) are where fictitious capital really forms. The Bank Act of 1844, which sought to discipline note issue by a rigid rule, is here shown to be "the clever Bank Act" precisely because it intensified the crisis at the moment when flexibility was most needed. Third, the chapter gives a devastating account of how the real economy is subordinated to the credit mechanism: firms produce and ship goods "not because the prices ... promised a profit ... but because former drafts ... would soon fall due, and must be provided for." The circuit of credit becomes autonomous relative to the circuit of production, and producers become servants of the paper they must continually roll over.

The theoretical consequences extend further. The distinction between the "circulation of capital" and the "circulation of currency" (Tooke, referenced here and developed in Chapter 28) separates credit that funds production from credit that merely transfers revenue — a distinction with large implications for the analysis of money demand. The empirical data on the Clearing House, the 15-to-1 ratio of settlements to money, the 9-to-1 ratio of Scottish deposits to currency, the 11-to-1 ratio of convertible liabilities to gold, and the £600,000 of bills erected on a single underlying transaction all express the same formal relation in different registers: credit organizes a vast economy of property transfers that does nothing but refer back, ultimately, to a narrow metallic and institutional base. The consequences of a break in that reference are catastrophic in the technical sense: the paper multiplies in reverse, and banks contract to the metallic foundation. The philosophical implication is that capitalist wealth, in its credit form, is not so much possessed as promised — and that capitalism's endemic tendency to crisis flows from the impossibility of fully reconciling the promises with the means of fulfillment. The chapter thus prepares the theoretical path to Chapters 28, 30, and 34, where Marx will develop the analysis of the Bank Act, the money market, and the relation of credit to the general law of capitalist accumulation. And its closing note on 1857 — the repetition of the same swindle a decade after 1847 — makes clear that the phenomenon is not a story of one crisis but the recurring signature of a system whose normal condition is the persistent overhang of fictitious capital over real wealth.
