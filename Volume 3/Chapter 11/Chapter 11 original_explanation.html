Volume 3 • Chapter 11: Effects of General Wage Fluctuations on Prices of Production

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 11 of Capital, Volume III — "The Effects of General Fluctuations in Wages on the Prices of Production" — occupies a deliberately subordinate yet strategically revealing position within Part II, "The Transformation of Profit into Average Profit." Once Chapters 9 and 10 have established how individual commodity values are "transformed" into prices of production and how a uniform rate of profit is equalized across competing capitals, Chapter 11 asks a follow-up, second-order question: what happens to those already-formed production prices when wages rise or fall everywhere at once?

The central thesis is precise and counterintuitive. A general rise or fall in wages does not change the value of commodities, and it does not change the production price of commodities produced by capital of average social composition — but it pushes the production prices of non-average capitals in opposite directions. A general wage rise raises the production prices of low-composition capitals (those whose outlay is dominated by the wage bill) and lowers the production prices of high-composition capitals (those dominated by constant capital), while a general wage fall produces exactly the reverse. The rises on one side and the falls on the other offset each other, so that the aggregate sum of production prices remains equal to the aggregate sum of values.

The intellectual objective is twofold. First, Marx wants to demonstrate rigorously that a change in wages is purely a redistribution of a value-sum whose magnitude is fixed by the quantity of labour set in motion — wages do not create value. Second, he wants to expose the one-sidedness of David Ricardo, who, in Marx's reading, analyzed only the case of a rising wage and its effect on prices, and who "did not understand the adjustment of values to production prices." The chapter's rhetorical climax is the footnote dismissing Ricardo's followers as the servum pecus imitatorum — the "slavish breed of imitators" — who could not even execute the "self-evident and in fact tautological practical application" of the master's own doctrine.

Methodologically, the chapter is a controlled thought experiment built on numerical examples. It presupposes that the general rate of profit, average profit, and the transformation of values into prices of production are "given facts," and confines itself to tracing how a single disturbance — a uniform change in the wage — ripples through capitals of differing organic composition. Marx himself calls this "a very secondary question compared with the other important points which have been dealt with in this Part." Its importance lies less in its own result than in what it reveals about the mechanics of the transformation and the correct causal ordering of value, profit, and price.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Initial Setup: Average Composition, the Wage Rise, and the Invariant Value-Sum

Marx opens by fixing a baseline. Let the average composition of the social capital be 80c + 20v, with profit at 20 per cent. Because the rate of surplus-value is defined as s/v and here equals 100 per cent (20 surplus-value on 20 variable capital), profit and surplus-value coincide for this average capital. This coincidence is not accidental; it is the defining property of the average capital: its price of production equals its value, because the transformation of value into price of production is, by construction, a redistribution around the average, and the average is the pivot that does not move.

The organic composition of capital is the ratio c:v — the proportion in which total capital is divided between constant capital (machinery, raw materials, auxiliary materials) and variable capital (the wage fund that purchases living labour). The value composition, by contrast, reflects the labour embodied in those inputs. In this chapter, because Marx holds technology and the value of means of production constant, the two track one another, and "composition" functions as a clean index of how much of any given capital is laid out on labour.

The core move is now simple. A general rise in wages, "everything else being equal," means a fall in the rate of surplus-value. Since the same labour-power is purchased, but at a higher price, the surplus extracted from it shrinks. Marx raises wages by 25 per cent. The same quantity of labour that previously cost 20 to set in motion now costs 25. The tableau therefore becomes:

80c + 25v + 15s, instead of 80c + 20v + 20s.

Here is the linchpin of the entire chapter: the labour set in motion by the variable capital still produces a value-sum of 40, exactly as before. The wage change does not alter how much new value the workers create; it only alters how that new value is split between the wage fund (v) and surplus-value (s). Previously v + s = 20 + 20 = 40. Now v + s = 25 + 15 = 40. The magnitude of newly created value is a function of labour time, not of the wage rate — this is Marx's labour theory of value in miniature.

Because v has risen from 20 to 25, the excess s (or p) is now only 15. That profit is realised on a total advanced capital of 105 (80c + 25v), so the new average rate of profit is 15 / 105 = 14 2/7 per cent. (The chapter's OCR renders this as "14t per cent"; the arithmetic confirms 1/7 ≈ 14.2857.)

Now comes the decisive consequence. Because the production price of the average capital coincides with its value, and its value remains 80c + 25v + 15s = 120 (unchanged), the production price of average-composition commodities does not change. The increase in wages therefore produces a decline in profit but no change in the value of commodities or their price of production.

The General Formula: Production Price = k + kp′

Marx now generalises. Before the wage rise, at a 20 per cent average profit rate, the production price of commodities produced in one turnover period equalled their cost price plus 20 per cent profit on that cost price:

k + kp′ = k + (20/100)k,

where k is the cost price — a variable magnitude that differs according to (a) the value of the means of production consumed and (b) the amount of depreciation that fixed capital surrenders to the product. Cost price k, in other words, is not the same across industries; it lumps together the worn-out constant capital transferred to the product plus the variable capital advanced.

After the wage rise, the same formula reappears with a new profit rate:

k + (14 2/7 / 100)k.

The essential structure is now visible: the production price is a function of two moving parts — the cost price k (which shifts because wages are part of it) and the profit rate p′ (which shifts because the general rate of profit has fallen). Everything that follows is the working-out of how these two movements interact differently according to the organic composition of the capital in question. Marx also notes that the average composition has itself changed — from 80c + 20v to a new percentage form of 76 4/21 c + 23 17/21 v (i.e., 80c + 25v reduced to a percentage of the 105 total).

Case II — Below-Average Composition (50c + 50v): The Price Rises

Marx now takes a capital below the original average composition — one heavily weighted toward labour, with a composition of 50c + 50v. He assumes, "for the sake of simplification," that all fixed capital is used up in the annual product as depreciation and that the turnover time matches the earlier case — a simplification that lets him treat the annual product as a single turnover and compare prices directly.

Before the wage rise, this capital's annual product sold at 50c + 50v + 20p = 120 (cost price 100, profit 20 at the 20 per cent rate).

A 25 per cent wage rise lifts variable capital from 50 to 62 1/2, for the same amount of labour set in motion. If the product were still sold at 120, the breakdown would be 50c + 62 1/2 v + 7 1/2 s, yielding a profit rate of only 7.5 / 112.5 = 6 2/3 per cent — well below the new social average. But competition equalises profit rates. The capital of 50c + 62 1/2 v = 112 1/2 must now earn the new average rate of 14 2/7 per cent:

112 1/2 × (14 2/7) / 100 = 112 1/2 / 7 = 16 1/14.

The production price is therefore 50c + 62 1/2 v + 16 1/14 p = 128 4/7. (The running text's figures "128 1/14" reflect OCR distortion; the accompanying statement that the price has risen "by more than 7 per cent" confirms the correct total, 128 4/7, since 128.5714 / 120 ≈ 1.0714 — a rise of just over 7 per cent.) So a 25 per cent wage rise lifts the production price of this low-composition product from 120 to 128 4/7, an increase of more than 7 per cent.

Why does the price rise? Because for a labour-intensive capital, the wage rise inflates the cost price k dramatically (from 100 to 112.5), and although the profit rate falls, the profit magnitude is now applied to a much larger base, so the gross profit (16 1/14) is not enough to offset the swollen cost. The two movements — higher cost, lower rate — net out to a higher price.

Case III — Above-Average Composition (92c + 8v): The Price Falls

Marx then takes a capital above the average composition, heavily weighted toward constant capital: 92c + 8v. Under identical assumptions (full depreciation, same turnover), its original production price is likewise 120 (cost price 100 + 20 profit).

The 25 per cent wage rise lifts variable capital from 8 to 10, so the cost price grows only from 100 to 102 — a mere 2-unit increase, because so little of this capital is spent on wages. The average profit rate again falls to 14 2/7 per cent. Applying the proportional relationship:

100 : 14 2/7 = 102 : 14 4/7.

The profit accruing to a cost price of 102 is therefore 14 4/7, and the product sells at 102 + 14 4/7 = 116 4/7. The production price has fallen from 120 to 116 4/7 — a fall of 3 3/7.

Here the mechanism is reversed: because wages are such a small fraction of outlay, the cost price barely moves (100 → 102), but the profit rate collapses from 20 per cent to 14 2/7 per cent. The reduced rate, applied to a nearly unchanged base, yields a profit too small to sustain the old price. Hence the price falls.

The Three Results, the Balancing, and the "No Compensation" Argument

Marx crystallises the findings:

(I) For capital of average social composition, the commodity's price of production remains unchanged. (II) For capital of lower composition, the production price rises — though not in the same ratio as the profit has fallen. (III) For capital of higher composition, the production price falls — though again not in the same ratio as the profit.

The crucial aggregate result follows immediately. Because the average capital's production price is unchanged and equals its value, the sum of production prices over all capitals is also unchanged, equal to the sum of values produced by the total capital. The rises in Case II and the falls in Case III "balance out at the level of the socially average capital," when the whole capital of society is considered. This is the invariance principle of the transformation: redistribution among branches leaves the total intact.

Marx then drives home the political-economic point. The opposite price movements already show there can be no compensation for the wage rise through prices:

  • In Case III, the fall in the price of production "can in no way compensate the capitalists for the fall in their profit."
  • In Case II, the rise in price "still does not prevent a fall in profit."

In both cases — whether the price rises or falls — the profit actually realised is the same as for the average capital: a fall in the average profit of 5 5/7 percentage points (the OCR "Sf per cent"), which is "somewhat over 25 per cent" of the original 20 per cent rate (5 5/7 ÷ 20 = 2/7 ≈ 28.57 per cent). Marx spells out the logic of counterfactual necessity: if the price did not rise in Case II and fall in Case III, then II would be sold below the new lower average profit and III above it — and competition would not have equalised profit rates. The price movements are the mechanism by which equalisation to the new, lower average rate is accomplished. This is why the effect is so uneven: "according to whether 50, 25 or 10 out of every 100 units of capital are laid out on labour, a rise in wages will necessarily have very different effects" on the three capitalists.

The Reverse Case: A General Fall in Wages

Marx now asks the mirror-image question — "a result which Ricardo does not investigate": what happens to production prices when wages fall generally, with a corresponding rise in the average profit rate? His method is explicit: "We have simply to turn the above example round."

Case I. Average capital, 80c + 20v = 100, surplus-value rate 100 per cent, production price = value = 80c + 20v + 20s = 120, profit rate 20 per cent. Wages fall by a quarter: variable capital drops from 20 to 15, while the same constant capital is still set in motion. The commodity value becomes 80c + 15v + 25s = 120 — still 120. The labour's newly created value is unchanged; only its distribution between capitalist and worker shifts. Surplus-value rises from 20 to 25 (a rate of surplus-value of 25/15 = 166 2/3 per cent). Profit is now 25 on a total capital of 95 (80c + 15v), giving a profit rate of 26 6/19 per cent (25/95 = 5/19). The new percentage composition becomes 84 4/19 c + 15 15/19 v = 100.

Case II. Below-average composition, originally 50c + 50v. The quarter wage cut reduces v to 37 1/2, so total capital is 50c + 37 1/2 v = 87 1/2. Applying the new profit rate of 26 6/19 per cent:

100 : 26 6/19 = 87 1/2 : 23 1/38.

The same mass of commodities that previously cost 120 now costs 87 1/2 + 23 1/38 = 110 10/19 — a fall in price of almost 10.

Case III. Above-average composition, originally 92c + 8v = 100. The quarter wage cut reduces 8v to 6v, and total capital to 98. Applying 26 6/19 per cent:

100 : 26 6/19 = 98 : 25 15/19.

The production price, previously 100 + 20 = 120, is now 98 + 25 15/19 = 123 15/19 — a rise of almost 4.

The conclusion is the exact mirror image. A general fall in wages leads to a general rise in surplus-value, in the rate of surplus-value, and (other things equal, though in a different proportion) in the profit rate. It causes a fall in the production prices of low-composition capitals and a rise in those of high-composition capitals — "exactly the opposite result as that which arose from a general rise in wages."

The Assumption Set: Constant Working Day, Constant Subsistence Prices

Marx is careful to fence off the argument. In both the rise and the fall scenarios, two things are held constant: the working day and the prices of all necessary means of subsistence. These assumptions matter enormously, because they define what the exercise actually isolates. If the working day were allowed to vary, or if the value of wage-goods were allowed to change, the effects would compound and the clean result would be muddied.

The corollary Marx draws is a logically tight one: under these assumptions, "a fall in wages is thus only possible here either if wages previously stood above the normal price of labour, or if they are now to be pushed below it." In other words, absent a change in the working day or in the value of subsistence goods, a general wage change can only be a deviation from the value of labour-power — above-natural or below-subsistence — not a change in that value itself.

The Qualification: When Wage Changes Originate in Subsistence Values

Marx then adds the qualification that anticipates the later section on ground-rent. If the rise or fall in wages results from a change in the value of the necessary means of subsistence (rather than from a redistribution of a fixed wage box), a single modification to the above analysis is required: the argument holds except when the commodities whose price-changes raise or lower variable capital also enter as constituent elements into constant capital — that is, when the same cheapened or dearened goods are both wage-goods and industrial inputs. In that case, the change in their value does not merely alter wages; it also alters c. "But in so far as they do only affect wages, the above argument contains all that has to be said." This is a precise scoping condition: the clean two-case result depends on the wage-change being isolated to the variable-capital channel.

Methodological Framing and the Polemic against Ricardo

The chapter closes by reasserting its own status. Throughout, Marx has assumed that the establishment of a general rate of profit, an average profit, and hence the transformation of values into production prices is "a given fact." The only question posed has been how a general wage change affects production prices that are "given in advance." Marx concedes this is "a very secondary question compared with the other important points" of Part II — but it happens to be "the only question Ricardo deals with which is relevant here," and Ricardo treats it "only in a one-sided and inadequate way."

The footnotes sharpen the polemic. Footnote 34 charges that Ricardo "did not understand the adjustment of values to production prices," and therefore "did not once consider this possibility [of a wage fall], but only the first case, a rise in wages and its influence on the production prices of commodities." By working only the wage-rise case, Ricardo missed the symmetry that Marx's method makes visible. The footnote then derides Ricardo's followers — the servum pecus imitatorum (Horace's "slavish breed of imitators") — for failing even to perform the "quite self-evident and in fact tautological practical application" of the doctrine. The closing note cross-references Theories of Surplus-Value, Part II, pp. 189–203, where Marx develops this critique at length.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Chapter 11 distils several fundamental principles. First, and most important, value is created by labour, not by wages; a general wage change merely redistributes a value-sum fixed by the quantity of labour set in motion. Second, the average capital is the invariant pivot of the price system: its production price equals its value and does not move when wages change, which is precisely why the sum of all production prices equals the sum of all values. Third, and this is the chapter's signature contribution, a uniform input-price shock propagates through different branches in opposite directions according to organic composition — a rise in wages lifts the production prices of labour-intensive capitals and lowers those of capital-intensive ones. This is the mechanism by which the new, lower (or higher) general rate of profit is equalised.

The broader theoretical implications are substantial. Against Ricardo, Marx shows that the analysis of wage effects on prices is inseparable from the theory of the transformation of values into production prices: one cannot correctly trace the price consequences of a wage change without first having a rigorous account of how a general profit rate is formed. The chapter also demonstrates that there is no price-level compensation for a wage change — the "wage-price spiral" is not a mechanism that restores capitalists' profits in real terms. On the contrary, in both the price-raising and price-lowering branches, the profit rate falls to the new average, and no branch escapes.

Finally, the chapter is a model of method as much as doctrine: it isolates a single variable (the wage), fixes everything else (working day, subsistence values, technology), traces the two symmetrical cases, and carefully notes the one condition under which the clean result would be qualified (when wage-goods are also constant-capital inputs). In doing so, it illuminates the deepest architecture of Volume III — the priority of value over price, the derivative character of profit rates, and the systemic, divergent, but ultimately invariant-preserving redistribution that constitutes the transformation from values into prices of production.
