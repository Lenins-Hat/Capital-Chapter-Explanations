Volume 3 • Chapter 10: Equalization of the General Rate of Profit through Competition

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem and Its Stakes

Chapter 10 of Capital, Volume III, stands at the strategic hinge of Marx's entire value-theoretic project. Its formal task is to answer a question that Marx himself flags as "the really difficult question": how does the equalization of profit rates among different spheres of production actually come about through competition? The difficulty is acute because the general rate of profit is manifestly a result of competitive processes and cannot be presupposed as a point of departure. If we assume equal profit rates from the start, we smuggle in the very thing to be explained. If we do not assume them — if we begin from the premise that commodities exchange at their values — we are forced to confront the fact that different spheres of production, with different organic compositions of capital, would then yield radically different profit rates. The chapter is Marx's attempt to get from the first state of affairs (value prices, unequal profit rates) to the second (prices of production, equal profit rates) without abandoning the labour theory of value.

The chapter's core thesis can be stated in three interlocking propositions:

  1. Prices of production are transformed forms of value. The equalization of the general rate of profit does not abolish the law of value; it realizes it at a higher level of capitalist development. "It is in this way that there prevails, and necessarily so, a tendency to make production prices into mere transformed forms of value, or to transform profits into mere portions of surplus-value."

  2. The general rate of profit is the total surplus-value distributed among capitals in proportion to their size. "The sum of the profits for all the different spheres of production must accordingly be equal to the sum of surplus-values, and the sum of prices of production for the total social product must be equal to the sum of its values." This is the famous "two equalities" — the aggregate identity that anchors the transformation of values into prices.

  3. Market value and market price are distinct determinations, and demand and supply explain neither. The chapter develops a sophisticated theory of market value (the social value of a whole branch's product) and of market price (the fluctuating price around which actual transactions occur). Marx argues that demand and supply can explain only the divergences of market price from market value, not the market value itself. "The real inner laws of capitalist production clearly cannot be explained in terms of the interaction of demand and supply... since these laws are realized in their pure form only when demand and supply cease to operate, i.e. when they coincide."

The Chapter's Architecture

The chapter moves through roughly five movements. First, it opens with the anchoring case of capitals of "mean composition" and the formula k + kp', establishes the tendency toward equalization around an "ideal mean position," and asserts the transformation of prices into transformed values. Second, it poses the difficult question (equalization as result) and conducts a thought experiment about commodity producers who own their own means of production, establishing the historical and theoretical priority of exchange at values over exchange at prices of production. Third, it develops the theory of market value: individual value versus social value, the three cases of market-value determination, the Ricardo–Storch controversy, and the role of demand pressure. Fourth, it dismantles the demand-and-supply explanation of value, exposing the tautology, the cancellation of the two forces, and the circular determination of price and demand/supply. Fifth, it returns to competition as the mechanism of equalization, specifies the social preconditions of capital and labour mobility, and derives the "capitalist freemasonry" — the structural unity of the capitalist class against the working class — before concluding with the concept of surplus profit.

The Intellectual Objective

Marx's objective is not merely technical (to solve the transformation problem) but polemical and philosophical. He is simultaneously:

  • Correcting Ricardo, who senses that prices of production depart from values but treats the inquiry as one into relative rather than absolute value, and who fails to grasp the average case in market-value determination.
  • Refuting the "vulgar economists" who treat prices of production as the ultimate data and who invoke "demand and supply" as if it explained everything — when in fact it explains nothing fundamental.
  • Demonstrating the historical materialist point that value exchange corresponds to a lower stage of development (peasant proprietors, handicraftsmen, guilds) and that prices of production require a definite degree of capitalist development.
  • Revealing the class content of the equalization process: capital, as "a social power," unites against labour even as individual capitals compete destructively among themselves.

Fit Within the Broader Framework

The chapter presupposes the results of Chapters 8 and 9 of Volume III (the formation of a general rate of profit from the different rates in different spheres, and the transformation of values into prices of production) and prepares the ground for the theory of ground-rent. It also presupposes Volume I's theory of value (socially necessary labour-time, the twofold character of the commodity, the metamorphosis of commodities) and A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (cited repeatedly in footnotes). It is, therefore, the passage point where the abstract law of value from Volume I is shown to operate concretely through the competitive surface of capitalist society. The chapter thus performs the crucial reconciliation: the law of value governs the movement of prices of production even though individual prices systematically depart from individual values.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Anchoring Case: Capitals of Mean Composition and the Formula k + kp'

Marx begins by isolating a privileged subset of capitals: those whose composition is "mean" or "average" — that is, whose ratio of constant to variable capital (their organic composition) is exactly or approximately the same as the average of the total social capital. In these spheres, the prices of production of commodities "coincide exactly or approximately with their values as expressed in money." This equality is the pivotal fact of the whole chapter, because it provides the mathematical and conceptual anchor for the equalization process.

The formula Marx gives is k + kp' — "cost price plus the product of the average rate of profit and the cost price." Let us unpack every term:

  • k is the cost price: the sum of the value of the means of production consumed (constant capital, c) plus the value of labour-power advanced as wages (variable capital, v). In the simple case where the entire capital advanced is consumed in the production period (no fixed capital carry-over), k = c + v.
  • p' is the average, or general, rate of profit, expressed as a fraction (e.g., 0.20 for 20 percent).
  • kp' is therefore the profit imputed to this cost price under the general rate — the average profit.
  • The price of production is thus k(1 + p').

There is an important technical nuance here. In Marx's fuller treatment, the price of production is k + p'·C, where C is the total capital advanced (including fixed capital not consumed in the period). The shorthand k + kp' is equivalent when the cost price equals the total capital advanced — that is, when there is no distinction between capital consumed and capital advanced. The text uses the shorthand, and we should note that it presupposes this simplified condition. Its function is pedagogical: it shows that the price of production is cost price plus the average profit, and that for mean-composition capitals this sum equals value.

Why does the equality hold for mean capitals? Because if a capital's composition equals the social average, its rate of profit equals the general rate, and its surplus-value equals the average profit. Competition then distributes social capital across spheres "in such a way that the prices of production in each of these spheres are formed after the model of the prices of production in the spheres of mean composition." The mean-composition spheres serve as the model or norm: their price of production is their value, and all other spheres are adjusted toward this model.

The Tendency Toward an "Ideal" Mean Position

Marx immediately qualifies the anchor: "Between these spheres that approximate more or less to the social average, there is again a tendency to equalization, which seeks the 'ideal' mean position, i.e. a mean position which does not exist in reality." This is a subtle but crucial dialectical point. The mean position is not an empirically observable sphere; it is an ideal norm — a gravitational center that real capitals approximate but never perfectly attain. The equalization process is therefore asymptotic: it "tends to shape itself around this ideal as a norm."

This introduces a methodological theme that recurs throughout the chapter: economic laws operate as tendencies, not as exact empirical regularities. The "general rate of profit" is a theoretical construction that captures the central tendency of competitive capitalism, not a number observable in any single transaction. This is the same logic Marx applies later to the equal rate of surplus-value, which he explicitly calls "a theoretical simplification... a tendency, like all economic laws."

Prices of Production as Transformed Forms of Value

The opening movement culminates in the identity that defines the transformation:

"In this way there prevails, and necessarily so, a tendency to make production prices into mere transformed forms of value, or to transform profits into mere portions of surplus-value that are distributed not in proportion to the surplus-value that is created in each particular sphere of production, but rather in proportion to the amount of capital applied in each of these spheres, so that equal amounts of capital, no matter how they are composed, receive equal shares (aliquot parts) of the totality of surplus-value produced by the total social capital."

Several concepts require definition here:

  • Transformed form of value: the price of production is not a replacement for value but a form of it — value in its competitive, redistributed appearance. The substance (socially necessary labour-time) remains; the form (price) is altered by the equalization of profit rates.
  • Aliquot parts: proportional shares. Just as 3 is an aliquot part of 12 (12/4), a capital of a given size is an aliquot part of the total social capital and receives a corresponding share of total surplus-value. The term emphasizes that distribution is proportional, not arbitrary.
  • Surplus-value created in each sphere versus surplus-value realized in each sphere: this is the heart of the transformation. A labor-intensive sphere creates more surplus-value than it realizes (because its price of production is lower than its value); a capital-intensive sphere creates less than it realizes. The equality of profit rates redistributes the total.

Marx then draws the two aggregate conclusions:

  • "The sum of the profits for all the different spheres of production must accordingly be equal to the sum of surplus-values."
  • "The sum of prices of production for the total social product must be equal to the sum of its values."

These are the two equalities that ground the entire transformation procedure. They guarantee that the redistribution is zero-sum with respect to total value: nothing is added or subtracted in the aggregate; only the distribution among spheres changes.

Two Supplementary Points: The Invariance of the Average Profit

Marx adds two clarifications to ward off misunderstandings:

First, wherever an average profit (general rate of profit) is established, "this average profit can be nothing other than the profit on the average social capital, the total sum of profit being equal to the total sum of surplus-value." The average profit is thus a macroeconomic magnitude determined at the level of total social capital — not a microeconomic datum derived from individual firms.

Second, the prices formed by adding this average profit to cost prices "can be nothing other than the values which have been transformed into prices of production." Again, the transformation is a form-change, not a substance-change.

Marx then anticipates an objection: what if some spheres are not subjected to equalization (e.g., because of monopoly, or because they are non-capitalist)? His answer is robust: "It would change nothing... The average profit would then be calculated on the portion of the social capital that was involved in the equalization process." The average profit is still "the total mass of surplus-value, distributed between the masses of capital in each sphere of production in proportion to their size." This is a significant concession to realism: the theory does not require that all capital be equalized, only that the equalized portion determines the general rate.

The phrase "the sum total of the realized unpaid labour" identifies surplus-value with unpaid labour time, and notes that this total is "represented, just like the paid labour, dead and living, in the total mass of commodities and money that accrues to the capitalists." The material basis of profit is the total labour-time — paid and unpaid, objectified and living — embodied in the social product.

The Concealed Difficulty: Equalization as Result, Not Premise

The chapter now poses its central question explicitly:

"The really difficult question here is this: how does this equalization lead to a general rate of profit, since this is evidently a result and cannot be a point of departure?"

This is the methodological crux. Marx refuses the Ricardian shortcut of assuming a general rate of profit. He insists on deriving it from the competitive process. The difficulty is that the general rate is both the outcome of equalization and the standard by which prices of production are formed. This is a genuine circularity that must be resolved diachronically (as a tendency over time) rather than synchronically (as a simultaneous equation).

The first step in the resolution is to note that "an assessment of commodity values in money... can only be a result of exchanging them." Value is not a pre-given magnitude stamped on commodities before exchange; it is realized in exchange. This prepares the historical argument that value exchange and price-of-production exchange correspond to different stages of development.

The Initial Hypothesis: Universal Sale at Value and the Resulting Chaos of Profit Rates

Marx conducts a thought experiment: "Let us assume to start with that all commodities in the various spheres of production were sold at their actual values. What would happen then? According to our above arguments, very different rates of profit would prevail in the various spheres of production."

The contrast is starkly stated: "It is, prima facie, a very different matter whether commodities are sold at their values... or whether they are sold at prices which make their sale yield equal profits on equal amounts of the capitals advanced for their respective production." If values governed, profit rates would diverge according to organic composition; if prices of production governed, profit rates would equalize but values would be systematically violated. The chapter's task is to show how the second state emerges from the first through competition.

The Equal Rate of Surplus-Value as Theoretical Presupposition: Worker Migration and the English Settlement Laws

Before proceeding, Marx specifies a crucial assumption: that the level of exploitation, or rate of surplus-value, is the same across spheres, "at least to a certain extent." This assumption rests on "competition among the workers, and an equalization that takes place by their constant migration between one sphere of production and another."

He immediately qualifies this with characteristic realism:

"We assume a general rate of surplus-value of this kind, as a tendency, like all economic laws, and as a theoretical simplification; but in any case this is in practice an actual presupposition of the capitalist mode of production, even if inhibited to a greater or lesser extent by practical frictions that produce more or less significant local differences, such as the settlement laws for agricultural labourers in England, for example."

The settlement laws (the old Poor Law settlement provisions) restricted the geographical mobility of agricultural labourers, thereby preventing the equalization of wage rates and exploitation levels between agriculture and industry. Marx cites them as a concrete example of a friction that makes the theoretical tendency approximate rather than exact. This is an important nuance: the theory does not deny frictions; it treats them as deviations from a tendency that operates "all the more exact, the more the capitalist mode of production is developed and the less it is adulterated by survivals of earlier economic conditions."

Why Commodities Are Not Exchanged as Commodities but as Products of Capital

The decisive conceptual move follows:

"The whole difficulty arises from the fact that commodities are not exchanged simply as commodities, but as the products of capitals, which claim shares in the total mass of surplus-value according to their size, equal shares for equal size."

This is the theoretical pivot of the chapter. In simple commodity production (Volume I's world), commodities exchange in proportion to the labour embodied in them. In capitalist production, commodities are the products of capitals, and each capital claims a share of total surplus-value proportional to its size — not proportional to the labour it directly employs. The total price of a capital's product "has to satisfy this demand." The equalization of profit rates is thus not a violation of value but the specific form that value distribution takes when production is organized by capital.

The Thought Experiment of Independent Producers: Profit Rates as Indifferent

To clarify, Marx imagines workers who own their means of production and exchange commodities with one another. These commodities "would not be products of capital." In this hypothetical:

  • The value of means and materials of labour varies by branch.
  • Different masses of means of production are required for a given amount of labour (a commodity might be prepared in one hour or take a day).
  • Assume equal working time (adjusted for intensity).

Under these conditions, each worker replaces their outlays (cost prices) and creates equal new value (wages + surplus-value, though the surplus belongs to themselves). If we translate into capitalist terms, "they would both receive the same wages plus the same profit, which would be equal to the value expressed in the product, say, of a 10-hour working day."

Crucially, the profit rates would differ, because profit rate is defined as the ratio of surplus-value to total value laid out on means of production (including means of subsistence). Worker I might have higher outlays (more constant capital) and thus a lower profit rate than Worker II. Yet "under these conditions, the difference in the profit rate would be a matter of indifference." Marx draws two analogies:

  • Wage-labourer analogy: "just as for a present-day wage-labourer it is a matter of indifference in what profit rate the surplus-value extorted from him is expressed."
  • International trade analogy: "just as in international trade the differences in profit rates between different nations are completely immaterial as far as the exchange of their commodities is concerned."

The point is that profit-rate equalization is a specific requirement of capital, not a universal requirement of exchange. Where producers own their means of production, unequal profit rates are perfectly compatible with exchange at value.

Historical Priority of Value Over Price of Production

Marx elevates the thought experiment into a historical claim:

"The exchange of commodities at their values, or at approximately these values, thus corresponds to a much lower stage of development than the exchange at prices of production, for which a definite degree of capitalist development is needed."

This is the famous "historical transformation" argument. It rests on the observation that value exchange prevails "among peasant proprietors and handicraftsmen who work for themselves" — in both the ancient and modern world. Marx links this to his earlier view (footnoted by Engels) that "the development of products into commodities arises from exchange between different communities, and not between the members of one and the same community."

Engels's footnote is historically significant: what was "simply Marx's 'opinion'" in 1865 had become, by the time of Engels's editing, "an established fact scarcely anywhere contested," thanks to the investigations of Georg Ludwig von Maurer (on the primitive German mark community) and Lewis Henry Morgan (Ancient Society, 1877). Engels notes that Marx "discovered" Morgan's book and made annotated extracts, partly used by Engels in The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884). This footnote documents the empirical grounding of Marx's historical claim: that private property in land is a subsequent development, and that commodity exchange originates between communities.

Marx extends the historical argument: value exchange also characterizes "later social conditions based on slavery and serfdom, and for the guild organization of handicraft production, as long as the means of production involved in each branch of production can be transferred from one sphere to another only with difficulty, and the different spheres of production therefore relate to one another, within certain limits, like foreign countries or communistic communities." The immobility of capital and labour — the very obstacles to equalization — preserve the regime of value prices. It is the mobility of capital, developed by capitalism, that makes price-of-production exchange possible.

The Law of Value as the Regulator of Price Movements

Marx states the law of value's regulatory role:

"Whatever may be the ways in which the prices of different commodities are first established or fixed in relation to one another, the law of value governs their movement. When the labour-time required for their production falls, prices fall; and where it rises, prices rise, as long as other circumstances remain equal."

This is a crucial delimitation: the law of value governs the movement of prices (comparative statics), even if individual prices depart from values. It is the differential of labour-time that drives the differential of prices.

The Three Conditions for Exchange at Value

Marx specifies what is needed for prices to correspond approximately to values:

  1. Exchange must cease to be purely accidental or merely occasional. Regular, repeated exchange is required.
  2. Direct exchange must involve commodities produced in relative quantities approximately corresponding to mutual need. This is "learned from the reciprocal experience of trading and which therefore arises precisely as a result of continuing exchange."
  3. No natural or artificial monopolies may enable one party to sell above value or force another to sell below value. "By accidental monopoly, we mean the monopoly that accrues to buyer or seller as a result of the accidental state of supply and demand."

These conditions define the ideal case of value exchange, and their gradual fulfillment is itself a historical process.

Market Value Emerges: Individual Value, Social Value, and the Modal Producer

Marx now introduces the crucial distinction between individual value and market value (or social value):

"The individual value of some of these commodities will stand below the market value (i.e. less labour-time has been required for their production than the market value expresses), the value of others above it."

Market value is defined in two complementary ways:

  1. "the average value of the commodities produced in a particular sphere," and
  2. "the individual value of commodities produced under average conditions in the sphere in question, and forming the great mass of its commodities."

The market value is thus the center of gravity around which market prices fluctuate — "these being the same for all commodities of the same species." Only "in extraordinary situations" do commodities produced under the worst or best conditions govern the market value.

The consequence for individual producers is decisive: if the supply at average value satisfies customary demand, then "the commodities whose individual value stands below the market price will realize an extra surplus-value or surplus profit, while those whose individual value stands above the market price will be unable to realize a part of the surplus-value which they contain." This is the origin of surplus profit (extra surplus-value) — the differential advantage accruing to lower-cost producers. It is a social redistribution within the sphere, not a creation of new value.

The Ricardo–Storch Controversy and the Resolution Through the Average Case

Marx refutes the either/or framing of the classical controversy:

"The controversy between Storch and Ricardo in connection with ground-rent... over the question whether market value (in their terms market price or price of production) is governed by commodities produced under the least favourable conditions (Ricardo) or the most favourable (Storch), is thus resolved in this way, that both are right and both are wrong, and also that both have entirely omitted to consider the average case."

Henri Storch (1766–1835), a Russian popularizer of classical political economy writing in French (Cours d'économie politique, 1815), held that market value is governed by the most favorable conditions. David Ricardo held that it is governed by the least favorable (worst) conditions — the marginal, highest-cost producer. Marx's resolution is dialectical: both can govern, depending on the proportions in which commodities are produced under different conditions and on the state of demand. What both missed is the average or middling case, which Marx treats as the norm.

Marx also cites Thomas Corbet (An Inquiry into the Causes and Modes of the Wealth of Individuals, 1841) on cases where price is governed by best-condition commodities, and quotes the anonymous Observations on Certain Verbal Disputes in Political Economy (1821): the relevant unit is "the description of commodity, not a particular individual hat, pair of shoes, etc. The whole labour which produces all the hats in England is to be considered, to this purpose, as divided among all the hats." This supports the macro-level determination of market value.

The Three Cases of Market-Value Determination

Marx systematically analyzes three cases:

Case I: Average conditions govern. Suppose "great quantities of these commodities are produced in something like the same normal social conditions, so that this value is also the individual value of the individual commodities making up this mass." A small proportion is produced in worse conditions (individual value above the mean) and another in better conditions (individual value below the mean). "These two extremes will cancel one another out, so that the average value of the commodities at the extremes is the same as the value of the mass of average commodities, and the market value is determined by the value of the commodities produced under average conditions." Strictly speaking, "the market value of the entire mass, as governed by the average values, is equal to the sum of its individual values." Those at the worst extreme sell below their individual value; those at the best extreme sell above it.

Case II: Worst conditions govern. Suppose "the value of those produced under worse conditions is not balanced out by the value of those produced under better conditions, so that the part of the total produced under worse conditions forms a relatively significant quantity, both vis-à-vis the average mass and vis-à-vis the opposite extreme." Then "it is the mass produced under the worse conditions that governs the market, or social, value." The market value is above the individual value of the favorable extreme and above the middle stratum, but below the individual value of the worst extreme. How close it comes to the worst extreme's individual value "depends completely on the volume of the commodities produced at the unfavourable extreme." If demand is only marginally predominant, the worst extreme's individual value governs the market price.

Case III: Best conditions govern. Suppose "the mass of commodities produced under better-than-average conditions significantly exceeds that produced under worse conditions and is itself of significant magnitude in relation to that produced under average conditions." Then "the market value would be regulated by the part produced under the most favourable conditions." The average value falls below the middle value; "if demand is weak in relation to supply, the favourably situated part, however big it might be, forcibly makes room for itself by drawing the price towards its individual value." The market value "can never coincide with this individual value... except in cases where supply sharply outweighs demand."

Marx explicitly brackets the case of general over-supply, where market price diverges from market value, because "here we are not dealing with market price in so far as this differs from market value, but simply with the various determinations of this market value itself."

The Role of Demand Pressure in Shifting the Governing Condition

Demand pressure interacts with the proportions of production conditions:

  • If the quantity supplied is less than demand, "it is always the commodities produced under the worst conditions that govern the market value."
  • If the quantity supplied is more than demand, "it is those produced under the best conditions."
  • "If the difference between the demand for the product and the quantity produced is more significant, the market price will diverge more sharply from the market value."

The divergence between quantity produced and quantity saleable at market value "can arise for two reasons":

  1. Supply changes: "reproduction would take place on a scale different from that which regulated the given market value" — relative overproduction or underproduction.
  2. Demand changes: "the reproduction, i.e. the supply, remains the same, but demand rises or falls" — the relative magnitude of supply changes.

If both change, the result still reduces to one of these two cases.

The Buyer's Side: Demand as Social Need with Money Behind It

Marx turns to the second condition for sale at value: that the commodity have use-value, i.e., "satisfies some kind of social need." For an individual commodity, the need could be taken as given; for "the product of a whole branch of production," the quantitative extent of social need becomes "a factor of fundamental importance."

The volume of social need must be considered. If the quantity supplied equals the customary demand, the commodity sells at its market value. "The mass of commodities not only satisfies a need, but it satisfies this need on its social scale."

The Tautology of Demand and Supply

Marx exposes the circularity:

"The real difficulty in pinning down the general concepts of demand and supply is that we seem to end up with a tautology."

  • Statement 1: Demand and supply coincide when the mass of commodities produced can be sold at its market value.
  • Statement 2: When commodities can be sold at their market value, demand and supply coincide.

"These two statements are identical." The definition of coincidence is circular.

Supply Defined: Physical Quantity and Labour-Time in a Fortuitous Relation to Need

Marx defines supply concretely: "the product which is actually on sale in the market or can be delivered to it" — the annual reproduction of each branch. This is expressed first as a definite quantity (measure or number) and second as a definite market value (a multiple of the individual commodity's market value).

Crucially:

"There is no necessary connection between the quantitative volume of commodities existing on the market and their market value."

Some commodities have generically high value, others low, so a given sum of value may be embodied in very small or very large quantities. The only connection is that "on a given basis of labour productivity in the sphere of production in question, the production of a particular quantity of this article requires a particular quantity of social labour-time." This proportion varies across spheres and has "no intrinsic connection with the usefulness of the article."

Marx then states the fundamental relation:

"There is no necessary connection, however, but simply a fortuitous one, between on the one hand the total quantity of social labour that is spent on a social article... and on the other hand the proportion in which the society demands satisfaction of the need appeased by that particular article."

This is a profound point. The allocation of social labour to particular branches is not automatically coordinated with social need under commodity production. It is coordinated only ex post, through price fluctuations, crises, and waste.

Wasted Labour and the Sale of Commodities Below Value

If production exceeds social need, "a part of the society's labour-time is wasted, and the mass of commodities in question then represents on the market a much smaller quantity of social labour than it actually contains." These commodities "must therefore be got rid of at less than their market value, and a portion of them may even be completely unsaleable." The converse holds if too little labour is spent.

Marx adds a parenthetical that points toward socialist planning: "(Only when production is subjected to the genuine, prior control of society will society establish the connection between the amount of social labour-time applied to the production of particular articles, and the scale of the social need to be satisfied by these.)" This is the ex ante coordination that capitalism lacks.

The normative conclusion:

"The exchange or sale of commodities at their value is the rational, natural law of the equilibrium between them; this is the basis on which divergences have to be explained, and not the converse, i.e. the law of equilibrium should not be derived from contemplating the divergences."

This is a methodological manifesto: equilibrium (value) is the norm; disequilibrium (price divergence) is the deviation to be explained.

Demand Defined: Productive and Consumer Demand, the Capitalist as Buyer

Demand is analyzed into two components:

  1. Productive demand (from capitalists, for means of production).
  2. Consumer demand (individual consumption).

Both at first appear to presuppose "a given volume of social needs." If the cotton industry is to reproduce at a given level, it requires the usual amount of cotton; accumulation requires additional quantities. Workers need the same (or somewhat differently distributed) means of subsistence; population growth requires additional quantities. The same holds for other classes.

But Marx immediately destabilizes this apparent fixity:

"In fact, however, the quantitative determination of this need is completely elastic and fluctuating. Its fixed character is mere illusion."

If means of subsistence were cheaper or money wages higher, workers would buy more; a greater "social need" would appear. Paupers' "demand" is below the narrowest physical need. If cotton became cheaper, capitalists' demand would grow, more excess capital would enter the industry, and so on.

Marx stresses the peculiar nature of capitalist productive demand:

"It must never be forgotten in this connection that the demand for productive consumption, on our assumptions, is the capitalist's demand, and that his true purpose is the production of surplus-value, so that it is only with this in mind that he produces a particular kind of commodity."

Yet the capitalist as buyer of cotton "is the representative of the need for cotton," since "it is completely unimportant for the seller of cotton whether the buyer transforms it into shirting or gun-cotton, or whether he uses it to stop up his own and the world's ears." The capitalist's purpose nevertheless shapes what kind of buyer he is: "His need for cotton is modified fundamentally by the fact that all it really clothes is his need to make a profit."

The difference between market demand and genuine social need varies across commodities and depends on "other money prices" and "the buyers being in different financial and living conditions."

Demand and Supply Cancel Out: The Law Emerges Only in Equilibrium

Marx now delivers the central methodological blow:

"If demand and supply coincide, they cease to have any effect, and it is for this very reason that commodities are sold at their market value. If two forces act in opposing directions and cancel one another out, they have no external impact whatsoever, and phenomena that appear under these conditions must be explained otherwise than by the operation of these two forces."

This is a brilliant application of the principle of sufficient reason. If demand and supply perfectly balance, they exert no net force, and therefore cannot explain why market value is this magnitude rather than another. "If demand and supply cancel one another out, they cease to explain anything, have no effect on market value and leave us completely in the dark as to why this market value is expressed in precisely such a sum of money and no other."

The real inner laws of capitalist production "clearly cannot be explained in terms of the interaction of demand and supply... since these laws are realized in their pure form only when demand and supply cease to operate, i.e. when they coincide."

Marx acknowledges that demand and supply "never coincide, or, if they do so, it is only by chance and not to be taken into account for scientific purposes." Why then does political economy assume they coincide? "In order to treat the phenomena it deals with in their law-like form, the form that corresponds to their concept, i.e. to consider them independently of the appearance produced by the movement of demand and supply. And, in addition, in order to discover the real tendency of their movement and to define it to a certain extent."

The resolution lies in the average over time: "since they constantly follow one another, they balance each other out in their movement in contrary directions, their contradiction." Thus "supply and demand always coincide if a greater or lesser period of time is taken as a whole; but they coincide only as the average of the movement that has taken place and through the constant movement of their contradiction." Market prices that diverge from market values "balance out on average to become market values, since the departures from these values balance each other as pluses and minuses."

Crucially, "this average figure is by no means of merely theoretical significance. It is, rather, practically important for capital whose investment is calculated over the fluctuations and compensations of a more or less fixed period of time."

The Mechanisms of Self-Correction: How Divergences Remove Themselves

Marx enumerates the adjustment mechanisms:

  • If demand falls and market price falls: this can lead to withdrawal of capital (reduced supply), or to a fall in market value itself through inventions reducing necessary labour-time.
  • If demand rises and market price rises above market value: this can lead to over-investment and consequent overproduction (price falls below market value), or to a price rise that depresses demand, or to a rise in market value itself (because part of the demanded product must be produced under worse conditions for a time).

The relationship between demand and supply "explains on the one hand simply the divergences of market price from market value, while on the other hand it explains the tendency for these divergences to be removed."

The Circularity of Price and Demand/Supply

Marx notes the multi-layered circularity:

"If demand and supply determine the market price, then market price in turn, and at a further remove market value, also determine demand and supply."

  • Demand moves opposite to price (expanding when price falls).
  • Supply is determined by the prices of means of production: "Cotton prices determine the supply of cotton goods."
  • "Demand also determines supply and conversely supply determines demand, production determines the market and the market determines production."

This is a general interdependence that cannot be resolved by treating any one factor as fundamental — unless one has a theory of value to anchor the system.

The Malthus Footnote: Cost of Production Governs Demand, Not the Reverse

A long footnote quotes Malthus (Observations on Certain Verbal Disputes, 1821, pp. 60–61), who argues that "the great principle of demand and supply is called into action to determine what A. Smith calls natural prices as well as market-prices." Marx's rebuttal: "This clever man does not understand that in the case in question it is precisely the change in cost of production, and also therefore in value, that has brought about the change in demand, i.e. in the relationship of demand and supply." The change in cost of production is not governed by demand and supply; "on the contrary is what governs this relationship." Malthus's own example thus proves the opposite of what he intends.

The "Two Natural Prices" Argument: Proof that Demand and Supply Do Not Determine Value

Marx quotes the Observations author:

"It is only when the supply is equal to the effectual demand, that is, to that demand which will neither more nor less than pay the natural price, that the natural price is in fact paid; consequently, there may be two very different natural prices, at different times, for the same commodity, and yet the proportion, which the supply bears to the demand, be in both cases the same, namely, the proportion of equality."

Marx's commentary is decisive:

"It is conceded, then, that in the case where we have two different 'natural prices' for the same commodity at different times, demand and supply can and must coincide each time, if the commodity is to be sold at its 'natural price' in both cases. But since there is no difference in the relationship between demand and supply from one occasion to the other, but rather a difference in the magnitude of the 'natural price' itself, the latter is evidently determined independently of demand and supply and can certainly not be determined by them."

This is a reductio ad absurdum: if demand and supply are in the same proportion (equality) at two different times yet the natural price differs, then the natural price cannot be determined by demand and supply. This formal argument is one of the chapter's most powerful weapons against subjectivist price theory.

The Metamorphosis and Why Money Analysis Could Ignore Price Divergences

Marx recapitulates the analysis of the commodity metamorphosis (C–M–C') from Volume I and A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy. In the relationship of demand and supply, "we have firstly a repetition of the relationship between use-value and exchange-value, commodity and money, buyer and seller; secondly, we have the relationship of producer and consumer, even though both may be represented by third parties, in the shape of merchants."

"Three persons are enough for the complete metamorphosis of a commodity": A sells to B, then buys from C with B's money. In analyzing money as means of circulation, Marx assumed commodities were sold at their values, "there was no reason at all to consider prices that diverged from values, as we were concerned simply with the changes of form." Once a commodity is sold and another bought, "we have the entire metamorphosis before us, and it is completely immaterial here whether the commodity's price is above or below its value." Value remains important as the basis: "any rational understanding of money has to start from this foundation, and price, in its general concept, is simply value in the money form."

This methodological note explains why the earlier analysis could abstract from price-value divergences: it was concerned with form, not with magnitude. But when we consider demand and supply, magnitude becomes central.

Aggregate Forces: Demand and Supply as Social Powers, the Individual as Atom

When we consider supply and demand at the social level, "the supply is equal to the sum of commodities provided by all the sellers or producers of a particular kind of commodity, and the demand is equal to the sum of all buyers or consumers... of that same kind of commodity." These totals "act on one another as unities, as aggregate forces." The individual "has an effect only as part of a social power, as an atom in the mass," and it is in this form that "competition brings into play the social character of production and consumption."

This is a crucial anti-individualist methodological point: the relevant forces are social aggregates, not individual preferences.

Competition's Asymmetric Play: The Weaker Side as Disunited

Marx describes the dynamics of competition:

  • "The side that is temporarily weaker in competition is also that in which the individual operates independently of the mass of his competitors, and often directly against them, illustrating precisely in this way the dependence of one on the other, whereas the stronger side always acts towards its opponent as a more or less united whole."
  • If demand exceeds supply: one buyer outbids others, raising price above market value for everyone; sellers all seek a high price.
  • If supply exceeds demand: one seller unloads cheaply, others follow; buyers depress price below market value.
  • "Each is only concerned with the common interest as long as he obtains more with it than he would against it. And this unity of action ceases as soon as one entire side or other weakens, when each individual independently tries to extract what he can."
  • If one seller produces more cheaply, he undercuts others, carving out a bigger market share; "the action once begun, it gradually forces the others to introduce the cheaper form of production and thereby reduces the socially necessary labour to a new and lower level."
  • If one side has the upper hand, "each of its members profits; it is as if they had a common monopoly to exert." On the weaker side, "every man for himself... devil take the hindmost."

A footnote quotes the Inquiry into those Principles Respecting the Nature of Demand (1821): where each man can increase his own share only by lessening the whole, he will do so — "this is competition"; whereas if shares were fixed, men would combine to raise the gain — "this is monopoly."

Demand and Supply Presuppose the Whole Capitalist Order

"Demand and supply imply the transformation of value into market value, and in as much as they act on a capitalist basis, and commodities are the products of capital, they imply capitalist processes of production, i.e. conditions that are much more intricate than the mere purchase and sale of commodities."

For simple buying and selling, "it is enough that commodity producers confront one another." But demand and supply at the capitalist level imply "various different classes and segments of classes which distribute the total social revenue among themselves and consume it as such" — a demand created out of revenue — and "the overall configuration of the capitalist production process" to comprehend producers' demand.

Earlier, Marx had specified the determinants of "social need": "basically conditioned by the relationship of the different classes and their respective economic positions; in the first place, therefore, particularly by the proportion between the total surplus-value and wages, and secondly, by the proportion between the various parts into which surplus-value itself is divided (profit, interest, ground-rent, taxes, etc.)." Hence "absolutely nothing can be explained by the relationship of demand and supply, before explaining the basis on which this relationship functions."

The Aim of Capitalist Production: Average Profit as Capital's Consciousness of Itself as a Social Power

Marx restates the capitalist objective:

"In capitalist production it is not simply a matter of extracting, in return for the mass of value thrown into circulation in the commodity form, an equal mass of value in a different form... but rather of extracting for the capital advanced in production the same surplus-value or profit as any other capital of the same size, or a profit proportionate to its size, no matter in what branch of production it may be applied."

The minimum requirement is to sell at prices of production. "This is the form in which capital becomes conscious of itself as a social power, in which every capitalist participates in proportion to his share in the total social capital."

This is a remarkable formulation: the equalization of profit rates is not merely a market outcome but a form of class consciousness — the mode in which capital recognizes itself as a collective social power.

The Indifference of Capital and Labour to Use-Value and Work

Two structural indifference properties support equalization:

  1. Capitalist production is indifferent to use-values and the specific character of commodities. "All that matters in any sphere of production is to produce surplus-value, to appropriate a definite quantity of unpaid labour in labour's product."
  2. Wage-labour is indifferent to the specific character of its work. "It must be prepared to change according to the needs of capital and let itself be flung from one sphere of production to another."
  3. "One sphere of production really is as good and as bad as any other; each yields the same profit and each would be pointless if the commodity it produced did not satisfy some kind of social need."

These indifference conditions are the subjective counterpart of the objective mobility of capital and labour.

Capital Migration and the Equalization of Profit Rates

Marx summarizes the mechanism:

"If commodities were sold at their values... this would mean very different rates of profit in the different spheres of production, as we have already explained, according to the differing organic composition of the masses of capital applied. Capital withdraws from a sphere with a low rate of profit and wends its way to others that yield higher profit. This constant migration, the distribution of capital between the different spheres according to where the profit rate is rising and where it is falling, is what produces a relationship between supply and demand such that the average profit is the same in the various different spheres, and values are therefore transformed into prices of production."

This is the mechanism of equalization: capital flight from low-profit to high-profit spheres alters supply-demand relations, which alters prices, which alters profit rates, until equalization is achieved. The causality runs: unequal profit rates → capital migration → altered supply → altered prices → equalized profit rates. The equalization is a dynamic process, not a static condition.

The Two Requirements for Rapid Equalization: Mobility of Capital and of Labour

Marx specifies the conditions that accelerate equalization:

First condition: mobility of capital. This requires: - "Completely free trade within the society in question and the abolition of all monopolies other than natural ones, i.e. those arising from the capitalist mode of production itself." - "The development of the credit system, which concentrates together the inorganic mass of available social capital vis-à-vis the individual capitalist." - "That the various spheres of production have been subordinated to capitalists." This is already implied by the assumption that we are dealing with the transformation of values into prices of production for all spheres exploited in the capitalist manner. But "this equalization comes up against major obstacles if several substantial spheres of production are pursued non-capitalistically (e.g. agriculture by small peasant farmers)." - "A high population density."

Second condition: mobility of labour-power. This requires: - "The abolition of all laws that prevent workers from moving from one sphere of production to another or from one local seat of production to any other." - "Indifference of the worker to the content of his work." - "Greatest possible reduction of work in all spheres of production to simple labour." - "Disappearance of all prejudices of trade and craft among the workers." - "Finally and especially, the subjection of the worker to the capitalist mode of production."

Marx defers further details "to the special study of competition" (referring to a later part of the work).

Capitalist Freemasonry: Universal Participation in the Exploitation of the Total Working Class

Marx draws the class conclusion:

"Each individual capitalist, just like the totality of all capitalists in each particular sphere of production, participates in the exploitation of the entire working class by capital as a whole, and in the level of this exploitation; not just in terms of general class sympathy, but in a direct economic sense, since... the average rate of profit depends on the level of exploitation of labour as a whole by capital as a whole."

The average rate of profit "coincides with the average surplus-value that capital produces for each 100 units." The only additional determinant is "the value of the capital advanced."

The particular interest of a capitalist or sphere is confined to "the possibility of taking an extra cut, making an excess profit over and above the average, either by exceptional overwork, by reducing wages below the average, or by exceptional productivity in the labour applied."

Marx then constructs two limiting cases:

  • A capitalist employing no variable capital at all (an exaggerated assumption) would still have "just as much an interest in the exploitation of the working class by capital" and derive profit from unpaid surplus labour.
  • A capitalist employing only variable capital (again exaggerated) would be in the same position.

"With a given working day, the level of exploitation of labour depends on its average intensity, and, conversely, given the intensity, on the length of the working day. The rate of surplus-value depends on the level of exploitation of labour, and thus, for a given mass of variable capital, the size of the surplus-value and the amount of profit also depend on this."

The special interest of a particular capital in exploiting its own workers is "paralleled by the interest of the individual capitalist, as distinct from his sphere, in the exploitation of the workers exploited personally by him."

Interest in Social Productivity

Each sphere and capitalist has "the same interest in the productivity of the social labour applied by the total capital." Two things depend on this:

  1. The mass of use-values in which the average profit is expressed — important both as accumulation fund and as revenue fund for consumption.
  2. The value level of the total capital advanced (constant and variable), which "with a given size of surplus-value or profit for the entire capitalist class, determines the profit rate, or the profit on a particular quantity of capital."

But "the specific productivity of labour in one particular sphere, or in one individual business in this sphere, concerns the capitalists directly involved in it only in so far as it enables this particular sphere to make an extra profit in relation to the total capital, or the individual capitalist in relation to his sphere."

The "Mathematically Exact Demonstration" of Class Solidarity

Marx concludes:

"We thus have a mathematically exact demonstration of why the capitalists, no matter how little love is lost among them in their mutual competition, are nevertheless united by a real freemasonry vis-a-vis the working class as a whole."

The "freemasonry" is not sentimental but structural: because the average rate of profit depends on the aggregate rate of exploitation, every capitalist benefits from the exploitation of every worker. Competition among capitalists is real, but it is contained within a shared dependence on the exploitation of the working class as a whole.

Terminology: Price of Production, Natural Price, Cost of Production, Prix Nécessaire

Marx clarifies terminology:

"The price of production includes the average profit. And what we call price of production is in fact the same thing that Adam Smith calls 'natural price', Ricardo 'price of production' or 'cost of production', and the Physiocrats 'prix nécessaire', though none of these people explained the difference between price of production and value."

Marx explains why he calls it "price of production": "because in the long term it is the condition of supply, the condition for the reproduction of commodities, in each particular sphere of production." The footnote cites Malthus (Principles of Political Economy, pp. 77–8).

Why Vulgar Economists Fixate on Prices of Production

Marx explains the ideological attraction of prices of production:

"We can also understand why those very economists who oppose the determination of commodity value by labour-time... always speak of the prices of production as the centres around which market prices fluctuate. They can allow themselves this because the price of production is already a completely externalized and prima facie irrational form of commodity value, a form that appears in competition and is therefore present in the consciousness of the vulgar capitalist and consequently also in that of the vulgar economist."

The price of production is value in its alienated, surface form — the form in which value appears to the practical consciousness of the capitalist. It seems "irrational" because it does not transparently correspond to embodied labour, yet it is derived from value through the equalization process. The vulgar economist mistakes the surface for the essence.

Surplus Profit: The Uniform Price and Differential Conditions

The chapter's final movement introduces surplus profit:

"We saw in the course of our argument how market value (and everything that was said about this applies with the necessary limitations also to price of production) involves a surplus profit for those producing under the best conditions in any particular sphere of production."

Excluding crises and overproduction, "this holds good for all market prices, no matter how they might diverge from market values or market prices of production. The concept of market price means that the same price is paid for all commodities of the same kind, even if these are produced under very different individual conditions and may therefore have very different cost prices."

The uniform market price, combined with differential cost prices, generates a surplus profit (extra surplus-value realized) for low-cost producers. Marx brackets "surplus profits that result from monopolies in the customary sense of the term, whether artificial or natural."

Monopoly Surplus Profit and the Two Forms of Surplus Profit

Marx distinguishes two forms of surplus profit:

  1. Differential surplus profit arising from the uniform market price under differential conditions (the subject of the preceding analysis).
  2. Monopoly surplus profit arising "if certain spheres of production are in a position to opt out of the transformation of their commodity values into prices of production, and the consequent reduction of their profits to the average profit."

He announces: "In the Part on ground-rent, we shall have to consider the further configuration of these two forms of surplus profit." This is the bridge to the theory of ground-rent and to the analysis of agriculture, where non-capitalist relations and natural monopolies (land) generate a second and distinctive form of surplus profit.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principle: Value Governs Through Its Transformation

The deepest lesson of Chapter 10 is that the law of value does not operate by directly stamping prices with embodied labour. It operates through the equalization of profit rates, which systematically distorts individual prices while preserving aggregate value. The two equalities — Σ profits = Σ surplus-value and Σ prices of production = Σ values — are the formal guarantee that the transformation is a redistribution, not a creation of value. The law of value thus governs prices of production "as a general law or as governing the fluctuations," even though individual prices diverge from individual values. This is the mature Marxian synthesis: freedom of prices from values is the form in which the law of value asserts itself under capitalism.

The Historical Materialist Claim: Value Exchange as a Lower Stage

Marx argues that exchange at values corresponds to a "much lower stage of development" than exchange at prices of production. This claim has several dimensions:

  • Empirical-historical: value exchange prevails among peasant proprietors and handicraftsmen, in slavery and serfdom, and in guild organization — conditions where capital and labour are immobile.
  • Theoretical: the immobility of capital preserves the proportionality of price to embodied labour; the mobility of capital, developed by capitalism, destroys it and substitutes prices of production.
  • Methodological: the logical priority of value over price of production reflects a historical priority. The "transformation problem" is thus not merely a formal puzzle but a historical process.

This argument has been intensely debated, but its strategic function is clear: it grounds the labour theory of value in the actual historical development of commodity production, rather than treating it as a timeless accounting convention.

The Critique of Demand and Supply: Equilibrium as Norm

The chapter's demolition of demand-and-supply explanation is one of its most enduring contributions. Marx's argument is structurally elegant:

  1. If demand and supply determine value, then value is explained only when demand and supply coincide.
  2. But when they coincide, they exert no net force and therefore explain nothing.
  3. Therefore, demand and supply can explain only divergences of market price from market value — not market value itself.
  4. The determination of market value must be sought elsewhere — in socially necessary labour-time.

This is not a denial that demand matters; it is a claim about explanatory priority. Demand and supply allocate labour-time across branches (through price fluctuations), but they do not create value. The "social need" that governs demand is itself conditioned by class relations — by the distribution of surplus-value between wages, profit, interest, rent, and taxes.

The Class Content of Equalization: Capitalist Freemasonry

The chapter's closing vision is of capitalism as a system of class solidarity among capitalists built on the exploitation of the working class as a whole. The average rate of profit depends on the aggregate rate of exploitation; therefore every capitalist, no matter how fiercely competitive, has a direct economic stake in the exploitation of every worker. This is the structural basis of the "real freemasonry" of capital. The individual capitalist's particular interest in exploiting "his own" workers is only an extra — an excess profit over the average — layered on top of the universal interest in the exploitation of the class as a whole.

This analysis has profound implications:

  • Class solidarity is structural, not psychological. Capitalists need not like each other; they need only share a dependence on aggregate exploitation.
  • Reformism within capitalism is constrained. Any improvement in workers' conditions that lowers the aggregate rate of exploitation lowers every capitalist's profit rate, uniting capital in opposition.
  • Competition is contained. The "war of all against all" among capitalists operates within the shared framework of exploitation; it does not dissolve class unity against labour.

The Concept of Surplus Profit and the Theory of Rent

The chapter's final section opens the door to the theory of ground-rent. Surplus profit arises from two sources:

  1. Differential conditions within a sphere (low-cost producers capture extra surplus-value because the market price is uniform).
  2. Monopoly or non-equalization (spheres that escape the transformation of values into prices of production retain their higher rates of profit).

The first form is the basis of differential rent; the second is the basis of absolute rent and of monopoly profit generally. Marx's announcement that he will treat "the further configuration of these two forms of surplus profit" in the Part on ground-rent shows the systematic connection: the analysis of profit rates is the precondition for the analysis of rent as a distinctive form of surplus-value appropriation.

Methodological Lessons: Tendencies, Abstractions, and the Priority of Essence

Several methodological principles crystallize in this chapter:

  • Economic laws are tendencies, not exact regularities. The general rate of profit, the equal rate of surplus-value, and the equalization process are all tendencies that approximate empirical reality to the degree that capitalist relations are developed and purified of earlier survivals.
  • Abstraction is not falsification. The assumption of mobile capital and labour is an abstraction that "in practice" is approximate — and it becomes more exact "the more the capitalist mode of production is developed."
  • Essence precedes appearance. Value is the essence; price of production is the appearance. The task of science is to derive the appearance from the essence, not to take the appearance as given.
  • Equilibrium is the norm; disequilibrium is deviation. "The exchange or sale of commodities at their value is the rational, natural law of the equilibrium between them; this is the basis on which divergences have to be explained, and not the converse."

Broader Practical and Philosophical Implications

For political economy: The chapter refutes both the Ricardian assumption of an unproblematic general rate of profit and the vulgar economist's reification of demand and supply. It demonstrates that the labour theory of value can account for the systematic deviations of prices from values without abandoning the labor substance of value — a result that eluded classical political economy.

For the theory of money: Marx's analysis of the metamorphosis (C–M–C) and his insistence that price is "value in the money form" shows that money and value are conceptually linked. A general nominal inflation of profit rates is impossible without corresponding changes in wages and constant capital prices, because total value governs total surplus-value.

For the theory of crisis: The chapter's analysis of wasted labour, overproduction, and the disequilibrium between social labour allocation and social need provides a foundation for the later analysis of crises. When the mass of commodities exceeds social need, "a part of the society's labour-time is wasted," and commodities must be sold below value. This is the germ of the crisis theory.

For the theory of class struggle: The "capitalist freemasonry" passage is one of Marx's most concentrated statements on the structural unity of the capitalist class. It grounds class solidarity in the objective mechanisms of profit-rate determination, not in subjective preference.

For socialist theory: The parenthetical remark that "only when production is subjected to the genuine, prior control of society will society establish the connection between the amount of social labour-time applied to the production of particular articles, and the scale of the social need to be satisfied by these" points toward the socialist alternative: ex ante coordination of labour-time and need, replacing the ex post coordination of the market with its waste, crises, and class exploitation.

Conclusion: The Chapter as the Keystone of Volume III

Chapter 10 accomplishes the reconciliation that Volume III exists to perform. It shows how the law of value, established in Volume I at the level of the individual commodity, governs the total social product through the competitive equalization of profit rates. It demonstrates that the "irrational" surface forms of capitalism — prices of production, market prices, surplus profit, rent — are all intelligible as necessary forms of value's self-valorization. It exposes the demand-and-supply explanation as a tautology that cannot ground itself. And it reveals the class content of the entire mechanism: capital, driven by the equalization of profit rates, unites as a social power against the working class whose total exploitation is the source of its average profit.

The chapter's final sentence — the promise to treat the "further configuration of these two forms of surplus profit" in the Part on ground-rent — signals that the analysis of profit rates is not an end in itself but the foundation for understanding rent, and beyond it, the full architecture of capitalist distribution. The equalization of the general rate of profit is thus the mechanism through which the law of value asserts itself in the concrete totality of capitalist production, distribution, and class relations.
