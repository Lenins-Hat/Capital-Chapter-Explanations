Volume 3 • Chapter 15: Exposition of the Internal Contradictions of the Law

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Core Thesis in One Sentence

Chapter 15 of Capital, Volume 3 — "Development of the Law's Internal Contradictions" — argues that the tendential fall in the rate of profit, far from being an empirical curiosity or an accidental by-product of competition, is the necessary and periodically explosive expression of a structural contradiction built into the very concept of capital: the contradiction between capital's historically revolutionary drive toward the absolute development of the productive forces and its restricted purpose of valorizing already-existing capital value.

Marx's objective in this chapter is not to restate the law of the falling rate of profit, which he had already derived in Chapters 13–14. His objective is to unfold the law's internal logic — to show how a purely quantitative tendency (a declining ratio of surplus-value to total capital advanced) generates, on the one hand, over-accumulation, surplus capital coexisting alongside surplus population, credit breakdown, and crisis; and, on the other hand, a set of recuperative mechanisms that re-establish the conditions of accumulation on an enlarged but ever more fragile scale. The transition from "the law as such" to "the law's internal contradictions" is therefore the transition from an abstract economic tendency to a theory of capitalist crisis and historical transitoriness.

The Central Problem

The chapter's central problem is why capitalism's most spectacular success — the relentless, geometric expansion of the productivity of social labour — systematically undermines capitalism's own incentive structure. The rate of profit is not merely one variable among many; it is, in Marx's phrase, "the spur to capitalist production," the "animating fire" of the entire system: "nothing is produced save what can be produced at a profit." Yet the very methods by which capital raises productivity — mechanization, rising organic composition, the substitution of dead for living labour — depress the rate of profit. Capital, in other words, is compelled by its own competitive logic to do precisely the thing that erodes its own motive force. The chapter's problem is to reconcile these two facts without dissolving them into an optimistic functionalism (as in Ricardo) or a Malthusian naturalism (as in the rent theory).

The Intellectual Objective

Marx's objective is threefold:

First, to expose Ricardo's category error. Ricardo, though "disquieted" by the falling rate of profit, conflates the rate of profit with the rate of surplus-value, assumes a constant working day, and thereby mistakes a value-compositional phenomenon for a natural one. Ricardo seeks the barrier to capitalist production "not in production but rather in nature (in the theory of rent)." Marx insists that the barrier is endogenous to the capital relation.

Second, to integrate the falling rate of profit into a general theory of over-accumulation and crisis. The chapter develops a rigorous account of what "overproduction of capital" means: not an excess of commodities relative to human needs, nor an excess relative to productive capacity in general, but an excess of capital that "can function as capital" — that is, that can exploit labour at a rate yielding the normal profit. This is the doctrine of absolute overproduction of capital, and the chapter establishes its arithmetic, its phenomenology (idle capital, devaluation, credit collapse), and its resolution (destruction, idling, and the forcible restoration of the conditions of profitability).

Third, to establish the historical-transitory character of capitalism. Because the falling rate of profit reveals that capitalism's barrier is capital itself, the chapter provides the decisive theoretical proof that capitalism is not "an absolute mode of production for the production of wealth" but "a relative mode of production" — one "corresponding to a specific and limited epoch in the development of the material conditions of production."

Placement Within the Broader Theoretical Framework

This chapter closes Part Three of Volume 3 ("The Law of the Tendential Fall in the Rate of Profit"), which began with Chapter 13's derivation of the law as such and passed through Chapter 14's enumeration of counteracting tendencies. Chapter 15 is the dialectical climax of that bloc. It is also the pivot on which the entire architecture of Volume 3 turns: having established the general rate of profit and its tendency to fall, the subsequent parts of the volume (commerce, interest, ground rent) will decompose this general profit into its specific forms — p₁, i, and r — under conditions where the aggregate s and the aggregate s/C move in opposite directions. Indeed, Section 1 of Chapter 15 already anticipates that decomposition with its algebraic treatment of p₁/C + i/C + r/C.

Beyond Volume 3, the chapter also references Volume 1 — Part Eight (primitive accumulation) — and Volume 1, Chapter 15 (machinery), establishing a systematic continuity: the divorce of the conditions of labour from the producers, first established historically in primitive accumulation, becomes a permanent, self-reproducing process in accumulation, concentration, and centralization. The chapter thus connects the genesis of capital to its historical dissolution, and does so through the mechanism of the falling profit rate.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 Opening Recapitulation: Profit Rate as a Falsified Expression of Exploitation

Marx opens the chapter with a compressed recapitulation of the essential result of Part Three. Two prior results are invoked. First, "the profit rate always expresses the rate of surplus-value lower than it actually is." This is because the rate of profit is computed on the total capital advanced (c + v), whereas the rate of surplus-value is computed only on the variable capital (v). Formally:

p′ = s / C = s / (c + v) = s′v / (c + v) = s′ / (1 + c/v)

where p′ is the rate of profit, s′ the rate of surplus-value, C the total capital, c constant capital, v variable capital. So long as c > 0, we have p′ < s′; and p′ = s′ only if c = 0, "i.e. if the total capital were laid out on wages." This is the first systematic obfuscation the capitalist form imposes on the underlying exploitation relation.

The second result is more dynamic: "even a rising rate of surplus-value tends to be expressed in a falling rate of profit." The derivative of p′ with respect to c/v is negative, so as the organic composition rises (c/v rises) — as it must under mechanization — p′ declines even when s′ rises. This is the "double asymmetry" that Marx has established: (i) exploitation is always understated; (ii) exploitation can be intensified while the appearance of profitability weakens.

From this, Marx draws a crucial diagnostic rule. "A falling rate of profit, then, expresses a falling rate of surplus-value only if the ratio between the value of the constant capital and the amount of labour-power that this sets in motion remains unchanged, or if this latter amount has risen in relation to the value of the constant capital." In other words: a fall in p′ is evidence of a fall in the exploitation rate only if the value-composition of capital is constant or falling. Absent that condition, the falling profit rate is not evidence of "lazy" or "unexploited" workers — it is evidence of the changing composition of capital.

2.2 Ricardo's Category Mistake and the Absolutization of Capitalism

Marx then turns sharply against Ricardo. "Ricardo, while claiming to be dealing with the rate of profit, actually deals only with the rate of surplus-value, and this only on the assumption that the working day is a constant magnitude, both intensively and extensively." Ricardo's law of the tendency is thus, in strict logical terms, not a law of the rate of profit at all: it is a law of what happens to profits when both the length and the intensity of the working day are frozen, and when the composition of capital is implicitly held in the background. Ricardo thereby commits a category error: he reads the surface form (p′) as if it directly expressed the deep structure (s′).

But Marx's critique extends beyond methodology to the ideological function of the mistake. "Economists like Ricardo, who take the capitalist mode of production as an absolute, feel here that this mode of production creates a barrier for itself and seek the source of this barrier not in production but rather in nature (in the theory of rent)." This is decisive. Because Ricardo absolutizes capitalism as the natural, permanent form of production, any barrier that capitalism generates must be displaced onto something external to it. The theory of rent — diminishing returns of the soil, assumed fixed supplies of "natural powers" — is the ideological vehicle for this displacement. Marx's counter-move is to internalize the barrier:

"The important thing in their horror at the falling rate of profit is the feeling that the capitalist mode of production comes up against a barrier to the development of the productive forces which has nothing to do with the production of wealth as such; but this characteristic barrier in fact testifies to the restrictiveness and the solely historical and transitory character of the capitalist mode of production."

Here is the chapter's first statement of its central dialectic. The falling profit rate is not evidence that wealth production has hit a natural limit; it is evidence that wealth production under capitalist social relations has hit a relational limit — a limit that "bears witness that this is not an absolute mode of production for the production of wealth but actually comes into conflict at a certain stage with the latter's further development."

2.3 The Decomposition of Profit and the Rent Question: The Algebraic Demonstration

Marx now introduces a more granular decomposition, crucial for the later parts of Volume 3. "Of course, Ricardo and his school were considering only industrial profit, within which they included interest. Yet the rate of ground-rent also has a tendency to fall, even though its absolute mass grows and it may even grow in relation to industrial profit." Marx credits Edward West (in a footnote citing Essay on the Application of Capital to Land..., by a Fellow of University College, Oxford, London, 1815) with having "put forward the law of ground-rent before Ricardo."

The analytical framework is then given:

If we take the total social capital C, and call the industrial profit that remains after deducting interest and ground-rent p₁, interest i, and ground-rent r, then:

p / C = s / C = (p₁ + i + r) / C = p₁/C + i/C + r/C

Two things follow from this identity. First, because s grows steadily in absolute mass while C grows even more quickly, s/C steadily falls. Second, it is entirely possible for p₁, i, and r each in absolute mass to increase, while p₁/C, i/C, and r/C each as a ratio decline. "It is no contradiction, therefore, that p₁, i and r may each increase even though p′ = s/C and its component parts p₁/C, i/C and r/C become ever smaller."

The proportions among the three portions are, in Marx's phrase, "simply a varying distribution of s under various headings." Thus any one of the three ratios may rise relative to the others while the general rate falls, "the only requirement [being] that the sum of all three = s/C."

2.4 Numerical Illustration: From 50 Per Cent to 25 Per Cent

Marx nails the point with a worked example, central to the chapter's methodological strategy of using arithmetic to dissolve apparent paradoxes:

  • Suppose the rate of profit falls from 50 per cent to 25 per cent because, with a rate of surplus-value of 100 per cent, the composition of capital alters from 50c + 50v to 75c + 25v.
  • In the first case, a capital of 1,000 yields a profit of 500.
  • In the second case, a capital of 4,000 yields a profit of 1,000.
  • So s (or p) has doubled while p′ has halved.

Now consider the decomposition. Suppose that, out of the original 50 per cent rate, 20 per cent was industrial profit proper, 10 per cent interest, and 20 per cent rent. Then p₁/C = 20%, i/C = 10%, r/C = 20%. If the three components keep the same proportions after the aggregate falls to 25 per cent, we get p₁/C = 10%, i/C = 5%, r/C = 10%. But suppose instead that p₁/C falls to 8 per cent and i/C falls to 4 per cent. Then the remainder must accrue to rent: r/C = 13 per cent. The ratio of rent would have risen relative to both industrial profit and interest, "but p′ would still have remained unchanged." And in both scenarios, the absolute sums accruing under all three headings grow, because total profit = 25% of 4,000 = 1,000 versus 50% of 1,000 = 500. "On both assumptions the sum total of p₁, i and r would have risen, since this is now the product of a capital four times larger than before."

The point is not that rent mechanically rises in inverse proportion to the profit rate. The point is that the algebraic space of possible redistributions among the three revenue forms is vast, and that all such redistributions are consistent with a falling general profit rate. This decouples the political economy of distributive class struggle (rentiers vs. industrialists) from the underlying tendency.

2.5 The Historical and Theoretical Falsity of Ricardo's Assumption

Marx now unmasks Ricardo's premise as doubly false. "Ricardo's assumption that industrial profit (plus interest) originally accounted for the entire surplus-value is both historically and theoretically false." Historically: rent predates the fully developed capitalist mode of production, appearing in pre-capitalist forms of landed appropriation. Theoretically: only with the progress of capitalist production does the industrial and commercial bourgeoisie appropriate the entire surplus-value in the first instance (later redistributing a portion to landlords and financiers), and only then can rent be reduced to "the surplus over and above profit."

But here Marx adds a dialectical twist: "On this capitalist basis, rent then grows once more, as a portion of profit (i.e. of the surplus-value considered as product of the total capital), but not the specific portion of the product pocketed by the capitalist." Rent, in other words, undergoes a double movement — at first reduced to a mere deduction from profit, then increasing as an absolute magnitude within the profit category, but never recovering its pre-capitalist status as an independent and prior claim.

2.6 The Immediate Process and Its Two Acts: Production vs. Realization

Marx then turns to what will become the analytical spine of the chapter: the distinction between the production of surplus-value and the realization of surplus-value. He begins by specifying the barriers internal to production itself. "Assuming the necessary means of production, i.e. a sufficient accumulation of capital, the creation of surplus-value faces no other barrier than the working population, if the rate of surplus-value, i.e. the level of exploitation of labour, is given; and no other barrier than this level of exploitation, if the working population is given."

The production process "essentially consists of this production of surplus-value, represented in the surplus product or the aliquot portion of commodities produced in which unpaid labour is objectified." And this is "the immediate purpose and the determining motive of capitalist production." Marx therefore rejects any depiction of capitalist production as consumption-driven: "This would be to ignore completely its specific character, as this is expressed in its basic inner pattern."

Now the crucial distinction. The extraction of surplus-value "is the first act in the capitalist production process, and its completion only brings to an end the immediate production process itself." With the development of this process — as expressed in the falling profit rate — "the mass of surplus-value thus produced swells to monstrous proportions." But the surplus has only been produced, not yet realized:

"Now comes the second act in the process. The total mass of commodities, the total product, must be sold, both that portion which replaces constant and variable capital and that which represents surplus-value."

Here is the pivotal analytical move. If the sale is incomplete, partial, or effected only below the price of production, then "although the worker is certainly exploited, his exploitation is not realized as such for the capitalist and may even not involve any realization of the surplus-value extracted... indeed, it may even mean a partial or complete loss of his capital." Hence:

"The conditions for immediate exploitation and for the realization of that exploitation are not identical. Not only are they separate in time and space, they are also separate in theory."

Marx now specifies separate barriers: - The barrier to exploitation: "restricted only by the society's productive forces." - The barrier to realization: "the proportionality between the different branches of production and by the society's power of consumption."

And the power of consumption itself is socially determined, not naturally: "it is determined neither by the absolute power of production nor by the absolute power of consumption but rather by the power of consumption within a given framework of antagonistic conditions of distribution, which reduce the consumption of the vast majority of society to a minimum level, only capable of varying within more or less narrow limits." It is further restricted by "the drive for accumulation, the drive to expand capital and produce surplus-value on a larger scale."

2.7 The Internal Contradiction Seeking External Resolution

The drive for accumulation is "the law governing capitalist production, arising from the constant revolutions in methods of production themselves, from the devaluation of the existing capital which is always associated with this, and from the general competitive struggle and the need to improve production and extend its scale, merely as a means of self-preservation, and on pain of going under." The result is that "The market, therefore, must be continually extended, so that its relationships and the conditions governing them assume ever more the form of a natural law independent of the producers and become ever more uncontrollable."

Marx's formula here is extraordinarily compact: "The internal contradiction seeks resolution by extending the external field of production. But the more productivity develops, the more it comes into conflict with the narrow basis on which the relations of consumption rest." World-market expansion does not abolish the contradiction; it migrates it, reproducing it at a higher spatial scale. Consequently, "It is in no way a contradiction, on this contradictory basis, that excess capital coexists with a growing surplus population; for although the mass of surplus-value produced would rise if these were brought together, yet this would equally heighten the contradiction between the conditions in which this surplus-value was produced and the conditions in which it was realized."

2.8 Accumulation as a Function of Mass and Impetus, Not of Rate

A crucial quantitative point follows. "Once a certain rate of profit is given, the mass of profit always depends on the magnitude of the capital advanced. But accumulation is then determined by the part of this mass that is transformed back into capital." This part equals profit minus capitalist revenue, so it depends both on the value of total profit and on the cheapness of the commodities the capitalist buys (consumption goods and constant capital, wages being taken as given).

Marx then draws a critical conceptual distinction between the value the worker maintains and the value the worker adds: "The mass of capital that the worker sets in motion, and whose value he maintains by his labour and makes reappear in the product, is completely different from the value that he adds." His two cases:

  • Capital = 1,000; labour added = 100; capital reproduced = 1,100. Rate of profit = 10%.
  • Capital = 100; labour added = 20; capital reproduced = 120. Rate of profit = 20%.

"Nevertheless, more can be accumulated out of 100 than out of 20." So:

"Thus the stream of capital (leaving aside its devaluation as the result of a rise in productivity), or its accumulation, flows on in proportion to the impetus that it already possesses and not in proportion to the rate of profit."

This is the mass/rate distinction in its most concentrated form. Accumulation momentum is a function of prior magnitude, not of percentage yield. It follows that the falling rate of profit is compatible with, indeed systematically drives, an acceleration of accumulation in mass — which itself further raises the composition of capital and deepens the very tendency that produces the fall.

2.9 Radical Counter-Case: High Profit with Low Productivity

Marx now presents the inverse case to prove that the rate of profit is not a direct measure of the exploitation or development of labour. "It is possible to have a high rate of profit even if labour is unproductive, if this is based on a high rate of surplus-value and the working day is very long; this is possible where the workers' needs are very slight and the average wage very low." In such circumstances — thin needs, low wages, long hours, slow accumulation, stagnant population, labour-time-intensive production — the rate is high while the level of development is low. Conversely: "The rate of profit does not fall because the worker is less exploited, but rather because less labour is generally applied in relation to the capital invested." This sentence is, in a sense, the theorem of the chapter in miniature: the fall is a compositional phenomenon, not an exploitation-intensity phenomenon.

2.10 Chalmers' Fantasy: Confusing Cause and Effect

Because a rising mass of profit can accompany a falling rate, "a greater part of the annual product of labour is appropriated by the capitalist under the heading of capital (as replacement for the capital used up) and a relatively smaller part is appropriated under the heading of profit." Marx here attacks "the fantasy of Reverend Chalmers to the effect that the smaller the mass of the annual product the capitalists spend as capital, the greater the profits they pocket" (footnote citing Thomas Chalmers, On Political Economy in Connexion with the Moral State and Moral Prospects of Society, 2nd edn, Glasgow, 1832, p. 88; and noting that in Theories of Surplus-Value, Part I, p. 290, Marx describes Chalmers as "one of the most fanatic Malthusians," himself a cleric and Professor of Divinity at Glasgow University). Chalmers — abetted by the Established Church's promotion of unproductive consumption of the surplus product — "confuses cause and effect": the growth of profit mass is a consequence of enlarged capital, not a consequence of restricted consumption. And that growth "brings about a simultaneous concentration of capital."

2.11 Concentration, Centralization, and the Machinery of Decapitalization

Concentration — growth in the minimum scale of viable operation — leads to centralization, "i.e. the swallowing-up of small capitalists by big, and their decapitalization." Marx interprets this as "simply the divorce of the conditions of labour from the producers raised to a higher power, these smaller capitalists still counting among the producers, since their own labour still plays a role." A quantitative law follows: "The work done by the capitalist, in general, stands in inverse proportion to the size of his capital, i.e. to the degree in which he is a capitalist." As the capitalist becomes a mere functionary of an alien thing, his personal labour contribution vanishes.

The divorce between the conditions of labour and the producers "forms the concept of capital, as this arises with primitive accumulation (Volume 1, Part Eight), subsequently appearing as a constant process in the accumulation and concentration of capital, before it is finally expressed here as the centralization of capitals already existing in a few hands, and the decapitalization of many."

Finally, the counterweight: "This process would entail the rapid breakdown of capitalist production, if counteracting tendencies were not constantly at work alongside this centripetal force, in the direction of decentralization." The chapter thus formally announces — though it does not here elaborate — the theory of counteracting tendencies that will appear as the systematic resolution mechanism in Section 3.

2.12 Section 2: The Double Form of Rising Productivity

Section 2 — "The Conflict Between the Extension of Production and Valorization" — begins by restating the development of social productivity in two forms. First, "in the size of the productive forces already produced, the scale of the conditions of production in both value and mass, in so far as these are the conditions for new production to take place, and in the absolute magnitude of the productive capital already accumulated." Second, "in the relatively low proportion of capital, out of the total, that is laid out on wages, i.e. in the relatively small amount of living labour that is required to reproduce and valorize a given capital, and for mass production." And "This presupposes at the same time the concentration of capital."

As far as labour-power applied is concerned, productivity development again takes a double form: (a) "an increase in surplus labour, i.e. a shortening of necessary labour-time, the time required for the reproduction of labour-power"; (b) "a decline in the total amount of labour-power (number of workers) applied to set a given capital in motion." These two movements "not only go hand in hand; they mutually condition one another, and are phenomena that express the same law. But they affect the profit rate in opposite directions."

This "opposite directions" thesis is then formalized. Since p′ = s / C, and total surplus-value is determined jointly by (i) the rate of surplus-value and (ii) the mass of labour applied at that rate — equivalent to the magnitude of variable capital — the two factors move in opposing directions: "One of these factors, the rate of surplus-value, is rising; the other factor, the number of workers, is falling (relatively or absolutely)." Productivity raises surplus-value by lifting s′, but reduces it by shrinking the multiplier (v) on which s′ operates.

2.13 The Limits of Compensation: The "Living on Air" Argument

A logical limit follows. "Two workers working for 12 hours a day could not supply the same surplus-value as 24 workers each working 2 hours, even if they were able to live on air and hence scarcely needed to work at all for themselves." The hours here differ (24 vs. 48) so the illustrative thrust is that mass of surplus-value ultimately requires mass of labour — the multiplier can be driven down only so far. Hence: "In this connection, therefore, the compensation for the reduced number of workers provided by a rise in the level of exploitation of labour has certain limits that cannot be overstepped; this can certainly check the fall in the profit rate, but it cannot cancel it out."

This is a crucial hedging clause: the rise in s′ is a counteracting tendency, not a cancelling one. The falling rate remains tendential in the strict sense.

2.14 Accumulation, Devaluation, and the Positive-Feedback Spiral

Marx then formalizes the dynamic relationship between the accumulated magnitude of capital and the rate of growth. "Once the rate is given, the absolute amount by which capital grows depends on its existing magnitude. But if this magnitude is given, the proportion in which it grows, i.e. its rate of growth, depends on the profit rate." A rise in productivity — which "always goes hand in hand with devaluation of the existing capital" — can increase the magnitude of capital only if it increases the part of annual profit converted back into capital by raising the profit rate. And this can occur (since productivity is not directly relevant to the value of existing capital) only in two ways: (i) by raising relative surplus-value, or (ii) by cheapening the elements of constant capital — that is, cheapening commodities entering labour-power reproduction or constant capital itself.

But the sting: "Both of these, however, involve a devaluation of the existing capital, and both go hand in hand with a reduction in the variable capital as against the constant. Both processes condition the fall in the profit rate, and both delay it."

This is the dialectical knot. Productivity both causes and delays the fall — causes it by raising the composition of capital, delays it by devaluing existing capital and cheapening constant elements. The tendency and its counter-tendencies are not external to one another; they are the same process viewed from two sides.

Marx then adds a demographic feedback: "In so far, moreover, as the higher rate of profit gives rise to an increased demand for labour, it leads to an increase in the working population and hence in the exploitable material which is precisely what makes capital capital."

2.15 The Material Substratum: Use-Value Growth Under Constant Exchange-Value

Marx now broadens the argument's ontological base. "Indirectly, however, the development of labour productivity contributes to an increase in the existing capital value, since it increases the mass and diversity of use-values in which the same exchange-value is represented, and which form the material substratum, the objective elements of this capital." The same capital and labour, he notes, "produce more things that can be transformed into capital, quite apart from exchange-value. These things can serve to absorb additional labour, and thus additional surplus labour also, and can in this way form additional capital."

Crucially, "The mass of labour that capital can command does not depend on its value but rather on the mass of raw and ancillary materials, of machinery and elements of fixed capital, and of means of subsistence, out of which it is composed, whatever their value may be." Because the mass of labour applied grows, "the value of the capital reproduced and the surplus-value newly added to it grow as well." The result is that the material scale of accumulation can expand even when the value scale is falling — a distinction the Critique of Ricardo glosses over: "Yet these two aspects involved in the accumulation process cannot just be considered as existing quietly side by side, which is how Ricardo treats them; they contain a contradiction."

2.16 The Four Contradictory Simultaneities and the Theory of Crisis

The contradiction is then presented as a set of simultaneous opposed tendencies:

  1. Population: "Simultaneously with impulses towards a genuine increase in the working population... we have those agencies that create a relative surplus population."
  2. Value and devaluation: "Simultaneously with the fall in the profit rate, the mass of capital grows, and this is associated with a devaluation of the existing capital, which puts a stop to this fall and gives an accelerating impulse to the accumulation of capital value."
  3. Composition: "Simultaneously with the development of productivity, the composition of capital becomes higher, there is a relative decline in the variable portion as against the constant."

"These various influences sometimes tend to exhibit themselves side by side, spatially; at other times one after the other, temporally; and at certain points the conflict of contending agencies breaks through in crises." And the definition of crisis follows, in one of the most quoted passages of Volume 3: "Crises are never more than momentary, violent solutions for the existing contradictions, violent eruptions that re-establish the disturbed balance for the time being." Crisis is therefore constitutive, not accidental — it is the form in which a structural contradiction achieves temporary resolution.

2.17 The General Expression of the Contradiction: Absolute Development vs. Restricted Valorization

Marx then articulates the contradiction "in the most general terms":

"The capitalist mode of production tends towards an absolute development of the productive forces irrespective of value and the surplus-value this contains, and even irrespective of the social relations within which capitalist production takes place; while on the other hand its purpose is to maintain the existing capital value and to valorize it to the utmost extent possible."

The mode of production is "directed towards using the existing capital value as a means for the greatest possible valorization of this value." Its methods "involve a decline in the profit rate, the devaluation of the existing capital and the development of the productive forces of labour at the cost of the productive forces already produced."

Consequences are drawn: - The "periodical devaluation of the existing capital... disturbs the given conditions in which the circulation and reproduction process of capital takes place, and is therefore accompanied by sudden stoppages and crises in the production process." - The relative decline in variable capital "gives a spur to the growth of the working population, while it continuously creates an artificial surplus population as well." - "The accumulation of capital, from the point of view of value, is slowed down by the falling rate of profit, which then serves yet again to accelerate the accumulation of use-value, while this in turn accelerates the course of accumulation in terms of value." - "Capitalist production constantly strives to overcome these immanent barriers, but it overcomes them only by means that set up the barriers afresh and on a more powerful scale."

The closing formula of the section is: "The true barrier to capitalist production is capital itself." Capital and its self-valorization are "the starting and finishing point... the motive and purpose of production; production is production only for capital, and not the reverse, i.e. the means of production are not simply means for a steadily expanding pattern of life for the society of the producers."

The section ends with the historic-mission framing: "If the capitalist mode of production is therefore a historical means for developing the material powers of production and for creating a corresponding world market, it is at the same time the constant contradiction between this historical task and the social relations of production corresponding to it."

2.18 Section 3: The Rising Minimum Capital and the Plethora of Capital

Section 3 — "Surplus Capital Alongside Surplus Population" — opens with the mechanism by which the falling rate transforms the structural landscape. "As the profit rate falls, so there is a growth in the minimum capital that the individual capitalist needs in order to make productive use of labour." This is a two-sided necessity: minimum capital is required both (i) to extract surplus-value at all (to hire sufficiently many workers) and (ii) to ensure that "the labour-time spent on the production of commodities is necessary labour-time and does not overstep the average labour-time that is socially necessary" — i.e. to remain competitive with the social average.

Concentration follows: "since beyond certain limits a large capital with a lower rate of profit accumulates more quickly than a small capital with a higher rate of profit." And "This growing concentration leads in turn, at a certain level, to a new fall in the rate of profit" — a positive-feedback loop.

Small capitals are then driven into speculative channels: "The mass of small fragmented capitals are thereby forced onto adventurous paths: speculation, credit swindles, share swindles, crises." The famous definition follows:

"The so-called plethora of capital is always basically reducible to a plethora of that capital for which the fall in the profit rate is not outweighed by its mass — and this is always the case with fresh offshoots of capital that are newly formed — or to the plethora in which these capitals, which are incapable of acting by themselves, are available to the leaders of great branches of business in the form of credit."

Key implication: the "plethora" is not a natural fact but a ratio, defined against profitability. And "This plethora of capital arises from the same causes that produce a relative surplus population and is therefore a phenomenon that complements this latter, even though the two things stand at opposite poles — unoccupied capital on the one hand and an unemployed working population on the other."

2.19 Absolute Overproduction of Capital: Definition and Arithmetic

Marx now poses the central question of the section. "Overproduction of capital and not of individual commodities... is nothing more than over-accumulation of capital. To understand what this over-accumulation is..., we have only to take it as an absolute." He then defines:

"There would be an absolute overproduction of capital as soon as no further additional capital could be employed for the purpose of capitalist production."

Conditions are spelled out: capital has grown in such proportion to the working population that neither the absolute labour-time supplied nor the relative surplus labour-time can be extended — the latter being impossible "in a situation where the demand for labour was so strong, and there was thus a tendency for wages to rise." In that case: "the expanded capital produces only the same mass of surplus-value as before." Formally, C + ΔC will not produce any more profit — or will produce less — than the capital C did before its increase by ΔC.

"there would even be a sharper and more sudden fall in the general rate of profit, but this time on account of a change in the composition of capital which would not be due to a development in productivity, but rather to a rise in the money value of the variable capital on account of higher wages and to a corresponding decline in the proportion of surplus labour to necessary labour."

In actual manifestation: one portion of capital lies idle (since it would first have to expel capital already functioning to be valorized), the other is valorized at a lower rate. The fall in the profit rate is now accompanied by an absolute decline in the mass of profit (since labour-power applied and the rate of surplus-value are unchanged, so mass of surplus-value is unchanged), and this reduced mass must be calculated on an enlarged total capital.

The arithmetic illustration: "If a total capital of 1,000 yields a profit of 100 and after being increased to 1,500 it still yields a profit of only 100, then in the second case 1,000 yields only 66⅔." So the valorization of the old capital suffers "an absolute decline": under the new conditions the capital of 1,000 would "not yield more than a capital of 666⅔ did earlier."

2.20 The Competitive Struggle and the "War of Enemy Brothers"

Such devaluation "would not take place without a struggle," and ΔC "could not function as capital without a struggle." Marx is emphatic that competition here is a consequence of the underlying condition, not its cause: "That competition which results from the overproduction of capital would not cause a fall in the rate of profit. Rather the reverse." The two spring from the same situation, and competition is unleashed as a form of the resolution process.

Three strategic behaviours are distinguished: 1. Existing capitalists let ΔC (already in their hands) lie idle, to avoid self-devaluing their original capital. 2. Or they "apply it so as to shift the idleness of the additional capital onto the more recent interlopers and onto their competitors in general, even at a temporary loss." 3. New hands attempt to place ΔC at the expense of old capital, displacing it from its former field.

The result: "one part of the old capital would have to lie idle as far as its property as capital was concerned" — that is, as the property of functioning and being valorized. The distribution of idleness is decided in the competitive struggle. And this transforms competition from freemasonry into fratricide:

"As long as everything goes well, competition acts, as is always the case when the general rate of profit is settled, as a practical freemasonry of the capitalist class, so that they all share in the common booty in proportion to the size of the portion that each puts in. But as soon as it is no longer a question of division of profit, but rather of loss, each seeks as far as he can to restrict his own share of this loss and pass it on to someone else... competition now becomes a struggle of enemy brothers."

Marx draws the socio-theoretical moral: "The opposition between the interest of each individual capitalist and that of the capitalist class as a whole now comes into its own, in the same way as competition was previously the instrument through which the identity of the capitalists' interests was asserted."

2.21 Resolution Through Idling, Destruction, and Devaluation

"How then is this conflict to be resolved?" Marx's answer is uncompromising: the resolution "involves this, that capital should lie idle, or even, in part, be destroyed, either to the entire value of the additional capital ΔC or at least to one part of this." The distribution of the loss is uneven: "one capital lies idle, another is destroyed, a third experiences only a relative loss or simply a temporary devaluation, and so on."

Moreover, the destruction is not merely of value but of material substance. "Part of the means of production, fixed and circulating capital, will not function and operate as capital, and a part of the productive effort that was begun will come to a halt." Though time damages all means of production (except the land), what occurs in crisis is "a far more intense actual destruction of means of production as the result of a stagnation in their function" — the chief effect being that these means of production "cease to be active as means of production."

Marx then ranks the destructiveness of these shocks by their form: "The chief disruption, and the one possessing the sharpest character, would occur in connection with capital in so far as it possesses the property of value, i.e. in connection with capital values." He enumerates the mechanisms: - The portion of capital value existing as claims on future surplus-value (promissory notes on production) is devalued along with the revenues on which it is reckoned. - A portion of ready gold and silver lies idle. - Commodities can complete their circulation and reproduction only at vastly reduced prices — a devaluation of the capital they represent. - Fixed capital elements are similarly devalued. - Because the reproduction process presupposes certain price relationships, their general collapse throws the process into "stagnation and confusion." - This paralyses money as a means of payment (a function dependent on those presupposed price relationships). - "The chain of payment obligations at specific dates is broken in a hundred places," aggravated by a breakdown of the credit system. - Conclusion: "All this therefore leads to violent and acute crises, sudden forcible devaluations, an actual stagnation and disruption in the reproduction process, and hence to an actual decline in reproduction."

2.22 The Recuperative Agencies and the Circular Return

But "other agencies come into play at the same time." Marx enumerates four restoration mechanisms:

  1. Wage depression via unemployment. Stagnation makes part of the working class idle, forcing employed workers to accept wages below average: "an operation that has exactly the same effect for capital as if relative or absolute surplus-value had been increased while wages remained at the average."
  2. Demographic expansion. Prosperity "facilitate[s] marriage among the workers and reduce[s] the decimation of their offspring," which, even without increasing the population actually working, "ha[s] the same effect on the relationship between the workers and capital as if the number of workers actually active had increased."
  3. Technological intensification. "The fall in prices and the competitive struggle... impel each capitalist to reduce the individual value of his total product below its general value by employing new machinery, new and improved methods of labour and new forms of combination" — that is, to raise productivity, reduce the ratio of variable to constant capital, dismiss workers, and "create an artificial surplus population."
  4. Cheapening of constant capital. "The devaluation of the elements of constant capital, moreover, itself involves a rise in the profit rate. The mass of constant capital applied grows as against the variable, but the value of this mass may have fallen."

The net effect: "The stagnation in production that has intervened prepares the ground for a later expansion of production — within the capitalist limits. And so we go round the whole circle once again. One part of the capital that was devalued by the cessation of its function now regains its old value. And apart from that, with expanded conditions of production, a wider market and increased productivity, the same cycle of errors is pursued once more." The "cycle of errors" — the phrasing is deliberate: the process is objectively cyclical, and each cycle is a repetition of a structurally identical error, not its correction.

2.23 Overproduction of Capital Is Not Overproduction of Wealth

Marx now carefully delimits the concept. "Even under the most extreme assumption that might be made, absolute overproduction of capital is not absolute overproduction in general, not absolute overproduction of the means of production. It is an overproduction of means of production only in so far as these function as capital, and hence have to produce an additional value in proportion to their value that has expanded together with their mass, i.e. have to valorize their value."

It is overproduction nonetheless, "since the capital is unable to exploit labour at the level of exploitation that is required by the 'healthy' and 'normal' development of the capitalist production process, at a level of exploitation that at least increases the mass of profit along with the growing mass of capital applied; that therefore excludes a situation in which the rate of profit falls to the same degree as capital grows, or even falls more quickly than this."

So "Overproduction of capital never means anything other than overproduction of means of production — means of labour and means of subsistence — that can function as capital, i.e. can be applied to exploiting labour at a given level of exploitation." The "given level" is critical because "a fall in the level of exploitation below a certain point produces disruption and stagnation in the capitalist production process, crisis, and the destruction of capital."

"It is no contradiction that this overproduction of capital is accompanied by a greater or smaller relative surplus population." The same causes that raised productivity, expanded markets, accelerated accumulation in mass and value, and lowered the profit rate, have also produced a relative surplus population — workers "who are not employed by this excess capital on account of the low level of exploitation of labour at which they would have to be employed, or at least on account of the low rate of profit they would yield at the given rate of exploitation."

2.24 Capital Export

Marx then addresses a common counter-argument: capital is not really surplus if it can be exported. His reply: "If capital is sent abroad, this is not because it absolutely could not be employed at home. It is rather because it can be employed abroad at a higher rate of profit. But this capital is absolutely surplus capital for the employed working population and for the country in question. It exists as such alongside the relative surplus population, and this is an example of how the two things exist side by side and reciprocally condition one another." Export is not refutation; it is confirmation, since it shows capital's mobility is constrained by relative profitability, not by any physical limit.

2.25 The Fall in the Rate Provokes Competition, Not Vice Versa

Marx restates the direction of causation. "Compensation for the fall in the profit rate by an increase in the mass of profit is possible only for the total social capital and for the big capitalists who are already established. New and independently operating additional capital finds no compensatory conditions of this kind ready made; it must first acquire them, and so it is the fall in the profit rate that provokes the competitive struggle between capitals, and not the reverse." This inverts the vulgar supposition that competition produces the falling rate; for Marx, the falling rate is the underlying tendency, and competition is one of its surface manifestations. He adds: "This competitive struggle, moreover, is accompanied by a temporary rise in wages and a further temporary fall in the profit rate, deriving from this."

2.26 The Polemic on Overproduction of Commodities

Marx now takes aim at a characteristic bourgeois evasion. "Since capital's purpose is not the satisfaction of needs but the production of profit, and since it attains this purpose only by methods that determine the mass of production by reference exclusively to the yardstick of production, and not the reverse, there must be a constant tension between the restricted dimensions of consumption on the capitalist basis, and a production that is constantly striving to overcome these immanent barriers." Because "capital consists of commodities, and hence overproduction of capital involves overproduction of commodities," there is the "singular phenomenon that the same economists who deny overproduction of commodities admit overproduction of capital."

He then dismantles three standard defences: - "No general overproduction — only disproportion between branches." Marx replies: this "means nothing more than that, within capitalist production, the proportionality of the particular branches of production presents itself as a process of passing constantly out of and into disproportionality." Because social interconnection imposes itself "as a blind law, and not as a law which, being grasped and therefore mastered by their combined reason, brings the productive process under their common control." - "Overproduction is only relative." "This is completely correct; but the whole capitalist mode of production is precisely such a relative mode of production, whose barriers are not absolute, but only absolute for it, on its basis." - "The capitalists have only to exchange and consume their commodities among themselves." "Then the whole character of capitalist production is forgotten... what is involved is the valorization of capital, not its consumption."

The cumulative diagnosis: the objection that "the barriers to capitalist production are not barriers to production in general" is true but irrelevant: "the contradiction in this capitalist mode of production consists precisely in its tendency towards the absolute development of productive forces that come into continuous conflict with the specific conditions of production in which capital moves, and can alone move."

2.27 The "Not Too Many / Too Few" Triptych

Marx then delivers his devastating inversion of common-sense Malthusianism:

  • "It is not that too many means of subsistence are produced in relation to the existing population. On the contrary. Too little is produced to satisfy the mass of the population in an adequate and humane way."
  • "Nor are too many means of production produced to employ the potential working population." Rather, the system produces (i) "too great a section of the population which is in fact incapable of work" — Marx's footnote clarifies this means those dependent on the labour of others or on work that "can only count as such within a miserable mode of production," with the reference most probably to petty commodity production, especially smallholding peasants; and (ii) produces "not enough means of production... to allow the whole potential working population to work under the most productive conditions."
  • "Periodically, however, too much is produced in the way of means of labour and means of subsistence, too much to function as means for exploiting the workers at a given rate of profit." Too many commodities are produced for the value and surplus-value they contain to be realized and reconverted into capital without "ever-recurrent explosions."

The final aphorism: "It is not that too much wealth is produced. But from time to time, too much wealth is produced in its capitalist, antagonistic forms."

2.28 The Two Great Barriers Enumerated

Marx condenses the entire analysis into two barriers:

(1) "in the way that the development of labour productivity involves a law, in the form of the falling rate of profit, that at a certain point confronts this development itself in a most hostile way and has constantly to be overcome by way of crises."

(2) "in the way that it is the appropriation of unpaid labour, and the proportion between this unpaid labour and objectified labour in general — to put it in capitalist terms, profit and the proportion between this profit and the capital applied, i.e. a certain rate of profit — it is this that determines the expansion or contraction of production, instead of the proportion between production and social needs, the needs of socially developed human beings."

"Barriers to production, therefore, arise already at a level of expansion which appears completely inadequate from the other standpoint. Production comes to a standstill not at the point where needs are satisfied, but rather where the production and realization of profit impose this."

2.29 Exertions and Swindles Under the Falling Rate

"If the rate of profit falls," Marx notes, two forms of desperate response emerge. On one side, genuine efficiency drives: "the individual capitalist drives down the individual value of his own particular commodities below their average social value, by using better methods, etc., and thus makes a surplus profit at the given market price." On the other, "swindling and general promotion of swindling, through desperate attempts in the way of new methods of production, new capital investments and new adventures, to secure some kind of extra profit, which will be independent of the general average and superior to it." This anticipates Section 4's discussion of speculation among "fragmented capitals."

2.30 The "Animating Fire" and the Assessment of Ricardo

"The rate of profit, i.e. the relative growth in capital, is particularly important for all new off-shoots of capital that organize themselves independently." Marx adds the following vivid claim:

"And if capital formation were to fall exclusively into the hands of a few existing big capitals, for whom the mass of profit outweighs the rate, the animating fire of production would be totally extinguished. It would die out."

This is a systemic claim: the rate of profit has functional significance as the incentive mechanism that animates new investment. Diminishing it to insignificance would not merely redistribute income; it would extinguish the motive force of the system. "It is the rate of profit that is the driving force in capitalist production, and nothing is produced save what can be produced at a profit. Hence the concern of the English economists over the decline in the profit rate."

Marx then offers a famously generous, dialectical appraisal of Ricardo: "If Ricardo is disquieted even by the very possibility of this, that precisely shows his deep understanding of the conditions of capitalist production. What other people reproach him for, i.e. that he is unconcerned with 'human beings' and concentrates exclusively on the development of the productive forces when considering capitalist production — whatever sacrifices of human beings and capital values this is bought with — is precisely his significant contribution." For Marx, Ricardo's indifference to human suffering is the very index of his scientific seriousness with respect to the laws of the system; the economist who weeps for individuals misses the historic mission: "The development of the productive forces of social labour is capital's historic mission and justification. For that very reason, it unwittingly creates the material conditions for a higher form of production."

"What disturbs Ricardo is the way that the rate of profit, which is the stimulus of capitalist production and both the condition for and the driving force in accumulation, is endangered by the development of production itself. And the quantitative relation is everything here. In actual fact, the underlying reason is something deeper, about which he has no more than a suspicion." What is visible here, "from the bourgeois standpoint, within the limits of capitalist understanding, from the standpoint of capitalist production itself, are its barriers, its relativity, the fact that it is not an absolute but only a historical mode of production, corresponding to a specific and limited epoch in the development of the material conditions of production."

2.31 Section 4: Supplementary Remarks — Uneven Development and Natural Conditions

Section 4 opens with a methodological caveat on the heterogeneity of the tendency. "Since the development of labour productivity is far from uniform in the various branches of industry and, besides being uneven in degree, often takes place in opposite directions, it so happens that the mass of average profit (= surplus-value) is necessarily very far below the level one would expect simply from the development of productivity in the most advanced branches." Moreover, uneven and often opposite trajectories "do not arise simply from the anarchy of competition and the specific features of the bourgeois mode of production" — they are also natural: "The productivity of labour is also tied up with natural conditions, which are often less favourable as productivity rises — as far as that depends on social conditions." Hence "progress here, regression there," illustrated by the influence of the seasons on raw materials and by "the exhaustion of forests, coal and iron mines, and so on."

2.32 Fixed vs. Circulating Constant Capital and the Value of the Individual Commodity

Marx next distinguishes the trajectories of circulating and fixed constant capital. "If the circulating part of the constant capital (raw material, etc.) steadily grows in mass together with the productivity of labour, this is not the case for the fixed capital — buildings, machinery, lighting and heating installations, and so on. Even though these become dearer in absolute terms as the physical mass of the machinery grows, they become relatively cheaper." Illustration: "If five workers produce ten times as many commodities as before, this does not mean that the outlay on fixed capital increases ten-fold."

He insists on a distinction within the composition relation itself, one he has "already emphasized several times": the constant/variable ratio as it is expressed in the fall in the profit rate is not identical to the same ratio as it presents itself with respect to the individual commodity and its price. This distinction is then worked out in the parenthetical passage.

2.33 The Parenthetical Excursus: Value, Living and Past Labour

Marx begins with a fundamental premise. "The value of a commodity is determined by the total labour-time contained in it, both past and living. The rise in labour productivity consists precisely in the fact that the share of living labour is reduced and that of past labour increased, but in such a way that the total sum of labour contained in the commodity declines; in other words the living labour declines by more than the past labour increases."

Past labour embodied in value = wear and tear of fixed capital + circulating constant capital (raw and ancillary materials). "The portion of value deriving from raw and ancillary materials must fall with the [rising] productivity of labour, since, as far as these materials go, this productivity is precisely expressed in the fact that their value has fallen." But "it is precisely a characteristic of rising labour productivity that the fixed portion of the constant capital should experience a very sharp increase, and with this also the portion of value that it transfers to the commodities as wear and tear."

Hence the criterion for genuine productivity advance: "For a new method of production to prove itself as a genuine advance in productivity, it must transfer a smaller additional share of value to the individual commodity for depreciation of the fixed capital than the portion of value that is deducted because less living labour is spared; it must in other words reduce the value of the commodity." Even in cases where an additional portion of value is added (more or dearer raw/ancillary materials), "All this additional value must be more than outweighed by the reduction in value that arises from the decrease in living labour."

Marx notes: "This reduction in the total quantity of labour going into the commodity appears accordingly as the fundamental characteristic of a rise in labour productivity, irrespective of the social conditions under which production is carried on. In a society where the producers govern their production by a plan drawn up in advance, or even in simple commodity production, the productivity of labour is in fact invariably measured by such a standard. But what is the situation in capitalist production?"

2.34 The Machine Example: The Capitalist's Calculus and the New Contradiction

The parenthetical continues with the chapter's most technically precise numerical illustration. Suppose a branch of capitalist production with the average social composition, so that its production price coincides with value:

Initial state (per item): - Depreciation of fixed capital: ½ shilling - Raw and ancillary materials: 17½ shillings - Wages: 2 shillings - Rate of surplus-value: 100%, so surplus-value: 2 shillings - Total value: 22 shillings - Cost price: ½ + 17½ + 2 = 20 shillings - Profit rate: 2/20 = 10 per cent - Production price = value = 22 shillings

Now let a machine be invented that (a) halves the living labour per item and (b) triples the share of value attributable to depreciation of fixed capital.

New state (per item): - Depreciation: 1½ shillings (3 × ½) - Raw and ancillary materials: 17½ shillings (unchanged) - Wages: 1 shilling (halved) - Surplus-value: 1 shilling (100% of wages) - Total value: 21 shillings — a full shilling reduction. So "The value of the commodity has now fallen by 1 shilling; the new machine has definitely raised the productivity of labour."

But for the capitalist: cost price = 1½ + 17½ + 1 = 20 shillings — "the same as before." Since the general rate of profit is unchanged, he keeps 10 per cent on his cost price = 2 shillings. So the price of production is unchanged at 22 shillings, though this is now 1 shilling above the value.

Marx's conclusion: "For a society producing under capitalist conditions, the commodity has not become cheaper and the new machine is not an improvement. The capitalist, therefore, has no interest in introducing the new machine. And since introducing it would also make his old machinery simply worthless, when it has not yet worn out, transforming it into nothing more than scrap-iron, so that he would actually suffer a positive loss, he refrains from what would be, for him, a piece of utopian stupidity."

The theoretical generalization follows: "For capital, therefore, the law of increased productivity of labour is not unconditionally valid. For capital, this productivity is not raised simply because more living labour in general is spared than is added in past labour, but only if more of the paid part of living labour is spared, as we have already indicated in brief in Volume I, Chapter 15, pp. 515 ff."

And here is the new contradiction the parenthetical announces: "At this point the capitalist mode of production falls into a new contradiction. Its historical mission is ruthlessly to expand the productivity of human labour, to drive it onwards in geometrical progression. It is untrue to its mission as soon as it starts to inhibit the development of productivity, as it does here. It thereby simply shows once more that it is becoming senile and has further and further outlived its epoch."

Engels' footnote 37 specifies: "I have put the above in parentheses because, although it is re-edited from a note in the original manuscript, it goes beyond the original material in certain particulars. — F.E."

2.35 Competition, Minimum Capital, and the Fate of Small Capitals

"In competition, the rising minimum amount of capital needed for the successful pursuit of an independent industrial business takes the following form, as productivity increases. Once the new and more expensive equipment has been generally introduced, smaller capitals are in future excluded from this line of business. Only when mechanical inventions in the various spheres of production are in their infancy can smaller capitals function independently."

At the other pole, "Very large undertakings... where the proportion of constant capital is extraordinarily high, such as railways, do not yield the average profit rate, but only a portion of this, an interest. If this were not so the general rate of profit would fall still lower. And yet a great accumulation of capital in the form of shares finds a direct field of employment here." This anticipates the later theory of fictitious capital: railways (and analogous enterprises) bear comparison to interest-bearing capital rather than productive capital, and the share form is the vehicle by which mass accumulation proceeds in these spheres.

2.36 Growth Without Organic Change: A Partial Counter-Tendency

Marx qualifies the transition from accumulation to falling rate. "Growth of capital, i.e. accumulation of capital, involves a reduction in the rate of profit only in so far as this growth brings with it those changes in the ratio between the organic components of capital that were considered above." Crucially:

"Yet despite the constant and daily transformations in the mode of production, a greater or smaller part of this total capital, now this, now that, continues to accumulate for a certain period of time on the basis of a given average ratio of these components, so that its growth does not involve any organic change and is thus no cause for a fall in the rate of profit."

"This constant enlargement of the capital, and therefore also an expansion in production on the basis of the old methods which goes smoothly forward while new methods are already introduced alongside, is a further reason why the rate of profit does not decline in the same measure as the total social capital grows." This is a significant hedge: the tendency operates unevenly across sectors and time, so that the empirical fall is always slower than a purely compositional projection would predict.

2.37 The Working Population and the Absolute Barrier of Surplus Labour-Time

"The increase in the absolute number of workers, despite the relative decline in the variable capital laid out on wages, does not take place in all branches of production and does not take place evenly in the branches where it does. In agriculture, the decline in the element of living labour may be absolute."

Nonetheless: "It is simply the needs of the capitalist mode of production... that lead the number of wage-labourers to increase absolutely, despite this relative decline. As far as this mode of production is concerned, labour-power is superfluous the moment it is no longer necessary to occupy it for 12 to 15 hours per day." A development in the productive forces that reduced the absolute number of workers and enabled "the whole nation to accomplish its entire production in a shorter period of time, would produce a revolution, since it would put the majority of the population out of action." Hence: "Here we have once again the characteristic barrier to capitalist production... it is in no way an absolute form for the development of the productive forces and the creation of wealth, but rather comes into conflict with this at a certain point in its development."

"One aspect of this conflict is presented by the periodic crises that arise when one or another section of the working population is made superfluous in its old employment." And the signature aphorism:

"The barrier to capitalist production is the surplus time of the workers. The absolute spare time that the society gains is immaterial to capitalist production. The development of productivity is only important to it in so far as it increases the surplus labour-time of the working class and does not just reduce the labour-time needed for material production in general; in this way it moves in a contradiction."

This is one of the chapter's most compressed theoretical statements: capitalism's criterion is not the reduction of socially necessary labour-time but its conversion into surplus labour-time. Absolute gains in spare time — the genuine measure of civilizational progress on any non-capitalist standard — are irrelevant.

2.38 Capital as Alienated Social Power

"We have seen how the growing accumulation of capital involves its growing concentration. Thus the power of capital grows, in other words the autonomy of the social conditions of production, as personified by the capitalist, is asserted more and more as against the actual producers."

The famous passage follows: "Capital shows itself more and more to be a social power, with the capitalist as its functionary — a power that no longer stands in any possible kind of relationship to what the work of one particular individual can create, but an alienated social power which has gained an autonomous position and confronts society as a thing, and as the power that the capitalist has through this thing." The dialectic then unfolds:

"The contradiction between the general social power into which capital has developed and the private power of the individual capitalists over these social conditions of production develops ever more blatantly, while this development also contains the solution to this situation, in that it simultaneously raises the conditions of production into general, communal, social conditions. This transformation is brought about by the development of the productive forces under capitalist production and by the manner and form in which this development is accomplished."

This is the chapter's most explicit statement that the material preconditions of socialism are generated by capitalist development itself, not least by the socialization of production that accompanies its concentration.

2.39 The Mechanism of Innovation: Why Capitalists Resist What They Ultimately Must Do

The next block (after an asterisk, marking it as a re-edited passage) provides the micro-logic of productivity advance under the falling rate:

"No capitalist voluntarily applies a new method of production, no matter how much more productive it may be or how much it might raise the rate of surplus-value, if it reduces the rate of profit."

The mechanism by which innovations are nevertheless introduced relies on temporary surplus profit. "But every new method of production of this kind makes commodities cheaper. At first, therefore, he can sell them above their price of production, perhaps above their value. He pockets the difference between their costs of production and the market price of the other commodities, which are produced at higher production costs. This is possible because the average socially necessary labour-time required to produce these latter commodities is greater than the labour-time required with the new method of production. His production procedure is ahead of the social average."

Then competition universalizes: "But competition makes the new procedure universal and subjects it to the general law. A fall in the profit rate then ensues — firstly perhaps in this sphere of production, and subsequently equalized with the others — a fall that is completely independent of the capitalists' will."

This reconciles the individual rationality (adopt an innovation yielding temporary surplus profit) with the collective consequence (falling general rate). The capitalist is a rational prisoner of the system: no individual can resist adopting an innovation that yields a surplus profit, even though the resulting general fall in the rate erodes all of them.

2.40 The Law Prevails Even in Branches Producing No Wage Goods

Marx extends the argument beyond the branches directly affecting labour-power reproduction: "the same law prevails even in those spheres of production whose products do not enter either directly or indirectly into the workers' consumption, or into the conditions of production of their means of subsistence." There, no cheapening can raise relative surplus-value or make labour-power cheaper. Nonetheless:

"(In fact, a cheapening of constant capital in any of these branches may increase the profit rate, if the level of exploitation of labour remains the same.) As soon as the new mode of production begins to spread, giving actual proof that these commodities can be produced more cheaply, then those capitalists who operate under the old conditions of production must sell their product below its full price of production; the value of this commodity has fallen, so that they need more labour-time to produce it than is socially necessary. In short, and this appears as the effect of competition, they must also introduce the new mode of production which reduces the ratio of variable capital to constant."

The condition of competitive survival — not the cheapening of wage goods — forces the raising of the composition of capital in all branches. This is a crucial generalization: the falling rate of profit is not merely a function of the wage-goods department.

2.41 Machinery and Depreciation

"The application of machinery reduces the price of the commodities produced with that machinery owing to various factors, which can always be reduced to the decline in the quantity of labour absorbed by each individual commodity; but in addition to this there is the decline in the portion of value that goes into the individual commodity as the depreciation element of the machinery. The slower the machinery's depreciation, the more commodities it is distributed over, the more living labour it replaces before the day when its reproduction falls due. In both cases the quantity and value of the fixed constant capital are increased as against the variable."

This restates the double mechanism: machinery cheapens commodities both by absorbing less living labour and by distributing its depreciation component over more units of output. But both effects presuppose — and intensify — the rise of fixed constant capital relative to variable capital, i.e. the very process that drives the profit rate down.

2.42 Richard Jones and the "Inducements and Faculties to Accumulate"

Marx concludes the chapter with an appreciative engagement with Richard Jones. He quotes Jones:

"All other things being equal, the power of a nation to save from its profits varies with the rate of profits, is great when they are high, less, when low; but as the rate of profit declines, all other things do not remain equal... A low rate of profit is ordinarily accompanied by a rapid rate of accumulation, relatively to the numbers of the people, as in England... a high rate of profit by a lower rate of accumulation, relatively to the numbers of the people."

Examples of the latter: "Poland, Russia, India, etc." The source is cited in a footnote as Richard Jones, An Introductory Lecture on Political Economy, London, 1833, pp. 50 ff.

Marx endorses Jones: "Jones is right to stress that, despite the falling rate of profit, the 'inducements and faculties to accumulate' increase." He then supplies six reasons:

  1. "on account of the growing relative surplus population" — the reserve army cheapens labour-power.
  2. "because as the productivity of labour grows, so does the mass of use-values represented by the same exchange-value, i.e. the material elements of capital" — the use-value substratum of accumulation expands.
  3. "because of the increasing diversity of branches of production" — new departments of investment.
  4. "through the development of the credit system, joint-stock companies, etc., and the ease with which the possessor of money can now transform it into capital without having to become an industrial capitalist" — the separation of capital ownership from capitalist function.
  5. "the growth in needs and the desire for enrichment" — the development of socially cultivated appetites and the incentive structure itself.
  6. "the growing mass of investment of fixed capital, and so on" — the scale of durable investment.

The list is systematic: demographic, material, structural, financial, motivational, and physical. It demonstrates that the fall of the profit rate does not mechanically dampen accumulation — a point that rescues the theory from vulgar Ricardian underconsumptionism.

2.43 The Three Cardinal Facts and the Final Contradiction

Marx closes the chapter with the three "cardinal facts about capitalist production":

(1) "The concentration of the means of production in a few hands, which means that they cease to appear as the property of the immediate workers and are transformed on the contrary into social powers of production. Even if this is at first as the private property of capitalists. The latter are trustees of bourgeois society, though they pocket all the fruits of this trusteeship."

This is a remarkably dialectical formulation. Concentration simultaneously privatizes ownership (a few hands) and socializes productive power (the means of production "cease to appear as the property of the immediate workers"). The capitalist is not a personal owner but a "trustee" — a functionary managing what has become a social power — who nonetheless appropriates the proceeds.

(2) "The organization of labour itself as social labour: through cooperation, division of labour and the association of labour with natural science." The labour process becomes collective in form: cooperation gathers many into a common process; division of labour links them reciprocally; the incorporation of science into production organizes labour according to general principles.

On (1) and (2) jointly: "the capitalist mode of production abolishes private property and private labour, even if in antithetical forms." The phrase "antithetical forms" is precise: capitalism abolishes private property as it consolidates it into a few hands, and abolishes private labour as it subordinates labour to capital.

(3) "Establishment of the world market." The contradiction is not confined to any one nation: the world market is the necessary spatial form of capital's tendency toward absolute development.

The closing paragraph draws the verdict:

"The tremendous productive power, in proportion to the population, which is developed within the capitalist mode of production, and — even if not to the same degree — the growth in capital values (not only in their material substratum), these growing far more quickly than the population, contradicts the basis on behalf of which this immense productive power operates, since this basis becomes ever narrower in relation to the growth of wealth; and it also contradicts the conditions of valorization of this swelling capital. Hence crises."

The chapter ends with the aetiological aphorism: "Hence crises." The falling rate of profit, over-accumulation, the surplus of capital alongside surplus population, the periodic explosions of crisis — all flow from the single structural fact that capital is both the means of production of wealth and the fetter upon its full development.

A footnote notes that Richard Jones (1790–1855) is "the third (with Malthus and Chalmers) in a trinity of economist parsons, though Marx ranks him somewhat higher than he does his two colleagues. See Theories of Surplus-Value, Part III, Chapter XXIV."

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

3.1 Fundamental Principles Revealed

First: the structural identity of the tendency and its counter-tendencies. The chapter's central methodological discovery is that the fall in the rate of profit is not a mechanical trend alongside which counteracting forces run as external brakes. Rather, the same process — rising productivity, mechanization, cheapening of commodities, devaluation of existing capital — is simultaneously cause and limit of the fall. Devaluation accelerates accumulation and deepens the tendency; the cheapening of constant capital raises the rate and raises the composition. This dialectical identity is what distinguishes Marx's theory from a Ricardian diminishing-returns story (which locates the barrier in nature) and from a simple underconsumptionism (which locates it in demand).

Second: crisis as form of resolution, not as pathology. "Crises are never more than momentary, violent solutions for the existing contradictions, violent eruptions that re-establish the disturbed balance for the time being." The systemic implication is profound: crisis is a functional moment of capitalist reproduction — the means by which the contradiction between production-as-end-in-itself and valorization-of-existing-capital is temporarily suspended. To abolish crisis would require abolishing the relation that makes crisis functionally necessary.

Third: the rate/mass distinction as the key to the entire dynamic. The chapter's most consistently deployed analytical device is the distinction between rate and mass. Falling rate does not entail falling mass; rising mass does not entail rising rate; the capitalist as individual behaves according to one, the system as a whole according to the other. The surplus population doctrine, the surplus capital doctrine, and the theory of crisis all rest on this distinction.

Fourth: the historical-transitoriness of capitalism is visible from within the bourgeois standpoint. The chapter is emphatic that the demonstration of capitalism's relativity does not require an external moral or philosophical critique. It is immanent — "from the bourgeois standpoint, within the limits of capitalist understanding... are its barriers, its relativity, the fact that it is not an absolute but only a historical mode of production, corresponding to a specific and limited epoch in the development of the material conditions of production." This is the mature form of Marx's critical method.

Fifth: capital's "historic mission" as the source of its supersession. "The development of the productive forces of social labour is capital's historic mission and justification. For that very reason, it unwittingly creates the material conditions for a higher form of production." The socialization of production, the concentration of the means of production into "social powers of production," the organization of labour "as social labour" through cooperation, division of labour, and the association of labour with natural science — these are the material preconditions generated by the very process that drives the profit rate down.

3.2 Systemic Laws and Essential Takeaways

The chapter yields a compact set of systemic laws:

  • Law of compositional grounding: The rate of profit falls because less labour is generally applied in relation to capital invested — not because workers are less exploited. Exploitation and profitability are in inverse relationship under conditions of advancing productivity.
  • Law of over-accumulation: Over-accumulation is defined not absolutely against human need, but relationally against the level of exploitation required for what Marx calls "the 'healthy' and 'normal' development of the capitalist production process."
  • Law of dual poles: Surplus capital and surplus population are produced by the same causes; they stand at opposite poles but reciprocally condition one another; their coexistence is not a paradox but a structural necessity.
  • Law of export as replication: Capital export does not resolve the contradiction; it spatially relocates it, and confirms the existence of surplus capital in the exporting country.
  • Law of the "animating fire": The rate of profit, not the mass, is the systemic incentive; if capital formation passed exclusively into the hands of a few big capitals "for whom the mass of profit outweighs the rate, the animating fire of production would be totally extinguished."
  • Law of double restraint: Production is restrained by both the barrier to exploitation (productive forces) and the barrier to realization (proportionality and consumption under antagonistic distribution). These are "separate in time and space... and in theory."

3.3 Broader Practical and Theoretical Implications

Against Ricardo and the naturalization of crisis. Marx's demolition of Ricardo's displacement of the barrier onto "nature" (the theory of rent) has enduring value. Every subsequent attempt to naturalize capitalist crisis — as environmental limit, as demographic limit, as technological limit — can be diagnosed along the same lines: an externalization of an internally generated contradiction. That West anticipated Ricardo on rent, and that Chalmers offered a Malthusian picture of desired under-consumption, shows that the "physiocratic" and "pastoral" evasions were structural temptations of the period, not idiosyncrasies.

Against Sismondi and underconsumptionism. By insisting that the purpose of capitalist production is valorization, not consumption, the chapter simultaneously rejects the accusation of underconsumptionism and rejects the crude denial of general overproduction. As Marx puts it: it is "no contradiction that this overproduction of capital is accompanied by a greater or smaller relative surplus population"; but it is likewise no contradiction that the very goods the mass of the people lack find no demand, because "it is only in this specific, capitalist context that the surplus product receives a form in which its proprietor can make it available for consumption as soon as it has been transformed back into capital for himself."

Against Say's Law and the "exchange among capitalists" apologetic. "If it is said, finally, that the capitalists have only to exchange their commodities among themselves and consume them, then the whole character of capitalist production is forgotten, and it is forgotten that what is involved is the valorization of capital, not its consumption." The refutation is not empirical but conceptual: it consists in demonstrating that the premise already assumes away the phenomenon in question.

For the theory of fictitious capital and finance. The chapter's treatment of claims on surplus-value (promissory notes), idle gold and silver, credit breakdown, "share swindles," railways yielding only interest, and the ease with which a money owner can transform money into capital "without having to become an industrial capitalist" provides the analytical seedbed for the later Parts of Volume 3 on interest-bearing and fictitious capital.

For the theory of the state and of class politics. The distinction between individual capitalist interest and class interest — "competition now becomes a struggle of enemy brothers" — has direct implications for the theory of capitalist class cohesion, its dissolution in crises, and the political forms that emerge when the "practical freemasonry" of the boom is broken.

For historical materialism. The chapter's conclusion — the convergence of concentration, socialization of labour, world market, and the growing contradiction between "the general social power into which capital has developed and the private power of the individual capitalists over these social conditions of production" — is among Marx's most explicit statements of the materialist thesis that capitalism generates the preconditions of its own supersession.

3.4 The Unfinished and Contested Character of the Chapter

The chapter bears the marks of its compositional history. Engels' footnote 37 to the parenthetical machine-example explicitly flags the passage as "re-edited from a note in the original manuscript" that "goes beyond the original material in certain particulars." The book has a patchwork quality in places: Section 1 recapitulates and formalizes, Section 2 opens the dialectic of production and realization, Section 3 develops the theory of over-accumulation and crisis in detail, and Section 4 gathers supplementary remarks on fixed capital, machinery, credit, and the demographic underpinning. But the argumentative unity is nonetheless impressive: from the initial recapitulation of p′ versus s′ to the closing triple of cardinal facts, the chapter traces a single, coherent line of reasoning — that the tendency of the rate of profit to fall is the form of appearance of the contradiction between the social development of productive forces and the private form of their appropriation, and that this contradiction must repeatedly discharge itself in crises.

The verdict of the chapter: capital contains within itself the barrier to its own historic mission. What begins as the most dynamic productive apparatus ever seen becomes, at a certain point in its development, a fetter on the very productive forces it fostered. The falling rate of profit is thus not merely an economic quantity; it is the numerical signature of a historical limit — one that, in Marx's framework, is not a limit of nature, technology, or the human spirit, but of the social relation that subordinates the development of the forces of production to the imperatives of valorization. Hence crises. Hence, in Marx's larger argument, the historical transitoriness of the capitalist mode of production itself.
