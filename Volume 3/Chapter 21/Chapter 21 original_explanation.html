Volume 3 • Chapter 21: Interest-Bearing Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 21 of Capital, Volume 3 opens Part Five, the section of Marx's great unfinished work dedicated to "The Division of Profit into Interest and Profit of Enterprise." This is a pivotal structural hinge in the whole architecture of the three volumes. The chapter's central problem is deceptively simple: how does money — a mere sum of value — acquire a price? Or, more precisely, once we admit that the average rate of profit exists and is uniform across industrial and commercial capital, how does a "price of capital" become possible at all, and what is the true nature of interest?

The chapter's core thesis can be stated in a compact phrase: interest-bearing capital is capital that has become a commodity, and interest is the price paid for the peculiar use-value of that commodity — the capacity to produce the average profit. But this compact thesis immediately generates a cascade of paradoxes that Marx is at pains to unpack. If interest is the "price" of capital, then capital appears to have a double value — its own value plus a qualitatively different price — which contradicts the very concept of price as the money-expression of value. Marx calls interest "a completely irrational expression right from the start." The chapter is therefore at once an economic investigation into the origin of interest and a logical/conceptual critique of the way classical political economy (and the socialist critique of Proudhon) handled the phenomenon.

The intellectual objective is threefold. First, Marx must rigorously distinguish the form of the movement of interest-bearing capital — the circuit M—M—C—M'—M' — from the circuit of industrial capital (M—C—M') and from the circuit of commercial capital. The interest-bearing circuit's hallmark is that the mediating process (production and circulation) is obliterated, leaving only "money that begets money." Second, Marx must demonstrate that the phenomenon of interest is not a deviation from the theory of value but a specific form of distribution of surplus-value already produced: the total profit is split into interest (the part falling to the money capitalist, the owner) and profit of enterprise (the part remaining to the functioning capitalist, the borrower). Third, the chapter must prepare the ground for the subsequent analysis in Chapters 22–24 of profit of enterprise, the money market, and credit, by establishing that there is no "natural" rate of interest — that the rate of interest is determined by competition in a way that has no internal "law" comparable to the law governing prices of production or wages.

The chapter thus sits at the precise intersection of value theory, distribution theory, and the theory of the credit system. In the earlier parts of Volume 3, Marx established that value is produced in production by the exploitation of labour-power, and that surplus-value is distributed among capitals via the formation of prices of production and the equalization of profit rates. In Part Four he had incorporated commercial capital into that equalization: commercial capital, even though it does not produce surplus-value, participates in the average rate of profit and yields commercial profit. Now, in Part Five, he introduces a third category of claim on surplus-value: the claim of the owner of capital as such, as distinct from the functioning capitalist who employs it. Interest is the revenue of mere ownership; profit of enterprise is the revenue of functioning. This distinction, once established, will ultimately allow Marx to treat the financial system (credit, banks, joint-stock companies) as the highest and most "fetishistic" form of the capitalist mode of production — the form in which the social character of capital appears as the property of a thing (money breeding money).

The method is characteristic of the mature Marx of Volume 3: he begins with a simple, almost pedagogical numerical illustration (the £100 yielding £20, then £5 paid as interest), uses it to isolate the essential relations, and then rigorously distinguishes the real movement of capital from the legal form in which that movement is enveloped. Throughout, he is engaged in a running polemic against Proudhon (and against the "natural justice" of Gilbart), whose confusion arises precisely from trying to apply the categories of simple commodity exchange — buying, selling, price — directly to capital. The chapter's analytical climax is the demonstration that the "price of capital" (interest) is a sui generis category, an irrational form that nonetheless expresses a real relation: the valorization of money capital, measurable only by comparing interest to the value advanced.

Finally, the chapter is crucial for understanding Marx's own intellectual honesty about the limits of theory. Having shown that commodity prices are governed by prices of production and that wages are governed by the value of labour-power, Marx admits that the rate of interest has no analogous "natural" law. Competition does not merely cause fluctuations around a law; here competition is the only determination. This is a remarkable concession about the analytical authority of competition in a specific domain, and it positions the interest rate as the paradigmatic case of a "lawless and arbitrary" market phenomenon — a theme that will recur in the analysis of fictitious capital and financial crisis.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Completed General Rate of Profit and the Transition to Interest

The chapter opens with a retrospective clarification. Marx reminds us that in Part Two, when he first derived the general or average rate of profit, that rate was not yet in its "finished form," because the equalization producing it appeared only as an equalization of industrial capitals across different spheres. It was in Part Four, with the introduction of commercial capital and commercial profit, that the average rate acquired its "more closely defined limits." From this point forward, Marx stipulates, when he speaks of "the general rate of profit or the average profit," he means this latter, finished sense — a rate that applies indifferently to industrial and commercial capital alike. This is not a trivial bookkeeping point. It is the precondition for the whole chapter, because it establishes that any capital of a given size yields the same annual average profit, whether it is invested in production or in circulation. The consequence is that "it is also no longer necessary to make a distinction between industrial and commercial profit, once it is a question of this average rate of profit."

This equalization is crucial for interest theory because it means the money capitalist does not need to know or care how the borrower will use the money (industrially or commercially). The loan is advanced as capital in general, and the return is reckoned on the basis of the average profit. The "average level of intelligence and activity appropriate to the intended purpose" is implicitly assumed — Marx is bracketing differential entrepreneurial skill, which will matter later in the analysis of profit of enterprise but not here.

Money as Potential Capital: The Emergence of a Special Commodity

The substantive argument begins with one of the most consequential moves in all of Capital: the claim that, on the basis of capitalist production, money can be transformed into capital, and through this transformation it becomes a self-valorizing value capable of increasing itself. Money produces profit; it enables the capitalist to extract and appropriate a certain quantity of unpaid labour, surplus product, and surplus-value. In this way money receives, besides the use-value it possesses as money, an additional use-value — namely, "the ability to function as capital." Its use-value here consists precisely in the profit it produces when transformed into capital. And it is in this capacity of potential capital, as a means to the production of profit, that it becomes a commodity, "but a commodity of a special kind." Or, as Marx immediately reformulates it: "capital becomes a commodity."

This is the conceptual keystone of the chapter. Let us unpack its components:

  1. Money as money has a use-value (it can buy commodities) and a value (it is the universal equivalent).
  2. Money as capital has an additional use-value: the capacity to produce surplus-value when advanced. This use-value is not intrinsic to money as a physical thing; it is a social property, arising from the relations of production (specifically, from the existence of wage-labour and the exploitation of labour-power).
  3. Because this additional use-value can be alienated (temporarily transferred) to another person, money-as-capital takes on the form of a commodity. It is offered on a market (the money market), and it has, so to speak, a "price" — interest.

The genius of this formulation is that it simultaneously explains why interest exists (there is a use-value to be paid for) and why interest is conceptually anomalous (the "commodity" being sold is not a thing but a social relation; the "price" is not the money-expression of value but a share in a surplus).

The Numerical Illustration: £100, 20%, and the £5 Interest

Marx then makes the relation concrete. Take the average annual rate of profit as 20 per cent. Under average conditions, a machine worth £100 applied as capital yields a profit of £20. Therefore, a man who has £100 at his disposal "holds in his hands the power of making this £100 into £120, and thus producing a profit of £20." His £100 is potential capital. If he transfers it for a year to someone else who actually uses it as capital, he gives that person the power to produce £20 profit — "a surplus-value that costs him nothing and for which he does not pay any equivalent." If the borrower pays the owner £5 at year's end, that £5 is interest: "nothing but a particular name, a special title, for a part of the profit which the actually functioning capitalist has to pay to the capital's proprietor, instead of pocketing it himself."

Three things are worth flagging here. First, the £20 is the total profit produced by the functioning capital; the £5 interest is a subdivision of that £20. Interest is not an addition to profit; it is a deduction from it. This is a theme Marx will hammer repeatedly against Proudhon. Second, the magnitude of interest (£5) is here stipulated arbitrarily; the chapter will later explain that its determination is a matter of competition, not of any natural law. Third, the illustration already contains the essential distinction between the money capitalist (owner) and the functioning capitalist (borrower) — a distinction between two functions that may be performed by the same person (the owner who also uses his own capital) but which are conceptually distinct.

The Critique of Gilbart: Justice, Legal Forms, and the Content of Relations of Production

Marx then pauses for a polemical aside on the idea of "natural justice." James Gilbart had claimed that "a man who borrows money with a view of making a profit by it should give some portion of this profit to the lender" is "a self-evident principle of natural justice." Marx's response is a compact statement of his materialist theory of justice:

"The justice of transactions between agents of production consists in the fact that these transactions arise from the relations of production as their natural consequence. The legal forms in which these economic transactions appear as voluntary actions of the participants, as the expressions of their common will and as contracts that can be enforced on the parties concerned by the power of the state, are mere forms that cannot themselves determine this content. They simply express it. The content is just so long as it corresponds to the mode of production and is adequate to it. It is unjust as soon as it contradicts it."

This passage has enormous implications. Marx is not denying that justice-talk has meaning; he is relocating it. Justice is not a transcendent standard imposed on economic relations; it is a judgment about the adequacy of legal forms to the underlying mode of production. What is "just" is what corresponds to and facilitates the prevailing relations of production. Thus "slavery, on the basis of the capitalist mode of production, is unjust; so is cheating on the quality of commodities." Note carefully: Marx does not say slavery is unjust absolutely; he says it is unjust on the basis of the capitalist mode of production — because it contradicts the specifically capitalist form of exploitation (free wage-labour). The aside serves the chapter's argument by dismissing moralistic objections to interest (Gilbart's "natural justice" defending it, Proudhon's "natural justice" condemning it) as irrelevant to the scientific question of what interest is.

The Sine Qua Non of the Capital Function: A Must Will It, and A Originally Spends It

Marx now returns to the conditions under which money can function as capital. The £100 produces £20 by functioning as capital, whether industrial or commercial. But the sine qua non of this function is that the money is "actually spent as capital" — laid out on the purchase of means of production (industrial) or commodities (commercial). And if it is to be spent, it must first be available. Here is the crucial dialectical point: if A, the proprietor of the £100, either spent it on private consumption or hoarded it, then B, the functioning capitalist, could not spend it as capital. B does not spend his own capital, but A's; yet he cannot spend A's capital unless A wills it. Therefore:

"In point of fact, therefore, it is A who originally spends the £100 as capital, even though his function as a capitalist is entirely restricted to this act of expenditure. As far as the £100 is concerned, B functions as a capitalist only because A turns the £100 over to him and hence spends it as capital."

This is a subtle and important claim about the ownership dimension of capital. The mere act of lending — of transferring the money to B — is itself the act by which A "spends" the money as capital (i.e., commits it to the capital circuit). A's "function" is reduced to this single act, but it is a decisive one. This prepares the later distinction between ownership and function and anticipates the analysis of the "rentier" as a pure owner who performs no productive or commercial function whatsoever.

The Characteristic Circulation of Interest-Bearing Capital

Marx announces two tasks: to examine the characteristic circulation of interest-bearing capital, and then "the specific way it is sold as a commodity, i.e. lent instead of being relinquished once and for all." He begins with the circuit.

The starting-point is money that A advances to B. (This can occur with or without security; the secured form is historically older, except for advances on commodities or papers such as bills and stocks — but these particular forms "do not concern us here." Marx is deliberately isolating "interest-bearing capital in its ordinary form.") In B's hands, the money really is transformed into capital, going through the circuit M—C—M' and then returning to A as M', as M + ΔM, where ΔM represents the interest. To simplify, Marx sets aside the case where the capital remains in B's hands for a protracted period and interest is paid at regular intervals.

The total movement is thus:

M — M — C — M' — M'

Two things "appear in duplicate": (1) the expenditure of the money as capital, and (2) its reflux as realized capital, as M' or M + ΔM.

This doubled structure is the formal signature of interest-bearing capital, and Marx contrasts it carefully with the other circuits. In the movement of commercial capital, M—C—M', the same commodity changes hands twice (or more); each change of place displays a metamorphosis, a purchase or sale. In simple commodity circulation, C—M—C, we have a double change of place by the same money, but it displays the complete metamorphosis of the commodity — first transformed into money, then out of money into another commodity. With interest-bearing capital, by contrast:

"M's first change of place is neither a moment of commodity metamorphosis nor of the reproduction of capital. This begins only the second time it is spent, in the hands of the functioning capitalist... M's first change of place here expresses nothing more than its transfer or making over from A to B; a transfer which customarily takes place under certain legal forms and provisions."

So the first M—M is not an economic act of exchange at all; it is a legal transfer. There is no purchase, no sale, no metamorphosis of value. Only the second expenditure (B's) is a genuine capital-advance. The doubled expenditure (A's transfer, then B's investment) is matched by a doubled reflux: the capital returns first to B as realized capital (M + ΔM), then B transfers it back to A, with a part of the profit as interest. The reflux to A is "not the whole profit, but simply the part of the profit that is interest."

Crucially, Marx emphasizes why the reflux must be total: "For its reflux movement to be complete, B has to transfer it again to A." A has not ceased to be the legal owner. And B must surrender not only the capital sum but also a part of the profit, "since A has given the money to him only as capital, i.e. as value that is not just maintained in the course of its movement, but creates a surplus-value for its owner." It remains in B's hands only as long as it functions as capital; when its function ceases (upon reflux), "it must be transferred back again to A, who has not ceased to be its legal owner."

Note the careful language: the capital remains A's property throughout, while B has only possession and use for a definite interval. This separation of ownership from function is the economic content of the legal form of the loan. The "form of lending" — as distinct from the form of sale — is thus not an arbitrary legal convention; it "results from capital's characteristic here of emerging as a commodity, or, in other words... from the fact that money as capital becomes a commodity."

Why Commodity Capital and Money Capital Are Not Yet Capital-as-Commodity

Marx now undertakes an extended clarification, recalling Volume 2, Chapter 1, where he had shown that capital functions in the circulation process as commodity capital and money capital. In neither of these forms does capital as capital become a commodity. This is a vital distinction, and the chapter's argument depends on it.

When productive capital has been transformed into commodity capital, it must be put on the market and sold as a commodity. In the act of sale, it functions simply as a commodity. The capitalist appears simply as seller, the buyer simply as buyer. The product must realize its value in circulation and assume its money form. It is "quite immaterial" whether the buyer is a consumer (buying means of subsistence) or a capitalist (buying means of production, i.e., a component of capital). In circulation, commodity capital "functions simply as a commodity, not as capital."

Marx then specifies two respects in which commodity capital is nevertheless distinct from a simple commodity:

  1. It is "already pregnant with surplus-value," so that the realization of its value is simultaneously the realization of surplus-value — "though this does not alter its simple existence as a commodity, as a product with a definite price."
  2. Its functioning as a commodity is a moment of its reproduction process as capital; hence its movement as a commodity (a partial movement within the total process) is also its movement as capital. But — and this is the key — "it does not become so by the mere act of selling, but only because this act is connected with the total movement of this particular sum of value as capital."

Similarly, money capital actually operates simply as money — as means of purchase for the elements of production. If this money is also money capital, a form of capital, this is not the result of the act of purchase considered in itself, but of the way that act is connected with the overall movement of capital (it introduces the capitalist production process). In no individual moment of the metamorphosis, taken by itself, does the capitalist sell the commodity to the buyer as capital, nor does the buyer alienate his money as capital. In both cases, the commodity is alienated simply as commodity, the money simply as money.

Only "in the context of the whole process, at the moment where the point of departure appears as simultaneously the point of return, in M—M' or C—C'," does capital emerge in the circulation process as capital. (And note the parenthetical: in the production process, capital emerges as capital "by the subordination of the worker to the capitalist and the production of surplus-value.") But at the moment of return, "the mediation has disappeared." What exists is M' or M + ΔM — "a sum of money equal to that originally advanced plus an excess over this, the realized surplus-value." At precisely this point of return, where capital exists as realized capital, as valorized value, taken as a "point of repose, imaginary or real," capital does not enter circulation but appears as withdrawn from circulation, as the result of the entire process. If it is spent again, it is never alienated to a third party as capital but is sold as a simple commodity or given in exchange for a commodity as simply money. It never appears in its circulation process as capital but only as commodity or money, "and here this is its only existence for others."

Marx's conclusion is emphatic: commodity and money are capital here "not because commodities are turned into money and money into commodities, not in their actual relationships to buyers or sellers, but simply in their ideal relationships, either to the capitalist himself (considered subjectively) or as moments of the reproduction process (considering it objectively)." And: "It is not in the process of circulation that capital exists as capital in its real movement but only in the process of production, the process of exploiting labour-power." This is a decisive statement of the primacy of production over circulation in Marx's system.

What Makes Interest-Bearing Capital Specific: Alienation as Capital from the Start

With the contrast established, Marx delineates the specificity of interest-bearing capital:

"The owner of money who wants to valorize this as interest-bearing capital parts with it to someone else, puts it into circulation, makes it into a commodity as capital; as capital not only for himself but also for others."

This is the crux. In the case of ordinary commodity capital, the commodity is capital only for the seller (ideally/subjectively); for the buyer it is just a commodity. In the case of interest-bearing capital, the money is made over to the other person as capital right from the start — "as value that possesses the use-value of creating surplus-value or profit; as a value that continues its movement after it has functioned and returns to the person who originally spent it, in this case the money's owner."

Consequently, the money "is removed from him only for a certain interval, only temporarily stepping from the possession of its proprietor into the possession of the functioning capitalist." It is "neither paid out nor sold, but simply lent; alienated only on condition that it is, first, returned to its starting-point after a definite period of time, and second, is returned as realized capital, so that it has realized its use-value of producing surplus-value."

This is the definitional core of the loan as a legal-economic form: temporary alienation of possession, retention of ownership, return of the value plus a share in the surplus it has produced. Every subsequent feature of interest (its apparent "lawlessness," its "irrational" price-character) flows from this peculiar structure.

Fixed Capital, Circulating Capital, and the Unity of Loan Capital as Money Capital

Marx then briefly classifies loan capital by the form in which it is repaid. A commodity lent as capital may be lent as fixed capital or as circulating capital, according to its specific properties. Money can be lent in both forms: it is lent as fixed capital if repaid as an annuity (a portion of the capital returning together with the interest). Certain commodities, by the nature of their use-value, can be lent only as fixed capital — houses, boats, machines. But — and this is the unifying claim — all loan capital, whatever its form, is always simply a special form of money capital. Why? Because "what is lent here is always a definite sum of money, and it is on this sum that the interest is reckoned." If what is lent is neither money nor circulating capital, repayment follows the pattern of fixed capital: the lender receives periodic interest plus a part of the used-up value of the fixed capital itself (an equivalent for depreciation over the period); at the end of the term, the unused portion of the fixed capital is returned in kind. If the loaned capital is circulating capital, it returns to the lender in the general mode of reflux of circulating capital.

The manner of reflux is thus determined in each case by "the actual cyclical movement of capital as it reproduces itself and its specific varieties." But for loan capital specifically, the reflux takes the form of repayment, "because the advance, the alienation of the loan capital, has the form of a loan." Marx announces that in this chapter he will deal "only with money capital proper, from which the other forms of loan capital are derived." (This is an important scoping decision: the analysis of credit instruments, bills, and joint-stock capital will follow in later chapters.)

The Double Reflux, Explicitly Named

Marx then states the double reflux with full clarity: "The capital lent out flows back in a double sense. In the reproduction process it returns to the functioning capitalist, and then its return is repeated once again as a transfer to the lender, the money capitalist, as a repayment to its real proprietor, a return to its legal starting-point."

The Reproduction Process as a Whole and the "Reflexive Relationship" of Capital

Marx next contrasts the isolated acts of circulation with the reproduction process viewed as a whole. In the actual circulation process, capital always appears as commodity or money; its movement reduces to a series of purchases and sales — in short, to "the metamorphosis of commodities." But when we consider the reproduction process as a whole, "if we proceed from money (and it is the same thing if we proceed from the commodity, for we are then proceeding from its value, and thus viewing it too in the guise of money), a certain sum of money is given out and it returns after a given period with an increment." What returns is the replacement for the sum advanced, plus a surplus-value. The money "has been maintained and increased in the course of a certain cyclical movement."

Money lent as capital is "hired out precisely as a sum of money that is maintained and increased, a sum which returns with an addition after a certain period and can go through the same process once again." It is not given out as money or as commodity (it is neither exchanged for a commodity when advanced as money, nor sold for money when advanced as a commodity); it is rather given out as capital. The "reflexive relationship in which capital presents itself when we view the capitalist production process as a whole and a unity, and in which capital appears as money breeding money, is here simply embodied in it as its character, its capacity, without the intervening mediating movement." And it is in this capacity that it is alienated when lent as money capital.

This passage contains one of Marx's most compressed formulations of the fetish character of interest-bearing capital. The "reflexive relationship" — the appearance of capital as self-valorizing, as money-breeding-money — is a result of the total process, but when the money is lent it is transferred as though this capacity were an intrinsic property of the thing itself, "without the intervening mediating movement." The mediation (production, exploitation, sale) is presupposed but invisible in the form of the loan. This is the formal root of the "irrationality" Marx keeps emphasizing.

Proudhon's Confusion: Lending as an "Evil" Because It Is Not Selling

Marx now launches into an extended critique of Proudhon, based on Gratuité du crédit. Discussion entre M. F. Bastiat et M. Proudhon (Paris, 1850). Proudhon's conception: lending appears to him "as an evil because it is not selling." A loan made on interest is "the ability to sell the same object over and over again, always receiving the price afresh without ever abandoning ownership over the thing sold" (p. 9 — Marx notes in a footnote that these are not Proudhon's own words but those of Charles-François Chevé, editor of La Voix du Peuple, paraphrasing Proudhon's idea).

Marx concedes a factual premise: the object — money, house, etc. — does not change its owner, as it does in buying and selling. But "Proudhon does not see that when money is given out in the form of interest-bearing capital, no equivalent for it is received in return." Marx then explains the general nature of exchange: in any act of buying and selling, "the object is given away. But the value is never given away." On sale, what is given away is the commodity, not its value — the value returns in the form of money (or a bill/entitlement to payment, "just another form of this"). On purchase, money is given away, but not its value, which is replaced in the form of commodities. The industrial capitalist "keeps the same value in his hands throughout the reproduction process (leaving aside the surplus-value), simply in different forms."

In so far as exchange takes place (exchange of objects), there is no change in value. While the capitalist is producing surplus-value, there is no exchange; by the time exchange takes place, the surplus-value is already contained in the commodities. Considering the overall circuit M—C—M', "a definite sum of value is constantly advanced, and this sum of value plus the surplus-value or profit is withdrawn from the circulation sphere." The mediation of this process "is not to be seen in the simple acts of exchange alone." And it is precisely this process of M as capital "which the interest of the lending money-capitalist is based on and from which it derives."

The Hat-Maker Example: Value, Price of Production, and the Distribution of Surplus-Value

Marx then deploys a numerical counterexample drawn from Proudhon's own text. Proudhon had written (p. 69): "the hat-maker who sells hats... retains their value, neither more nor less. But the lending capitalist... not only receives his capital back without deduction; he receives more than this capital, more than he puts into the exchange. On top of the capital, he receives an interest." Marx reads the hat-maker as standing for the productive capitalist in contrast to the lending capitalist, and observes that Proudhon "has evidently not managed to penetrate the secret of how the productive capitalist can sell commodities at their values... and by that very act receive a profit over and above the capital he puts into the exchange."

Marx's illustration: assume the price of production of 100 hats is £115, and that this price of production happens to be equal to the value of the hats (i.e., the capital producing them is of average social composition). If the profit is 15 per cent, the hat-maker realizes a profit of £15 by selling the commodities at their value of £115. To him, they cost only £100. If he has produced them with his own capital, he pockets the entire excess of £15; if with borrowed capital, he "has possibly to give up £5 of this as interest." Marx's conclusion: "This in no way affects the value of the hats, but simply the distribution of the surplus-value already contained in the hats among different people."

This is the analytical demolition of Proudhon's claim that interest enters into the price of the commodity and thereby makes it impossible for the worker to buy back the product of his labour. Proudhon had written (p. 105): "Since in trade, the interest on capital is added to the worker's wages to make up the price of the commodity, it is impossible for the worker to buy back the product of his own labour. Vivre en travaillant is a principle that involves a contradiction, under the rule of interest." Marx's response is that interest does not enter into the value of the commodity; it is a deduction from the surplus-value already contained in it. The worker's inability to buy back the product has nothing to do with interest as such; it is a consequence of the wage relation and the extraction of surplus-value. Proudhon has mistaken a distributional phenomenon for a price-determining one. (In a footnote, Marx glosses the slogan: "Vivre en travaillant, mourir en combattant (Live working, die fighting) was a traditional slogan of the French working class, particularly associated with the Lyons insurrection of 1834." The footnote also records Luther's superior insight: "They turn buying also into usury. But this is too much to tackle at once. We must confine ourselves for the time being to dealing with usury in lending, and after setting this right (after the day of judgement), we shall go on to give usury in buying its lesson too." Marx's point: Luther already knew that profit-making is independent of the form of lending or buying — a level of clarity above Proudhon's.)

Proudhon's Fundamental Error: Reading All Capital Through the Lens of Interest-Bearing Capital

Marx then identifies Proudhon's deepest confusion with a quotation: "Since, through exchange, money capital always returns to its source with an accumulation of interest, reinvestment enables the same individual to draw a continual profit" (p. 154). Proudhon here describes the movement of capital in general in terms of the characteristic movement of interest-bearing capital. What remains a puzzle to him, Marx says, are "the categories: buying, price, alienation of goods, and the immediate form in which surplus-value appears here; in brief the phenomenon that here capital has become a commodity as capital, that buying has therefore been transformed into lending, and price into a share in the profit."

This is a crucial theoretical point: Proudhon's error is not merely a factual mistake; it is a category mistake. He generalizes the superficial form of interest-bearing capital (M—M') to all capital, thereby losing the distinction between production and circulation, between value-creation and value-distribution, and between the ownership and the functioning of capital.

The Return of Capital as the General Form — and Its Superficial Form in Interest-Bearing Capital

Marx then clarifies that "the return of capital to its point of departure is always the characteristic movement of capital in its overall circuit. This is in no way something exclusively distinctive of interest-bearing capital." What distinguishes interest-bearing capital is "the superficial form of the return, separated off from the mediating circuit."

The lending capitalist parts with his capital, transfers it to the industrial capitalist, without receiving an equivalent. But this is "in no way an act of the actual cyclical process of capital; it simply introduces this circuit." This first change of place "does not express any act of metamorphosis, neither purchase nor sale." Ownership is not surrendered, because no exchange takes place and no equivalent is received. The return of the money from the industrial capitalist to the lending capitalist simply supplements the first act. Advanced in money form, the capital returns to the industrial capitalist in money form via the cyclical process. But since the capital did not belong to him when he gave it out, it cannot belong to him on its return. Its progress through the reproduction process "cannot possibly transform the capital into his property." Hence he must give it back.

The initial act (lender → borrower) is a legal transaction that "has nothing to do with the actual reproduction process of capital, but simply introduces it." The repayment (borrower → lender) is "a second legal transaction, the complement of the first; the one introduces the real process, the other is a subsequent act after that is completed." The point of departure and the point of return — lending-out and recovery — thus appear as "arbitrary movements mediated by legal transactions, which take place before and after the real movement of capital and have nothing to do with it as such." And Marx adds the counterfactual: "It would make no difference to this real movement if the capital belonged to the industrial capitalist from the start and returned to him alone therefore as his own property."

This passage establishes the formal separation of the legal from the economic. The real capital circuit (production and circulation) is one thing; the legal envelopment of lending and repayment is another. The form of interest-bearing capital obscures the economic process by foregrounding the legal one.

The General Movement of Lending and Borrowing

Marx then generalizes: in so far as the transaction between lender and borrower is concerned (setting aside interest), the two legal acts (separated by a longer or shorter interval, during which the real reproduction movement takes place) "encompass the whole of this movement." This movement — "the act of giving out money on condition of repayment" — is "the general movement of lending and borrowing, this specific form of a merely conditional alienation of money or commodities."

The characteristic movement of capital in general (the return of money to the capitalist, the return of capital to its point of departure) receives, in the case of interest-bearing capital, "a completely superficial form, separated from the real movement whose form it is." A hands over his money "not as money but rather as capital."

No Change in the Capital Itself: The Obliteration of Mediation

Marx emphasizes: "There is no change here in the capital itself. It simply changes hands." Its actual transformation into capital is accomplished only in B's hands. But for A, it "has become capital simply by having been given to B." The actual reflux of capital from production and circulation only takes place for B; for A, the reflux takes place "in the same form as the alienation."

"The giving-out or lending of money for a certain time, and the repayment of this with interest (surplus-value), is the entire form of the movement attributable to interest-bearing capital as such."

The real movement of the money lent as capital "is an operation lying beyond the transactions between lenders and borrowers." In those transactions, taken by themselves, "this mediation is obliterated, invisible and not directly involved." Capital as a special kind of commodity "also has a kind of alienation peculiar to it."

Here the return does not appear as a consequence and result of a definite series of economic processes, but rather "as a consequence of a special legal contract between buyer and seller." The period of reflux depends on the course of the reproduction process, but for interest-bearing capital "its return as capital seems to depend simply on the contract between lender and borrower." And so the reflux of capital "no longer appears as a result determined by the production process, but rather as if the capital lent out had never lost the form of money."

Marx then adds a crucial empirical caveat: "Of course, these transactions are actually determined by the real refluxes. But this is not apparent in the transaction itself. It is also in no way always the case in practice. If the real reflux does not take place at the right time, the borrower must look to see what other sources of help he can draw on to fulfil his obligations to the lender." This is one of the rare moments where Marx gestures toward the credit crisis problematic: the legal obligation to repay is not automatically synchronized with the economic capacity to repay. The "mere form of capital" — money given out as A and returning as A + ΔA after a certain period, "without any other mediation besides this temporal interval" — is "simply the irrational form of the real capital movement."

The Real Movement of Capital and the Presupposition of the Loan

Marx then restates the real movement: money is first transformed into means of production; the production process transforms it into a commodity; by sale of the commodity it is transformed back into money, and in this form it returns to the hands of the capitalist who first advanced it. But in the case of interest-bearing capital "the return, like the giving out, is simply the result of a legal transaction between the owner of the capital and a second person. All that we see is the giving-out and the repayment. Everything that happens in between is obliterated."

Yet — and here Marx provides the real foundation of the appearance — "because money advanced as capital has the property of returning to the person advancing it, to whoever spends it as capital, because M—C—M' is the immanent form of the capital movement, for this very reason the owner of money can lend it as capital, as something which possesses the property of returning to its point of departure and of maintaining and increasing itself in the movement it undergoes." He gives it out as capital "because, after being applied as capital, it flows back to its starting-point; the borrower can repay it after a given period of time precisely because it flows back to himself."

Thus: "The real cyclical movement of money as capital is the assumption behind the legal transaction by which the borrower of the money has to return it to the lender." If the borrower does not apply it as capital, "that is his affair. The lender lends it as capital, and as capital it has to pass through the functions of capital, which include the circuit of money capital right through to its return to its starting-point in the money form."

Lending as the Appropriate Form of Alienation for Capital

Marx concludes this section: the acts of circulation M—C and C—M', in which the sum of value functions as money or commodity, are "simply intermediary processes, particular moments of its total movement." As capital, it undergoes the total movement M—M'. The lender "does not spend this on purchasing a commodity, or if the sum of value exists in commodities he does not sell these in exchange for money; he rather advances it as capital, as M—M', as value which returns again to its point of departure at a definite date." Instead of buying or selling, he lends. "This lending is thus the appropriate form for its alienation as capital, instead of as money or commodity." And Marx adds a characteristic caveat: "This in no way implies that lending cannot also be a form for transactions that have nothing to do with the capitalist reproduction process at all" — a nod to consumption credit, usury, etc., which lie outside the present analysis.

The Investigation of Interest Proper: Valorization, Not Mere Return

Marx now turns explicitly to interest: "Up to now we have only considered the movement of the capital lent between its owner and the industrial capitalist. We must now turn to investigate interest."

The lender puts his money out as capital; the value sum he alienates is capital, "and this is why it flows back to him." But this return alone would not be "the reflux of a value sum lent as capital, as opposed to the simple repayment of a sum of value previously loaned." For the sum to flow back as capital, it must not only have maintained itself but valorized itself — increased its value — so as to return with a surplus-value as M + ΔM, where ΔM is interest, "that part of the average profit which does not remain in the hands of the functioning capitalist, but falls rather to the money capitalist."

Marx notes a complication: interest typically flows back at regular intervals in the meantime, before the capital sum is repaid at the end of an extended period. He will address this temporal structure later; here the essential point is that interest is a share of profit, not a separate creation.

What the Money Capitalist Alienates: The Use-Value of Capital

The pivotal question: "What does the money capitalist give the borrower, the industrial capitalist? What actually does he alienate to him?" For "it is only the act of alienation that makes the lending of money into the alienation of money as capital, i.e. the alienation of capital as a commodity."

Marx compares with ordinary sale. In ordinary sale, what is alienated is not the value of the commodity (this only changes form — from commodity to money); what is really alienated and transferred to the buyer's consumption is the use-value of the commodity. So what use-value does the money capitalist alienate for the duration of the loan?

"It is the use-value that money receives through the fact that it can be transformed into capital, that it can function as capital so as to produce in its movement a definite surplus-value, the average profit (anything more or less than this quantity appears here as merely accidental), besides conserving its original value."

And here is the distinctive property: "With other commodities, the use-value is ultimately consumed, and in this way the substance of the commodity disappears, and with it its value. The commodity of capital, on the other hand, has the peculiar property that the consumption of its use-value not only maintains its value and use-value but in fact increases it."

So: "It is this use-value that money has as capital — the capacity to produce the average profit — that the money capitalist alienates to the industrial capitalist for the period during which he gives him control of the capital loaned."

The Analogy with Labour-Power — and Its Limit

Marx draws a careful analogy: "The money loaned in this way is to a certain extent analogous in this respect to labour-power, in its position vis-à-vis the industrial capitalist." The difference: "the industrial capitalist pays the value of the labour-power, whereas he simply repays the value of the loaned capital." The use-value of labour-power for the industrial capitalist is "that of producing more value (profit) in its use than it possesses and costs itself. This excess value is its use-value for the industrial capitalist. And the use-value of the loaned money capital similarly appears as a capacity to represent and increase value."

The analogy is illuminating but must be handled with care: in the case of labour-power, the worker sells it and receives its value (wages) — the excess value it produces goes to the capitalist. In the case of loaned money capital, the lender alienates a use-value (capacity to produce profit) and receives back the capital plus interest — but the ownership of the capital is retained throughout. The two cases belong to different moments: labour-power is the source of surplus-value; loaned capital is a claim on surplus-value already produced elsewhere. The analogy is a formal one (both involve alienation of a use-value that produces more value than it costs), not a substantive one.

The Completeness of the Analogy with Commodity Exchange

Marx then spells out the analogy with ordinary commodity exchange in two respects:

  1. Value is transferred from hand to hand. In simple commodity exchange, both buyer and seller retain the same value, only in different forms (commodity vs. money). "The difference in the case of the loan is that in this transaction the money capitalist is now the only one who gives out value; but he preserves this by the subsequent repayment. In the loan, only one party receives value, since only one party gives value out."
  2. One party alienates a real use-value, the other receives and uses it. But unlike an ordinary commodity, "this use-value is itself a value, i.e. the excess of the value that results from the use of the money as capital over its original magnitude. The profit is this use-value."

The Price of Capital: What the Borrower Pays

The use-value of money lent out is "its capacity to function as capital and as such to produce the average profit under average conditions." Marx cites Massie: "The equitableness of taking interest depends not upon a man's making or not making profit, but upon its [i.e., the sum borrowed's] being capable of producing profit if rightly employed" (An Essay on the Governing Causes of the Natural Rate of Interest, London, 1750, p. 49). And: "Rich people, instead of employing their money themselves... let it out to other people for them to make profit of, reserving for the owners a proportion of the profits so made" (op. cit., pp. 23–4). This is a striking citation: an eighteenth-century writer already grasps that interest is a share of profit, not a price of a thing.

What does the industrial capitalist pay — what is the "price" of the capital lent out? Drawing on Massie: "That which men pay as interest for the use of what they borrow... is a part of the profit it is capable of producing." Marx then contrasts the buyer of an ordinary commodity (who buys use-value and pays value) with the borrower of money (who buys its use-value as capital). What does the borrower pay? "Certainly not its price or value, as with other commodities." The value does not change its form between lender and borrower, as it does between buyer and seller. The sum of value is given out without an equivalent and returned after a certain period. "The lender remains the owner of this value throughout, even after it has been transferred from him to the borrower."

Note the striking reversal: "With simple commodity exchange, the money is always on the side of the buyer; but with lending, the money is on the side of the seller. It is he who gives the money away for a certain time, and it is the buyer of the capital who receives it as a commodity." (Here "seller" = the lender of capital; "buyer" = the borrower of capital. The terminology is deliberately jarring to highlight the anomaly.)

The Determination of the Interest Magnitude: A Share, Not a Whole

But this is possible only in so far as the money functions as capital and is therefore advanced. The borrower borrows the money as capital, as self-valorizing value. It does not become capital in itself (like any capital at its starting-point) until the moment of its advance; only by its use is it valorized and realized as capital. But it is as realized capital that the borrower must pay it back — as value plus surplus-value (interest). And the latter "can only be a part of the profit he has realized. Only a part, and not the whole." Why not the whole? Because the use-value for the borrower is that it produces him a profit; "otherwise the lender would not have been able to alienate the use-value." And why must some be paid? Because if the whole profit fell to the borrower, "he would pay nothing for the alienation of the use-value. He would only repay the money advanced to the lender as simple money, not as capital, as realized capital, for it is realized capital only as M + ΔM."

The upshot: "Both lender and borrower put out the same sum of money as capital. But it is only in the hands of the borrower that it functions as such. Profit is not doubled by the double existence of the same sum of money as capital for two persons. It can only function for both of them as capital by a division of the profit." The share accruing to the lender is interest; the remainder is profit of enterprise. This distinction — the economic content of the difference between ownership and function — is the central positive result of the chapter.

The Irrationality of Interest as "Price"

Marx then returns to the conceptual critique. "It must never be forgotten that capital as capital is a commodity here, and that the commodity we are dealing with is capital. All the relationships that appear here, therefore, would be irrational from the standpoint of the simple commodity, or even from the standpoint of capital in so far as it functions as commodity capital in its reproduction process." Lending and borrowing (instead of selling and buying) is "a distinction proceeding from the specific nature of the commodity of capital." Similarly, the fact that what is paid is interest instead of the price of the commodity.

"If interest is spoken of as the price of money capital, this is an irrational form of price, in complete contradiction with the concept of the price of a commodity." Marx explains: "Here, price is reduced to its purely abstract form, completely lacking in content, as simply a particular sum of money that is paid for something which somehow or other figures as a use-value; whereas in its concept, price is the value of this use-value expressed in money."

Marx elaborates the contradiction: "Interest as the price of capital is a completely irrational expression right from the start. Here a commodity has a double value, firstly a value, and then a price that is different from this value, although price is the money expression of value." Money capital "is at first nothing more than a sum of money, or the value of a certain quantity of commodities assessed as a sum of money. If a commodity is lent as capital, this is only the disguised form of a sum of money. For what is lent as capital is not a certain number of pounds of cotton, but rather a certain amount of money that exists in the form of cotton as the cotton's value." The price of capital thus relates to it "as a sum of money, even if not as currency, as Mr Torrens believes" (Marx here cites Torrens' On the Operation of the Bank Charter Act of 1844, 2nd edn, 1847, pp. 5–6: "The term 'value', when applied to currency, has three several meanings... (2) currency, actually in hand... compared with the same amount of currency to be received upon a future day. In this case the value of currency is measured by the rate of interest, and the rate of interest being determined by the ratio between the amount of liable capital and the demand for it.")

Then: "How then is a sum of value to have a price besides its own price, besides the price that is expressed in its own money form? Price, after all, is the value of the commodity as distinct from its use-value (and this is also the case with market price, whose distinction from value is not qualitative, but merely quantitative, bearing exclusively on the magnitude of value). A price that is qualitatively distinct from value is an absurd contradiction." In a footnote, Marx cites Tooke (Inquiry into the Currency Principle, p. 77): "The ambiguity of the term 'value of money' or 'of the currency', when employed indiscriminately as it is, to signify both value in exchange for commodities and value in use of capital, is a constant source of confusion." Marx comments: "The major confusion here (which in fact lies in the thing itself), i.e. that value as such (interest) comes to be the use-value of capital, is something that Tooke does not see." The footnote identifies Tooke as "the last English economist of any value" (cf. A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, pp. 185–7).

This is a crucial nuance: the confusion is not merely in the heads of the economists; it "lies in the thing itself." The form of interest-bearing capital objectively produces the appearance that capital has a price distinct from its value. Marx's conceptual critique is therefore an exposition of the necessary illusions of the capitalist mode of production, not a debunking of mere errors.

Interest as the Measure of Valorization

Marx resolves the apparent contradiction by re-interpreting interest: "Capital manifests itself as capital by its valorization; the extent of this valorization expresses the quantitative extent to which it is realized as capital. The surplus-value or profit produced by it — the rate or level of this — is measurable only in comparison with the value of the capital advanced. And so the greater or lesser valorization of interest-bearing capital is also measurable only by comparing the amount of interest, the part of the total profit falling to this capital, with the value of the capital advanced. If price thus expresses the value of a commodity, interest expresses the valorization of money capital and appears therefore as the price that the lender is paid for it."

So the "price" of capital is not a price in the strict sense, but a rate of valorization — a ratio of surplus to capital advanced. The term "price" is a façon de parler, an "irrational form" that nonetheless has a definite quantitative content (the interest rate). Marx concludes: "We see from this how completely absurd it is to try to apply directly the simple relationships of exchange mediated by money, buying and selling, to this phenomenon, as Proudhon does. The basic assumption is precisely that money functions as capital and hence can be made over to someone else as potential capital."

Capital as a Commodity: Use-Value Is Profit, and Value Is Determined by Surplus-Value Produced

Marx then summarizes the "commodity" character of capital. "Capital itself appears here as a commodity in so far as it is offered on the market and the use-value of money as capital really is alienated. Its use-value however is to produce a profit. The value of money or commodities as capital is not determined by their value as money or commodities but rather by the quantity of surplus-value that they produce for their possessor." "The product of capital is profit."

On the basis of capitalist production, "the difference between money spent as money and money advanced as capital is simply a difference of application." Money or a commodity is already potential capital in itself, "just as labour-power is potential capital." Marx gives two reasons:

  1. Money can be turned into elements of production, and is already, just as it is, "simply an abstract expression of these elements, their existence as value."
  2. The material elements of wealth "possess the property of being already potential capital, because their complementary antithesis, the thing that makes them capital — namely wage-labour — is present as soon as capitalist production is assumed."

This is a profound claim about the social determination of wealth under capitalism. The "antithetical social determination of material wealth — its antithesis to labour as wage-labour — is already expressed in capital ownership as such, quite apart from the production process." This one moment, "separated from the capitalist production process itself, whose constant result it is, and as whose constant result it is also its constant presupposition," is expressed thus: "that money, and likewise commodities, are in themselves latent, potential capital, i.e. can be sold as capital; in this form they give control of the labour of others, give a claim to the appropriation of others' labour, and are therefore self-valorizing value." And Marx draws the political conclusion: "It also emerges very clearly here how this relationship is the title to, and the means to the appropriation of, the labour of others, and not any kind of labour that the capitalist is supposed to offer as an equivalent."

This is the class-theoretic core of the chapter. Interest-bearing capital is not a technical device for allocating savings; it is, in its very form, a title to the appropriation of others' labour. The capacity to lend money at interest rests on the existence of wage-labour, and the interest received is a claim on surplus-value extracted from workers. The "commodity" character of capital is thus not a neutral market phenomenon but the expression of a specific social relation of production.

Competition and the Absence of a "Natural" Rate of Interest

Marx next examines the analogy between the determination of interest and the determination of commodity prices by supply and demand. Capital appears as a commodity "in so far as the division of profit into interest and profit proper is governed by supply and demand, i.e. by competition, just like the market prices of commodities. But here the distinction is just as striking as the analogy."

He then lays out the general theory of price determination under supply and demand. If supply and demand coincide, the market price of a commodity corresponds to its price of production — its price is then governed by the inner laws of capitalist production, independent of competition. Fluctuations in supply and demand explain nothing but divergences between market prices and prices of production — divergences which are mutually compensatory, so that over certain longer periods the average market prices equal the prices of production. "As soon as they coincide, these forces cease to have any effect, they cancel each other out, and the general law of price determination then emerges as the law of the individual case as well." Market price then corresponds to price of production "in its immediate existence and not only as an average of all price movements," and the price of production "is governed by the immanent laws of the mode of production."

Similarly with wages. If supply and demand coincide, their effect ceases, and wages are equal to the value of labour-power.

Interest on money capital is different. "Here competition does not determine divergences from the law, for there is no law of distribution other than that dictated by competition; as we shall go on to see, there is no 'natural' rate of interest. What is called the natural rate of interest simply means the rate established by free competition. There are no 'natural' limits to the interest rate. Where competition does not just determine divergences and fluctuations, so that in a situation where its reciprocally acting forces balance, all determination ceases, what is to be determined is something inherently lawless and arbitrary. More about this in the next chapter."

This is a remarkable passage. In the case of commodity prices and wages, competition is the mechanism through which an underlying law (value, price of production, value of labour-power) asserts itself. The underlying law is independent of competition. In the case of interest, there is no underlying law; competition itself is the sole determinant. The interest rate is "inherently lawless and arbitrary" — subject only to the reciprocal pressures of supply and demand, with no "natural" anchor. This is not a failure of Marx's theory but a substantive claim about the nature of the interest rate as a distributive magnitude. The division of profit into interest and profit of enterprise has no internal "law" of the same kind as the law of value; it is determined by the relative strength of lenders and borrowers in the money market. This admission will be crucial for the later analysis of the money market and the credit system, and for understanding why the interest rate is the most "fetishistic" and crisis-prone of all prices.

The Superficiality of Interest-Bearing Capital: Time and the Appearance of Self-Valorization

The chapter's final section returns to the theme of superficiality. In the case of interest-bearing capital, "everything appears in a superficial manner: the advance of capital as a mere transfer from lender to borrower; the reflux of the realized capital as a mere transfer back, a repayment with interest from the borrower to the lender." So, too, does a genuine property of the capitalist mode of production appear superficially: "the property inherent to the capitalist mode of production, that the rate of profit is determined not simply by the ratio of the profit made in one individual turnover to the capital value advanced, but also by the length of this turnover time itself, i.e. as the profit that industrial capital yields in particular periods of time." In interest-bearing capital, this too takes a superficial form: "it appears that a certain interest is paid to the lender for a certain interval of time."

This brings Marx to Adam Müller (the "romantic" of Elemente der Staatskunst, Berlin, 1809, part iii, p. 138): "In determining the price of things, time is unimportant; in determining interest, time is the principal thing involved." Marx's response: "He does not see how production time and circulation time come into play in determining the prices of commodities, and how it is precisely in this way that the rate of profit is determined for a given period of turnover of capital, while interest is determined precisely by this determination of profit for a given period. His wisdom here, as always, lies in the way that he sees clouds of dust on the surface and pretentiously proclaims this dust to be something mysterious and significant."

The chapter thus closes with a methodological warning: the appearance of interest-bearing capital — money paying for time — is superficial, but it is not arbitrary. It is grounded in the real determination of the rate of profit by turnover time. The task of theory is to trace the appearance back to its real foundations, not to celebrate or mystify the surface.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles Revealed

The chapter yields several systemic laws and principles that operate well beyond its immediate subject.

First, the unity of value theory and distribution theory. Interest is not a price of a thing but a share of surplus-value. The total profit produced by the functioning capital is divided into interest (falling to the owner) and profit of enterprise (remaining with the borrower). No new value is created by the loan. This means that the entire edifice of finance — interest, dividends, rent on loan capital — rests on surplus-value extracted in production. The chapter thus closes the circuit: value produced in production is distributed among industrial profit, commercial profit, interest, and (later) rent and profit of enterprise. The theory of distribution is not a separate branch of economics; it is an extension of the labour theory of value.

Second, the objective reality of "irrational forms." Marx insists that interest as the "price of capital" is a contradiction in terms — a price qualitatively distinct from value. Yet this irrationality is not a mere error of economists; it "lies in the thing itself." The form of interest-bearing capital objectively produces the appearance that money begets money, that capital has a price apart from its value, that ownership as such is productive. This is a general methodological principle in Capital: the surface forms of capitalist production (wages, prices, interest, profit) are not illusions to be dismissed but necessary appearances whose real foundations must be reconstructed by theory. The critique of political economy is not the dismissal of appearances but the explanation of their necessity.

Third, the separation of ownership and function. Interest-bearing capital formalizes a distinction that runs through the entire capitalist economy: the distinction between the owner of capital, who performs no productive function, and the functioning capitalist, who organizes production or circulation. Interest is the revenue of mere ownership; profit of enterprise is the revenue of functioning. This distinction, once established, prepares the analysis of the rentier, the joint-stock company, and the "abolition of the capitalist as a functionary" in the era of credit. It also grounds the later analysis of "fictitious capital."

Fourth, the specific status of the interest rate. The interest rate is unique among economic magnitudes in that it has no underlying "natural" law. Competition, which elsewhere merely enforces a law determined by the mode of production, here is the law. The rate of interest is "inherently lawless and arbitrary." This is a striking admission of the limits of law-governed determination within capitalism, and it identifies the interest rate as a privileged site for the analysis of contingency, power, and crisis in the financial system.

The Practical and Theoretical Implications

For the theory of credit and crisis. The chapter's analysis of the "obliterated mediation" — the fact that the legal form of the loan hides the economic process on which it depends — directly anticipates the theory of credit crises. If the legal obligation to repay is detached from the economic capacity to repay, then a disruption in the real circuit (a failure to sell, a decline in profit) becomes a credit crisis. The chapter's remark that "if the real reflux does not take place at the right time, the borrower must look to see what other sources of help he can draw on" anticipates the analysis of the money market, the discount rate, and the lender-of-last-resort function.

For the critique of Proudhon and "credit socialism." The chapter is a systematic demolition of the idea that the ills of capitalism can be cured by reforming credit. Proudhon's proposal — that capital should be lent at cost, that interest is an unjust "surplus" — is shown to rest on a category mistake. Interest is not a price added to the commodity; it is a deduction from surplus-value already produced. Abolishing interest without abolishing wage-labour and the production of surplus-value would merely redistribute surplus-value among capitalists; it would not liberate the worker. The critique extends to all "credit reforms" that treat finance as the root of exploitation rather than its branch.

For the philosophical anthropology of capital. The chapter culminates in the claim that capital, as loanable money, is a "title to, and the means to the appropriation of, the labour of others, and not any kind of labour that the capitalist is supposed to offer as an equivalent." This is the clearest expression in Volume 3 of the social relation embodied in money capital. The lender's claim to interest is grounded not in any contribution to production but in the institution of private property in the means of production — an institution that presupposes the existence of wage-labour. The "productivity of capital" is thus revealed as the productivity of a social relation, not of a thing.

For the theory of fetishism. The chapter's analysis of interest-bearing capital as the form in which capital appears as "money breeding money," "without the intervening mediating movement," is a direct extension of the fetishism analysis of Volume 1. In the commodity, the social relation between producers appears as a relation between things. In interest-bearing capital, the social relation between classes appears as a natural property of money — its capacity to "breed." This is the highest form of fetishism, because it inverts the true order: it makes the result of the total process (M') appear as the immediate property of M. The chapter thus provides the theoretical foundation for the critique of finance capital and of the "rentier" as the purest embodiment of the fetishistic character of capital.

The Chapter's Place in the Broader Framework

Chapter 21 is the gateway to the most abstract and most politically explosive parts of Volume 3. It establishes the conceptual machinery — the distinction between interest and profit of enterprise, the analysis of the loan as a legal form, the irrational character of the "price of capital," the absence of a natural rate of interest — that will be deployed in the subsequent chapters on the money market, the credit system, the joint-stock company, and the "abolition of the capitalist mode of production within the capitalist mode of production" itself. Its compact, rigorous, and at times polemical argumentation makes it one of the most important short chapters in the entire work: it is where Marx shows that the most "mysterious" and most "fetishistic" revenues of capitalism — interest and the profits of financial ownership — are, at bottom, nothing but shares in the surplus-value produced by wage-labour, and that the entire apparent autonomy of the financial sphere rests on the obscured but ineliminable foundation of exploitation in production. The "irrational form" is real; but it is a form, and the task of critique is to reveal the real movement that this form conceals.
