Volume 3 • Chapter 4: The Effect of the Turnover on the Rate of Profit

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 4, “The Effect of the Turnover on the Rate of Profit,” is the point in Marx’s Capital, Volume 3, where the turnover analysis developed in Volume 2 is formally integrated into the theory of the profit rate. The chapter was written by Engels, as the footnote and Preface indicate, and it carries Engels’s characteristic combination of theoretical recapitulation, industrial-historical illustration, and numerical demonstration. Its central problem is precise: Volume 2 had already shown that the turnover time of capital affects the mass of surplus-value because capital cannot be simultaneously active in production in its entirety. A portion of capital always lies fallow—money capital, raw-material stocks, finished but unsold commodities, or outstanding debts not yet due—and this idle portion produces no surplus-value. The shorter the turnover time, the smaller the idle portion and the greater the mass of surplus-value appropriated, other conditions being equal. But the rate of profit is not the mass of surplus-value as such; it is the ratio of the mass of surplus-value produced to the total capital engaged in producing it. Therefore, any reduction in turnover time that raises the mass of surplus-value relative to the same total capital must also raise the rate of profit. This is the chapter’s core thesis.

The intellectual objective is to show that turnover is not a secondary or merely technical matter. It is a distinct determinant of the profit rate, alongside the organic composition of capital, the rate of surplus-value, the length of the working day, and the level of wages. Engels seeks to present this effect “in its pure form” by holding all other circumstances equal, then to demonstrate the mechanism numerically, and finally to test it against a real industrial example. The chapter thus serves as a bridge between Volume 2’s theory of turnover and Volume 3’s theory of profit. It explains why the same rate of surplus-value and the same capital composition can yield different profit rates if capitals turn over at different speeds. It also explains why the formula for the profit rate must be corrected: the simple expression (p' = s' v/C) is valid only when the variable capital in the numerator is the same as the variable capital in the denominator. When variable capital turns over several times a year, the annual surplus-value is greater than the surplus-value of a single turnover, and the formula must become (p' = s'n v/C), where (n) is the number of turnovers of variable capital in a year and (s'n) is the annual rate of surplus-value.

The chapter also has a broader critical and empirical dimension. Engels illustrates the reduction of production time through advances in metallurgy and chemical industry—Bessemer, Siemens, Gilchrist-Thomas, alizarin from coal-tar—and the reduction of circulation time through railways, steamships, telegraph cables, and the Suez Canal. He notes that these changes can raise the profit rate if they do not require a major increase in total capital investment, but that expensive new machinery can raise the composition of capital and counteract the effect. He then shows, through the example of a Manchester spinning mill, how a high annual rate of surplus-value can coexist with a very small proportion of variable capital in the total capital. Finally, he observes that statistical material on the social ratio of constant to variable capital is almost absent, with only the U.S. Census offering any data, and even that data is dubious because it relies on unchecked information from industrialists. In this way, the chapter links the abstract theory of turnover to the concrete history of capitalism, the accounting categories of the capitalist, and the ideological limits of official statistics.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Summary: Idle Capital, Active Capital, and the Turnover Constraint

Engels begins by recalling the central result of Volume 2: the effect of turnover on the production of surplus-value, and consequently on profit, has already been discussed. The summary is compact but theoretically dense. The time required for turnover has the effect that the whole capital cannot be simultaneously employed in production. One part of this capital therefore always lies fallow, whether in the form of money capital, stocks of raw materials, finished but still unsold commodity capital, or outstanding debts that are not yet due for payment. This is not a minor qualification. It is the material basis of the turnover effect. Capital is not a homogeneous fluid that can be fully present in the production process at every instant. It must pass through the phases of money capital, productive capital, and commodity capital. While it is in the money form, or in the form of raw materials waiting to be used, or in the form of finished goods waiting to be sold, or in the form of debts waiting to be collected, it is not actively producing surplus-value. The capital that is in active production, active in the production and appropriation of surplus-value, is always reduced by this amount. The surplus-value that is produced and appropriated is reduced in the same proportion. The shorter the turnover time, the smaller is this idle portion of capital compared with the whole; the greater therefore is the surplus-value appropriated, other conditions being equal.

This opening already introduces the crucial distinction between capital advanced and capital active. The total capital advanced is the denominator of the profit rate. But not all of it is active at any given moment. The rate of profit is therefore affected by how much of the advanced capital is actually functioning in the production and appropriation of surplus-value. A shorter turnover time does not change the total capital advanced, but it increases the proportion of that capital that is active. It is as if the same total capital were concentrated into a more effective working mass. Engels explicitly connects this to the rate of profit: since the rate of profit simply expresses the ratio of the mass of surplus-value produced to the total capital engaged in producing it, it is evident that any reduction of turnover time raises the rate of profit as well. The points made in Part Two of Volume 2 with respect to surplus-value apply equally here to profit and the rate of profit, and do not need to be repeated. There are simply a few key aspects to emphasize.

This opening also contains an important methodological claim: the analysis of surplus-value and the analysis of profit are not separate theoretical realms. The same turnover mechanism that governs the mass of surplus-value also governs the rate of profit, because the rate of profit is a transformed expression of the same underlying surplus-value. The chapter therefore does not introduce a new theory of profit; it extends the turnover theory of Volume 2 into the profit-rate framework of Volume 3. The nuance here is that the rate of profit depends on the total capital advanced, not merely on the variable capital that directly produces surplus-value. Thus, a faster turnover can raise the rate of profit only insofar as it raises the annual mass of surplus-value relative to the total capital advanced. If faster turnover requires a larger total capital, the effect may be offset or reversed. This caveat is introduced immediately after the opening summary and is developed through the examples of industrial progress.

Production Time, Industrial Progress, and the Counteracting Weight of Fixed Capital

Engels next identifies the two component sections of turnover time: production time and circulation time. The main means whereby production time is reduced is an increase in the productivity of labour, which is commonly known as industrial progress. This is a crucial link between the technical conditions of production and the rate of profit. If productivity increases, the same capital can produce a given output in less time, or a larger output in the same time. In terms of turnover, the production period is shortened, so the capital returns to its starting point more quickly, and the number of turnovers in a year increases. If this does not also involve a major increase in the total capital investment, due to the installation of expensive machinery etc., and therefore a fall in the rate of profit as reckoned on the total capital, then this profit rate must rise. This conditional formulation is essential. Engels is not claiming that industrial progress always raises the profit rate. He is claiming that the production-time-reducing effect of industrial progress, considered in isolation, raises the profit rate, but that it may be accompanied by a counteracting rise in the composition of capital.

He then gives concrete historical examples. The newly discovered methods of iron and steel preparation associated with Bessemer, Siemens, Gilchrist-Thomas and others shorten what were previously very protracted processes to a minimum. The preparation of alizarin dye from coal-tar gives the same result in a few weeks, and using apparatus that is already in use for coal-tar dyes, as previously took several years. The madder from which the dye was previously prepared needed a year to grow, and the roots were left to mature for several years after that before they were used. These examples are not mere curiosities. They show that production time is historically variable and can be drastically shortened by scientific and technical revolutions. In the case of alizarin, the new method not only shortens production time but also uses apparatus already employed in coal-tar dye production, which means that the additional capital investment may be relatively small. In such cases, the turnover effect on the profit rate is strongly positive. In the case of Bessemer and Siemens steel, the new methods may require large fixed capital investments, which could raise the organic composition of capital and lower the profit rate as reckoned on total capital. The net effect depends on the balance between the turnover acceleration and the increase in capital advanced.

This is a nuance that runs through the chapter: the turnover effect is real and analytically distinct, but it is not the only force at work. The rate of profit is a complex result of several determinants. Engels explicitly says that if the reduction of production time “does not also involve a major increase in the total capital investment,” then the profit rate must rise. The phrase “does not also” signals the counteracting tendency. Later, in the Manchester example, he will show a case where the total capital is dominated by fixed capital, and the variable capital is only a small fraction, yet the profit rate is high because the variable capital turns over many times. But the high rate is also explained by “extremely favourable temporary conditions.” So the chapter consistently avoids a one-sided technological determinism. It presents turnover acceleration as a powerful but conditional force.

Circulation Time, Communications, and the Shrinking World Market

The main means of cutting circulation time has been improved communications. Engels describes a revolution in transport and communication over the last fifty years that is comparable only with the industrial revolution of the second half of the previous century. On land the Macadamized road has been replaced by the railway, while at sea the slow and irregular sailing ship has been driven into the background by the rapid and regular steamer line; the whole earth has been girded by telegraph cables. It was the Suez Canal that really opened the Far East and Australia to the steamer. The circulation time for a shipment of goods to the Far East, which in 1847 was at least twelve months, has now been more or less reduced to as many weeks. This is a dramatic illustration of how circulation time, no less than production time, is subject to historical transformation. The turnover time of capital is not a natural constant; it is a social and technological variable.

Engels then makes a striking observation about crisis. The two major foci of crisis between 1825 and 1857, America and India, have been brought 70 to 90 per cent closer to the industrial countries of Europe by this revolution in the means of commerce, and have lost in this way a good deal of their explosive potential. The turnover time of world trade as a whole has been reduced to the same extent, and the efficacy of the capital involved in it has been increased two or three times and more. It is evident that this cannot but have had its effect on the profit rate. This passage connects the turnover analysis to the geography of capitalist crisis. Improved communications shorten circulation time, increase the efficacy of capital, and thereby tend to raise the profit rate. At the same time, they integrate distant markets more tightly into the European industrial system, reducing the time lags and information gaps that had previously made those markets explosive foci of crisis. This is a nuanced claim: the same revolution in communications that raises the profit rate also alters the spatial and temporal pattern of crisis. It does not abolish crisis; it changes its form and location. The passage also shows Engels’s historical materialism: the means of communication are not neutral infrastructure but part of the material conditions of capital circulation and profitability.

The theoretical point is that circulation time is a deduction from production time. The longer the circulation time, the longer the capital is tied up in the commodity or money form, and the smaller the proportion of total capital that is active in production. By reducing circulation time, improved communications allow the same capital to be turned over more frequently, which increases the annual mass of surplus-value relative to the capital advanced. This is why the revolution in transport and communication “cannot but have had its effect on the profit rate.” The effect may be partially offset by the large fixed capital investments required for railways, steamships, and telegraphs, but the turnover acceleration itself is a powerful force.

The Pure Case: Equal Conditions and the Inverse Relation Between Turnover and Profit Rate

Engels now moves from historical illustration to theoretical proof. In order to present the effect of the turnover of the total capital on the profit rate in its pure form, we must assume that all other circumstances are equal for the two capitals we are comparing. The percentage composition in particular must be taken as the same, as well as the rate of surplus-value and the length of the working day. This is the method of isolation: vary only the turnover time, hold everything else constant, and observe the effect on the profit rate. He then sets up a comparison. Let us take a capital A with a composition of 80c + 20v = 100C, a rate of surplus-value of 100 per cent, and a twice-yearly turnover. Its annual product is then 160c + 40v + 40s. But for the purposes of the profit rate we calculate this 40s not on the capital value of 200 turned over, but rather on the capital value of 100 that was advanced, and we thus get p' = 40 per cent.

Let us compare this with a capital B = 160c + 40v = 200C, with the same rate of surplus-value, but turning over only once in the year. Its annual product is then 160c + 40v + 40s, the same as above. This time however the 40s has to be calculated on a capital advance of 200, which results in a profit rate of 20 per cent, i.e. only half the rate for A. The result is therefore that for capitals of the same percentage composition, with the same rate of surplus-value and the same working day, the profit rates of two capitals vary inversely as their turnover times. If either the composition or the rate of surplus-value or the working day or the wage of labour is not the same in the two cases to be compared, further differences in the rate of profit are also brought about, but these are independent of the turnover, and do not concern us here; they have already been discussed in Chapter 3.

This is the analytical heart of the chapter. The comparison shows that two capitals can produce the same annual product and the same annual mass of surplus-value, yet have different profit rates if their turnover times differ. Capital A advances only 100, but turns it over twice, producing an annual surplus-value of 40 on an advance of 100, giving 40 per cent. Capital B advances 200, turns it over once, and produces the same annual surplus-value of 40 on an advance of 200, giving 20 per cent. The difference is entirely due to the denominator: the total capital advanced. The faster turnover allows A to produce the same surplus-value with half the advanced capital. Conversely, if A and B had the same advanced capital but different turnover times, A would produce twice the annual surplus-value and thus twice the profit rate. The phrase “vary inversely as their turnover times” means that if turnover time doubles, the profit rate is halved, other things equal. This is a precise, testable proposition within the abstract model.

The caveat is equally important. If composition, rate of surplus-value, working day, or wages differ, those differences also affect the profit rate, but they are independent of turnover. This prevents the turnover effect from being confused with other determinants. It also means that in empirical reality, the observed profit rate is a composite result. One cannot infer the turnover effect directly from observed profit rates unless one controls for composition, exploitation rate, working day, and wages. This methodological caution is a recurring feature of Marx’s and Engels’s procedure.

Variable Capital's Enhanced Efficacy and the Annual Rate of Surplus-Value

The direct effect of the abbreviated turnover time on the production of surplus-value, and therefore also on profit, consists in the increased effectiveness which this gives to the variable portion of capital. This is the mechanism behind the inverse relation. Engels refers back to Volume 2, Chapter 16, “The Turnover of Variable Capital.” There it was seen how a variable capital of 500 which turns over ten times in the year appropriates just as much surplus-value in this period as a variable capital of 5,000, with the same rate of surplus-value and the same wages, which turns over only once in the year. This comparison is crucial. The variable capital of 500, turning ten times, purchases labour-power ten times in the year. Each time, it produces surplus-value at the given rate. The total annual surplus-value is therefore ten times the surplus-value of one turnover. The variable capital of 5,000, turning once, purchases labour-power once in the year, and produces only the surplus-value of one turnover. The result is the same annual surplus-value, but the advanced variable capital is ten times larger in the second case. If the total capital advanced is also larger in the second case, the rate of profit will be lower. If the total capital advanced is the same, the rate of profit will be higher in the first case.

This is the key to the entire chapter: the turnover of variable capital affects the annual rate of surplus-value. The rate of surplus-value in a single turnover period is (s' = s/v), where (s) is the surplus-value produced in that period and (v) is the variable capital advanced for that period. But the annual mass of surplus-value is (S = s n = s' v n), where (n) is the number of turnovers in the year. The annual rate of surplus-value is (S' = S/v = s' n). Thus, a variable capital of 500 with (s' = 100\%) and (n = 10) produces an annual surplus-value of 5,000, and an annual rate of surplus-value of 1,000 per cent. The same variable capital of 5,000 with (n = 1) produces the same annual surplus-value of 5,000, but an annual rate of surplus-value of only 100 per cent. The difference is entirely due to turnover.

This distinction between the simple rate of surplus-value and the annual rate of surplus-value is essential for the profit rate. The profit rate is an annual magnitude. It compares the surplus-value produced in a year to the total capital advanced. Therefore, it must use the annual rate of surplus-value, not the simple rate. If one uses the simple rate, one will understate the profit rate whenever variable capital turns over more than once a year. This is why Engels insists that the formula for the annual rate of profit must be (p' = s'n v/C). The (n) converts the simple rate of surplus-value into the annual rate, or equivalently converts the advanced variable capital into the annual variable capital laid out in wages. The increased effectiveness of variable capital is not a mysterious property; it is simply the consequence of the same capital being used repeatedly to purchase labour-power within the year.

The Three Capitals and the Algebra of Annual Profit Rate

Engels now constructs three numerical examples to illustrate the relationship between turnover, annual surplus-value, and the profit rate. Let us take a capital I, consisting of 10,000 fixed capital, its annual depreciation being 10 per cent = 1,000, 500 circulating constant capital, and 500 variable capital. With a rate of surplus-value of 100 per cent, the variable capital turns over ten times in the year. For the sake of simplicity we shall assume in all the following examples that the circulating constant capital turns over in the same period as the variable, which will generally be the case in practice. The product of such a turnover period will then be: 100c (depreciation) + 500c + 500v + 500s = 1,600. And the product of the whole year, with ten turnovers: 1,000c (depreciation) + 5,000c + 5,000v + 5,000s = 16,000. C = 11,000, s = 5,000, p' = 5,000/11,000 = 45 5/11 per cent.

Capital II: fixed capital 9,000 with annual depreciation 1,000, circulating constant capital 1,000, variable capital 1,000, rate of surplus-value 100 per cent, turnovers of variable capital five per year. The product of one of these turnover periods of the variable capital will then be: 200c (depreciation) + 1,000c + 1,000v + 1,000s = 3,200. And the total annual product over five turnovers: 1,000c (depreciation) + 5,000c + 5,000v + 5,000s = 16,000. C = 11,000, s = 5,000, p' = 5,000/11,000 = 45 5/11 per cent. Capital III: no fixed capital, but simply 6,000 circulating constant capital and 5,000 variable capital. It turns over once a year, say, with a rate of surplus-value of 100 per cent. The total annual product is then: 6,000c + 5,000v + 5,000s = 16,000. C = 11,000, s = 5,000, p' = 45 5/11 per cent.

In all three cases, therefore, we have the same annual mass of surplus-value = 5,000, and since the total capital is the same in all these cases, i.e. 11,000, we have the same profit rate of 45 5/11 per cent. This is a remarkable result. Three capitals with very different compositions of fixed and circulating capital, and very different turnover rates of variable capital, can have the same profit rate. The reason is that the annual variable capital set in motion is the same in all three cases: in Capital I, 500 × 10 = 5,000; in Capital II, 1,000 × 5 = 5,000; in Capital III, 5,000 × 1 = 5,000. Since the rate of surplus-value is 100 per cent, the annual surplus-value is 5,000 in each case. And since the total capital advanced is 11,000 in each case, the profit rate is the same. This illustrates the general principle that what matters for the annual surplus-value is the product (v n), the variable capital actually laid out in wages over the year, not the variable capital advanced at any single moment.

But Engels immediately shows what happens when turnover changes while the other elements remain constant. If in the case of the above capital I there took place not ten but only five turnovers of its variable portion, the matter would be different. The product of one turnover would then be: 200c (depreciation) + 500c + 500v + 500s = 1,700; or the annual product: 1,000c (depreciation) + 2,500c + 2,500v + 2,500s = 8,500; C = 11,000, s = 2,500, p' = 2,500/11,000 = 22 8/11 per cent. The profit rate has now fallen by half, as the turnover time has doubled. This is the inverse relation in action. The total capital advanced is unchanged at 11,000. The composition of capital is unchanged. The rate of surplus-value is unchanged. Only the number of turnovers has fallen from ten to five. As a result, the annual surplus-value has fallen from 5,000 to 2,500, and the profit rate has fallen from 45 5/11 per cent to 22 8/11 per cent. This is a clean demonstration of the turnover effect.

The general formula that emerges is (S = sn) and (S' = s'n). If we call the surplus-value or profit annually appropriated (S), the surplus-value appropriated in one turnover period (s), and the number of turnovers made by the variable capital in a year (n), then (S = sn). The annual rate of surplus-value is (S' = s'n). This formula is already set out in Volume 2, Chapter 16, I. It is the bridge between the turnover of variable capital and the annual profit rate. The profit rate then becomes (p' = S/C = s'n v/C), where (C = c + v) is the total capital advanced. This formula is the corrected form of the profit-rate equation. It shows that the profit rate depends on the rate of surplus-value, the number of turnovers, the proportion of variable capital in the total capital, and the total capital itself. The turnover effect is captured by (n).

The Corrected Formula: Why the v in the Numerator Must Equal the v in the Denominator

Engels now turns to a subtle algebraic point that is essential for avoiding error. The formula for the profit rate (p' = s' v/C = s' v/(c+v)) is correct only if the (v) in the numerator is the same as that in the denominator. The (v) in the denominator is the entire part of the total capital that is spent on average as variable capital, on wages. The (v) in the numerator is initially determined simply by the fact that a certain quantity of surplus-value = (s) has been produced and appropriated by it, related to it by the rate of surplus-value (s'), which equals (s/v). It is only in this way that the equation (p' = s/(c+v)) was transformed into the equation (p' = s' v/(c+v)). The (v) in the numerator can now be more accurately defined by the condition that it must be equal to the (v) in the denominator, i.e. to the entire variable part of the capital (C). In other words, the equation (p' = s/C) can be transformed into (p' = s' v/C) without risk of error only if (s) stands for the surplus-value produced in a single turnover period of the variable capital. If (s) comprises only a part of this surplus-value, (s = s'v) is still correct, but this (v) is now smaller than the (v) in (C = c + v), as it is smaller than the whole of the variable capital that is laid out on wages. But if (s) comprises more than the surplus-value of one turnover of (v), a part of this (v) or even the whole of it functions twice, firstly in the first turnover, then in the second or further turnovers; the (v) that produces surplus-value and is the sum of all wages paid is thus greater than the (v) in (c + v), and the calculation is false.

This is a dense but crucial passage. Let us unpack it. The profit rate is defined as (p' = S/C), where (S) is the annual surplus-value and (C) is the total capital advanced. The rate of surplus-value is (s' = S/v), where (v) is the variable capital. If we write (S = s' v), then (p' = s' v/C). This is the familiar formula. But here is the catch: which (v)? The (v) in the denominator (C = c + v) is the variable capital advanced, the amount of money the capitalist must have on hand to pay wages. The (v) in the numerator, if (S = s' v), must be the variable capital that actually produced the annual surplus-value. If variable capital turns over more than once a year, the same advanced (v) is used repeatedly to pay wages. The total wages paid over the year are (v n), not (v). The annual surplus-value is (S = s' v n). Therefore, if we use the advanced (v) in the numerator, we understate the annual surplus-value. To correct the formula, we must either replace (s') with the annual rate of surplus-value (S' = s' n), or replace the advanced (v) with the annual variable capital (v n). Thus, the correct formula for the annual rate of profit is (p' = s' n v/C = S' v/C).

Engels states this explicitly: “In order that the formula for the annual rate of profit may be completely correct, we must replace the simple rate of surplus-value with the annual rate, (S') or (s'n) in place of (s'). In other words, we must multiply (s'), the rate of surplus-value—or else multiply the (v), the variable capital (v) contained in (C)—by (n), the number of turnovers that this variable capital makes in a year, and we then obtain (p' = s'n v/C), the formula for calculating the annual rate of profit.” This is the algebraic correction that makes the turnover effect explicit. Without the (n), the formula would be valid only for a single turnover, or for the special case where (n = 1). With the (n), it becomes a general formula for the annual rate of profit. This is why the chapter is necessary: it shows that the simple profit-rate formula must be revised when turnover is taken into account.

The passage also has an epistemological dimension. The capitalist himself does not know in most cases how much variable capital he employs in his business. We have already seen in Chapter 8 of Volume 2, and we shall now see further, that the only distinction within his capital that impresses itself on the capitalist as fundamental is the distinction between fixed and circulating capital. From the same till that contains the part of his circulating capital that exists in his hands in the money form, in so far as this is not placed in the bank, he fetches both money for wages and money for raw and ancillary materials, and enters both of these in the same cash account. Even if he were to keep a separate record for wages paid, this would simply indicate the total sum paid at the end of the year, i.e. (v n), and not the variable capital (v) itself. In order to arrive at this sum he would have to make a special calculation. This is a profound point about capitalist accounting. The capitalist’s practical categories obscure the distinction between constant and variable capital. He sees fixed and circulating capital, not constant and variable capital. He sees total wages paid, not the advanced variable capital that produces surplus-value. Therefore, he cannot easily calculate the annual rate of surplus-value or the true profit rate in Marx’s sense. The turnover effect is hidden from him by the very structure of his bookkeeping.

The Manchester Spinning Mill: A Concrete Calculation

Engels now provides a concrete example to test the formula. Let us take the spinning mill described in Volume 1 [Chapter 9, I, pp. 327–8], with its 10,000 spindles, and assume that the data given for one week in April 1871 are the same for the whole year. The fixed capital in the form of machinery was £10,000. The circulating capital was not given; we shall take it to be £2,500, a fairly high figure, but one that is justified by the assumption we must constantly make at this stage, that there are no credit operations, i.e. no permanent or temporary use of other people’s capital. The week’s product was composed, as far as its value was concerned, of £20 for depreciation of machinery, £358 advance of circulating constant capital (rent £6, cotton £342, coal, gas and oil £10), £52 laid out as variable capital on wages, and £80 surplus-value, i.e. 20c (depreciation) + 358c + 52v + 80s = 510. The weekly advance of circulating capital was therefore 358c + 52v = 410, and its percentage composition 87.3c + 12.7v. Calculated on the whole circulating capital of £2,500, this gives a constant capital of £2,182 and a variable capital of £318. Since the total outlay on wages for the whole year comes to 52 times £52, i.e. £2,704, the upshot is that the variable capital of £318 has turned over almost exactly 8½ times in the course of the year. The rate of surplus-value is 80/52 = 153 11/13 per cent. From these elements we can calculate the rate of profit by using the formula (p' = s'n v/C), with (s' = 153 11/13), (n = 8½), (v = 318), (C = 12,500). The result is that (p' = 153 11/13 × 8½ × 318/12,500 = 33.27) per cent.

We can test this by using the simple formula (p' = S/C). The total surplus-value or profit over the whole year amounts to £80 × 52 = £4,160, and this divided by the total capital of £12,500 gives 33.28 per cent, pretty well the same figure as above. This is an abnormally high rate of profit, which can only be explained by extremely favourable temporary conditions (very cheap cotton prices combined with very high prices for yarn) and would certainly not have prevailed for a whole year in actual fact.

This example is rich in theoretical implications. First, it shows the formula in action. The weekly rate of surplus-value is 153 11/13 per cent. The variable capital of £318 turns over 8½ times a year. The annual rate of surplus-value is therefore (153 11/13 × 8½ = 1,307 9/13) per cent. This is an enormous figure. It means that the same £318 of variable capital is used to purchase labour-power more than eight times in the year, and each time it produces surplus-value at a high rate. The annual surplus-value of £4,160 is the result. When divided by the total capital of £12,500, it yields a profit rate of about 33 per cent. The simple formula (p' = S/C) gives the same result, which confirms the corrected formula. The example also shows the difference between the annual rate of surplus-value and the simple rate. The simple rate is 153 11/13 per cent; the annual rate is over 1,300 per cent. This is why Engels says that if a “worthy” was shocked by the enormous annual rate of surplus-value of 1,000 per cent given in one of the examples in Volume 2, he can console himself with the actual fact of an annual rate of surplus-value of more than 1,300 per cent taken from a practical example in Manchester. In periods of greatest prosperity, such a rate is by no means rare.

Second, the example reveals the actual composition of capital in modern large-scale industry. The total capital is divided into £12,182 constant and £318 variable, making £12,500 altogether. In percentages, 97½c + 2½v = 100C. Only a fortieth part of the total is needed for the payment of wages, though this serves more than eight times in the course of a year. This is a striking illustration of the small proportion of variable capital in advanced capitalist industry. The source of surplus-value is a tiny fraction of the total capital, yet it is the only fraction that produces surplus-value. The large constant capital—machinery, raw materials, etc.—merely transfers its value to the product. The variable capital, though small, is the source of profit. The high turnover of variable capital is what makes it possible for this small fraction to produce a substantial annual surplus-value. This is a powerful demonstration of the theoretical point that the rate of profit depends on the annual rate of surplus-value, not merely on the simple rate.

Third, the example is qualified by its historical context. The 33.27 per cent profit rate is abnormally high. It can only be explained by extremely favourable temporary conditions: very cheap cotton prices combined with very high prices for yarn. Engels explicitly says that it would certainly not have prevailed for a whole year in actual fact. This caveat prevents the example from being taken as a universal law. It is an illustration of a possibility, not a typical case. The high annual rate of surplus-value is possible in periods of greatest prosperity, but such periods are exceptional. This historical specificity is part of Engels’s method: abstract theory must be tested against concrete data, but the data must be interpreted in light of temporary conditions and institutional context. The assumption of no credit operations is also a simplifying abstraction. In reality, credit can shorten turnover times and alter the relationships, but Engels abstracts from it to isolate the turnover effect.

The Social Composition of Capital and the Statistical Lacuna

Finally, Engels draws out a broader implication for social statistics. Since there are certainly only a few capitalists who make calculations of such a kind about their businesses, statistical material is almost completely absent on the ratio of the constant part of the total social capital to the variable part. Only the U.S. Census gives what is possible under present-day conditions, the sum of the wages paid in each branch of business and the profits made. Dubious as these data are, owing to the way they rely on the unchecked information of the industrialists themselves, they are none the less extremely valuable and the only data that we have on the subject. In Europe we are far too kind-hearted to expect such revelations on the part of our great industrialists.

This passage is a critique of official statistics and a comment on the limits of empirical knowledge under capitalism. The ratio of constant to variable capital is central to Marx’s theory of the profit rate and the tendency of the rate of profit to fall. But capitalists do not publish the necessary data. Their accounting categories do not distinguish constant from variable capital. They report wages and profits, but not the advanced variable capital, the turnover rate, or the organic composition. The U.S. Census is the only source that even attempts to provide wages and profits by branch, but it relies on self-reported data from industrialists, which is likely biased or incomplete. Engels describes this as “dubious” but “extremely valuable” because it is the only data available. The sarcastic remark about Europe being “far too kind-hearted” to expect such revelations is a sharp critique of the secrecy and ideological self-presentation of capitalist enterprise. It also reinforces the earlier point that the capitalist’s practical categories obscure the source of surplus-value. The statistical lacuna is not accidental; it is a product of the social relations of production and the class interests of capitalists.

This conclusion to the chapter is not a mere appendix. It shows that the theoretical analysis of turnover and profit rate has political and epistemological consequences. If the key variable—the ratio of constant to variable capital—is hidden from statistical view, then the public debate about profits, wages, and inequality is necessarily distorted. The capitalist can present profit as a return on total capital, a reward for enterprise and risk, while concealing the fact that profit arises from the exploitation of variable capital, which is a small fraction of total capital and turns over many times. The annual rate of surplus-value can exceed 1,300 per cent, yet the capitalist’s books show only a 33 per cent profit rate on total capital. This discrepancy is not a mathematical trick; it is the real appearance of the capitalist process. The chapter thus ends by pointing beyond political economy to a critique of capitalist accounting and statistics.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The fundamental lesson of Chapter 4 is that turnover is a determinant of the rate of profit in its own right. The rate of profit is not simply a function of the rate of surplus-value and the organic composition of capital. It also depends on how quickly capital circulates. A faster turnover allows the same advanced variable capital to purchase labour-power more times in a year, producing more annual surplus-value. Because the profit rate is an annual ratio of surplus-value to total capital advanced, this turnover acceleration raises the profit rate, other things equal. The precise formula is (p' = s'n v/C), where (s') is the rate of surplus-value, (n) is the number of turnovers of variable capital per year, (v) is the variable capital advanced, and (C) is the total capital advanced. Equivalently, (p' = S/C), where (S = s' v n) is the annual surplus-value. The turnover effect is captured by the annual rate of surplus-value (S' = s'n). This is the chapter’s central analytical contribution.

The chapter also reveals a series of systemic laws and essential takeaways. First, capital advanced is not the same as capital active. The total capital advanced includes capital that lies fallow in money, raw materials, unsold goods, or debts. The shorter the turnover time, the smaller the fallow portion and the greater the active portion. This is why turnover affects surplus-value production. Second, the variable capital is the only part of capital that produces surplus-value, but its effectiveness depends on its turnover. A small variable capital turning over many times can produce as much surplus-value as a large variable capital turning over once. This is why the annual rate of surplus-value can be enormous even when the profit rate on total capital is moderate. Third, the profit rate is a composite result of several forces: turnover, composition, exploitation rate, working day, and wages. The turnover effect can be offset by a rise in the composition of capital, especially when production-time reduction requires expensive fixed capital. Fourth, the history of capitalism is marked by continuous revolutions in production time and circulation time. Metallurgy, chemistry, railways, steamships, telegraphs, and canals have dramatically shortened turnover times, increasing the efficacy of capital and altering the geography of crisis. Fifth, capitalist accounting and official statistics obscure the variable capital and the annual rate of surplus-value. The capitalist sees fixed and circulating capital, wages and profits, but not the source of surplus-value. This obscures the exploitation at the heart of the system.

The broader practical and theoretical implications are significant. For the theory of the profit rate, the chapter demonstrates that the tendency of the rate of profit to fall cannot be analyzed without considering counteracting forces such as turnover acceleration. A rise in the organic composition of capital may be partially offset by faster turnover, especially through improved transportation and communication. Conversely, a slowdown in turnover—due to war, blockade, financial crisis, or supply-chain disruption—can depress the profit rate independently of changes in exploitation or composition. For crisis theory, the chapter suggests that the spatial and temporal integration of markets through transport and communication can reduce the explosive potential of certain crisis foci, even as it increases the overall efficacy of capital. This does not abolish crisis; it changes its form. For the critique of political economy, the chapter shows that the capitalist’s own categories are inadequate for understanding the system. The distinction between fixed and circulating capital, which dominates business accounting, conceals the distinction between constant and variable capital, which is essential for understanding surplus-value. The absence of statistical data on the social composition of capital is not a neutral gap; it is a consequence of class relations and the ideological presentation of profit as a return on total capital.

Finally, the chapter has a methodological lesson. Engels isolates the turnover effect by assuming equal composition, equal rate of surplus-value, equal working day, and equal wages. He then demonstrates the effect with numerical examples, tests it against a real industrial case, and qualifies the result with historical conditions. This is a model of dialectical analysis: abstract isolation of a single causal force, concrete numerical illustration, empirical testing, and historical qualification. The result is not a simple law but a complex, conditional tendency. The rate of profit rises when turnover accelerates, provided that the acceleration does not require a disproportionate increase in total capital. The annual rate of surplus-value can be extremely high, yet the profit rate on total capital can be moderate. The capitalist system hides this reality behind its accounting categories and statistical silences. Chapter 4 thus closes the loop between Volume 2’s analysis of turnover and Volume 3’s analysis of profit, showing that time itself—the time of production and circulation—is a fundamental dimension of capitalist profitability.
