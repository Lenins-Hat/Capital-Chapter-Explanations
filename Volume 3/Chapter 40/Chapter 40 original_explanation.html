Volume 3 • Chapter 40: Second Form of Differential Rent (Differential Rent II)

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 40 of Capital, Volume III, "The Second Form of Differential Rent (Differential Rent II)," is the analytical hinge of Marx's theory of ground-rent. It stands at the precise point where the analysis of differential rent, having been developed in Chapter 38–39 as Differential Rent I (hereafter DR I)—the rent arising from the simultaneous investment of equal capitals on equal-sized tracts of differing fertility—must be extended to Differential Rent II (hereafter DR II)—the rent arising from the successive investment of capital on the same piece of land. The chapter's central problem is deceptively simple: Does it matter whether capital sums are invested one after another in time on the same soil, or side by side on different soils, so long as the results are the same?

Marx's answer has two halves, and the tension between them is the chapter's whole intellectual engine. On the one hand, the formation of surplus profit is wholly indifferent to this distinction. Whether £10 of capital is divided into four £2½ portions placed on four different acres (A, B, C, D of ascending fertility) or applied in four successive £2½ doses to one and the same acre of D, the mechanism that generates surplus profit is identical: each portion of capital is measured against the least productive portion, whose individual production price regulates the market price, and every portion that out-yields it throws off a surplus profit proportional to the difference. In this respect DR II is "simply a different expression of differential rent I"—the same thing in its nature. On the other hand, the transformation of surplus profit into ground-rent—the transfer of that surplus from the capitalist farmer to the landed proprietor—is not indifferent. When capital is invested successively on one piece of land, the limits within which surplus profit can be converted into rent are "both narrower and unstable," because rent has been contractually fixed at the moment the lease was signed, and every subsequent increment of surplus profit generated by additional investment accrues to the farmer for the duration of the tenancy. Hence the class struggle over lease length, over agricultural statistics, and over the profession of rent assessment.

The chapter thus fits into Part VI of Capital ("The Transformation of Surplus Profit into Ground-Rent") as the bridge between the pure theory of differential rent and its concrete historical-formal dynamics. It is the chapter in which Marx demonstrates (1) that DR I is both the historical and the logical starting-point of DR II; (2) that the rate of rent on capital and the level of rent per acre are distinct quantities whose divergence explains the peculiar behaviour of rents and land prices under intensive cultivation; and (3) that Ricardo's reduction of all differential rent to the single case of declining returns raising the production price is one-sided and mistaken. Its objective is not merely taxonomic—it is to show that the "combination" of the two forms produces a far more complicated field of possible outcomes (rising rent with falling prices, falling rates of surplus profit with rising rent per acre) than the classical political economists admitted. The final sentence—"We now proceed to show the combinations of differential rent II, which presupposes differential rent I as its basis"—hands the reader to Chapter 41, where those combinations are systematically tabulated.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

I. The Opening Frame: Extensive versus Intensive Investment (p. 812)

Marx opens by restating what has been established so far. Until this point, differential rent has been considered only as the result of the varying productivity of equal capital investments on equal land areas of different fertility. This is DR I. The defining features are: equal capitals, equal areas, unequal fertility. Its measure is "the difference between the yield of capital invested on the worst, non-rent-bearing land, and that of capital invested on better land." This is the origin of the entire theory.

The crucial structural characteristic of this case, Marx stresses, is spatial dispersion: "capital investments in different land areas alongside one another, so that each new investment of capital corresponded to a more extensive cultivation and an expansion of the cultivated area." Extensive cultivation in this technical sense means cultivation that expands outward across the soil rather than concentrating capital on already-cultivated soil.

Against this, Marx poses the question that defines the chapter: "Can it make a difference, then, whether sums of capital are invested successively in time on the same piece of land with varying productivity, or invested alongside one another on different pieces of land, as long as we assume that the results are the same?" The phrase "as long as we assume that the results are the same" is doing decisive work: Marx is not asking about differences in output, but about the form of investment (temporal succession vs. spatial coexistence) as such, holding the terminal results constant.

II. The Formal Identity of Surplus-Profit Formation (pp. 812–813)

Marx immediately concedes the first half of the answer: "It cannot be denied, first of all, that as far as the formation of surplus profit is concerned, it is all the same." He then builds the canonical comparison.

Case (i): £3 of production costs is spent on each of four acres of differing quality: - Acre A (worst): yields 1 qr; £3 is therefore the production price of 1 qr and its governing market price. Rent = 0. - Acre B: yields 2 qrs; surplus profit = £3. - Acre C: yields 3 qrs; surplus profit = £6. - Acre D: yields 4 qrs; surplus profit = £9.

Case (ii): The same results are obtained "by the application of this £12 in production costs or £10 of capital in the same sequence to one and the same acre." So the same physical product—1, 2, 3, 4 quarters in successive or simultaneous doses—is produced by successive investment on a single acre.

Two technical clarifications must be registered here. First, "production price" (price of production) = cost price + average profit; in this example £10 of capital corresponds to £12 of production costs, implying a 20% rate of profit (£2 profit on £10 capital), and therefore each £2½ portion of capital carries a production cost of £3. Second, the "quantum" (qr = quarter of wheat) is the unit of corn product, and it is crucial that the production price per quarter is set not by the average cost but by the least productive capital portion—the one that yields only 1 qr.

On these assumptions, Marx writes: "In each case there is a capital of £10, with successive portions of £2½ being invested." Whether these £2½ portions lie side by side on four differently fertile acres or are applied one after another to the same acre, "one of these capitals of £2½ yields no surplus profit, while the other portions give a surplus profit, each in proportion to the difference between its yield and that of the non-rent-bearing investment." The rate of surplus profit for each portion of capital is thus formed in a uniform way in both cases. And since rent is nothing but a form of this surplus profit—surplus profit forming its substance—the magnitude of the potential rent is the same in both cases.

III. The Divergence Appears: The Problem of Transformation (p. 813)

Having established identity, Marx drives in the wedge: "None the less, the second method does give rise to certain difficulties as regards the transformation of surplus profit into rent, this change in form that involves the transfer of surplus profits from the capitalist farmer to the proprietor of the land."

This is the chapter's first and in some ways most important move. The "change in form" is the legal-economic transmutation of surplus profit (which belongs to whoever invests the capital) into ground-rent (which accrues to the landowner as the price of permission to use the soil). Under DR I, this transformation is comparatively stable: the different capitals sit on different farms, and the rental for each farm can be assessed and fixed independently. Under DR II, however, the surplus profits arise after the lease is signed, from investments the farmer himself makes. Marx draws out the concrete consequences:

  • The farmers' resistance to agricultural statistics. "Hence the stubborn resistance of the English farmers to any official agricultural statistics." Farmers know that accurate yield data would arm the landlord to recalibrate the rent at renewal.
  • The class struggle over lease terms. "The rent here is fixed when the farms are leased, and the subsequent surplus profits arising from the successive investments of capital accrue to the farmer as long as the tenancy contract lasts." Therefore "the farmers' battle for long tenancies," and conversely the landlords' preference for "tenancies at will" (leases terminable at a year's notice), which let the proprietor capture the fruits of the tenant's improvements at short intervals.
  • The professionalization of rent assessment. Marx invokes John Morton's Resources of Estates to describe the rise of the "assessor of rents," a "very important, complicated and difficult profession" peculiar to countries where agriculture is intensive—and he defines "intensive" economically: "the concentration of capital on the same piece of land instead of its distribution over adjacent tracts."

Two further mechanisms of transformation are noted. In the case of permanent improvements (drainage, soil amendment, etc.), "the artificially inflated differential fertility of the land is its new natural fertility when the tenancy contract expires"—that is, an improvement that is artificial (capital-produced) at the time of investment becomes indistinguishable from natural fertility at the moment of re-letting, and is accordingly capitalized into the rent. In the case of working capital, "the level of rent for a working capital of given size is added to the average rent for the land," so that the incoming tenant must be guaranteed enough capital "to continue cultivation in the same intensive manner"—a device that insulates the landlord's rental stream from the risk of under-capitalized successors.

IV. First Stress-Point: DR I as the Historical and Logical Basis (pp. 814–815)

Marx now lists the points that must be stressed in analysing DR II. Firstly, its basis and point of departure, historically and dynamically, is DR I. The historical argument unfolds in stages:

  1. Colonies and pre-capitalist agriculture. In colonies, "the colonists need only invest a little capital; the main agencies of production are labour and the soil itself. Each individual family head seeks an independent field of employment for himself and his people to work on." Agriculture proper, even in pre-capitalist modes, has this dispersed character.
  2. Pastoralism. In sheep-farming and stock-raising as an independent branch, exploitation of land is "more or less communal" and "fundamentally extensive from the outset."
  3. The genesis of the capitalist mode. Capitalist production "develops out of earlier modes of production in which the means of production are either in law or in fact the property of the tiller himself"—i.e., out of agriculture as a handicraft. Concentration of the means of production into capital (over against wage-labourers) happens only gradually.
  4. The characteristic early form. Capitalist agriculture first assumes its characteristic form in sheep-farming and stock-raising—but, crucially, not by concentrating capital on a small area. The saving is "on the keeping of horses and other production costs," and the mode is "production on a larger scale," i.e., extensive. This is a subtle point: early capitalism in agriculture economizes by spreading capital widely, not by intensifying it.
  5. The natural-law turning point. "It follows from the natural laws of farming... that given a certain level of agriculture and the corresponding exhaustion of the soil, capital... becomes the decisive element in cultivation." As long as tilled land is a small fraction of untilled land and the soil's natural resources are unexhausted (the phase when stock-raising and meat-eating predominate), the capitalist mode distinguishes itself from peasant production chiefly "by the amount of land that is tilled for the account of one capitalist"—i.e., extensive use of capital on a greater area.

The conclusion is double-edged: DR I is the historical starting-point from which development proceeds, and simultaneously the "variegated basis" on which DR II's movement at any given moment occurs. Even at a developed stage, successive investments on one acre presuppose a landscape of coexisting lands of differing fertility whose differentials provide the reference points.

V. Second Stress-Point: Capital Distribution, Creditworthiness, and the Uneven Development of Capitalist Agriculture (pp. 815–816)

Secondly, Marx introduces a factor that supplements fertility differences: differences in the distribution of capital (and creditworthiness) among farmers. The argument is an analogy with manufacture. In any branch of industry, a minimum scale of business and a corresponding minimum capital emerge, below which a business cannot be conducted at all; above the minimum, a normal average amount of capital emerges, which the bulk of producers possess. Capital above this norm yields extra profit; capital below it "does not receive even the average profit."

But here is the disanalogy. Capitalist production "takes hold of agriculture only in a slow and uneven manner, as we can see in the case of England, the classical land of the capitalist mode of production in this sector." Where there is no free import of corn (or where its effect is restricted by the Corn Laws), "the market price is determined by those producers who work on inferior soil, i.e., producers whose conditions of production are less favourable than the average"—and a large part of the total agricultural capital is in the hands of just such producers.

Marx inserts a sharp aside on the peasant: "the peasant, for example, devotes a great deal of labour to his small parcel of land. But this labour is isolated, and deprived of the objective social and material conditions of productivity; it is denuded of them." The peasant's labour, unassisted by the social organization of production and material aids, does not constitute productive power in the capitalist sense. From the coexistence of under-capitalized producers with genuinely capitalist farmers, Marx derives the conclusion: "the genuinely capitalist farmers are in a position to appropriate a portion of surplus profit; this would disappear... if the capitalist mode of production were as uniformly developed in agriculture as in manufacture." In other words, a portion of what appears as differential rent under DR II is not a fertility-differential at all but a capital-and-credit differential—a surplus accruing to the better-financed farmer against the inferior producer whose costs regulate the market price.

VI. The Core Numerical Demonstration: DR II Reproduces DR I on a Single Acre (pp. 816–817)

Setting aside for the moment "the conditions under which this surplus profit can be transformed into ground-rent," Marx turns to pure formation. His conclusion is stated flatly: "differential rent II is simply a different expression of differential rent I, and the same thing as far as its nature is concerned." The reasoning: the differing fertility of different land types affects DR I only insofar as capitals give unequal results; "it can make no difference to this differing fertility or its product... whether this inequality marks different capitals invested successively on the same piece of land or whether the capitals are invested on several pieces of land of different types." In DR I different kinds of land do for different capitals of equal size what in DR II the same land does for successive portions of capital. The land simply exhibits differential fertility toward successive doses.

The proof is a re-computation of Table I. The £10 that was spread as four independent £2½ capitals across A, B, C and D is now invested successively on one acre of D, with the first dose yielding 4 qrs, the second 3 qrs, the third 2 qrs, and the last 1 qr (or in the inverse sequence). The £3-per-quarter price set by the least fruitful dose (the 1-qr dose) still governs, and "does not yield any differential rent, though it would determine the production price as long as it is necessary to supply wheat whose production price is £3." Assuming capitalist production, the £3 includes the average profit any £2½ capital yields, so the three remaining doses each throw off a surplus profit proportional to the excess of their product over the 1-qr dose. The result: (4−1) + (3−1) + (2−1) = 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 qrs = £18, exactly the aggregate rent of B, C and D in Table I. The minimum product of the worst land (1 qr per £2½) is simply transposed into the minimum product of the marginal capital dose. This is the concrete meaning of "DR II presupposes DR I."

VII. Declining Returns: When They Matter and When They Do Not (p. 817)

Marx now unfolds a four-dose example on D, in which the doses yield 4, 1, 3, 2 quarters. The first dose (£2½ → 4 qrs) bears 3 qrs of differential rent. The second (£2½ → 1 qr) is a non-rent-bearing capital investment—"just like the same capital on the worst land A"—yielding only the average profit, with no surplus profit to transform into rent. Critically, "this declining product of the second capital investment on D would not have any effect on the profit rate"—it is as if £2½ were freshly invested on another acre of A. It therefore leaves the surplus profit, and hence the differential rent, on A, B, C and D untouched.

The farmer then makes two further £2½ investments yielding 3 qrs and 2 qrs. Compared with the original 4-qr dose, these represent a decline in the level of surplus profit (2 and 1 qrs respectively instead of 3), but this decline "would affect neither the average profit nor the governing production price." It is only a fall in the surplus-profit rate on the marginal doses—an internal matter for the farmer's bookkeeping.
