Volume 3 • Chapter 1: Cost Price and Profit

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS:

This chapter is the opening hinge of Volume 3 of Marx's Capital, and its function is architectural. Volumes 1 and 2 had successively dissected, first, the immediate production process (where "all secondary influences external to this process were left out of account") and, second, the circulation process, culminating in the demonstration that "the capitalist production process, taken as a whole, is a unity of the production and circulation processes." The opening pages explicitly refuse the temptation to "make general reflections on this unity." Instead, the chapter announces the concrete task of the volume: "to discover and present the concrete forms which grow out of the process of capital's movement considered as a whole." These concrete forms are the categories visible "on the surface of society, in the action of different capitals on one another, i.e. in competition, and in the everyday consciousness of the agents of production themselves."

The chapter's central problem is therefore essentially epistemological as much as economic: How does the surplus-value produced in the hidden recesses of the labour process reappear on the surface of society as profit? The core thesis is that this reappearance is not a mere change of label but a genuine, structurally necessary transformation of form — a "mystification" that arises inevitably from the capitalist mode of production itself. The surplus-value that Volume 1 proved to originate exclusively in the exploitation of living labour is, on the surface, detached from its source and re-attributed to the total capital advanced. The category that performs this displacement is the cost price (k), and the category that results from it is profit (p).

The argument unfolds in two movements. First, Marx isolates the cost price as the portion of commodity value that simply replaces the capital value expended — the sum c + v as it appears from the capitalist's standpoint. The formula C = c + v + s collapses into C = k + s. Second, he shows that because the cost price obliterates the distinction between constant and variable capital — the very distinction on which Volume 1's theory of surplus-value depended — the surplus-value comes to appear as an emanation of the total capital applied rather than of the variable portion alone. It thus takes on the transformed form of profit, giving the surface formula C = k + p.

Intellectually, this chapter occupies a decisive place in the whole structure of Capital. It is the first step in the long "descent" from essence to appearance that the remainder of Volume 3 will pursue: from surplus-value to profit, from profit to the general rate of profit, from value to price of production, from surplus-profit to rent, from interest and profit of enterprise. The chapter is therefore the seedbed for the "law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall," the transformation problem, and the theory of the "trinity formula." It also performs a polemical role: it exposes the errors of the "vulgar economists" — Torrens, Malthus, Proudhon, and (via a footnote) N. W. Senior — who either confused cost price with value, or claimed that profit springs from circulation rather than production. The Balzac allusion to Les Paysans grounds the theoretical point that in a society dominated by capitalist production, "even the non-capitalist producer is dominated by capitalist ways of thinking."

The intellectual objective is thus threefold: (1) to derive category-wise the form of profit from the form of surplus-value; (2) to identify the cost price as the mechanism of concealment; and (3) to establish, before proceeding to the general rate of profit, that a range of sale prices lies between the value and the cost price of commodities — a fact that will be essential for explaining under-selling and the determination of production prices.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS:

The Transitional Problem: From the Hidden Process to the Surface of Society

The chapter opens by situating itself within the architecture of the whole work. Volume 1 studied "the immediate production process" in isolation, deliberately excluding external secondary influences. Volume 2 added the circulation process, showing in its Part Three — where circulation mediates social reproduction — that the capitalist production process is a "unity of production and circulation." Now, however, the concern is not to repeat these abstractions but to capture "the concrete forms which grow out of the process of capital's movement considered as a whole." The key phrase is "concrete forms." The categories developed in Volume 1 (constant capital, variable capital, surplus-value) were essential forms; the categories to be developed in Volume 3 are surface forms — the forms in which capital "confronts one another in competition, and in the everyday consciousness of the agents of production themselves." The gap between essence and appearance is not a flaw in perception but a real, necessary inversion generated by the mode of production. This is the methodological premise on which everything else in the chapter rests: to explain the surface, one must first derive it from the essence, not simply accept it.

The Fundamental Formula and the Birth of the Cost Price

The analysis begins with the value formula for any commodity produced capitalistically: C = c + v + s, where c is constant capital (means of production), v is variable capital (labour-power), and s is surplus-value. Marx immediately asks what happens if we subtract s. The residue is "a mere equivalent or replacement value in commodities for the capital value c + v laid out on the elements of production."

The concrete illustration is precise. Suppose production requires an outlay of £500: £20 for wear and tear of instruments of labour, £380 for raw materials, and £100 for labour-power. Assuming a rate of surplus-value of 100 per cent:

C = 400c + 100v + 100s = £600.

Subtracting the £100 surplus-value leaves a commodity value of £500, which "simply replaces the capital expenditure of £500." This portion — "which replaces the price of the means of production consumed and the labour-power employed" — is what the commodity costs the capitalist, and therefore constitutes, from his standpoint, the cost price of the commodity. The conceptual identification is subtle: it is not merely that the capitalist calls it the cost price; rather, because he must buy back these elements of production out of the sale proceeds, the category of cost price acquires a genuine functional independence within the circuit of capital.

The pivotal distinction, hammered home in the next paragraph, is between two incommensurable magnitudes: "What the commodity costs the capitalist, and what it actually does cost to produce it, are two completely different quantities." The surplus-value portion costs the capitalist nothing "for the very reason that it costs the worker his unpaid labour." But because the worker is "himself an ingredient of the functioning productive capital that belongs to the capitalist," the capitalist — being "the actual commodity producer" — necessarily perceives the cost price as the real cost. Here Marx's characteristic move is visible: the illusion is not arbitrary but rooted in a real structural position. The capitalist is correct about his own cash expenditure; he is wrong only when he universalizes that partial standpoint into a theory of value. Marx now formalizes the transformation: if cost price is k, then C = k + s, or "commodity value = cost price + surplus-value."

The Dual Nature of Cost Price: Capitalist Account vs. Actual Value

The chapter now unpacks the two-sided character of the cost price as a category. On one hand, the reduction of heterogeneous value components to a single heading of "cost price" expresses "the specific character of capitalist production": the capitalist's cost is measured by capital expenditure, whereas the actual cost is measured by labour expenditure. This is the quantitative distinction: "The capitalist cost price of the commodity is thus quantitatively distinct from its value or its actual cost price; it is smaller than the commodity's value, for since C = k + s, k = C − s."

On the other hand, the cost price is "by no means simply a category that exists only in capitalist book-keeping." It acquires real independence because it must "constantly be transformed back again into the form of productive capital by way of the circulation process" — the cost price must "continuously buy back the elements of production consumed in its production." This is crucial: the cost price is not a subjective miscounting. It is a real functional moment in the turnover of capital, and because it is real, the illusion it generates has objective force. Yet Marx insists that the category "has nothing to do with the formation of commodity value or the process of capital's valorization." Knowing that five-sixths of a £600 value is merely replacement value tells us nothing about how that value was produced, nor about the origin of the final sixth. The cost price is thus at once real (as a circuit requirement) and epistemologically misleading (as a theory of value). Marx promises that his investigation "will show, however, that cost price does none the less, in the economy of capital, present the false semblance of an actual category of value production."

The Labour-Time Decomposition of Cost Price

To expose the heterogeneity hidden within the homogeneous figure of £500, Marx converts money into labour-time. Assuming the value produced by one worker in an average social working day is expressed by 6 shillings, the advanced £500 = 400c + 100v represents "the value product of 1,666⅔ of such 10-hour working days." Of these, 1,333⅓ days are "crystallized in the value of the means of production" (= 400c) and 333⅓ days "in the value of the labour-power" (= 100v). Given the 100 per cent rate of exploitation, the actual production of the new commodity costs, in labour-power, 100v + 100s = 666⅔ 10-hour working days. This calculation performs a dual function: it shows that the £600 total breaks into £400 "reappearing value" and £200 "newly produced value," and it makes visible what the money-form conceals — that the £500 cost price is composed of two completely different elements in origin: (1) the reappearing 400c and (2) one half of the newly produced £200 (i.e., 100v).

The fate of these two components is then traced in detail. The £400 of means of production is transferred to the product "by the purposive character of the labour spent," so that the old value "reappears therefore as a component of the product's value, though it does not originate in the production process of this commodity." It is a component of the product's value only because it previously existed as a component of the capital advanced. Its dual significance: it enters the cost price because it is a component of value, and it is a component of value because it was the value of capital used up.

With the variable component, "it is quite the reverse." The 666⅔ days' labour forms a new value of £200. Out of this new value, one part replaces the £100 advanced, but "this advance of capital value does not go in any way into the formation of the new value." The distinction is between labour-power as value in the capital advance and labour-power as creator of value in production. "In place of the value of the labour-power, which is what figures in the capital advance, we have the living, value-creating labour-power that actually functions as productive capital." This is the decisive conceptual pivot of the whole chapter: the two halves of the cost price are not merely numerically different portions of a homogeneous fund — one is transferred, the other replaced. Their apparent homogeneity in the cost price is precisely the illusion.

The Empirical Decomposition Test: Changes in Constant vs. Variable Capital

Marx then offers his most powerful analytic device: varying each component while holding others constant, to demonstrate the asymmetric behaviour of c and v.

Case A — change in constant capital. Suppose the price of means of production rises from £400 to £600. Then not only does the cost price rise from £500 to 600c + 100v = £700, but the commodity value itself rises to 600c + 100v + 100s = £800. If it falls to £200, both cost price (200c + 100v = £300) and value (200c + 100v + 100s = £400) fall. Why? "Because the constant capital that is used up transfers its own value to the product," the product's value rises or falls with it. Constant capital is value-transferring: it contributes exactly (and only) the value it loses.

Case B — change in variable capital. Suppose the price of labour-power rises from £100 to £150. The cost price rises to 400c + 150v = £550. But the commodity value remains unchanged at £600 — it becomes 400c + 150v + 50s. If the wage falls to £50, the cost price falls to 400c + 50v = £450, while the value stays at £600, now as 400c + 50v + 150s. Variable capital "does not add its own value to the product. In place of its value, it is the new value created by labour that enters the product." A change in the price of labour-power merely reshuffles the division of the £200 new value between wages and surplus-value: it "affects only the ratio between the two components of this new value."

This contrast establishes the whole logic of the chapter. The two halves of the cost price share only one thing in common: "they are both portions of commodity value which replace capital that was advanced." That is their sole commonality — a commonality from the standpoint of replacement, not from the standpoint of creation. Constant capital and variable capital behave in radically different ways in value formation while appearing identical in cost-price accounting.

The Inversion: From Farmhand to Wages, from Surplus-Value to Profit

Having decoupled the two components analytically, Marx now performs the crucial reversal: "From the standpoint of capitalist production, however, this actual state of affairs necessarily appears upside down." The mechanism of the inversion is the wage-form. Capitalist production, "distinguished from the mode of production founded on slavery," expresses "the value or price of labour-power ... as the value or price of labour itself, i.e. as wages" (citing Vol. 1, Ch. 19). Consequently, the variable component of capital "appears as capital spent on wages, as a capital value which pays the value or price of all labour spent in production."

The numerical reworking is again precise. The £100 variable capital is the monetary expression of value produced in 333⅓ 10-hour days. But "the value of the labour-power purchased, which figures here in the capital advance, does not form any part of the actually functioning capital." If the rate of exploitation is 100 per cent, the living labour-power is used for 666⅔ 10-hour days and adds £200 of new value to the product. Yet the £100 in the advance figures as the price "of the labour performed in these 666⅔ 10-hour days." Dividing £100 by 666⅔ yields 3 shillings as the price of one 10-hour working day — "the value product of 5 hours' labour."

This produces the catastrophic theoretical result. He then compares capital advance and commodity value side by side: - I. Capital advance of £500 = £400 in means of production (price of means of production) + £100 in capital spent on labour (price of 666⅔ working days, i.e., wages). - II. Commodity value of £600 = cost price of £500 (£400 price of means of production + £100 price of 666⅔ working days) + £100 surplus-value.

In this formula, "the portion of capital laid out on labour is distinguished from that laid out on means of production only by the fact that it serves as payment for a materially different element of production, and in no way by the fact that it plays a functionally different role in the process of forming commodity value." The vital distinction between constant and variable capital — the foundation of the entire theory of surplus-value in Volume 1 — "has disappeared." What we see in the cost price are "only finished and existing values — the value portions of the capital advanced which enter the formation of the product's value — and not an element that creates new value."

The £500 cost price thus acquires a double significance: first, it is the component of the £600 commodity value that replaces the capital consumed; second, it exists as such only because it existed previously as the cost price of the elements of production, i.e., as a capital advance. "The capital value returns as the commodity's cost price, because and in so far as it was spent as a capital value."

The Substitution of Fixed/Circulating for Constant/Variable

Having erased the constant/variable distinction, Marx shows what distinction does remain visible in the cost price: the distinction between fixed and circulating capital. The material diversity of the value components of the advanced capital (means of labour, raw and ancillary materials, and labour itself) "only means that the cost price of the commodity must buy back again these materially distinct elements of production." But regarding the formation of the cost price, "the only distinction that matters is the distinction between fixed and circulating capital."

The illustration is carefully constructed. Depreciation of means of labour was £20, so 400c = £20 (depreciation of means of labour) + £380 (materials). The means of labour formerly worth £1,200 now exists in two forms: £20 as part of the commodity's value, and £1,180 as the remaining value of the means of labour, still in the capitalist's possession as an element of productive capital. Production materials and wages, by contrast, are "used up completely," so their entire value enters the commodity's value. Hence: the capital advanced is £1,680 = a fixed capital of £1,200 plus a circulating capital of £480 (£380 materials + £100 wages). The cost price of the commodity is £500 = £20 depreciation of fixed capital + £480 circulating capital.

The discrepancy between £1,680 (advanced) and £500 (cost price) is not a paradox but a confirmation: the cost price "is formed exclusively by the capital actually used up on the commodity's production." The £1,200 of fixed capital is applied but only £20 is lost; fixed capital enters the cost price only partially, "in proportion to the magnitude of their value," while circulating capital enters completely. Marx drives the point home with a rhetorical question: if the partial entry were not the rule, "there would be no reason why the fixed capital of £1,200 that is advanced should not also add to the value of the product the £1,180 that it does not lose in the production process, instead of just the £20 that it actually does lose."

The crux of the mystification now emerges with full force. The fixed/circulating distinction "only confirms the apparent origin of the cost price in the capital value expended, or the price that the expended elements of production, labour included, cost the capitalist himself." But "as far as value formation is concerned, the variable portion of capital, that laid out on labour-power, is expressly identified here with constant capital ... under the heading of circulating capital, and thus the valorization process of capital is completely mystified." The footnote underscores the practical stakes by citing N. W. Senior (1790–1864), "one of the principal exponents of 'vulgar economics' in England and particularly notorious for his opposition to the legal restriction of working hours, on the basis of his theory of the 'last hour'" — the doctrine that all profit is produced in the final hour of the working day, which Marx had demolished in Vol. 1, Ch. 9.

Surplus-Value Becomes Profit: The Second Transformation

The chapter's second great movement now begins. "So far we have considered only one element of commodity value, the cost price. We must now take a look at the other component, the excess over the cost price or the surplus-value." Initially, surplus-value is simply "an excess commodity value over and above the cost price." But because the cost price equals the value of the capital expended and is continuously transformed back into the material elements of that capital, "this additional value is a value accruing to the capital expended in the production of the commodity and returning from its circulation."

Marx traces the algebraic transmutation of the formula. Originally, s "derives only from a change in the value of v, the variable capital, and is therefore originally simply an increment to the variable capital." But once production is complete, s "can also ... form a value increment to c + v, the total capital expended." Thus the formula c + (v + s) — which signals that s arises specifically from the conversion of v into a variable magnitude — "can also be represented as (c + v) + s." Before production: capital £500. After: capital £500 plus a value increment of £100. (The footnote quotes Vol. 1, Ch. 9, p. 322, where Marx had already noted that the fact "that it is v alone that varies ... [is] obscured by the circumstance that in consequence of the increase in the variable component of the capital, there is also an increase in the sum total of the capital advanced.")

The generalization deepens. The surplus-value "forms an addition not only to the part of the capital advanced that enters the process of valorization, but also to the part that does not enter this process." Using the revised numbers: before production, capital value is £1,680 (£1,200 fixed capital laid out on means of labour, of which only £20 enters the commodity's value as depreciation, plus £480 circulating capital). After production: £1,180 as the value component of the productive capital plus a commodity capital of £600 = £1,780. Deducting the £1,680 advanced leaves an additional value of £100. "Thus the £100 surplus-value forms as much an addition to the total capital applied of £1,680 as to the fraction of this, £500, that is used up in the course of production."

This is the decisive claim. To the capitalist it is "clear enough ... that this additional value derives from the productive activities which he undertakes with his capital, i.e. that it derives from the capital itself. For after the production process he has it, and before the production process he did not." As regards the capital actually used up, the surplus-value "appears to derive equally from the different value elements of this capital, both means of production and labour," because these elements "are both equally involved in the formation of the cost price."

The "All-Wages" and "All-Means-of-Production" Reduction Tests

Marx then deploys a second analytic experiment: imagine the capital expended consists either exclusively of wages or exclusively of means of production. If exclusively wages, instead of 400c + 100v + 100s, we would have 500v + 100s. The £500 laid out on wages would be the value of all the labour applied, and would form the cost price of the whole £600 product. "The formation of this cost price ... is however the only process in the formation of this commodity value that we know of. We know nothing of where its surplus-value component of £100 comes from." Exactly the same happens if the value is taken as 500c + 100s.

The point of this thought-experiment is to isolate the form of surplus-value abstracted from its real source. In both cases, "the surplus-value derives from a given value because this value was advanced in the form of productive capital, leaving aside the question whether this took the form of labour or that of means of production." But — and this is the dialectical pivot — "the capital value advanced cannot form surplus-value simply by virtue of its having been used up and forming therefore the cost price of the commodity." For to the extent that it forms the cost price, "it does not form any surplus-value, but simply an equivalent, a replacement value." Therefore, to the extent that this value does form surplus-value, "it forms this not in its specific capacity as capital that has been used up, but rather as advanced and therefore applied capital in general."

The conclusion: "Surplus-value thus derives as much from the part of the capital advanced that does not enter the cost price of the commodity as from the part of it that does enter the cost price; in short, it derives equally from the fixed and circulating components of the capital applied." Materially, the entire capital "serves to form the product, the means of labour as much as the production materials and labour itself." The entire capital is materially involved in the labour process "even if only a part of it is involved in the process of valorization." Marx notes with characteristic irony: "This is perhaps the very reason why it contributes only in part towards the formation of the cost price, but in full towards the formation of surplus-value." The upshot is that "the surplus-value springs simultaneously from all parts of the capital applied."

Malthus and the Definition of Capital and Profit

The theoretical deduction is then condensed into an aphorism. Marx quotes Malthus: "The capitalist ... expects an equal profit upon all the parts of the capital which he advances." The citation (Principles of Political Economy, 2nd ed., 1836, p. 268) demonstrates that the practical consciousness of the capitalist and the superficial economics of the vulgar school agree: both attribute the increment to total capital. The footnote adds Marx's critical judgment of Malthus as "a serious if minor economist, despite the reactionary ideology in which his theoretical contributions were buried," and cites Malthus's definition ("Capital: that which is expended with a view to profit").

From here, the chapter's central transformation is completed. "As this supposed derivative of the total capital advanced, the surplus-value takes on the transformed form of profit." A sum of value "is therefore capital if it is invested in order to produce a profit, or alternatively profit arises because a sum of value is employed as capital." If we call profit p, then C = c + v + s = k + s is converted into C = k + p, or "commodity value = cost price + profit."

Marx then gives the canonical statement: "Profit, as we are originally faced with it, is thus the same thing as surplus-value, save in a mystified form, though one that necessarily arises from the capitalist mode of production." The mechanism of the mystification is restated with precision: "Because no distinction between constant and variable capital can be recognized in the apparent formation of the cost price, the origin of the change in value that occurs in the course of the production process is shifted from the variable capital to the capital as a whole. Because the price of labour-power appears at one pole in the transformed form of wages, surplus-value appears at the other pole in the transformed form of profit." This is a mirror-structure of fetishism: disguise at one pole (labour-power → wages) produces counterpart disguise at the other (surplus-value → profit).

The Range of Sale Prices and the "Secret" of Competition

The analysis then turns to a consequence that is at once theoretical and empirically explosive: the existence of a continuum of possible sale prices between the cost price and the value. Since C = k + s, "k = C − s." The reduced formula C = k would require s = 0, "a case that never arises in conditions of capitalist production." If the commodity sells at its value, the profit realized equals the entire surplus-value. But — and here is the key — "the capitalist can sell the commodity at a profit even if he sells it at less than its value. As long as its sale price is above its cost price, even if below its value, a part of the surplus-value contained in it is always realized, i.e. a profit is made."

The numerical illustration: value £600, cost price £500. If the commodity is sold at £510, £520, £530, £560, or £590, it is sold respectively at £90, £80, £70, £40, or £10 below its value, and yet a profit of £10, £20, £30, £60, or £90 is realized. "An indefinite series of sale prices is evidently possible between the value of a commodity and its cost price. The greater the element of commodity value consisting of surplus-value, the greater the practical room for these intermediate prices."

Marx then announces the strategic importance of this result. It enables the explanation of "such everyday phenomena of competition as, for instance, certain cases of under-selling, an abnormally low level of commodity prices in certain branches of industry." More vitally, "the basic law of capitalist competition, which political economy has so far failed to grasp, the law that governs the general rate of profit and the so-called prices of production determined by it, depends, as we shall see, on this difference between the value and the cost price of commodities, and the possibility deriving from this of selling commodities below their value at a profit." This is the forward link to the entire subsequent argument of Volume 3, and it identifies the cost-price-to-value gap as the structural precondition of equalized profit rates across branches of industry with differing organic compositions.

The Minimum Limit and the Illusion That Flows from It

The cost price functions as the lower bound: sold beneath it, "the components of productive capital that were expended cannot be fully replaced"; if the sale continues below it, "the capital value advanced will disappear completely." From this standpoint the capitalist "is inclined to treat the cost price as the real inner value of the commodity, as it is the price he needs merely to preserve his capital." And because the cost price is also the purchase price the capitalist has paid for its production, the surplus-value realized on sale "appears to the capitalist as an excess of its sale price over its value, instead of an excess of its value over its cost price, so that the surplus-value concealed in the commodity is not simply realized by its sale, but actually derives from the sale itself." Marx notes that this illusion was already treated in Vol. 1, Ch. 5 ("Contradictions in the General Formula").

Polemic I: Torrens and "Creation From Nothing"

Marx next returns to a modern restatement of the illusion by Colonel Robert Torrens (1780–1864, an advocate of the "Currency Principle"). Torrens argues: "The natural price, consisting of the cost of production, or, in other words, of the capital expended in raising or fabricating commodities, cannot include the profit." The farmer who expends 100 quarters of corn and reaps 120 has a profit of 20 quarters, but "it would be absurd to call this excess, or profit, a part of the expenditure." The manufacturer's finished work "must possess a higher exchangeable value than the materials, tools, and subsistence, by the advance of which it was obtained." Torrens concludes that the excess of sale price over cost price derives from consumers who, "either by immediate or circuitous barter[,] give some greater portion of all the ingredients of capital than their production costs."

Marx dismantles this. "In actual fact, the excess over a given magnitude can in no way form part of that magnitude, and so profit ... cannot form any part of these outlays." If commodity value is formed solely out of the capitalist's advance, "there is no way of seeing how any more value is to come out of production than went into it, unless something is to come out of nothing." Torrens's escape is to shift the miracle from production to circulation. But this fails: "Profit cannot derive from commodity exchange, Ramsay answers him, unless it is already present before this exchange takes place." The sum of values of products exchanged "is evidently not affected by the exchange of the products whose value sum this is." Marx notes that Malthus appeals to Torrens's authority, even though Malthus explains selling above value differently "— or rather does not explain it, as all arguments of this kind are unfailingly reducible, in effect, to the same thing as the negative weight of phlogiston, which was so renowned in its time." Engels's footnote formalizes the analogy: this is Marx's theory of value against the "phlogiston" theory the way Lavoisier refuted Stahl.

Polemic II: Balzac, the Peasant, and the Pervasiveness of Capitalist Categories

The chapter then pivots, with remarkable literary economy, to a demonstration that capitalist categories dominate even non-capitalist producers. In Balzac's Les Paysans, "the small peasant eager to retain the good-will of the money-lender performs all kinds of services for him unpaid, yet does not see himself as giving something for nothing, as his own labour does not cost him any cash expenditure." The peasant's illusion is structurally identical to the businessman's: he measures cost by cash outlay, not by labour-time, and thus fails to see that unpaid labour is a real gift. The money-lender "kills two birds with one stone": he spares cash expenditure on wages, and, as the peasant is gradually ruined by depriving his own fields of labour, "he enmeshes him ever deeper in the web of usury." The theoretical point is that the appearance-forms of capital are not merely theories produced by economists; they are lived consciousness, imposed by capitalist social relations on everyone, including those the system exploits.

Polemic III: Proudhon and the People's Bank

Marx then turns to Proudhon (1809–1864), whose "People's Bank" (established briefly in Paris in January 1849 and placed in forced liquidation after two months) was founded on the claim that "the cost price of the commodity is its real price and that surplus-value springs from selling the commodity above its value, i.e. that commodities are sold at their values when their sale price is equal to their cost price — i.e. equal to the price of the means of production consumed in them, plus wages." Marx calls this "pseudo-scientific quackery" presenting an old error "as a newly discovered secret of socialism." He had already criticized the theoretical basis in The Poverty of Philosophy (1847).

The refutation is algebraic and visual. Following Vol. 1, Ch. 9, we may represent value components as proportionate parts of the product itself. If the value of 20 lb. of yarn is 30 shillings, made up of 24s. means of production, 3s. labour-power, and 3s. surplus-value, then the surplus-value can be represented as one-tenth of the product, i.e., 2 lb. of yarn. If the 20 lb. are sold at cost price (27s.), the buyer receives 2 lb. of yarn for nothing; "or the commodity is sold at one-tenth below its value." The worker has still performed his surplus labour, "but now for the buyer of the yarn instead of for the capitalist yarn producer." The exploitation is redistributed to the purchaser — it is not abolished. Proudhon's scheme would transfer surplus-value to the buyers of commodities without eliminating surplus labour.

Finally, Marx exposes a second and more sweeping error in Proudhon's reduction. Even if all commodities were sold at cost prices, "it would be quite wrong to suppose that the result would in fact be the same as if they were all sold above their cost prices but at their values." For even with equal value of labour-power, equal length of working day, and equal rate of exploitation, "the amounts of surplus-value that the values of the various different kinds of commodities contain are completely unequal, according to the differing organic compositions of the capitals advanced for their production." The footnote quotes Vol. 1, Ch. 11: masses of value and surplus-value produced by different capitals "vary directly as the amounts of the variable components of these capitals." This is a crucial forward link: it prefigures the transformation problem. Selling at cost price would not neutralize exploitation, because capitals of differing organic composition produce different masses of surplus-value. The very variability of surplus-value across branches of production is what makes the equalization of profit rates (and hence the divergence of production prices from values) necessary in a competitive economy.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS:

The chapter establishes a fundamental structural law of capitalist appearance: profit is surplus-value in a necessarily mystified form. The mystification is not an error to be corrected; it is a real product of the mode of production, generated by the wage-form and the capital-circuit through the category of cost price. Because wages represent the price of all labour (not merely of labour-power), and because the cost price treats means of production and labour as homogeneous elements of advances, the origin of the value increment is displaced from the variable component of capital onto the capital as a whole. The formula C = k + p is therefore not merely a restatement of C = c + v + s; it is its inverted surface-form. The distinction between essence and appearance becomes a structural necessity of capital: a distinction Marx will generalize into the theory of the fetishism of capital, the trinity formula (land–rent, capital–profit, labour–wages), and the eventual identification of profit with the entire surplus under the umbrella of the average rate of profit.

The second great lesson concerns the range of sale prices between cost price and value. This is not a marginal curiosity. It is the indispensible precondition for two central results of Volume 3: the possibility of under-selling and abnormal prices in certain branches, and the general equalization of profit rates with the resulting divergence of production prices from values. If commodities always sold at their values, competition could not accomplish this equalization, because capitals of different organic compositions would produce unequal rates of profit by definition. The cost-price/value gap is precisely what allows capitals to redistribute surplus across branches through price competition, producing a uniform general profit rate. This is why Marx calls the resulting law "the basic law of capitalist competition, which political economy has so far failed to grasp."

The third lesson is the reductionist refutation of Proudhonian socialism. The fantasy of a "People's Bank" that sells commodities at cost price so as to eliminate exploitation is exposed as a category error. It neither abolishes surplus labour (the worker continues to give it) nor distributes it justly (it simply transfers it to the buyer). And even if all commodities were sold at cost price, the amounts of surplus-value across branches would still be unequal because of differing organic compositions. The radical implication is that exploitation cannot be abolished by circulation reform (credit, exchange, pricing); it can be abolished only by abolishing the wage-relation and the separation of the producers from the means of production. This connects the chapter to the broader socialist programme: reform of circulation is a mirage; the terrain of struggle is production.

The fourth lesson concerns the pervasiveness of capitalist categories. The Balzac example demonstrates that the forms of appearance of capital are not merely the false theories of economists but the lived thought of every participant in a capitalist society, including peasants and other non-capitalist producers. This is Marx's answer to any view that treats "the everyday consciousness of the agents of production" as a source of truth: that consciousness is generated by the system itself, and it systematically reverses the real causation. The epistemological implication is radical: science must derive the categories of the surface from the underlying value-form, not merely describe or accept the surface.

Fifth, the chapter firmly links the theory of value to the critique of the previous political economy. Malthus's aphorism, Torrens's exchange-based theory of profit, and the analogous "creation from nothing" are all shown to depend on the same error. Marx's "phlogiston" analogy, reinforced by Engels in the footnote, marks the theoretical rupture: the classical economists (Smith, Ricardo) had discovered value but had not derived profit correctly; the vulgar economists, from Senior to Torrens, retreated into appearance-worship. The chapter therefore asserts the primacy of the Volume 1 theory of surplus-value as the sole scientific foundation on which the concrete forms of Volume 3 can be built.

Finally, the broader philosophical implication: the entire analysis demonstrates the dialectic of scientific representation. In the realm of capital, essence and appearance are not merely different but systematically inverted, and the inversion has real effects (competition, prices, the practical consciousness of capitalists, the legitimacy of profit). Capital's own movement disguises its own source. This is the deepest claim of Volume 3: the "surface of society" is not a place where truth is finally revealed but rather the site where truth is systematically obscured. The task of critique is not to describe that surface but to reconstruct its hidden grammar. From this point forward, every category of Volume 3 — the general rate of profit, price of production, commercial profit, interest, rent — will be shown to flow from this initial transformation of surplus-value into profit, and every category will continue, in Marx's sense, to be a form of mystification that must be continually dissolved by theory.
