Volume 3 • Chapter 48: The Trinity Formula

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem

This chapter is the terminal knot of Marx's Capital Volume 3, and it is written as a kind of intellectual autopsy. Its object of study is not capital, land, or labour as such, but a formula — a piece of ordinary economic common sense — and the way that formula systematically inverts the causal order of the capitalist production process. The formula is the one every economic agent of a capitalist society knows by heart: capital yields profit, land yields rent, labour yields wages. Three sources, three revenues, three classes. Marx's task is to demonstrate that this apparently innocent triple identity is "a conflation of three things which is prima facie illegitimate," a "uniform and symmetrical incongruity," and — in the chapter's most memorable phrase — an "economic three-in-one" that "completes the mystification of the capitalist mode of production."

The central thesis, stated flatly, is this: the trinity formula is the perfected epistemological form of the capitalist mode of production — the form of appearance (Erscheinungsform) in which the social relations of production become unrecognizable to the very agents who carry them out, and in which the essence of value (objectified social labour) is replaced by three quasi-natural, quasi-personal powers (capital, earth, labour) that supposedly create the value of the annual product out of themselves. The chapter's thesis is thus simultaneously a thesis about the logic of value, a thesis about the historical specificity of social forms, and a thesis about the ideological function of economic appearances.

The Intellectual Objective

Marx's objective is threefold, and the chapter's structure reflects it:

  1. Categorial critique. He must show that each of the three terms commits a logical offence. Land is a use-value, rent is a portion of value; you cannot place them in a proportional relationship. Capital is a sum of value whose formula "capital–interest" asserts that value is unequal to itself — "4 = 5," "100 shillings = 110 shillings." Labour–wages is an "irrational expression," "as irrational as a yellow logarithm," because it identifies the price of a thing (labour) with what is in fact the price of a capacity (labour-power). This is the "dimensional analysis" of the chapter.

  2. Historical critique. He must show that capital, landed property, and wage-labour are not natural but historically specific social forms belonging to one economic formation of society — and that the trinity's crime is to fuse the material elements of every labour process (earth, produced means of production, purposive activity) with their capitalist social form (monopolized earth, capital, wage-labour), so that the social form appears as a material character "immanently ingrown," "eternally so, as it were."

  3. Critique of ideology. He must show why this fusion is not merely an error but a necessary illusion, generated by the surface of the capitalist mode of production itself, deepening as the system develops, and why it is useful to the dominant classes: "it preaches the natural necessity and perpetual justification of their sources of income and erects this into a dogma."

The Broader Theoretical Framework

The chapter is neither a standalone essay nor a mere appendix. It is the retrospective closure of Volume 3's entire movement. Volume 3 began with surplus-value metamorphosing into profit, explored the equalization of profit rates into average profit, transformed values into prices of production, subdivided profit into profit of enterprise and interest, and interpolated ground-rent and landed property as a further claim on surplus-value. Chapter 48 asks: once the whole itinerary has been traversed, what does the system look like from the standpoint of the agents inside it? The answer is the trinity formula. The chapter is thus the phenomenology of the completed system — the analysis of its surface as seen by its practitioners.

Its counterpart in Volume 1 is the section on the fetishism of commodities. But the trinity formula is a higher-order fetishism: whereas the commodity fetish consists in a social relation appearing as a property of a thing (or, later, of money), the trinity fetish consists in social relations appearing as three heterogeneous natural powers with their own autonomous revenues, each personified: "Monsieur le Capital and Madame la Terre." Marx states the continuity explicitly: all forms of society involving commodity production are subject to the original distortion, "but in the capitalist mode of production, where capital is the dominant category... this bewitched and distorted world develops much further." The trinity is the endpoint of that development — the fetishism of the system as a whole.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Editorial Frame: A Chapter Assembled from Fragments

Before a single argument is engaged, the reader must register something fundamental about the text's status. Footnote 48, appended by Engels, states: "The following three fragments were found at various points in the manuscript of Part Six." Later, footnote 49 marks the sentence at which "Chapter 48 begins in the manuscript." Two further editorial notes — "(Here the manuscript breaks off. – F. E.)" and "(Here a folio sheet of the manuscript is missing. – F. E.)" — punctuate the argument, while the chapter's final sentence breaks off mid-clause: "Only in the capitalist mode of production ..."

This matters for three reasons.

First, the chapter as published is a reconstruction. Engels took three prefatory fragments (numbered 1, 2, 3 in the text) that Marx had drafted for the manuscript of Part Six ("The Revenues and Their Sources") and placed them at the head of the chapter, then appended the main body that begins with the sentence about the capitalist production process as a historically specific form. The result is a chapter with a thematic spine but structural seams.

Second, the lacunae are substantive. Where the manuscript breaks off after "(2)" in fragment one, we have just been promised a two-part argument about why natural fertility conditions the product but not the value; only the first part arrives. Where the folio sheet is missing, we jump abruptly from the distribution of the annual value into the middle of an argument about differential rent. The reader is thus invited to reconstruct what is missing by inference from what surrounds it — and Marx's argument is robust enough for the exercise.

Third, the fragmentary character of the chapter is thematically apt. The trinity formula is itself a set of fragments — three disconnected pieces of economic common sense held together by nothing but the fact that the agents of production repeat them daily. Marx's analytical task is to show that what appears as a coherent "three-in-one" is in fact an assemblage of incommensurables; the text's own seams silently mirror its argument.

2.2 Fragment One, Part A: The Formula and Its Reduction to a Second Trinity

The chapter opens by throwing down the formula in full:

"Capital-profit (profit of enterprise plus interest), land-ground-rent, labour-wages, this trinity form holds in itself all the mysteries of the social production process."

Two things happen in this opening sentence. First, the formula is decomposed: "profit" is not a unity but a composite — profit of enterprise plus interest. This is not a minor refinement; it is the hinge on which the first, covert reduction of the chapter depends. Marx cites his earlier discussion ("See Chapter 23 above" — the chapter on "Interest and Profit of Enterprise") where he showed that interest presents itself as the specific and characteristic product of capital as such, whereas profit of enterprise appears, by contrast, as a wage independent of capital — the "wage of superintendence," the payment for the capitalist's own labour of managing and exploiting.

The consequence is devastating for the formula's apparent rationality. Because interest is the incarnation of capital-as-such, and profit of enterprise is a disguised wage, the first trinity can be rewritten in a purer — Marx says "fortunately" — form:

"Capital-interest, land-ground-rent, labour-wages, where profit, the form of surplus-value specifically characteristic to the capitalist mode of production, is fortunately set aside."

The word "fortunately" drips with irony, and the irony is precise. "Profitable" as a term still carries a memory of its origin: profit emerges from surplus-value, which emerges from the exploitation of labour. Under the heading "profit," the trinity's first term is still flecked with its actual history. Under "interest," that memory is erased entirely: interest-bearing capital is that form in which value appears to generate value out of itself, without labour, without production, without process — the "capital fetish" in its most estranged figure. The vulgar economist therefore prefers the second formula; it is "much more consistent than 'capital-profit,' since profit still retains a memory of its origin which in interest is not simply obliterated but actually placed in a form diametrically opposed to this origin." We will see this argument returned to, amplified and applied, near the chapter's close.

2.3 Fragment One, Part B: The Three "Sources" Are Not One Kind of Thing

Marx next performs what is effectively a categorial dissection. Each of the three supposed sources is interrogated as to what it actually is.

Capital. "Capital is not a thing, it is a definite social relation of production pertaining to a particular historical social formation, which simply takes the form of a thing and gives this thing a specific social character." The point needs to be handled carefully, because Marx is neither denying that capital exists as objects nor lapsing into nominalism. He is making a claim about what kind of entity capital is. There is a physical side — "the material and produced means of production" — and a social side — the specific relation of production. The trinity formula's error consists in identifying the two.

Marx's positive account is worth unpacking term by term: capital is "the means of production monopolized by a particular section of society, the products and conditions of activity of labour-power, which are rendered autonomous vis-à-vis this living labour-power and are personified in capital through this antithesis." Three ingredients are welded together here: (a) monopoly — a particular section of society has exclusive control; (b) autonomy/alienation — the products and conditions of labour face the living worker as an independent power, "masters and buyers of their producers"; (c) personification — through this antithesis, capital acquires a personal representative. The famous sentence that follows extends this beyond commodity fetishism: not only do the products become independent powers over the producers, "but the social powers and interconnecting form of this labour also confront them as properties of their product." Marx's warning — "at first sight a very mysterious form" — signposts that "at first sight" is doing work: the mystery is a real appearance, not a gratuitous obscurity.

Land. "And now to take land, inorganic nature as such, rudis indigeslaque moles in its primeval wilderness." The Latin citation from Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book I, 7) invokes the chaos before creation — the formless mass out of which the ordered cosmos was made. Land is not merely unproduced; it precedes production as its given arena. Against this primeval mass Marx lays the analytic axe:

"Value is labour. So surplus-value cannot be earth."

The argument unfolds in three steps. (1) The land's absolute fertility produces no value; it merely sets the ratio in which a given quantum of labour yields a given product, under the given natural conditions. (2) Relative fertility — differences in natural conditions — means the same amounts of labour and capital are expressed in different quantities of product, so that these products have different individual values (their per-unit labour content differs). (3) The equalization of these individual values into market values — the competitive formation of a single social standard — is the mechanism by which the superior fertility's advantage is transferred to the landlord rather than captured by the cultivator. This is the germ of the theory of differential rent, and Marx anchors it in a quotation from Ricardo's Principles (Pelican edition, p. 98): "the advantages of fertile over inferior lands are ... transferred from the cultivator, or consumer, to the landlord."

Note the analytic pressure this exerts on the trinity formula. Land can determine how much product a given quantum of labour-realized time yields, but it can never determine how much value the product bears. Land conditions use-value; value is a social determination. Yet the trinity formula asserts that land — a use-value — is a source of value, and specifically of the value-component called rent. This is the category error that will be repeated with devastating precision in fragment three.

Labour. "Lastly, as the third in the league, a mere spectre — labour, which is nothing but an abstraction and taken by itself cannot exist at all." The accusation here is subtler than it first appears. Marx concedes that labour can mean "the entire productive activity of man, through which his metabolic interchange with nature is mediated" (Stoffwechsel — the material metabolism with nature). But even in this sense, "it is only divested of any social form and specific character; even in its mere natural existence, independent of society, it is lifted right out of society altogether." The abstraction of "labour" in general — labour as such, present in all epochs, "externalization and confirmation of life" alike for the unsocial man and for man socialized in some form — is a Robinsonade: a thought-experiment false to every historical reality. The phrase "externalization and confirmation of life" (Entäußerung und Bewährung des Lebens) carries a deliberate philosophical charge: it is the Hegelian-Feuerbachian vocabulary of self-realization, here deployed with a corrosive irony because what the trinity formula calls "labour" is an abstraction stripped not merely of society but of the very flesh of any particular labouring body.

The three sources of the trinity, therefore, are of three radically heterogeneous kinds: capital is a social relation masquerading as a thing; land is a material thing without any relation to value at all; labour is a theoretical abstraction that no society has ever contained. Marx's metaphor for their mutual relation is exact: "Their mutual relationship is like that of lawyer's fees, beetroot and music."

2.4 Fragment Two: The Historicity of the Three Forms and the Foundational Category Mistake

Fragment two (starting "2") restates the formula in its historicized form:

"Capital-interest; landed property, private property in the earth, and indeed modern private property, corresponding to the capitalist mode of production — rent; wage-labour — wages of labour."

The point of the restatement is that the middle term has changed register. It is no longer "land" but "landed property" — not earth as physical substance but a specific, historical, legal-economic institution. And the chapter immediately flags what is at stake in this shift:

"Wage-labour and landed property, like capital, are historically specific social forms; one of labour, and the other of the monopolized earth, both in fact being forms corresponding to capital and belonging to the same economic formation of society."

This is the chapter's foundational proposition. The three terms are co-eval: they arise together, they presuppose one another, and they belong to one another. Their historical unity is the capitalist mode of production.

And yet — the first striking thing about the formula is that it does not respect this unity:

"The first striking thing about this formula is that alongside capital, this form of an element of production belonging to a specific mode of production, to a specific historical shape of the social production process, alongside an element of production amalgamated with and presented in a specific social form, we have ranked without further ado: the earth, on the one hand, labour on the other, two elements of the actual labour process, which are material elements of any process of production and have nothing to do with its social forms."

This is the categorial error in its clearest form: the formula places a social-historical determination (capital) side by side with two trans-historical material determinations (earth, labour) and declares them homogeneous. The two sides are not merely different — they are of different logical types. Capital belongs to the relations of production; earth and labour belong to the process of production in abstraction from relations. Placing them in a common formula is like placing a contract and a stone in a common equation.

Second striking thing: the causal grammar. The formula does not merely list; it asserts derivation. Each source "is related to its product as something extruded from it and produced by it." Capital–interest, earth–rent, labour–wages — the dash marks causation. Each term is a cause, each product an effect, each "fruits" or "the result" of its own wellspring. The three revenue-forms — interest, rent, wages — are all portions of value, and hence of price when money is the medium. And here lies the difficulty: how can the earth produce a portion of value?

2.5 Fragment Two, Continued: Why the Earth Cannot Create Value

"Capital-interest is certainly the most irrational formula for capital, but it is a formula for it." Marx grants the first term a certain minimal propriety: interest is at least of capital, however mystified its genesis. He does not grant the second term any such propriety:

"But how is the earth to have a value, how can it create a socially specific quantum of labour, and the particular portion of value of its own products that forms rent at that?"

The argument that follows is one of the sharpest passages of value-theoretical clarification in the whole of Capital. Marx grants the land everything as a use-value producer: "The earth, for example, is active as an agent of production in the production of a use-value, a material product, say wheat." But: "it has nothing to do with producing the value of the wheat." Why? Because value is defined as objectified social labour, "this labour being quite indifferent to the particular material in which it is expressed or to the particular use-value of this material."

Marx then anticipates an objection that a naive reader would raise — namely, that if land is more fertile we get more wheat, and doesn't more wheat mean more value? He answers carefully:

"It does not contradict this that (1) if other factors remain constant, whether wheat is cheap or dear depends on the earth's productivity. The productivity of agricultural labour is linked to natural conditions, and according to their productivity the same quantum of labour is expressed in more products or fewer, more or fewer use-values."

The distinction is between how much product a quantum of labour is embodied in and how much value that quantum of labour is. Natural productivity governs the first magnitude; it has no purchase on the second. To put it in Marx's formula: "The quantity of product that the value represents depends here on the earth's productivity; but this value is given, and is independent of this distribution."

There follows a summary principle that does a great deal of work in the chapter: "Value is expressed in use-value, and use-value is a condition for the creation of value." The relation is asymmetric: use-value is necessary for value to appear, but it is not a source of value. From this asymmetry Marx derives the illegitimacy of the trinity: "it is foolish to counterpose a use-value, the earth, on the one hand, and value on the other, and a particular portion of value at that."

The parenthetical "(2)" — the second half of the argument — is precisely what the manuscript loses. We are left with the promise of a second consideration cut off before we can hear it.

2.6 Fragment Three: Vulgar Economics and the Epistemology of Appearance

Fragment three (starting "3") opens with the chapter's most-quoted aphorism and its philosophical pivot:

"Vulgar economics actually does nothing more than interpret, systematize and turn into apologetics the notions of agents trapped within bourgeois relations of production. So it should not surprise us that precisely in the estranged form of appearance of economic relations ... that precisely here vulgar economics feels completely at home, these relationships appearing all the more self-evident to it, the more their inner connections remain hidden, even though they are comprehensible to the popular mind."

Underpinning this is the methodological principle that underpins all of Capital Volume 3:

"— and all science would be superfluous if the form of appearance of things directly coincided with their essence."

This is the chapter's philosophical manifesto. The distinction between appearance (Erscheinung) and essence (Wesen) is not a distinction between false opinion and truth in the ordinary sense. It is a distinction between two levels of the same reality, where the second level is systematically generated by the first and cannot be abolished by the mere observational acumen of the agents within the first. The trinity formula is not a mistake a competent economist could simply correct; it is the necessary surface of a system whose depth only scientific analysis can reconstruct. And crucially, the surface's growing self-evidence is proportional to its growing distance from the depth: the more the inner connections are hidden, the more obvious the appearance seems.

2.7 Fragment Three, Continued: The Three Absurdities Dissected

Marx now walks through the three terms of the trinity and shows that each is absurd — and that its absurdity is precisely its apologetic utility. The trinity is "a conflation of three things which is prima facie illegitimate."

(i) Land–rent: the incommensurable pair. "First we have the use-value land, which has no value, and the exchange-value rent; here, then, a social relation, conceived as a thing, is placed in a relationship of proportion with nature; i.e. two incommensurable magnitudes are supposed to have a proportionate ratio." Two moves are being combined: the thingification of a social relation (rent, which is a magnitude of value, is treated as if it were a property of the earth) and the proportionalization of the incommensurable (a physical fact and a social magnitude are declared to stand in a determinate ratio). This is the sharpest expression of the categorial error latent in the whole chapter.

(ii) Capital–interest: the value that exceeds itself. "If capital is conceived as a certain sum of value with its independent expression in money, it is prima facie nonsense that a value should have more value than it is worth." The formula's logic is a formal contradiction — Marx gives it arithmetical dress: "the feeling that 4 is not 5 and hence 100 shillings cannot possibly be 110 shillings." But this is not the trivial observation that investing money can yield a return; it is the more radical observation that in the form "capital–interest," all mediation has disappeared. There is no labour, no time, no process — only a value that begets more value, "value-creating value." Marx notes that this very absence of mediation is what makes the formula "the most general formula" and — for the same reason — "absurd and inexplicable in its own terms."

The vulgar economist's preference for it is therefore no accident: "With its occult quality of a value that is to be unequal to itself," capital–interest is preferred "to the formula capital–profit, as here we already get somewhat nearer to the actual capital-relation." The occultism is the selling point.

But — and this is a fine dialectical touch — the vulgar economist cannot even rest in this occultism. "Disturbed by the feeling that 4 is not 5," he flees from capital as value to the material substance of capital — machinery, raw material, etc. — "Instead of the incomprehensible first relationship in which 4 = 5, to construct this time a completely incommensurable relationship between a use-value, a thing, on the one hand, and a specific social relation of production, surplus-value, on the other; as in the case of landed property." The economist's flight from one absurdity only leads him into a structurally identical absurdity on the other side of the ledger. He swaps an internal contradiction of value for an incommensurability between value and matter — and mistakes the swap for progress. "As soon as this incommensurability is attained, everything becomes clear to the vulgar economist, and he feels no need for any further reflection. For he has precisely reached what is 'rational' to the bourgeois mind."

(iii) Labour–wages: the "yellow logarithm." "Finally, labour–wages, the price of labour, is an expression ... which prima facie contradicts the concept of value and equally therefore that of price, this being in general only a specific expression of value; and 'price of labour' is just as irrational as a yellow logarithm." The comparison with a yellow logarithm is precisely calibrated: a logarithm can be computed, manipulated, used; what it cannot be is coloured. To speak of a coloured logarithm is not to say something false; it is to combine two categories — number and colour — that admit no common predicate. So with the price of labour: prices are of things; labour is not a thing; what is sold is labour-power. The very grammar of "wage" involves a category conflation.

The vulgar economist, however, is "completely satisfied here, since he has now reached the profound insight of the bourgeois that he pays money for labour, and the very contradiction between this formula and the concept of value relieves him from the obligation of understanding the latter." This is one of the most cutting sentences in the chapter. The contradiction that ought to trigger analysis is instead the licence to stop analysing: precisely because the formula "price of labour" cannot be squared with the theory of value, the vulgar economist treats the theory of value as the disposable term. The thought-stopping function of the absurdity is not a defect of the vulgar economist's method; it is his method.

At this point the third fragment ends, and the main body of Chapter 48 begins.

2.8 The Main Body Begins: The Capitalist Production Process as Historically Specific Social Form

The chapter's central text commences with a definitional statement of what the capitalist production process is:

"We have seen how the capitalist process of production is a historically specific form of the social production process in general. This last is both a production process of the material conditions of existence for human life, and a process, proceeding in specific economic and historical relations of production, that produces and reproduces these relations of production themselves, and with them the bearers of this process, their material conditions of existence, and their mutual relationships, i.e. the specific economic form of their society."

The definition contains a double-barrelled claim that is indispensable for everything that follows. (1) Production has a material side: it produces the means of life. (2) Production has a social side: it reproduces the relations of production and their bearers (the classes and their functionaries). These two sides are analytically distinct — and their fusion is precisely the mirage of the trinity formula. But they are also really inseparable in any actual society: "For the totality of these relationships which the bearers of this production have towards nature and one another, the relationships in which they produce, is precisely society, viewed according to its economic structure."

Marx then makes his crucial methodological point about circular causality:

"Those conditions, like these social relations, are on the one hand the presuppositions of the capitalist production process, on the other its results and creations; they are both produced by it and reproduced by it."

This is the famous Voraussetzung/Resultat structure: the material and social conditions of capitalist production are simultaneously the premises from which it starts and the outcome it produces. This is exactly why the trinity formula is so hard to see through: the conditions seem given in advance — capital appears as a pre-existing stock of means of production; wage-labour as a pre-existing condition of the workers; landed property as a pre-existing feature of the earth. But each of these "givens" is in fact a product of the same process it seems to precede. The trinity formula reads off the premise side and suppresses the result side — turning what is historically produced into what is naturally given.

The section closes with the capitalist as "personified capital": "the capitalist is simply personified capital, functioning in the production process simply as the bearer of capital." The personification theme — which will culminate in "Monsieur le Capital" — is here introduced. Capital is a social relation; the capitalist is the person who carries it.

2.9 Surplus Labour, Forced Labour, and the Civilizing Vocation of Capital

Marx now states the fundamental operation of capitalist production:

"Capital ... pumps out a certain specific quantum of surplus labour from the direct producers or workers, surplus labour that it receives without an equivalent and which by its very nature always remains forced labour, however much it might appear as the result of free contractual agreement."

Two things to note. First, "pumps out" (auspumpt) is a deliberately mechanical, extractive metaphor — the same figure that will return at the chapter's end as "a perpetual pumping machine for surplus labour." Second, the phrase "however much it might appear as the result of free contractual agreement" is the moment of demystification. The contract is free in form and forced in substance; the appearance of freedom is itself a part of the mechanism.

Marx then embeds the concept of surplus labour in a trans-historical framework, refusing the temptation to treat it as a capitalist peculiarity:

"Surplus labour in some form must always remain, as labour beyond the extent of given needs. It is just that in the capitalist, as in the slave system, etc., it has an antagonistic form and its obverse side is pure idleness on the part of one section of society."

The class-antagonistic form is historically specific; the existence of surplus labour is not. It is required (a) "as insurance against accidents" and (b) "for the progressive extension of the reproduction process that is needed to keep pace with the development of needs and the progress of population."

This trans-historical anchor permits what is perhaps the chapter's most surprising passage: a positive assessment of capital's historical mission.

"It is one of the civilizing aspects of capital that it extorts this surplus labour in a manner and in conditions that are more advantageous to social relations and to the creation of elements for a new and higher formation than was the case under the earlier forms of slavery, serfdom, etc."

The sentence contains, in embryo, the dialectic on which the trinity formula's critique ultimately depends. Capital is extractive and exploitative, but it is civilizing in a determinate sense: it develops productive forces, generalizes needs, and creates the material preconditions of a higher society. And Marx immediately draws the consequence:

"Thus on the one hand it leads towards a stage at which compulsion and the monopolization of social development ... by one section of society at the expense of another disappears; on the other hand it creates the material means and the nucleus for relations that permit this surplus labour to be combined, in a higher form of society, with a greater reduction of the overall time devoted to material labour."

2.10 The Arithmetic of Exploitation and the Realm of Freedom

The civlizing-aspect argument is made rigorous with a piece of arithmetic:

"For, according to the development of labour productivity, surplus labour can be great when the total working day is short and relatively small when the total working day is long. If the necessary labour-time is 3 hours and surplus labour also 3 hours, the total working day is 6 hours and the rate of surplus labour 100 per cent. If the necessary labour is 9 hours and the surplus labour 3 hours, the total working day is 12 hours and the rate of surplus labour only 33⅓ per cent."

The example establishes that the rate of surplus-labour is a ratio, not an absolute magnitude. The same three hours of surplus labour, in a system with three hours of necessary labour, yields a 100% rate (3/3); in a system with nine hours of necessary labour, the same three surplus hours yield only 33⅓% (3/9). Consequently, the length of the working day relative to necessary labour is determined by productivity, not by the "extractiveness" of the system as such. And:

"It then depends on the productivity of labour how much use-value is produced in a given time, and also therefore in a given surplus labour-time. The real wealth of society and the possibility of a constant expansion of its reproduction process does not depend on the length of surplus labour but rather on its productivity and on the more or less plentiful conditions of production in which it is performed."

What is at stake in this argument for the trinity formula? Everything. The formula's implicit story is that surplus (interest, rent) is the product of capital and land — natural, inevitable, permanent. Marx's argument demystifies: surplus labour is the permanent and inevitable thing (given need and the extension of reproduction); its quantum depends on productivity; and the form under which it is appropriated — the split into profits and rents — is historical. By shifting the question from the magnitude of surplus labour to its productivity, Marx lays the ground for a non-moralistic normative claim: not that the system should stop extracting surplus, but that the realm of freedom lies beyond the sphere of necessity and becomes possible once the development of productive forces allows the reduction of the working day.

This is the celebrated passage on the two realms. It deserves careful unpacking because its placement here — in the middle of the chapter, at the hinge between the analysis of surplus labour and the analysis of distribution — is intentional, not decorative.

"The realm of freedom really begins only where labour determined by necessity and external expediency ends; it lies by its very nature beyond the sphere of material production proper. Just as the savage must wrestle with nature to satisfy his needs, to maintain and reproduce his life, so must civilized man, and he must do so in all forms of society and under all possible modes of production. This realm of natural necessity expands with his development, because his needs do too; but the productive forces to satisfy these expand at the same time."

The argument is naturalistic (all societies wrestle with nature) and dynamic (needs expand, forces expand). The realm of necessity is not abolished by history; it is enlarged by need and opened up by productivity. Freedom within necessity is then defined:

"Freedom, in this sphere, can consist only in this, that socialized man, the associated producers, govern the human metabolism with nature in a rational way, bringing it under their collective control instead of being dominated by it as a blind power; accomplishing it with the least expenditure of energy and in conditions most worthy and appropriate for their human nature."

And freedom beyond necessity:

"But this always remains a realm of necessity. The true realm of freedom, the development of human powers as an end in itself, begins beyond it, though it can only flourish with this realm of necessity as its basis. The reduction of the working day is the basic prerequisite."

The structure of these paragraphs dramatizes what the trinity formula occludes. The formula pictures capital, land, and labour as three autonomous sources of wealth, with the labour term supplying only the worker's subsistence-wage. Marx's picture is opposite: material production is a necessary base; its historical development in capitalism is what makes a genuine freedom possible; and the pivot from one to the other is not a moral conversion but a time question — the shortening of the working day. The trinity formula, by presenting the wage as the natural product of the labour-source, erases the whole question of the surplus-labour-time produced beyond the wage — the very surplus whose duration, rate, and appropriation are at stake.

2.11 The Skeleton of Distribution: Profit, Rent, Wages

Marx now turns to the distributive mechanism that concretely produces the trinity's three revenue-forms out of the already-produced surplus-value:

"In capitalist society, this surplus-value or surplus product is divided among the capitalists as dividends in proportion to the quota of social capital that belongs to each. (If we ignore accidental fluctuations in the distribution and consider simply the law governing them, their regulating limits.)"

The parenthetical is methodologically important. Marx is not describing the actual scatter of profits (which are subject to accident, competition, conjunctural luck) but the law of distribution — what he will later call the "ideal average." The "regulating limits" are the gravitational center around which the accidental movements oscillate.

Next: surplus-value "appears as the average profit that accrues to capital, an average profit that is divided again into profit of enterprise and interest and can accrue under these two categories to different sorts of capitalist." Distribution among capitals is presupposed (the theory of the average rate of profit from Parts II–III); sub-division into profit of enterprise and interest is presupposed (Chapter 23).

Then a barrier is erected:

"This appropriation and distribution of surplus-value or surplus product by capital, however, meets with a barrier in landed property. Just as the functioning capitalist pumps out surplus labour from the worker, and thus surplus-value and surplus product in the form of profit, so the landowner pumps out a part of this surplus-value or surplus profit in turn from the capitalist in the form of rent, according to the laws developed earlier."

Note the successive pumping structure: the capitalist pumps surplus labour from the worker; the landowner pumps part of surplus-value from the capitalist. Marx then makes terminological precision a condition of understanding:

"If we speak here therefore of profit as the share of surplus-value accruing to capital, what we mean is an average profit (equal to profit of enterprise plus interest) that is already less than the total profit by the deduction of rent; the deduction of rent is presupposed."

This is a lovely piece of internal precision. In the trinity formula, "profit" and "rent" seem co-original — two fruits from two trees. In Marx's analysis, rent is a deduction from what capital has already extracted; profit is a residue after that deduction. The two revenues are not parallel; they are sequential. The trinity's parallelism is an illusion produced by the simultaneity of their appearance on the surface.

The class-mapping follows:

"Capital-profit (profit of enterprise plus interest) and ground-rent are thus nothing but particular components of the surplus-value; categories in which this surplus-value is distinguished according to whether it accrues to capital or landed property; designations which in no way affect its essence. Added together, they form the total social surplus-value."

Then the division of roles is drawn sharply:

  • Capital: "directly pumps from the workers the surplus labour that is expressed in surplus-value and surplus product. It can be considered in this sense as the producer of surplus-value." (Note the careful phrasing: "in this sense." Marx does not grant capital substantive productive power; he grants it the function of pumping out surplus-value — the sense in which it can be considered a producer.)
  • Landed property: "has nothing to do with the actual production process. Its role is limited to transferring a part of the surplus-value produced from capital's pocket into its own." Yet even this parasitism has a real social condition: "not only by the pressure that he exerts on capital and not simply by the fact that large landed property is a premise and condition of capitalist production, but particularly by the way that he appears as the personification of one of the most essential conditions of production." The landowner, whatever his uselessness, is personified earth — the bearer of a condition without which nothing could be produced.
  • The worker: "as owner and seller of his personal labour-power, receives under the name of wages a part of the product; in this there is expressed the portion of his labour that we call necessary labour, i.e. labour necessary for the maintenance and reproduction of this labour-power, whether the conditions of this maintenance and reproduction are poorer or richer, more favourable or less."

The section ends with a passage that captures the surface appearance at its most beguiling — one that must be quoted in full:

"Disparate as these relations may now appear, they have one thing in common: capital yields the capitalist profit, year in year out: land yields the landowner ground-rent; and labour-power — under normal conditions, and as long as it remains a usable labour-power — yields the worker wages. These three components of the total value annually produced ... can be consumed by their respective owners each year, and the sources of their reproduction will not run dry. (We leave accumulation aside here at first.) They appear as fruits of a perennial tree for annual consumption, or rather fruits of three trees; they constitute the annual incomes of three classes ... revenues distributed by the functioning capitalist, as the person who directly pumps out surplus labour and makes use of labour in general."

The "perennial tree" image is exquisitely chosen. It captures the appearance of natural, self-renewing productivity: the tree yields fruit, the fruit is consumed, the tree yields again, without visible input or history. But Marx has already told us, three pages earlier, that this "perennial" yield depends entirely on the continuous pumping of surplus labour — an operation wholly invisible to the trinity's tree-metaphor.

The chapter then names the three "sources" in their trinitarian form and shows them to be the personified media of exploitation:

"Capital to the capitalist, land to the landowner and labour-power to the worker, or rather his labour itself — since he sells labour-power only in its actual externalization, and the price of labour-power, as already shown, is necessarily expressed on the basis of the capitalist mode of production as the price of labour — these appear as the three respective sources of their specific revenues: profit, ground-rent and wages."

And they actually are so — but only in a very specific, non-trinitarian sense:

"And they actually are so in the sense that capital for the capitalist is a perpetual pumping machine for surplus labour, land for the landowner a permanent magnet for attracting a part of the surplus-value pumped out by capital and finally labour the constantly self-renewing condition and means for the worker to obtain a part of the value he has produced..."

The three "sources," then, are not creative. They are, respectively, a machine, a magnet, and a condition. Capital does not create profit; it draws it out of labour. Land does not create rent; it draws off a portion of what capital has drawn. Labour does not create the worker's wage as some kind of fruit; it is the mere condition under which the worker can reclaim a portion of what he has himself produced. The nomenclature is fateful: the trinity's causal language ("source," "cause," "produces") is being systematically dismantled and replaced by a mechanical language of pumping, attracting, and conditioned recovery.

2.12 The Subjective Illusion: "Sources of Revenue" Become "Real Sources"

The chapter now makes its crucial epistemological distinction. The three do function as sources of revenue in a legitimate sense:

"They are also sources of revenue in the sense that capital fixes one portion of the value of a year's labour and hence of its product in the form of profit, landed property fixes another part in the form of rent and wage-labour a third portion in the form of wages, and that it is precisely by this transformation that these portions are converted into the revenues of the capitalist, the landowner and the worker, without creating the substance itself that is transformed into these various categories. The distribution rather presupposes this substance as already present, i.e. the total value of the annual product, which is nothing more than objectified social labour."

Here is the philosophical nucleus of the chapter. There are two senses of "source of revenue," and the trinity formula conflates them:

  1. Legitimate sense: the existing value is channeled into profit, rent, and wages. Distribution operates upon an already-given quantum of objectified social labour. The three classes are distributive claims representing portions of this quantum.
  2. Illegitimate sense: the existing value originates from the three sources. Distribution is mistaken for creation. The trinity formula takes the distribution of the value to be its genesis.

The second sense is the formula proper, and Marx identifies it as the illusion of the agents:

"But it is not in this form that the matter presents itself to the agents of production, the bearers of the various functions of the production process, but rather in a distorted form. Why this happens we shall see in the further course of our analysis. Capital, landed property and labour appear to those agents of production as three separate and independent sources, and it appears that from these there arise three different components of the annually produced value (and hence of the product in which this exists); from these sources, therefore, there arise not only the different forms of this value as revenues which accrue to particular factors of the social production process, but this value itself arises, and with it the substance of these forms of revenue."

The clause "Why this happens we shall see in the further course of our analysis" is the promise that the second half of the chapter fulfils — the account of how, step by step, the inner connection is lost. At this point we reach the editorial lacuna.

2.13 The Missing Folio and the Social Character of Differential Rent

"(Here a folio sheet of the manuscript is missing. – F. E.)"

When the text resumes, we are in the middle of a discussion of differential rent — evidently, the missing sheet bridged from the general distribution of surplus-value to the rent analysis of Part Six. The surviving argument concerns the heterogeneous grounds of differential rent:

  • On one side: "Differential rent is bound up with the relative fertility of different land, i.e. with properties that arise from the land as such. But in as much as it depends firstly on the differing individual values of the products of different types of land, it is simply the characteristic already mentioned."
  • On the other side: "in as much as it depends, secondly, on the governing general market price, which is different from these individual values, this is a social law brought about by competition which has nothing to do either with the land or with the various degrees of its fertility."

This is the culminating refutation of the land–rent trinity term, and its structure repays attention. Differential rent is the quantitative outcome of a social mechanism: natural fertility produces differing individual labour-times; competition produces a single market price; the difference is captured by the landlord. Nature enters only at the level of individual differences in use-value per unit labour — it determines the raw material of the rent phenomenon, so to speak. The law that converts those differences into a rent paid to the landowner is a law of the market, a social law. The trinity formula takes the natural half of this dual determination and treats it as the whole.

2.14 Labour–Wages: The Rationality That Isn't

Marx next examines the third term. "It might appear as if at least in 'labour–wages' a rational relationship was expressed." After all, labour is the substance of value; wages are a portion of value; the connection seems at first glance sound. Marx denies it:

"But this is just as little the case as with 'land-ground-rent'. In as much as labour is value-forming and is expressed in the value of commodities, it has nothing to do with the distribution of this value among the different categories. And as far as its specific social character as wage-labour goes, it is not this that is value-forming."

The argument moves by a two-way exclusion. Take labour as value-forming: labour-as-value-forming has nothing to do with distribution. Take labour as wage-labour: the social form of wage-labour is precisely not what forms value. The two characterizations, value-former and wage-labour, are mutually exclusive in the relevant respect. Hence "labour–wages" cannot capture the real relation, however satisfying it may feel.

Marx then repeats his basic point: wages are "an irrational expression for the value or price of labour-power." And crucially: "the particular social conditions in which this labour-power is sold have no bearing on labour as a general agent of production." Then:

"Labour is objectified also in that value component of the commodity that forms the price of labour-power, as wages; it creates this portion just as much as it does the other portions of the product; but it is objectified in this portion only in precisely the same way as in the portions that form rent or profit."

The last sentence is quietly devastating. Labour is objectified in all three portions: in wages, in profit, in rent. Labour is the substance of the whole value of the product. But this is not a special relationship between labour and wages: it is a universal relationship between labour and all portions of value. To single out labour–wages as a "rational relationship" while treating profit and rent as fruits of other sources is to mistake a universal for a particular. "When we have labour as value-forming in mind, we are not considering it in its concrete form as a condition of production, but rather in a social characteristic that is different from that of wage-labour."

2.15 "Capital–Profit" Is Incorrect; the Entire Undivided Surplus-Value

The parallel argument is then made for capital:

"Even the expression 'capital-profit' is incorrect here. If capital is conceived in the only connection in which it produces surplus-value, i.e. in its relationship to labour, in which it extorts surplus labour by the compulsion it exerts on labour-power, i.e. on the worker, then this surplus-value comprises not only profit (profit of enterprise plus interest), but also rent, i.e. the entire and undivided surplus-value."

The trinity formula's "capital–profit" line is thus doubly wrong. It treats capital as a source of only profit, whereas, viewed in its actual relation to labour, capital is the source of the entire surplus-value including rent. And the moment the formula is transformed into "capital–interest," "the context disappears even more" — the entire relation to labour and to the extraction of surplus-value is effaced.

The formula in its purity, then, is worse than the formula in its loose form; the more coherent the trinity is as exposition, the more completely it effaces its own substance. This is the second time the chapter has made this dialectical point about "consistency."

2.16 The Uniform and Symmetrical Incongruity; the Naturalization of Social Forms

Marx now adds two further structural observations, then arrives at the chapter's great argument about naturalization.

Second observation: The three revenues (interest, rent, wages) "all belong to the same sphere, that of value." That is, they share a common measure. But this common sphere is covertly cancelled: "this is cancelled out (this relationship not only between incommensurable magnitudes, but also between quite heterogeneous, unconnected and incomparable things) by the fact that capital, like the earth and labour, is considered simply from the standpoint of its material substance, i.e. simply as produced means of production, in which connection abstraction is made both from capital as a relation to the worker and from capital as value." In other words: the revenues are stipulated to be commensurable (they are all portions of value), while the sources are stipulated to be material (produced means of production, earth, labour). The formula requires homogeneity at the bottom and heterogeneity at the top, or vice versa, depending on how you look — and the resulting structure is unstable.

Third observation: "In this sense, therefore, the formula capital-interest (profit), earth-rent, labour-wages presents a uniform and symmetrical incongruity." The phrase "uniform and symmetrical incongruity" is deliberately paradoxical. Each of the three terms is internally incongruous — land–rent pairs a use-value with a value; capital–interest pairs a value with a value-greater-than-itself; labour–wages pairs a non-thing with a price. And the three incongruities are symmetrical: they are congruent in their incongruity. The formula achieves a kind of false consistency by repeating the same category error three times.

The naturalist argument then unfolds. Marx begins from the inversion of priority:

"In fact, since it is not that wage-labour appears as a socially specific form of labour, but rather that all labour appears as wage-labour by nature (presenting itself like this to those trapped within the capitalist relations of production), the determinate and specific social forms which the objective conditions of labour — the produced means of production and the earth — assume vis-a-vis wage-labour (as they in turn presuppose wage-labour) coincide directly with the material existence of these conditions of labour, or with the shape that they generally possess in the actual labour process, independent of any historically specific social form, even independent of any social form of this whatsoever."

Unpacking this: because wage-labour is (falsely) treated as the natural state of all labour, the historically specific forms of capital (produced means of production monopolized by a class) and landed property (earth monopolized by a class) are fused with the material existence of the means of production and the earth. Their social form — "alienated from labour, objectified in relation to it and accordingly transformed" — is treated as identical with their material existence.

"These means of production are in and for themselves, by nature, capital; capital is nothing but a mere 'economic name' for those means of production; and similarly the earth is in and for itself, by nature, the earth as monopolized by a certain number of landed proprietors."

The phrase "a mere 'economic name'" is a jab at the apologetic cliché of bourgeois political economy (that "capital" is simply a name for produced means of production). Marx's point is that this nominalist reduction already contains the trinity's naturalizing move: if capital is merely a name for means of production, then means of production are capital by nature; and if earth-as-monopolized is merely a name for earth, then earth is landed property by nature.

Then the celebrated image of the anthropomorphized land:

"Just as the products become an independent power vis-a-vis the producers in capital and in the capitalist — who in actual fact is nothing but personified capital — so land is personified in the landowner, he is the land similarly standing up on its hind legs and demanding its share, as an independent power, of the products produced with its aid; so that it is not the land that receives the portion of the product needed to replace and increase its productivity, but instead the landowner who receives a share of this product to be sold off and frittered away."

The image "standing up on its hind legs" is a call-back to the famous "table-turning" of Volume 1's fetishism section, where Marx describes a table under the spell of commodity fetishism as "not only standing on its head ... it stands on its head before all other commodities and evolves out of its wooden brain grotesque ideas." Here, the land itself rises like a being and demands its share. Two things are crucial: (1) the personification is not mere metaphorical flourish — Marx has just told us the landowner is the personification of earth, so the image is the argument; (2) Marx immediately notes the practical consequence: with the landowner in place of the land, the product that might have replenished the soil's productivity is instead received by the owner "to be sold off and frittered away." The fetishism has a real, material, ecological cost.

The mutual presupposition is then asserted:

"It is clear that capital presupposes that labour is wage-labour. It is just as clear, however, that once you proceed from labour as wage-labour, so that the coincidence between wage-labour and labour in general appears self-evident, capital and the monopolized earth must also appear as the natural form of the conditions of labour vis-a-vis labour in general."

And the naturalization becomes a positively legal form of mystification:

"It now appears as the natural form of the means of labour that they should be capital, as a purely material character which arises from their function in the labour process in general."

Or as Marx restates it a page later:

"The formal autonomy these conditions of labour acquire vis-a-vis labour, the particular form of this autonomy they possess, is then a property inseparable from them as things, as material conditions of production, an immanently ingrown character that necessarily falls to them as elements of production. Their social character in the capitalist production process, determined by a particular historical epoch, is an innate material character natural to them, and eternally so, as it were, as elements of the production process."

"An immanently ingrown character" (ein solches eingewachsenes) and "eternally so, as it were" ("sozusagen ewig") — these capture precisely the mechanism of ideological naturalization. The historical form is not merely added to the thing; it appears to have grown into the thing from within, inseparable and eternal. This is the deepest sense in which the trinity formula is ideological: not that it tells a false story about history, but that it abolishes history* by presenting the product of a specific epoch as the nature of matter.

From this Marx derives the trinity's three revenue-forms as if they "grow out of the roles that the earth, the produced means of production and labour play in the simple labour process, considering this labour process simply as proceeding between man and nature and ignoring any historical specificity." And the capstone:

"It is only the same thing again in a different form to say that the product in which the wage-worker's labour presents itself for him as his proceeds, his revenue, is simply the wage, the portion of value (and hence of the social product measured by this value) that represents his wage. If wage-labour coincides with labour in general, wages must coincide with the product of labour, and the portion of value that wages represents must coincide with the value created by labour in general. But in this way the other portions of value, profit and rent, confront wages just as independently and must arise from sources of their own that are specifically distinct from labour and independent; they must arise from the collaborating elements of production to whose owners they accrue, i.e. profit from the means of production, the material elements of capital, and rent from the earth, or nature, as represented by the landowner. (Roscher.)"

The parenthetical "(Roscher.)" is the one name of a living economist cited in the main body of the chapter, and it is not a compliment. Wilhelm Roscher, of the German historical school, is named as an exemplar of the apologetic reduction — the economist who says: if wages are the reward of labour, then rent must be the reward of land and profit the reward of capital. Marx's point is that the premise ("wage-labour coincides with labour in general") entails the trinity's conclusion ("so profit and rent must arise from independent sources"). Once wage-labour has been naturalized as the form of all labour, the other two revenue-forms logically require their own natural sources. The trinity is thus a valid inference from a false premise — which is why it is so compelling.

2.17 The Transformation of Sources of Revenue into "Real Sources"

Marx now restates the whole transformation in one long, formally rigorous sentence, tracing the shift from legitimate "sources of revenue" (channels of distribution) into "real sources" (creative causes of the value). Landed property, capital, and wage-labour are "transformed from sources of revenue in the sense that capital attracts to the capitalist a portion of the surplus-value which it extracts from labour, in the form of profit; monopoly in the earth attracts another part to the landowner in the form of rent; and labour gives the worker the final portion of value that is still available in the form of the wage — from sources by virtue of which one part of the value is transformed into the form of profit, a second into the form of rent and a third into the form of wages — into real sources from which these portions of value themselves arise, together with the portions of the product related to them, in which they exist or against which they are convertible, the value of the product therefore itself arising from these as its ultimate source."

Footnote 50 attaches two of the great names of classical political economy to this doctrine. Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations (Pelican edition, p. 155): "Wages, profit and rent are the three original sources of all revenue, as well as of all exchangeable value." And Storch, Cours d'économie politique, I, p. 259: "Thus the causes of material production are at the same time the sources of the original revenues that it yields." Marx refers the reader to Volume 2, Chapter 19, 2, and to Theories of Surplus-Value, Part I, Chapter III, sections 4–7, for the detailed critique of Smith's doctrine — the "absurd dogma" that the value of the annual product resolves entirely into wages, profit, and rent, omitting the constant capital consumed in production.

Why does this matter? Because it shows that the trinity formula is not a vulgar-economist invention. It has its systematic origin in the greatest name of classical political economy. Smith, unlike the vulgar economists Marx will indict at the chapter's close, did recognize labour as the source of value; but in his analysis of distribution he allowed the value of the product to dissolve without remainder into the three revenues — thereby, despite himself, endorsing the very "three sources" doctrine whose apologetic form he would have rejected. This is Smith's failure, and the chapter's devastating indictment of classical political economy ("even its best representatives remained more or less trapped in the world of illusion their criticism had dissolved") takes its start from this specific error.

2.18 Fetishism's Deepening: From Commodity to Trinity

The chapter now begins its developmental narrative — the account of why the agents of production see the system in this distorted form. Marx explicitly connects the trinity's fetishism to the earlier fetishism of Volume 1:

"We have already shown in connection with the most simple categories of the capitalist mode of production and commodity production in general, in connection with commodities and money, the mystifying character that transforms the social relations for which the material elements of wealth serve as bearers in the course of production into properties of these things themselves (commodities), still more explicitly transforming the relation of production itself into a thing (money)."

The commodity form and money form already possess this mystificatory structure: social relations appear as properties of things. But:

"All forms of society are subject to this distortion, in so far as they involve commodity production and monetary circulation. In the capitalist mode of production, however, where capital is the dominant category and forms the specific relation of production, this bewitched and distorted world develops much further."

The developmental narrative proceeds in stages:

Stage 1: the immediate process of production. "If we view capital first in the immediate process of production, as a pumper-out of surplus labour, this relationship is still very simple; the real connection impresses itself on the bearers of this process, the capitalists, themselves, and is still in their consciousness. The fierce struggle over the limits of the working day shows this in a striking way." At this stage, the exploitation is legible: the capitalist knows he is extracting surplus labour, and the struggle over the working day presupposes precisely this knowledge on both sides.

Stage 2: relative surplus-value. "But even within this immediate sphere, the sphere of the immediate process between labour and capital, the matter does not rest at this simple stage. With the development of relative surplus-value in the specifically capitalist mode of production, involving the growth of the productive forces of social labour, these productive forces and the social context of labour appear in the immediate labour process as shifted from labour to capital. Capital thereby already becomes a very mystical being, since all the productive forces of social labour appear attributable to it, and not to labour as such, as a power springing forth from its own womb."

This is a decisive step. With relative surplus-value — productivity increases generated by cooperation, machinery, science, large-scale organization — the productive forces appear to belong to capital rather than to social labour. Capital acquires a "mystical being" within the immediate process itself, before any circulation has intervened.

Stage 3: the circulation process. "Then the circulation process intervenes, with all sections of capital, even agricultural, participating in it to the same degree. In this sphere, the conditions of the original production of value fall completely into the background. Even in the immediate production process, the capitalist is active also as commodity producer, as manager of commodity production." The value and surplus-value contained in commodities must be realized in circulation, and the appearance forms that "both the restoration of the values advanced in production, and particularly the surplus-value contained in the commodities, seem not just to be realized, only in circulation but actually to arise from it."

Two reinforcing circumstances are named: (a) profit on alienation — "which depends on cheating, cunning, expertise, talent and a thousand and one market conjunctures"; and (b) circulation time — "the fact that a second determining element intervenes here besides labour-time, i.e. the circulation time. Even though this functions simply as a negative limit on the formation of value and surplus-value, it gives the appearance of being just as positive a ground as labour itself and of involving a determination independent of labour that arises from the nature of capital."

Marx's point about circulation time is exactly parallel to his earlier point about land: a factor that functions as a limit (a "negative" determination) appears as a positive ground (a productive cause). This is one of the recurring formal patterns in the chapter: the illusion operates by converting limits into sources, conditions into producers.

Marx then makes a methodological note that will be echoed in the final paragraphs of the chapter: "In Volume 2, of course, we had to present this sphere of circulation only in relation to the determinations of form it produces, to demonstrate the further development of the form of capital that takes place in it. In actual fact, however, this sphere is the sphere of competition, which is subject to accident in each individual case; i.e. where the inner law that prevails through the accidents and governs them is visible only when these accidents are combined in large numbers, so that it remains invisible and incomprehensible to the individual agents of production themselves."

This is the epistemology of the market: the law governing market movements is statistical — it emerges only in aggregate — and therefore cannot be grasped by the individual agent operating from within the fluctuations. The invisibility of the law is not a defect of the agent's intellect; it is a structural feature of a system whose regulating law exists only as the outcome of the very accidents whose meaning the agent cannot read.

The section closes with a compact statement of the general process:

"Further, however, the actual production process, as the unity of the immediate production process and the process of circulation, produces new configurations in which the threads of the inner connection get more and more lost, the relations of production becoming independent of one another and the components of value ossifying into independent forms."

"Ossifying" (verknöchern) is a key term, and will recur ("the ossification of its form as against its substance, its essence"). The image of an originally fluid social process hardening into rigid, thing-like forms is the presiding metaphor of the chapter's developmental narrative.

2.19 Profit, Average Profit, Prices of Production: The Dissolution of the Origin

The next three transitions in the developmental narrative deepen the mystification by converting surplus-value first into profit, then into average profit, then into prices of production.

Profit. "The transformation of surplus-value into profit is, as we saw, just as much determined by the circulation process as by the process of production. Surplus-value in the form of profit is no longer related to the portion of capital laid out on labour, which is where it derives from, but rather to the total capital. The profit rate is governed by its own laws, which permit it to vary while the rate of surplus-value remains the same, and even require this variation. All this conceals the true nature of surplus-value more and more, concealing therefore the real mechanism of capital."

Note the crucial phrase "and even require this variation." The mystification is not superadded; the rate of profit must vary independently of the rate of surplus-value — for example, because of changes in the organic composition of capital — and this objective necessity is the mechanism of the concealment. Marx's account of ideology is, in this respect, materialist: the concealment is generated by the actual laws of the system, not by self-interested obfuscation.

Average profit and prices of production. "This happens still more with the transformation of profit into average profit and of values into prices of production, the governing averages of market price." Here "a complex social process intervenes, the equalization of capitals, which cuts the relative average prices of commodities loose from their values, and the average profits in the various spheres of production from the actual exploitation of labour by the particular capitals involved." The link between profit and exploitation, though not actually broken, is mediated through so many intervening terms that it becomes untraceable in the individual case. Average prices "not only seem to differ from their value ... but actually do differ"; average profit "differs from the surplus-value this capital has extracted from the workers employed by it."

And then a very fine point:

"The value of commodities appears directly only in the influence of the changing productivity of labour on the rise and fall of prices of production; on their movement, not on their final limits."

Value is still real — it still governs the movement of prices of production through changes in productivity. But it does not govern their level in any directly observable way; the level is set by the equalization of capitals. The connection to labour is therefore dynamic rather than static: invisible in the snapshot, visible only in the animation. And of course the trinity formula, being a snapshot, sees nothing.

Profit of enterprise and interest. "Profit now appears as determined only secondarily by the direct exploitation of labour, in so far as, given market prices that are seemingly independent of this exploitation, it permits the capitalist to realize a profit departing from the average. Normal average profit as such seems immanent in capital independently of exploitation; abnormal exploitation or even average exploitation under exceptionally favourable conditions seems only to determine divergences from average profit, and not this average profit itself."

Exploitation appears as a deviation from an already-given norm, rather than as the norm's source. The "secondary" character assigned to exploitation in the phenomenal world is the exact inverse of its primary character in the real world. Ideological inversion, here, is a consequence of the developed form of the system, not a falsification imposed on it from outside.

2.20 Interest-Bearing Capital: The Most Estranged Form

Marx now draws the decisive inference about the trinity's first term:

"The division of profit into profit of enterprise and interest (not to speak of the intervention of commercial profit and money-dealing profit, which are founded in the circulation sphere and seem to derive entirely from this, and not from the production process itself at all) completes the autonomization of the form of surplus-value, the ossification of its form as against its substance, its essence."

The sequence is cumulative: profit, average profit, prices of production, profit of enterprise, interest, commercial profit, money-dealing profit. Each step ossifies the form further. And the split into profit of enterprise and interest completes the process:

"One portion of profit, in contrast to the other, separates itself completely from the capital-relation as such and presents itself as deriving not from the function of exploiting wage-labour but rather from the wage-labour of the capitalist himself. As against this, interest then seems independent both of the wage-labour of the worker and of the capitalist's own labour; it seems to derive from capital as its own independent source."

Two mutually reinforcing autonomizations occur here. Profit of enterprise is transformed into a wage — compensation for the capitalist's managerial labour. Interest is transformed into a natural emanation of capital — the fruit of property as such. The two halves of profit thus acquire two separate "justifications," neither relating to the exploitation of labour.

The result is the transformation of capital into "value-creating value" in its purest form:

"If capital originally appeared on the surface of circulation as the capital fetish, value-creating value, so it now presents itself once again in the figure of interest-bearing capital as its most estranged and peculiar form."

And from this Marx draws the conclusion promised at the chapter's beginning:

"This is why the form 'capital-interest', as a third in the series to 'earth-rent' and 'labour-wages', is much more consistent than 'capital-profit', since profit still retains a memory of its origin which in interest is not simply obliterated but actually placed in a form diametrically opposed to this origin."

The dialectic here is precise and unexpected. Consistency with the trinity formula is a function of the completeness of the mystification. The purer the trinitarian abstraction, the more internally coherent the formula is — and the more completely it effaces the real relation. This is the "science would be superfluous" principle in its sharpest application: the better the surface hangs together, the more it distorts. A less consistent trinity would be more truthful. Ideological coherence is inversely related to scientific truth.

2.21 Landed Property and the Completion of the Alienation

The developmental narrative now adds its final element — landed property:

"Finally, besides capital as an independent source of surplus-value, there appears landed property, as a limit to the average profit which transfers a portion of the surplus-value to a class that neither works itself nor directly exploits workers, and cannot even, like interest-bearing capital, launch forth in edifying homilies about the risk and sacrifice in lending capital."

The landed class is singled out for a special irony: it is worse than the interest-bearing capitalist, who can at least pretend that lending capital involves risk and sacrifice. The landowner's claim is pure monopoly; no ideological fig-leaf of "risk" is available. And yet the trinity formula treats landed property's revenue as fully legitimate — because the earth appears as a natural force whose contribution must be rewarded.

"Since in this case one part of the surplus-value seems directly bound up not with social relations but rather with a natural element, the earth, the form of mutual alienation and ossification of the various portions of surplus-value is complete, the inner connection definitively torn asunder and its source completely buried, precisely through the assertion of their autonomy vis-a-vis each other by the various relations of production which are bound up with the different material elements of the production process."

"Definitively torn asunder and its source completely buried" — the developmental narrative has reached its terminus. With the naturalization of rent, the alienation of the revenue-forms from their real origin is complete. The three "sources" are now three autonomous things, each producing its own fruit.

2.22 The Trinity Completed: Monsieur le Capital and Madame la Terre

The chapter's rhetorical summit is now reached:

"Capital-profit (or better still capital-interest), land-ground-rent, labour-wages, this economic trinity as the connection between the components of value and wealth in general and its sources, completes the mystification of the capitalist mode of production, the reification of social relations, and the immediate coalescence of the material relations of production with their historical and social specificity: the bewitched, distorted and upside-down world haunted by Monsieur le Capital and Madame la Terre, who are at the same time social characters and mere things."

Every concept in this sentence is loaded. "Reification" (Versachlichung) names the transformation of social relations into properties of things. "Immediate coalescence of the material relations of production with their historical and social specificity" is the chapter's analytic formula for what the trinity does: it fuses the material and the historical into a single apparent fact. "Bewitched, distorted and upside-down" (verhexte, verkehrte, auf den Kopf gestellte) invokes the Witches' Sabbath imagery that runs throughout Capital, culminating here in the image of Monsieur le Capital and Madame la Terre as figures in a fantastical social ball.

The French names and the gendered pairing are not decorative. They signal a social comedy: the two protagonists of the capitalist mode of production, one male (capital, active, masculine, master) and one female (earth, passive, feminine, mother), stroll through the world as a couple, accepting the homage of the labour that supports them. And yet — this is the crucial qualifier — they are "at the same time social characters and mere things." Monsieur le Capital is a capitalist, but he is also merely the money in his pocket; Madame la Terre is a landowner, but she is also merely a piece of rocky ground. The personification is real, and the thingness is real, and the identity of the two — social character equals material thing — is precisely the illusion. The trinity is thus, in the most precise sense, a personification of things and a reification of persons, and Marx's phrase captures both sides simultaneously.

Note the theological resonance of "trinity" and "mystification" and "haunted." Marx's chosen vocabulary is not accidental. The trinity formula is the religion of everyday life (a phrase Marx uses a few lines later) — a folk theology of capital, land, and labour as three persons of one economic God, with their own respective revenues as sacrifices. The chapter's demonstration is that this theology is not a proto-scientific error or a merely inherited superstition; it is the actual appearance of a system, generated by the system itself, and believed by everyone within it.

2.23 Classical Economics and Its Trapped Consciousness; Vulgar Economics as Apologetics

Having diagnosed the mature consciousness, Marx now gives an historical critique of economic theory:

"It is the great merit of classical economics to have dissolved this false appearance and deception, this autonomization and ossification of the different social elements of wealth vis-a-vis one another, this personification of things and reification of the relations of production, this religion of everyday life, by reducing interest to a part of profit and rent to the surplus above the average profit, so that they both coincide in surplus-value; by presenting the circulation process as simply a metamorphosis of forms, and finally in the immediate process of production reducing the value and surplus-value of commodities to labour."

The three-fold merit of classical political economy corresponds, inversely, to the three-fold structure of the trinity: it dissolves the autonomization of revenues (interest and rent into surplus-value), the autonomization of the circulation process (circulation as mere metamorphosis of forms), and the autonomization of value (value and surplus-value reduced to labour). Classical economics, at its best, performed the analytic reduction that this chapter has reconstructed in theoretical form.

But:

"Yet even its best representatives remained more or less trapped in the world of illusion their criticism had dissolved, and nothing else is possible from the bourgeois standpoint; they all fell therefore more or less into inconsistencies, half-truths and unresolved contradictions."

The parenthetical argument — "nothing else is possible from the bourgeois standpoint" — is the chapter's central claim about the limit of bourgeois science. It is not a moral accusation; it is a structural claim. From the standpoint of a member of bourgeois society, the appearances are reality: the trinity formula is what is "lived." To reject it entirely would be to reject the form of the world in which one exists. So classical political economy could see through the trinity but not beyond it; it dissolved the illusion in one respect and fell back into it in another. Adam Smith is the exemplary case: he reduced value to labour in the abstract, and then resolved the same value back into the three revenues in the concrete analysis of distribution.

Then Marx turns to the modern heirs:

"It is also quite natural, on the other hand, that the actual agents of production themselves feel completely at home in these estranged and irrational forms of capital-interest, land-rent, labour-wages, for these are precisely the configurations of appearance in which they move, and with which they are daily involved. It is equally natural, therefore, that vulgar economics, which is nothing more than a didactic and more or less doctrinaire translation of the everyday notions of the actual agents of production, giving them a certain comprehensible arrangement, finds the natural basis of its fatuous self-importance established beyond all doubt precisely in this trinity, in which the entire inner connection is obliterated."

Vulgar economics is here defined with clinical precision: "a didactic and more or less doctrinaire translation of the everyday notions of the actual agents of production, giving them a certain comprehensible arrangement." Its object is not truth but arrangement — the systematic presentation of what the practical man already believes. Its "fatuous self-importance" (aufgeblasenen Wichtigtuerei) is the affect appropriate to a knowledge that cannot admit its own limitations.

Finally, the class dimension:

"This formula also corresponds to the self-interest of the dominant classes, since it preaches the natural necessity and perpetual justification of their sources of income and erects this into a dogma."

Note the word "dogma." The trinity formula is not merely an error of understanding but a creed — the doctrinal core of an apologetic project. Its function is justificatory: it presents the revenues of the dominant classes as naturally and necessarily produced by the natural sources owned by those classes. The critique of political economy thus reveals its social function in one final stroke.

2.24 The World Market, Competition, and the "Ideal Average"

The chapter's penultimate section draws an explicit boundary around the analysis:

"In presenting the reification of the relations of production and the autonomy they acquire vis-a-vis the agents of production, we shall not go into the form and manner in which these connections appear to them as overwhelming natural laws, governing them irrespective of their will, in the form that the world market and its conjunctures, the movement of market prices, the cycles of industry and trade and the alternation of prosperity and crisis prevails on them as blind necessity. This is because the actual movement of competition lies outside our plan, and we are only out to present the internal organization of the capitalist mode of production, its ideal average, as it were."

Two ideas are packed here. First, the phenomenological consequence of reification in the developed system is the experience of economic life as subjection to overwhelming natural laws — the market feels like weather: "blind necessity." The agents do not experience themselves as masters of their circumstances; they experience themselves as objects of forces beyond their control. And the trinity formula is the nomenclature of this experience: it names the "sources" (capital, land, labour) as if they were natural elements, and their "fruits" as if they were the products of natural processes.

Second, the methodological self-limitation: "the actual movement of competition lies outside our plan." Marx is explicitly declaring that he is analyzing the internal organization of capital — the "ideal average" (ideale Durchschnitt) — and not the concrete, contingent, oscillating movements of the competitive surface. The trinity formula is the general form of appearance of that internal organization, abstracted from the conjunctural static. This is why it can be analyzed in its "ideal average" even as its concrete movement is deferred.

2.25 The Historical Counterpoint: When Mystification Is Absent

The chapter's final moment is a historical counterpoint, in part recoverable and in part lost. Marx argues that in pre-capitalist formations, the mystification proper to the trinity formula is largely absent:

"In earlier forms of society, this economic mystification comes in principally in connection with money and interest-bearing capital. It is excluded by the very nature of the case, firstly, where production is predominantly for use-value, for the producers' own needs; secondly, where, as in Antiquity and the Middle Ages, slavery or serfdom forms the broad basis of social production."

The argument here is that of a two-fold exclusion. Where production is largely for use (not for value), the trinitarian appearance cannot arise: because value itself, and hence the trinity of value-sources, has no dominance. And where visible domination (slavery, serfdom) forms the base of production, the trinity cannot arise in its proper form either:

"In the latter case, the dominance of the conditions of production over the producers is concealed by the visible relations of domination and servitude, which appear as direct mainsprings of the production process."

The argument is subtle. In a slave or serf system, exploitation is open: the master directly compels the slave or serf. The social relation of domination is legible, and the extraction of surplus labour appears for what it is: compulsion, not a "revenue" arising from a "source." Paradoxically, the transparency of pre-capitalist domination prevents the mystification of the capitalist trinity from taking hold. In capitalism, by contrast, exploitation is mediated by free contracts, the wage-form, and market circulation — so the extraction becomes invisible, and the trinity formula steps in to give an account of the revenues without ever mentioning exploitation.

Marx adds two further pre-capitalist cases:

"In the primitive communities where an indigenous communism prevails, and even in the urban communities of Antiquity, it is the actual community and its conditions that presents itself as the basis of production, the reproduction of this community being production's final purpose. Even in the guild system of the Middle Ages, neither capital nor labour appear unrestrained; their connections are determined by the system of corporations and the relationships this involves, as well as by the corresponding ideas of professional obligation, craftsmanship, etc."

Three non-capitalist patterns are thus contrasted with the trinity formula: the communist community (production's end is the reproduction of the community, not value); the ancient commune (the community presents itself as the basis of production); and the medieval guild (capital and labour are constrained by institutional bonds and by the corresponding ideological forms — "professional obligation, craftsmanship"). In each case, the trinity formula cannot take shape because the prius is not value but the community, or the guild-bond, or the master's domination.

The chapter's final sentence breaks off:

"Only in the capitalist mode of production ..."

Engels's note: "(Here the manuscript breaks off.)" The reader is left to complete the thought. What Marx was drafting, given the trajectory of the preceding sentences, is a statement of the singularity of capitalist mystification: only in the capitalist mode of production is production for value universalized, domination concealed under contract, and the relations of production alienated into the trinitarian form. Only in capitalism can the whole population move within a "bewitched, distorted and upside-down world" that treats socially produced revenues as natural fruits.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

3.1 The Chapter's Central Lessons

1. The trinity is a categorial error that is also a historical necessity. Marx does not treat the trinity formula as a mistake, a sloppy analytic habit, or a deliberate deception — he treats it as the form of appearance generated by the capitalist mode of production itself. The three terms of the formula commit three structurally identical but materially distinct category errors (use-value/value, value/self-multiplying value, non-thing/price). These errors are not corrigible by better observation because they are generated by the real surface of the real system. The lesson is methodologically decisive for any critical social science: the appearance of a system is not a veil to be lifted but a product to be explained.

2. Naturalization is the trinity's operative move. The specific mechanism by which the trinity's illusions are produced is the coalescence of material relations of production with their historical and social specificity. Earth becomes "landed property by nature"; produced means of production become "capital by nature"; labour becomes "wage-labour by nature." The chapter gives a general name to this move — "an immanently ingrown character," "an innate material character natural to them, and eternally so" — and a general consequence: the abolition of history by nature.

3. Alienation deepens with development. Measured against the immediate process of exploitation, in which the capitalist knows he is extracting surplus labour (as the struggle over the working day proves), the developed system (relative surplus-value, circulation, average profit, prices of production, profit of enterprise, interest, rent) is more mystified, not less. The trinity formula is the terminus of this progressive deepening — the trinity's "consistency" (interest rather than profit; capitalized wealth without memory of exploitation) is a measure of its distance from truth.

4. Distribution is not production. The trinity's deepest inversion is to present the distribution of a value as the creation of that value. Marx's counter-thesis is exacting: distribution "presupposes this substance as already present, i.e. the total value of the annual product, which is nothing more than objectified social labour." This principle undercuts the entire family of apologetic doctrines (the "three sources" of Smith and Storch) that naturalize the distributive order by deriving value from the distributive categories.

5. Ideological coherence is inversely proportional to truth. "Capital–interest" is more consistent than "capital–profit" precisely because interest has completely forgotten its origin. The same holds at the level of the whole chapter: the trinity's "uniform and symmetrical incongruity" hangs together as a doctrine because it is untrue. Every increase in the formula's formal coherence corresponds to a loss of contact with the real relation. This principle reverses a common assumption that a good theory should be internally consistent — insisting instead that under conditions of fetishism, the better arranged is the more obfuscatory.

6. Ideology serves class interest without being reduced to conspiracy. The trinity is not an invention of the dominant classes; it is the ordinary consciousness of production agents, codified. But it "corresponds to the self-interest of the dominant classes," because its practical effect is to preach "the natural necessity and perpetual justification of their sources of income." Marx's account of ideology is materialist-dialectical: the illusion is a product of the system, and its utility for the dominant class is a real function, without requiring the class to have invented the illusion.

7. The "realm of freedom" is a material, not moral, question. The chapter's placement of the realm-of-freedom passage between surplus-labour and distribution is deliberate. Freedom is not a normative stance toward the trinity's injustice; it is a real possibility released by the development of the productive forces — and its basic prerequisite is the reduction of the working day. The trinity formula obscures this because it obscures the question of surplus-labour-time entirely — the very question that the "reduction of the working day" raises.

3.2 Broader Implications

For the theory of value. The trinity formula is the point at which a labour theory of value is structurally invisible from within capitalism. Every mediation between labour and revenue — profit, average profit, prices of production, profit of enterprise, interest, rent — removes the theory further from direct observability. Marx's claim is not that the theory is refuted by this distance but that it must be recovered against the surface. Science, in this sense, is the labor of "dissolving the false appearance."

For the critique of political economy. The chapter establishes a distinction between classical political economy (which dissolves the trinity analytically but cannot escape the bourgeois standpoint) and vulgar political economy (which "translates" the practical consciousness of the agents into "doctrine"). Classical political economy is honored for its achievements — dissolving rent into surplus, showing circulation to be metamorphosis, reducing value to labour — and indicted for its limits: even its "best representatives" fell back into "inconsistencies, half-truths and unresolved contradictions," because "nothing else is possible from the bourgeois standpoint."

For the theory of ideology. The chapter's account is a critique of everyday economic reason rather than a conspiracy theory. Ideology is structurally produced, systematically coherent, and useful without being invented. Its theological form — "the religion of everyday life" — marks its character as a lived creed, not a private opinion. Its function is to confer on distributive outcomes the aura of natural necessity, and hence to make social change appear as interference with nature rather than as historical transformation.

For the theory of history. The chapter's historical counterpoint is critical to its structure. Pre-capitalist formations are described as less mystified in the trinity's sense — even though they are more openly unfree. The argument is that freedom and mystification advance together under capitalism: the free contract conceals the extraction that overt domination would have made visible. This is one of the chapter's most provocative implications: transparency of power and unfreedom can coincide; formal freedom and structural opacity can coincide; and the growth of formal equality is compatible with a deepening of real subjection. The trinity formula is the ideological signature of this historical arrangement.

For method in the social sciences. The "ideal average" is a formal declaration of scope: the chapter presents the internal organization of the capitalist mode of production as a system, abstracting from the "actual movement of competition." The trinity formula is its surface form; competition is left outside the plan. This same move — isolating a system's structure from its conjunctural movement — is the chapter's own epistemology. Its implication is that the critique of the trinity is not a critique of market events but of market form: the trinity is what the capitalist mode of production looks like when it is working as its own structure dictates.

3.3 Conclusion: The Formula as Monument

Read as a whole, Chapter 48 builds a single, massive argument: the trinity formula is the monument of the capitalist mode of production — the form in which its practitioners contemplate their own world, and in which capital, land, and labour appear as three equal, independent, natural persons, each generating its own revenue out of a source peculiar to it. Every category the earlier volumes and parts of Capital had developed — value, surplus-value, profit, average profit, prices of production, profit of enterprise, interest, rent, circulation time, landed property — is mobilized here to show why the monument has this shape and what it conceals. And the monument's culmination is a vision: the bewitched world of the everyday, in which reified social relations walk the earth as persons and persons stand before them as things; capital and land, dressed as Monsieur and Madame, receive the homage of the labour that produces their revenue.

Marx's final gesture in the chapter is to leave this vision suspended — the manuscript breaks off mid-sentence — and yet the suspension is itself the argument. The critique of the trinity is not a completed transcript of the world beyond it. It is the diagnosis of a world whose own surface guarantees that the diagnosis will be unintelligible to those who live within it. Science, as Marx defines it in this chapter, is the labor of seeing what the system was constructed to hide — and the only guarantee of that labor's necessity is the trinity formula's continued self-evidence to those it rules.
