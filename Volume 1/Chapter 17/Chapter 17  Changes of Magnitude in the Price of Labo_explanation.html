1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 17, "Changes of Magnitude in the Price of Labour-Power and in Surplus-Value," is the analytic keystone of Marx's theory of surplus-value production in Capital, Volume 1. It is not a narrative or historical chapter but a work of rigorous, almost geometric comparative statics. Its central problem is deceptively simple: given that a working day produces value, and that this value is divided between the worker's wage (the value of labour-power) and the capitalist's surplus-value, how do changes in the underlying determinants of production redistribute that value between the two classes? Marx answers by constructing a formal combinatorial system around exactly three variables—the length of the working day (its extensive magnitude), the intensity of labour (its intensive magnitude), and the productivity of labour (its efficiency in yielding use-values). By holding two variables constant at a time and allowing the third to vary, then by examining simultaneous variations, he derives a complete taxonomy of capitalist exploitation dynamics.

The chapter's core thesis is that the relationship between the value of labour-power and surplus-value is governed by strict, law-like regularities that can be specified with mathematical precision—but only once Marx has disentangled surplus-value "as such" from its phenomenal forms (profit, rent). In the Introduction to the chapter Marx defines the value of labour-power as determined by the value of the means of subsistence habitually required by the average worker, treating the quantity of those means as a constant for any given epoch and society, so that only their value varies. He explicitly brackets two complicating factors: the cost of developing labour-power (which varies with the mode of production) and the natural diversity of labour-power between men and women, adults and children—factors that, as Engels' footnote 1 notes, also exclude the case treated on pp. 433–6 where wages are forced below the value of labour-power. He then imposes two foundational assumptions: (1) commodities are sold at their value, and (2) the price of labour-power may occasionally rise above its value but never sinks below it. These assumptions quarantine the analysis from market-price noise and from the violent history of wage depression, isolating the pure inner logic of exploitation.

The intellectual objective is threefold. First, Marx wants to demonstrate that the confrontation between capital and labour is not a matter of contractual accident or moral failing but a structural necessity: the zero-sum logic of a fixed working day (Section I) and the positive-sum logic of lengthened or intensified labour (Sections II–III) are both built into the capital relation itself. Second, he wants to expose the analytical defects of classical political economy—specifically Ricardo's failure to distinguish the rate of surplus-value (s/v) from the rate of profit (s/C), and his unexamined assumption that the working day and labour intensity are fixed. Third, and most importantly for the architecture of Capital, the chapter prepares the ground for the theory of accumulation and for Volume 3's analysis of the rate of profit by establishing once and for all that the laws of surplus-value are not the laws of profit.

Within the broader theoretical framework, Chapter 17 functions as the synthesis of Parts III and IV of Volume 1. Part III developed absolute surplus-value (lengthening the working day); Part IV developed relative surplus-value (raising productivity); Chapter 17 unifies them by showing how the three determinants interact in all possible combinations. It is the hinge upon which the entire subsequent argument of Capital turns: without these laws, neither the historical analysis of the working day (Chapter 10) nor the theory of accumulation (Part VII) nor the falling tendency of the profit rate (Volume 3) can be properly understood. The chapter's final pages—touching on the "universality of labour" and the conversion of the masses' lifetime into labour-time—also carry an unmistakable emancipatory horizon, connecting the analytic machinery to Marx's vision of a society beyond capitalist production.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Architectonic Setup: Assumptions, Variables, and the Analytical Frame

Marx opens by defining the value of labour-power: it "is determined by the value of the means of subsistence habitually required by the average worker." Crucially, the quantity of these means of subsistence is "given at any particular epoch in any particular society, and can therefore be treated as a constant magnitude." What varies is the value of this quantity—i.e., the socially necessary labour-time required to produce the subsistence basket. This distinction between a constant physical basket and a variable value of that basket is the foundational move that allows the subsequent analysis: productivity changes alter the value of the basket, not its size, and thus alter the value of labour-power without altering the worker's material standard of living in kind.

He then identifies two factors that enter into the determination of the value of labour-power but are excluded from the analysis. The first is the cost of developing that power, which "varies with the mode of production"—the historical and skill-formation costs embedded in producing a particular type of worker. The second is the natural diversity of labour-power, "the difference between the labour-power of men and women, children and adults." The utilization of these different sorts of labour-power is "in turn conditioned by the mode of production," and it "makes for great variations in the cost of reproducing the worker's family, and in the value of the labour-power of the adult male." Both are excluded. Engels' footnote 1 adds that the case considered earlier on pp. 433–6—where wages are depressed below the value of labour-power—is also excluded "of course."

On top of these exclusions Marx imposes two assumptions: "(1) that commodities are sold at their value, (2) that the price of labour-power occasionally rises above its value, but never sinks below it." This is a critical methodological choice. By assuming commodities sell at value, he neutralizes market-price deviations; by forbidding the price of labour-power from falling below its value, he brackets the systematic wage-depression that characterized early capitalism and primitive accumulation. The analysis that follows is therefore not a description of empirical wage levels but a specification of the inner laws that govern the capital–labour relation once these contingent distortions are set aside.

Given these assumptions, Marx restates the three circumstances that determine "the relative magnitudes of surplus-value and of price of labour-power": (1) the length of the working day, or the extensive magnitude of labour; (2) the normal intensity of labour, or its intensive magnitude, "whereby a given quantity of labour is expended in a given time"; and (3) the productivity of labour, "whereby the same quantity of labour yields, in a given time, a greater or a smaller quantity of the product, depending on the degree of development attained by the conditions of production." He notes the combinatorial richness: one factor can be constant while the other two vary; two can be constant while one varies; all three can vary. Moreover, when all three vary simultaneously, "the amount and direction of their respective variations may differ." He announces that only "the chief combinations alone are considered"—a signal of analytical economy, not incompleteness, since the method developed in the first three sections can handle any combination.

Section I: Law One — The Constant Working Day as a Constant Magnitude of Value

Section I holds the length of the working day and the intensity of labour constant and allows productivity to vary. Under these assumptions three laws govern the value of labour-power and the magnitude of surplus-value. The first law states: "a working day of a given length always creates the same amount of value, no matter how the productivity of labour, and, with it, the mass of the product and the price of each single commodity produced may vary." Marx illustrates: "If the value created by a working day of 12 hours is, say, 6 shillings, then, although the mass of the use-values produced varies with the productivity of labour, the value represented by 6 shillings will simply be spread over a greater or a less number of commodities." The underlying principle is the fundamental distinction from Chapter 1 between value and use-value: productivity changes the quantity of use-values produced but not the value embodied in them, because the total labour-time expended remains the same.

Section I: Law Two — The Inverse Variation of Labour-Power's Value and Surplus-Value

The second law declares: "the value of labour-power and surplus-value vary in opposite directions. A variation in the productivity of labour, its increase or diminution, causes the value of labour-power to move in the opposite direction, while surplus-value moves in the same direction." The mechanics are strictly arithmetical. The value created by a 12-hour day is a constant quantity, say 6 shillings, which "is the sum of the surplus-value plus the value of the labour-power." Because the worker replaces the value of labour-power with an equivalent, the 6 shillings is partitioned into two parts. "It is self-evident that if a constant quantity consists of two parts, neither of them can increase without the other diminishing." Marx gives a numerical demonstration: if v = 3s and s = 3s, then "the value of the labour-power cannot rise from 3 shillings to 4 without the surplus-value falling from 3 shillings to 2; and the surplus-value cannot rise from 3 shillings to 4 without the value of the labour-power falling from 3 shillings to 2." Hence under these conditions "no change can take place in the absolute magnitude either of the surplus-value or of the value of the labour-power without a simultaneous change in their relative magnitudes. It is impossible for them to rise or fall simultaneously."

Marx then tightens the causal chain: "the value of the labour-power cannot fall, and consequently surplus-value cannot rise, without a rise in the productivity of labour." The concrete mechanism is given: "the value of the labour-power cannot sink from 3 shillings to 2 unless an increase in the productivity of labour makes it possible to produce in 4 hours the same quantity of means of subsistence as could only be produced previously in 6 hours." Conversely, "the value of the labour-power cannot rise from 3 shillings to 4 without a decrease in the productivity of labour, so that 8 hours are required to produce the same quantity of means of subsistence as were previously produced in 6 hours." The general conclusion: "an increase in the productivity of labour causes a fall in the value of labour-power and a consequent rise in surplus-value, while, on the other hand, a decrease in the productivity of labour causes a rise in the value of labour-power and a fall in surplus-value."

Section I: Ricardo's Oversight — The Asymmetry of Proportional Change

Here Marx inserts a crucial corrective against Ricardo. In formulating this law, "Ricardo overlooked one thing: although a change in the magnitude of the surplus-value or surplus labour causes a change in the opposite direction in the magnitude of the value of labour-power, or in the quantity of necessary labour, it by no means follows that they vary in the same proportion." They do increase or diminish by the same quantity—but their proportional change depends on their original magnitudes before the productivity change. Marx works a concrete example. Suppose the value of labour-power is 4 shillings (= 8 hours necessary labour) and surplus-value is 2 shillings (= 4 hours surplus labour). If productivity rises so that v falls to 3 shillings (= 6 hours) then s rises to 3 shillings (= 6 hours), a uniform quantity of 1 shilling or 2 hours. But "the proportional change of magnitude is different in the two cases. The value of the labour-power falls from 4 shillings to 3, i.e. it falls by ¼, or 25 per cent, but the surplus-value rises from 2 shillings to 3, i.e. it rises by ½, or 50 per cent." The general rule: "the proportional increase or diminution in surplus-value resulting from a given change in the productivity of labour depends on the original magnitude of that portion of the working day which is embodied in surplus-value; the smaller that portion, the greater the proportional change; the greater that portion, the less the proportional change."

The footnote attaches Ricardo's original formulation—"There can be no rise in the value of labour without a fall of profits"—and offers Marx's reformulation: "There can be no rise in the value of labour-power without a fall in surplus-value," directing readers to the fuller discussion in Theories of Surplus-Value, Part 2.

Section I: Law Three — Consequence, Not Cause

The third law states: "Increase or diminution in surplus-value is always the consequence, and never the cause, of the corresponding diminution or increase in the value of labour-power." The derivation is a compressed chain of reasoning: because the working day is constant and represented by a constant value, because every variation in surplus-value has an inverse variation in the value of labour-power, and because the value of labour-power "cannot change except as a result of a change in the productivity of labour," it follows that every change in surplus-value arises from an inverse change in the value of labour-power. And if no change in absolute magnitudes can occur without a change in relative magnitudes, "it now follows that no change in their relative magnitudes is possible without a change in the absolute magnitude of the value of labour-power." The altered value of labour-power sets the limit of the change.

Section I: The Subsidiary Movement and the Class-Struggle Residual

Marx immediately qualifies the pure law with a crucial empirical intrusion: "even when circumstances allow the law to operate, subsidiary movements may occur." If productivity increase drives the value of labour-power down from 4 shillings to 3, "the price of labour-power might well fall only to 3s. 8d., 3s. 6d. or 3s. 2d., thus allowing the amount of surplus-value to rise only to 3s. 4d., 3s. 6d., or 3s. 10d." The amount of the fall—whose lowest limit is the new value of 3 shillings—"depends on the relative weight thrown into the scale by the pressure of capital on the one side, and the resistance of the worker on the other." This is a decisive insertion: the laws determine the boundaries within which the wage can move, but where within those boundaries it actually settles is a function of class struggle. The analysis is thus both deterministic and open-ended.

Marx then dispatches a rival theory. MacCulloch added "the following absurd addition to this third law: he says that a rise in surplus-value which is not accompanied by a fall in the value of labour-power can occur as a result of the abolition of taxes formerly payable by the capitalist." Marx's refutation is surgical: "the abolition of such taxes makes no change whatever in the quantity of surplus-value extorted by the capitalist at first hand from the worker. It only alters the proportion in which that surplus-value is divided between the capitalist himself and third persons. It therefore produces no change whatsoever in the relation between surplus-value and the value of labour-power." MacCulloch's exception "proves only his failure to understand the rule"—a misfortune Marx archly notes "as often happens to him in the vulgarization of Ricardo as it does to J. B. Say in the vulgarization of Adam Smith."

Section I: The Paradox of Simultaneous Improvement — Rising Above Value

A subtle and important case follows. Marx notes that it is possible "owing to an increase in the productivity of labour both the worker and the capitalist may simultaneously be able to appropriate a greater quantity of means of subsistence, without any change in the price of labour-power or in surplus-value." He sets up: v = 3s (6 hours necessary), s = 3s (6 hours surplus). If productivity doubles "without any alteration in the ratio between necessary labour and surplus labour, there would be no change in the magnitude either of the surplus-value or of the price of labour-power." Each would represent twice as many use-values and each use-value would be half as cheap. "Although labour-power would be unchanged in price, it would have risen above its value." The value of labour-power has fallen to 1s. 6d. (since productivity halved the labour-time needed to reproduce the subsistence basket), yet its price remains at 3s. The worker is being paid above value.

Now suppose the price falls, "not as far as 1s. 6d., the lowest possible point consistent with its new value, but to 2s. 10d. or 2s. 6d." Even this lower price "would still represent an increased quantity of means of subsistence." The dialectical conclusion is one of Marx's most powerful: "it is possible, given increasing productivity of labour, for the price of labour-power to fall constantly and for this fall to be accompanied by a constant growth in the mass of the worker's means of subsistence. But in relative terms, i.e. in comparison with surplus-value, the value of labour-power would keep falling, and thus the abyss between the life-situation of the worker and that of the capitalist would keep widening." This is the formal, quantitative grounding of the thesis of absolute improvement combined with relative immiseration. The Cazenove footnote (note 3) supports the distinction between the proportion of wages and the quantity they represent.

Section I: Ricardo's Two Defects — The Rate of Surplus-Value versus the Rate of Profit

Marx concludes Section I by acknowledging that "Ricardo was the first to give an accurate formulation of the three laws we have just stated," but his presentation suffers two defects. First, "he views the special conditions under which these laws hold good as the self-evident, universal, and exclusive conditions of capitalist production. He recognizes no change either in the length of the working day or in the intensity of labour, so that with him the productivity of labour becomes the only variable factor." Second, and more serious, "he has not, any more than the other economists, investigated surplus-value as such, i.e. independently of its particular forms, such as profit, ground rent, etc. He therefore fails to differentiate between the laws governing the rate of surplus-value and those governing the rate of profit."

Marx then provides the crucial definitions. The rate of profit is "the ratio of the surplus-value to the total capital advanced"; the rate of surplus-value is "the ratio of the surplus-value to the variable part of that capital." Using C = £500, of which c (raw materials, instruments of labour) = £400 and v (wages) = £100, and s = £100, he computes: rate of surplus-value = s/v = £100/£100 = 100 per cent; rate of profit = s/C = £100/£500 = 20 per cent. The rate of profit "may depend on circumstances which in no way affect the rate of surplus-value." Volume 3 will demonstrate "that the same rate of surplus-value may be expressed in the most diverse rates of profit, and that different rates of surplus-value may, under certain circumstances, be expressed in the same rate of profit." The footnote references Volume 3, Chapter 3. This distinction is foundational: it isolates the exploitation relation (s/v) from the appearance of profitability within which class interests are typically articulated.

Section II: The Intensive Magnitude — Why Intensity Is Not Productivity

Section II holds the length of the working day and productivity constant and varies intensity. Marx defines: "Increased intensity of labour means increased expenditure of labour in a given time. Hence a working day of more intense labour is embodied in more products than is one of less intense labour, the length of each working day being the same." The key analytical distinction from productivity is then drawn with precision. An increase in productivity will also supply more products in a given day, "but in that case the value of each single product falls, for it costs less labour than before, whereas in the case mentioned here that value remains unchanged, because each article costs the same amount of labour as before." Hence: "an increase in the number of products unaccompanied by a fall in their individual prices: as their number increases, so does the sum of their prices, whereas in the case of an increase in productivity, a given value is spread over a greater mass of products."

The consequence: with constant length of day, "a day's labour of increased intensity will be incorporated in an increased amount of value, and, assuming no change in the value of money, in an increased amount of money." The value created varies with intensity's divergence from the normal social level. "A given working day, therefore, no longer creates a constant value, but a variable one; in a day of 12 hours of ordinary intensity, the value created is, say, 6 shillings, but, with increased intensity, the value created may be 7, 8 or more shillings." If the value created rises from 6 to 8 shillings, then "the two parts into which this value is divided, namely the price of labour-power and surplus-value, may both increase simultaneously, and either equally or unequally. They may both simultaneously increase from 3 shillings to 4." Crucially, "here, the rise in the price of labour-power does not necessarily imply that it has risen above the value of labour-power. On the contrary, this rise in price may be accompanied by a fall below its value. This always occurs when the rise in the price of labour-power does not compensate for its more rapid deterioration."

Section II: The Dissolution of the Consumption-Article Limitation

Marx then notes a crucial structural implication. We know that "with exceptions which are purely temporary, a change in the productivity of labour does not cause any change in the value of labour-power, nor, consequently, in the magnitude of surplus-value, unless the products of the industries affected are articles habitually consumed by the workers." Productivity only affects the value of labour-power through the value of the wage-goods bundle. But with intensity this limitation "falls to the ground. Whether the magnitude of the labour changes in extent or in intensity, there is always a corresponding change in the magnitude of the value created, independent of the nature of the article in which that value is embodied." Intensity is therefore a more generally applicable lever of exploitation than productivity: it works regardless of what is being produced, because it changes the amount of value created within the working day itself.

Section II: The International Dimension — Intensity and the Law of Value Between Nations

Marx adds a geopolitical corollary. "If the intensity of labour were to increase simultaneously and equally in every branch of industry, then the new and higher degree of intensity would become the normal social degree of intensity, and would therefore cease to count as an extensive magnitude." The norm would simply shift. "But even so, the intensity of labour would still be different in different countries, and would modify the application of the law of value to the working days of different nations." A more intensive working day "would be represented by a greater sum of money than the less intensive day of another nation." Footnote 4 cites the Reports of the Inspectors of Factories of 31 October 1855: "All things being equal, the English manufacturer can turn out a considerably larger amount of work in a given time than a foreign manufacturer, so much as to counterbalance the difference of the working days, between 60 hours a week here, and 72 or 80 elsewhere." Marx adds, with characteristic polemical irony: "The most infallible means of reducing this difference between the product of the English and of the Continental working hour would be a law shortening the length of the working day in Continental factories." What appears as a technical comparison of international productivity is thus a covert argument for factory legislation.

Section III: The Three Laws of the Variable Working Day

Section III holds productivity and intensity constant and varies the length of the working day. The working day may be shortened or lengthened. Marx lays out three laws. First: "The working day creates a greater or lesser amount of value in proportion to its length—thus, a variable and not a constant quantity of value." Second: "Every change in the relation between the magnitude of surplus-value and the value of labour-power arises from a change in the absolute magnitude of the surplus labour, and consequently of the surplus-value." Third: "The absolute value of labour-power can change only in consequence of the reaction exercised by the prolongation of surplus labour upon the wear and tear of labour-power. Every change in this absolute value is therefore the effect, but never the cause, of a change in the magnitude of surplus-value." Note the structural inversion: in Section I, the value of labour-power was the independent variable (via productivity) and surplus-value the dependent one; here, surplus-value is the independent variable and the value of labour-power reacts only through wear and tear. A parenthetical note (present only in the 1872 French translation, presumably inserted by Engels into the first English translation) frames the two cases: shortening first, then lengthening.

Section III: Shortening the Day — The Capitalist's Compensation Problem

Case (1): a shortening of the working day under the given conditions "leaves the value of labour-power, and therefore the necessary labour-time, as it was before. It reduces the surplus labour and the surplus-value. Along with the absolute magnitude of the latter, its relative magnitude also falls, i.e. its magnitude in relation to the magnitude of the value of labour-power, which remains constant. Only by reducing the price of labour-power below its value could the capitalist compensate himself for this fall." This is a crucial point: a shorter working day at the same wage is an unambiguous loss to capital, and capital's only way to recover is to push the wage below the value of labour-power—i.e., to violate Assumption 2, which is exactly what happens historically. Marx then adds a dialectical reversal: "All the usual arguments against the shortening of the working day depend on the assumption that the phenomenon occurs under the conditions presupposed here. But in reality the very opposite is the case: a shortening of the working day either follows upon, or immediately precedes, a change in the productivity and the intensity of labour." Footnote 5 cites the Reports of the Inspectors of Factories of 31 October 1848 on the "compensating circumstances" that the Ten Hours' Act brought to light. In other words, the static model's prediction of loss is empirically falsified by the dynamic model, in which shortening is bundled with productivity and intensity gains.

Section III: Lengthening the Day — Relative Fall Without Absolute Fall

Case (2): lengthening of the working day. Marx sets v = 3s (6 hours necessary), s = 3s (6 hours surplus), total day 12 hours = 6s. "If the working day is lengthened by 2 hours and the price of labour-power remains the same, the surplus-value increases both absolutely and relatively. Although there is no absolute change in the value of labour-power, it suffers a relative fall." This is a critical structural difference from Section I: "Under the conditions assumed in I, there could not be a change of relative magnitude in the value of labour-power without a change in its absolute magnitude. Here, on the contrary, the change of relative magnitude in the value of labour-power is the result of the change of absolute magnitude in surplus-value." Since the value-product increases with the day's length, "the surplus-value and the price of labour-power may simultaneously increase, either by equal or unequal quantities." Marx identifies two cases in which simultaneous increase is possible: "an absolute increase in the length of the working day," and "an increase in the intensity of labour unaccompanied by an increase in length."

Section III: The Commensurability Break — Wear and Tear Beyond the Threshold

Marx then introduces a profound qualification. "When the working day is prolonged, the price of labour-power may fall below its value, although that price nominally remains unchanged, or even rises." Why? Because "the value of a day's labour-power is estimated, as will be remembered, on the basis of its normal average duration, or the normal duration of the life of a worker, and on the basis of the appropriate normal standard of conversion of living substances into motion as it applies to the nature of man." Footnote 6 cites Grove's On the Correlation of Physical Forces on the chemical changes that measure bodily labour. "Up to a certain point, the increased deterioration of labour-power inseparable from a lengthening of the working day may be compensated for by making amends in the form of higher wages. But beyond this point deterioration increases in geometrical progression, and all the requirements for the normal reproduction and functioning of labour-power cease to be fulfilled. The price of labour-power and the degree of its exploitation cease to be commensurable quantities." This is a subtle and chilling point: nominal wage gains may mask a real fall below value, and beyond a threshold, the wage relation itself loses its coherence as a measure of exploitation. The physical limits of the worker's body override the logic of equivalence.

Section IV: The Method of Analysis and the Two Chosen Cases

Section IV addresses simultaneous variations in duration, productivity, and intensity. Marx notes the combinatorial explosion—"Any two of the factors may vary and the third remain constant, or all three may vary at once"; variations may be equal or unequal, same or opposed in direction, and "the variations cancel each other out, either wholly or in part." His methodological claim is that "every possible case can easily be analysed by using the results obtained in cases I, II and III. The effect of every possible combination may be found by treating each factor in turn as variable, and the other two as constant for the time being." He then selects two important cases: (1) diminishing productivity with simultaneous lengthening of the working day, and (2) increasing intensity and productivity with simultaneous shortening of the working day.

Section IV: Case One — Diminishing Productivity with a Lengthening Day

Marx limits the case "particularly" to "those industries whose products determine the value of labour-power; we have in mind, for example, diminishing productivity resulting from the decreasing fertility of the soil, and from the corresponding increase in the prices of its products." He sets up: a 12-hour day, a value-product of 6s, half replacing v (=3s), half s (=3s). Then "let a rise in the prices of the products of the soil occur, so that the value of labour-power goes up from 3 shillings to 4, and the necessary labour-time therefore rises from 6 hours to 8." Three sub-cases follow:

  • Day unaltered (12 hours): surplus labour falls from 6 to 4 hours, surplus-value falls from 3s to 2s.
  • Day lengthened by 2 hours (to 14): necessary labour = 8 hours, surplus labour remains at 6 hours. The text states "the surplus-value will remain at 6 shillings"—this is almost certainly a transcription error for 3 shillings, since the value-product of a 14-hour day is 7s, and 7 − 4 = 3. The subsequent claim "the relative magnitude of the surplus-value will decrease in comparison with the magnitude of the value of labour-power" only makes sense if s = 3s relative to v = 4s (a ratio of 3:4, down from 1:1). The apparent inconsistency is worth flagging: the logic of the passage requires the corrected figure.
  • Day lengthened by 4 hours (to 16): value-product = 8s; v = 4s; s = 4s. "The proportional magnitudes of surplus-value and value of labour-power, of surplus labour and necessary labour, will continue unchanged [8:8 = 1:1], but the absolute magnitude of surplus-value will rise from 3 shillings to 4, and that of the surplus labour from 6 hours to 8, an increment of ⅓, or 33⅓ per cent."

The general conclusion: "with diminishing productivity of labour and a simultaneous lengthening of the working day, the absolute magnitude of surplus-value may continue unaltered, at the same time as its relative magnitude diminishes; its relative magnitude may continue unaltered at the same time as its absolute magnitude increases; and, finally, if the working day is lengthened to a sufficient extent, both may increase."

Section IV: The Historical Intervention — 1799–1815 and the Ricardo–West Error

Marx grounds the abstract case in a historical refutation. "In the period between 1799 and 1815 an increase in the prices of the means of subsistence led in England to a nominal rise in wages, although there was a fall in real wages, as expressed in the quantity of the means of subsistence they would purchase." From this fact "West and Ricardo drew the conclusion that the diminution in the productivity of agricultural labour had brought about a fall in the rate of surplus-value, and they made this assumption (which was purely a product of their own imagination) the starting-point of important investigations into the relative magnitudes of wages, profits and ground rent." The footnote cites West's Essay on the Application of Capital to Land (1815) and Ricardo's An Essay on the Influence of a Low Price of Corn on the Profits of Stock (1815)—"contributions to the controversy of 1815 over the Corn Laws." Marx's counter-claim: "as a matter of fact surplus-value had at that time increased both in absolute and in relative magnitude, thanks to the increased intensity of labour, and to the prolongation of the working day which had been forced upon the workers." He paints the period as one in which "the right to prolong the working day without any restriction at all became accepted as one of the basic rights of the citizen," characterized by "a rapid growth of capital" and "a rapid growth of pauperism."

Footnote 7 quotes Malthus: "Corn and labour rarely march quite abreast; but there is an obvious limit, beyond which they cannot be separated. With regard to the unusual exertions made by the labouring classes in periods of dearness... they are most meritorious in the individuals, and certainly favour the growth of capital. But no man of humanity could wish to see them constant and unremitted." Marx praises Malthus for drawing attention to the lengthening of hours, noting that Ricardo and others "flying in the face of the most notorious facts, make invariability in the length of the working day the groundwork of all their investigations." But he immediately criticizes Malthus: "the conservative interests Malthus served prevented him from seeing that unlimited prolongation of the working day, combined with an extraordinary development of machinery and the exploitation of the labour of women and children, inevitably made a great portion of the working class 'redundant', particularly after the cessation of wartime demand and the ending of the English monopoly of the world market." Malthus preferred to explain "over-population" by "the eternal laws of nature" rather than "the merely historical laws of the nature of capitalist production." Footnote 8 quotes the Essays on Political Economy (1830) on how the increase of capital during the war proceeded "from the greater exertions, and perhaps the greater privations of the labouring classes."

Section IV: Case Two — Rising Intensity and Productivity with a Shortening Day

The second chosen case pairs increasing intensity and productivity with a shortening of the working day. Marx observes: "Increased productivity and greater intensity of labour both have a similar effect. They both augment the mass of articles produced in a given time. Both therefore shorten that portion of the working day which the worker needs to produce his means of subsistence or their equivalent." The minimum length of the working day "is fixed by this necessary component, which is however itself capable of further contraction. If the whole working day were to shrink to the length of its necessary component, surplus labour would vanish, something which is impossible under the regime of capital." This is the absolute limit internal to capitalist production: the system cannot allow the working day to collapse into necessary labour, because surplus labour is its raison d'être. "Only the abolition of the capitalist form of production would permit the reduction of the working day to the necessary labour-time."

But Marx immediately complicates the counterfactual: "even in that case the latter would expand to take up more of the day, and for two reasons: first, because the worker's conditions of life would improve, and his aspirations become greater, and second, because a part of what is now surplus labour would then count as necessary labour, namely the labour which is necessary for the formation of a social fund for reserve and accumulation." This is a critical theoretical move. The distinction between necessary and surplus labour is not a physical constant but a social-historical category. Under socialism, necessary labour expands both because the worker's needs are historically elevated and because a portion of what was surplus is reclassified as socially necessary (for reserve and accumulation). The boundary between necessary and surplus is thus a function of the mode of production, not of nature—a point that anticipates the critique of "naturalizing" the wage relation.

Section IV: The Emancipatory Horizon — Necessity, Freedom, and the Universality of Labour

The chapter's final paragraphs pivot from analytic to normative-historical. Marx writes: "The more the productivity of labour increases, the more the working day can be shortened, and the more the working day is shortened, the more the intensity of labour can increase." This reciprocal dynamic is the historical trajectory of capitalist development in compressed form. He adds: "From the point of view of society the productivity of labour also grows when economies are made in its use. This implies not only economizing on the means of production, but also avoiding all useless labour." Capitalist production achieves economy within each individual business, yet "begets, by its anarchic system of competition, the most outrageous squandering of labour-power and of the social means of production, not to mention the creation of a vast number of functions at present indispensable, but in themselves superfluous." This is the contradiction between private economy and social waste—a theme that runs through Marx's critique of capitalism.

The final formulation is classically Marxian: "The intensity and productivity of labour being given, the part of the social working day necessarily taken up with material production is shorter and, as a consequence, the time at society's disposal for the free intellectual and social activity of the individual is greater, in proportion as work is more and more evenly divided among all the able-bodied members of society, and a particular social stratum is more and more deprived of the ability to shift the burden of labour (which is a necessity imposed by nature) from its own shoulders to those of another social stratum." The key variable, then, is the distribution of labour across the population: the more evenly work is shared, the more free time for free intellectual and social activity becomes possible for all.

The conclusion strikes a definitive chord: "The absolute minimum limit to the shortening of the working day is, from this point of view, the universality [Allgemeinheit] of labour. In capitalist society, free time is produced for one class by the conversion of the whole lifetime of the masses into labour-time." The German term Allgemeinheit—universality or generality—names the historical condition in which labour is shared among all and no group can offload its burden onto another. In capitalism, free time for the few is literally purchased by the lifetime labour of the many. The chapter thus closes by returning the reader to the historical and moral stakes of the analytic machinery: what looked like a technical taxonomy of variable magnitudes turns out to be an anatomy of the deepest structural antagonism of modern society.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Chapter 17 yields several fundamental principles that together form a kind of "conservation law" of capitalist exploitation. The first is the zero-sum logic of the fixed working day: when length and intensity are constant, the value produced is a fixed magnitude, so any gain to the worker is a loss to the capitalist, and vice versa. This is the structural reason why wage bargaining under fixed hours is a class struggle, and why capital has an intrinsic interest in shifting to the second and third dimensions—intensity and length—where the pie can grow.

The second principle is the positive-sum logic of lengthened or intensified labour: when the working day is extended or its intensity increased, the total value produced grows, so both the wage and surplus-value can rise simultaneously. This reconciles the seemingly paradoxical historical record (rising wages alongside rising profits) without abandoning the theory of exploitation. It also explains why capital historically favored lengthening the working day over raising wages, and why factory legislation was so fiercely resisted.

The third principle is the asymmetric logic of proportional change: the same quantity change produces a larger proportional effect on the smaller portion. Since surplus-value is typically the smaller portion of the working day relative to necessary labour in the empirical cases Marx analyses, productivity and intensity gains tend to increase surplus-value more rapidly (in proportional terms) than they reduce the value of labour-power. This is the quantitative core of Marx's critique of Ricardo.

The fourth principle is the distinction between the value and the price of labour-power, and the resulting possibility that wages can rise above value (when productivity outpaces wage adjustment) or fall below value (when intensity or length increase beyond the threshold of normal reproduction). The price of labour-power is thus a floor-and-ceiling variable bounded by value on one side and by class struggle on the other.

The fifth principle is the social-historical character of the necessary/surplus boundary. Whether a given hour of labour counts as necessary or surplus is not fixed by biology or technology but by the historically conditioned standard of the worker's needs and the social organization of the mode of production. This is why capitalism cannot tolerate the collapse of the working day into necessary labour, and why socialism would not simply preserve that collapse but redefine necessary labour to include the social fund for reserve and accumulation.

The broader implications are profound. Politically, the chapter grounds the strategic logic of the labor movement: since capital can offset shorter hours by intensification or productivity gains, workers must fight on all three dimensions simultaneously—which is exactly what the historical Ten Hours' movement and later struggles over the length, intensity, and productivity of work effectively did. Theoretically, the chapter delivers the definitive critique of classical political economy: Ricardo's identification of "profits" with surplus-value, and his conflation of the rate of surplus-value with the rate of profit, are shown to be category errors with cascading consequences. Marx's separation of the two rates—demonstrated numerically with the £500 example (100% rate of surplus-value, 20% rate of profit)—lays the foundation for Volume 3 and for the entire theory of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall.

Philosophically, the chapter enacts Marx's method: rigorous abstraction (isolating variables), concrete differentiation (distinguishing productivity from intensity, value from price, rate of surplus-value from rate of profit), and historical specification (grounding abstraction in the 1799–1815 Corn Law controversy, the Ten Hours' Act, and the class struggles of the period). The dialectical tension between absolute improvement and relative immiseration—where workers can consume more than before while their social position deteriorates relative to the capitalist class—captures the peculiar character of modern capitalism: it does not necessarily impoverish workers in absolute material terms, but it reproduces and deepens class inequality as a structural feature of accumulation. And the closing invocation of Allgemeinheit—the universality of labour—points beyond capitalism toward a society in which free time is no longer the privilege of one class purchased by the lifetime labour of another. The chapter thus achieves what few technical treatises achieve: it is at once a precise analytic specification of exploitation and a compressed historical and political argument for its abolition.
