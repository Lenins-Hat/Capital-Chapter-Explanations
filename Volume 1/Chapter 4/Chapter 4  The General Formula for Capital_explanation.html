Volume 1 • Chapter 4: The General Formula for Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 4 of Capital, Volume I, entitled "The General Formula for Capital," constitutes the opening salvo of Part Two, "The Transformation of Money into Capital." Its central thesis is deceptively simple but philosophically immense: capital is not a thing but a movement. Specifically, capital is the movement of value through the circulatory form M–C–M′, in which money (M) is advanced to purchase a commodity (C), which is then sold for more money (M′), the increment being surplus-value. The chapter's intellectual objective is to isolate, with surgical precision, the purely formal difference between the circulation of money as mere money (C–M–C) and the circulation of money as capital (M–C–M), and to demonstrate that this formal difference conceals a difference in content — namely, that C–M–C terminates in consumption (use-value) whereas M–C–M terminates in exchange-value itself and is therefore, by its very form, boundless.

Marx's problem is situated at a critical hinge in his argument. Having analyzed in Part One the commodity and money in their "simple" forms, he must now cross the threshold into the analysis of capital. He does so by asking a deceptively ordinary question: What distinguishes money that is simply money from money that becomes capital? His answer — that the distinction lies in the form of circulation — provides the scaffolding for the rest of the volume. The chapter thus functions as a formal deduction of capital's general formula, which will in Chapter 5 reveal a contradiction (the famous contradiction of the general formula) resolvable only by the discovery of the peculiar commodity labour-power, treated in Chapter 6. Everything in Marx's theory of surplus-value — exploitation, the working day, the labor process, accumulation — is anticipated here in embryo.

The chapter's ambition is therefore double. First, it is a phenomenology of capital, tracing capital to its "first form of appearance" in money. Second, and more profoundly, it is a Hegelian-flavored ontology of value, in which value is progressively revealed not as a passive relation but as an "automatic subject" that "puts itself into circulation," "preserves and multiplies itself," and "throws off surplus-value from itself" — a self-moving substance. This is where Marx's critique of political economy becomes, in the fullest sense, a critique of the categories of bourgeois thought.

The reader must therefore hold two registers simultaneously: the technical-economic (the difference between the two circulatory forms) and the philosophical (the constitution of value as subject). The chapter's dramatic arc moves from empirical observation (merchants and peasants buying and selling) to logical deduction (the formal distinction) to speculative confrontation (the God-Father/God-Son metaphor) — and closes by reclaiming the general formula as the true form not of merchants' capital alone but of all capital, including industrial and interest-bearing capital. Its final sentence is its thesis: "M–C–M′ is in fact therefore the general formula for capital, in the form in which it appears directly in the sphere of circulation."

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

I. The Historical Presupposition and the Everyday Appearance of Capital

Marx opens with a genealogical claim that doubles as a logical one: "The circulation of commodities is the starting-point of capital." Two historical conditions must be met before capital can arise — the production of commodities and their circulation "in its developed form, namely trade." Both crystallize historically in the sixteenth century, when world trade and the world market emerge and "the modern history of capital starts to unfold." This is not ornamental history. It establishes that capital is neither a natural given nor a trans-historical category, but a specific historical formation whose presuppositions were themselves the product of a prior development — the enclosure, colonization, and long-distance commerce that forged a global market.

The methodological pivot follows immediately. If we "disregard the material content of the circulation of commodities" (i.e., the exchange of use-values) and consider only "the economic forms brought into being by this process," we find that its ultimate product is money. Money is thus both the empirical residue of commodity circulation and the "first form of appearance of capital." Marx distinguishes two registers here. Historically, capital "invariably first confronts landed property in the form of money — monetary wealth, merchants' capital and usurers' capital." In footnote 1 he sharpens this with two French proverbs: "Nulle terre sans seigneur" ("No land without its lord") and "L'argent n'a pas de maître" ("Money has no master"). The first expresses landed property's power as personal domination and servitude; the second money's power as impersonal, masterless power. The historical rise of capital is thus the victory of the abstract, impersonal power of money over the personal, concrete power of the land — a theme that will recur throughout Marx's historical work. Logically and synchronically, however, "we do not need to look back at the history of capital's origins." Every day new capital "steps onto the stage" as money, whether in the commodity market, the labour market, or the money market, "money which has to be transformed into capital by definite processes." This "definite processes" is the entire business of the book.

II. The Formal Antithesis: C–M–C Contrasted with M–C–M

The first distinction between money as money and money as capital, Marx insists, "is nothing more than a difference in their form of circulation." He then names the two forms:

  • C–M–C: the "direct form of the circulation of commodities" — commodity into money, money back into commodity; selling in order to buy.
  • M–C–M: the "quite distinct" second form — money into commodity, commodity back into money; buying in order to sell.

Money that describes this second course is "transformed into capital, becomes capital, and, from the point of view of its function, already is capital." This is the chapter's first theorem: capital is defined formally, by the shape of its circuit. Marx then anatomizes M–C–M. It "passes through two antithetical phases": the purchase M–C (money into commodity) and the sale C–M (commodity back into money). Taken as a unity, the movement exchanges money for a commodity and the same commodity for money — or, "if one neglects the formal distinction between buying and selling, buys a commodity with money and then buys money with a commodity." (Here he cites footnote 2, Mercier de la Rivière: "With money one buys commodities, and with commodities one buys money.") The result, "in which the whole process vanishes," is the exchange of money for money, M–M. His concrete illustration is decisive: purchase 2,000 lb. of cotton for £100, resell for £110 — and you have "in fact exchanged £100 for £110, money for money."

III. The Problem of Absurdity and the Anatomy of Reflux

Having stated the form, Marx confronts its apparent absurdity. "It is evident that the circulatory process M–C–M would be absurd and empty if the intention were... to exchange two equal sums of money, £100 for £100." The miser would have been wiser to keep the £100 rather than expose it to "the dangers of circulation." Yet Marx refuses to reduce M–C–M to mere folly: whether the merchant sells at £110, £100, or even £50, "his money has at all events described a characteristic and original path, quite different in kind from the path of simple circulation" — for example, the peasant who sells corn and buys clothes. So Marx returns to the formal comparison to uncover the content hidden behind it.

He first establishes what the two forms have in common. Both divide into the same two antithetical phases, C–M (sale) and M–C (purchase). In each phase the same material elements confront each other — a commodity and money — and the same economic dramatis personae: a buyer and a seller. Each circuit is "the unity of the same two antithetical phases," mediated through three contract participants — one who only sells, one who only buys, and one who both buys and sells.

The differences then emerge with precision:

  1. Inverted order of succession. C–M–C begins with a sale and ends with a purchase; M–C–M begins with a purchase and ends with a sale.
  2. Inverted extremes and mediation. In C–M–C both starting and terminating points are commodities; the process is mediated by money. In M–C–M both extremes are money; the process is mediated by a commodity.
  3. Spent vs. advanced. In C–M–C, money is "in the end converted into a commodity which serves as a use-value; it has therefore been spent once and for all." In M–C–M, the buyer "lays out money in order that, as a seller, he may recover money." He releases money "only with the cunning intention of getting it back again." The money "is not spent, it is merely advanced." Marx anchors this conceptual distinction in footnote 3 (James Steuart): "When a thing is bought in order to be sold again, the sum employed is called money advanced; when it is bought not to be sold, it may be said to be expended."
  4. The twofold displacement and the reflux. In C–M–C "the same piece of money is displaced twice" — received from one buyer, paid to another — effecting "its definitive transfer from one hand into another." In M–C–M "it is not the piece of money which is displaced twice, but the commodity": the buyer takes it from the seller and passes it to another buyer, and "the twofold displacement of the same commodity causes the money to flow back to its initial point of departure." This reflux of money to its starting point is the "palpable difference" between money circulating as capital and money circulating as mere money. Crucially, "this reflux does not depend on the commodity's being sold for more than was paid for it. That only has a bearing on the amount of money which flows back." Reflux occurs as soon as the commodity is resold, i.e., as soon as the cycle M–C–M completes — regardless of profit or loss.

The contrast is then sharpened against C–M–C. In simple circulation, money is "irreversibly spent" — if I sell a quarter of corn for £3 and buy clothes, the money "belongs to the clothes merchant." If I sell a second quarter, money flows back to me, "not however as a result of the first transaction, but of its repetition." So in C–M–C "the expenditure of money has nothing to do with its reflux," whereas in M–C–M "the reflux of the money is conditioned by the very manner in which it is expended. Without this reflux, the operation fails, or the process is interrupted and incomplete."

IV. Teleological Inversion: Use-Value vs. Exchange-Value as the Motion's Final Cause

Having established the mechanical difference (reflux), Marx now reveals the difference in purpose. C–M–C "proceeds from the extreme constituted by one commodity, and ends with the extreme constituted by another, which falls out of circulation and into consumption." Its final goal is therefore "Consumption, the satisfaction of needs, in short use-value." M–C–M "proceeds from the extreme of money and finally returns to that same extreme. Its driving and motivating force, its determining purpose, is therefore exchange-value."

This telescoping of purpose out of form is one of the chapter's most elegant moves. In C–M–C the two extremes "have the same economic form" — they are both commodities of equal value — but they are "qualitatively different use-values" (corn and clothes). The content of the movement is "the exchange of products, the interchange carried out between the different materials in which social labour is embodied" — that is, the material metabolism of society, the distribution of concrete labor's products. Here Marx shows he is not, as vulgar critics claimed, indifferent to use-value; the use-value interchange is the entire point of C–M–C.

M–C–M is otherwise. "At first sight this appears to lack any content, because it is tautological." Both extremes are money — "the converted form of commodities, in which their particular use-values have been extinguished." To exchange £100 for cotton and then the same cotton for £100 is "merely a roundabout way of exchanging money for money, the same for the same." Because both extremes are money, they cannot differ qualitatively. "One sum of money is distinguishable from another only by its amount." Therefore, "the process M–C–M does not... owe its content to any qualitative difference between its extremes... but solely to quantitative changes. More money is finally withdrawn from circulation than was thrown into it at the beginning." The full form is therefore M–C–M′, where M′ = M + ΔM — "the original sum advanced plus an increment." And here Marx introduces, for the first time in the work, the category that will rule the rest of Capital: "This increment or excess over the original value I call 'surplus-value.'" (The translator's footnote flags that both Mehrwert and Verwertung are introduced at this juncture; English lacks a word for Verwertung adequate to Marx's meaning, hence "valorization.")

Worth noting is the irony in footnote 4, where Marx assembles the political economists against themselves. Mercier de la Rivière exclaims to the Mercantilists, "One does not exchange money for money." Corbet (An Inquiry into the Causes and Modes of the Wealth of Individuals, 1841) insists "trade is advantageously contrasted with gambling, which consists in a mere exchange of money for money" — yet Corbet concedes that M–M "is the characteristic form of circulation, not only of merchants' capital, but of all capital." Then "MacCulloch comes on the scene, and asserts that to buy in order to sell is to speculate, and thus the distinction between speculation and trade vanishes." Pinto, "the Pindar of the Amsterdam Stock Exchange," remarks: "Trade is a game... If one won everything from everybody for long, it would be necessary to give back voluntarily the greater part of the profit in order to begin the game again." Marx's real target here is the apologetic tendency to dissolve the formal specificity of capital — the very move that the chapter reverses.

V. Valorization, the Boundlessness of Capital, and the Aristotelian Precedent

With surplus-value on the table, Marx draws out its consequences for the whole process: "The value originally advanced, therefore, not only remains intact while in circulation, but increases its magnitude, adds to itself a surplus-value, or is valorized [verwertet sich]. And this movement converts it into capital." The word "verwertet sich" — valorizes itself — is reflexive: the value doesn't merely receive an increment, it does something to itself. This is the pivot on which Marx's idealist-sounding ontology of value turns.

Marx takes care to confront a potential counterexample. Could not C–M–C also involve quantitative inequality — the peasant sells corn above value, or buys clothes below value, or is cheated by the merchant? "Yet, for this particular form of circulation, such differences in value are purely accidental." Equivalence is "rather a necessary condition of its normal course" — the exchange of use-values is intelligible even when equivalents are exchanged. But in M–C–M, "The fact that the corn and the clothes are equivalents does not deprive the process of all sense and meaning, as it does in M–C–M" — this sentence is surely a typographic slip in the text for C–M–C at the end; what Marx means is that equivalence destroys the meaning of M–C–M (as it does not destroy that of C–M–C). The distinction therefore lies not in the empirical presence or absence of unequal exchange but in the logical requirement of the form itself.

The consequence is the famous limitlessness of capital. The repetition of C–M–C "finds its measure and its goal... in a final purpose which lies outside it, namely consumption, the satisfaction of definite needs." But in M–C–M "the end and the beginning are the same, money or exchange-value, and this very fact makes the movement an endless one." £100 becomes £110 — but £110, qualitatively, "is the same as £110, namely money"; quantitatively, "£110 is, like £100, a sum of definite and limited value." If £110 "is now spent as money, it ceases to play its part. It is no longer capital. Withdrawn from circulation, it is petrified into a hoard" — and could stay so until the Last Judgment without "a single farthing accruing to it." Both £100 and £110 "have the same vocation, to approach, by quantitative increase, as near as possible to absolute wealth." The momentary distinction between original capital (£100) and profit (£10) "vanishes immediately" at the end of the process: what emerges is a value of £110, "in exactly the same form... as the original £100." Engels' Umrisse zu einer Kritik der Nationalökonomie (1844) is cited in footnote 5 on this point — that capital divides into original capital and profit "although in practice profit is immediately lumped together with capital and set into motion with it."

The logical result follows: "The simple circulation of commodities — selling in order to buy — is a means to a final goal which lies outside circulation, namely the appropriation of use-values... As against this, the circulation of money as capital is an end in itself, for the valorization of value takes place only within this constantly renewed movement. The movement of capital is therefore limitless."

Marx localizes this insight in the Western philosophical tradition through the extended footnote 6 on Aristotle. Aristotle distinguishes economics (household management, aimed at procuring the articles necessary to existence, whose wealth is limited by use) from chrematistics (the art of acquiring wealth, aimed at money as such, with no limit). Aristotle writes that "there appear to be no limits to riches and property" in chrematistics, because "circulation is the source of riches," "money is the beginning and the end of this kind of exchange," and "just as every art which is not a means to an end, but an end in itself, has no limit to its aims... so with chrematistics there are no bounds to its aims, these aims being absolute wealth." The editor's note to the translation adds that Marx "quotes so as to bring out the meaning relevant to his argument" — turning Aristotle's "gaining wealth through exchange" into "circulation," "the art of household management" into "economics," and "the art of getting wealth" into "chrematistics." Marx is not merely citing Aristotle; he is making Aristotle say the structural distinction he himself has just derived formally. There is a deeper point: Aristotle had glimpsed the form of unbounded acquisitiveness, but could not explain it, because he was confined to a society in which the use of money as capital was subordinate to household economy. Only with capitalism does the sheer form of chrematistics become the dominant, universalizing form of wealth. Footnote 10 underscores the family resemblance of hoarding and capitalism through language: the Greek σωζειν ("to save") is "a characteristic Greek expression for hoarding," and English "to save" means both to rescue and to spare. The miser and the capitalist inhabit the same linguistic universe.

VI. The Capitalist as Bearer: Personification of the Movement

Marx now shifts registers from the form of movement to the human bearer of that form. "As the conscious bearer [Träger] of this movement, the possessor of money becomes a capitalist." This is the chapter's celebrated doctrine of personification. The capitalist is not an autonomous agent who happens to pursue profit; rather, "the objective content of the circulation... the valorization of value — is his subjective purpose, and it is only in so far as the appropriation of ever more wealth in the abstract is the sole driving force behind his operations that he functions as a capitalist, i.e. as capital personified and endowed with consciousness and a will." The capitalist is thus the mask capital wears; his will is a function of the movement.

From this follows the rationale for capital's cultural forms. "Use-values must therefore never be treated as the immediate aim of the capitalist; nor must the profit on any single transaction. His aim is rather the unceasing movement of profit-making." Marx cites footnote 7 (Chalmers: "Commodities are not the terminating object of the trading capitalist, money is his terminating object") and footnote 8 (Genovesi: "Though the merchant does not count the profit he has just made as nothing, he nevertheless always has his eye on his future profit"). The distinction between capitalist and miser is then famously cast: "This boundless drive for enrichment, this passionate chase after value, is common to the capitalist and the miser; but while the miser is merely a capitalist gone mad, the capitalist is a rational miser." The miser "seeks to attain by saving his money from circulation" what the capitalist achieves "by means of throwing his money again and again into circulation" — hence the citation of Galiani (footnote 11): "Things possess an infinite quality when moving in a circle which they lack when advancing in a straight line."

In footnote 9, Marx inserts a barbed aside about MacCulloch, who invokes the auri sacra fames ("accursed hunger for gold") to explain the capitalist's drive — but who, "when in theoretical difficulties, as for example in the treatment of over-production," transforms that same capitalist "into a good citizen, whose sole concern is for use-values, and who even develops an insatiable hunger for boots, hats, eggs, calico and other extremely common kinds of use-value." The point is methodological: apologetic economics oscillates between a capital driven by boundless value-greed (in good times) and a capital motivated by consumption needs (when defending the system against crises).

The philosophical culmination now comes: In M–C–M both money and commodity function "only as different modes of existence of value itself, the money as its general mode of existence, the commodity as its particular or, so to speak, disguised mode." Value perpetually "chang[es] from one form into the other, without becoming lost in this movement; it thus becomes transformed into an automatic subject." Hence the famous proposition: "capital is money, capital is commodities." This is not a mere tautology but the expression of a deeper structure. "In truth, however, value is here the subject of a process in which, while constantly assuming the form in turn of money and commodities, it changes its own magnitude, throws off surplus-value from itself considered as original value, and thus valorizes itself independently." The word "itself" is doing real philosophical work: "its valorization is therefore self-valorization [Selbstverwertung]. By virtue of being value, it has acquired the occult ability to add value to itself. It brings forth living offspring, or at least lays golden eggs." The irony of "occult ability" and "golden eggs" is the mark of Marx's critique: this self-generating power belongs, for political economy, to value as such; Marx's project will be to explain how this appears to be so by explaining the labor process that secretly underlies it. (Footnote 12 cites J.B. Say — "It is not the material which forms capital, but the value of that material" — and footnote 13 cites Macleod and James Mill.)

Value is then named "the dominant subject [übergreifendes Subjekt]" of the process. Because it "alternately assumes and loses the form of money and the form of commodities, but preserves and expands itself through all these changes," it requires "an independent form by means of which its identity with itself may be asserted. Only in the shape of money does it possess this form. Money therefore forms the starting-point and the conclusion of every valorization process." Money is thus not merely the beginning but the logical home of capital — the form in which value can recognize itself as the same across its metamorphoses.

The chapter reaches its speculative climax in the famous theological analogy. Value "enters into a private relationship with itself." It "differentiates itself as original value from itself as surplus-value, just as God the Father differentiates himself from himself as God the Son, although both are of the same age and form, in fact one single person; for only by the surplus-value of £10 does the £100 originally advanced become capital, and as soon as this has happened, as soon as the son has been created and, through the son, the father, their difference vanishes again, and both become one, £110." This is a deliberate parodic appropriation of the Hegelian logic of the Trinity (as read by Feuerbach and, in Marx's day, by the young Hegelians): the metaphysical self-differentiation of a subject that remains identical with itself through its "other." Marx is making two points: (1) the structure of value is speculative — it constitutes itself by producing its own other; (2) this constitution is fantastic, i.e., a form of appearance whose real ground lies elsewhere. The subsequent volume of Capital will unmask the "golden eggs" as the product of exploited labor-power. This chapter deliberately stops at the surface. In footnote 14, Sismondi's "capital... permanent self-multiplying value" is cited as an expression of this same truth from a different direction.

VII. The General Formula Applied: Mercantile, Industrial, and Interest-Bearing Capital

The chapter now generalizes. "Value therefore now becomes value in process, money in process, and, as such, capital." Its rhythm: "It comes out of circulation, enters into it again, preserves and multiplies itself within circulation, emerges from it with an increased size, and starts the same cycle again and again." The Mercantilists, "its first interpreters," described this as "M–M, 'money which begets money.'"

Marx then defends the generality of the formula. "Buying in order to sell, or, more accurately, buying in order to sell dearer, M–C–M, seems admittedly to be a form peculiar to one kind of capital alone, merchants' capital." But, he insists, "industrial capital too is money which has been changed into commodities, and re-converted into more money by the sale of these commodities. Events which take place outside the sphere of circulation, in the interval between buying and selling, do not affect the form of this movement." This parenthesis is decisive, and it is the hinge on which the coming chapters turn. It means that the form M–C–M′ is sufficient to define capital as capital, even though the actual production of surplus-value occurs in the interval — in production — not in exchange. Marx is preparing the move: the formula is universally correct as a form, but internally self-contradictory if we try to explain surplus-value from within circulation alone.

Finally, "in the case of interest-bearing capital, the circulation M–C–M′ presents itself in abridged form, in its final result and without any intermediate stage, in a concise style, so to speak, as M–M′ — i.e. money which is worth more money, value which is greater than itself." This is the most "mystified" and shortest rendering of capital's formula, the form Marx will later call the "mother of all insane forms." Here it serves to demonstrate the universality of the formula precisely by showing its most compressed, least transparent embodiment.

The chapter's final sentence performs the closure: "M–C–M′ is in fact therefore the general formula for capital, in the form in which it appears directly in the sphere of circulation." The qualifier — "in the form in which it appears directly in the sphere of circulation" — is a promissory note. What appears on the surface as self-valorizing value must still be explained; the surface is not yet the substance.

A textual note demands attention: on page 12 of the chapter, Marx writes that the capitalist knows all commodities are "in faith and in truth money, are by nature circumcised Jews, and, what is more, a wonderful means for making still more money out of money." The phrase is one of the notorious antisemitic figures in Marx's text — historically significant as evidence of the antisemitic idiom inherited by nineteenth-century German socialism, and one Marx deploys as a metaphor for the convertibility of commodities into money. It should be read both as an analysis of a rhetorical figure and as a mark of the ideological milieu in which the book was written; its theoretical content is that, for capital, all commodities are functions of money, "money in disguise."

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Three fundamental principles emerge from this chapter.

First, capital is defined formally, by its circulation. Marx's breakthrough is to distinguish two circuits that superficially involve the same phases (buying, selling, money, commodity) but have inverted orders and opposite purposes. C–M–C terminates in use-value and is therefore bounded by consumption. M–C–M′ terminates in exchange-value itself and is therefore, by its logical form, limitless. Once this formal distinction is grasped, the moralizing debates about greed, luxury, or usury become secondary — the boundlessness is built into the form, not the character, of the agent. This is what Marx means by "capital personified": the capitalist is the historical bearer of a structural logic he does not invent.

Second, the chapter makes surplus-value a formally necessary category. Once circulation is understood as M–C–M′, the name for ΔM — surplus-value — forces itself upon us. The very form of capital contains, as an internal demand, the production of an increment. From this follows the self-valorization (Selbstverwertung) of value, the "automatic subject," and the Trinity of Father, Son, and £110. Marx takes seriously but criticizes the surface appearance that value begets value "as naturally as trees bear fruit." The rest of Capital will be the systematic demonstration that this apparently self-grounded productivity rests on the exploitation of living labor — the substance that alone can create more value than it costs.

Third, the chapter initiates the discovery of the contradictions of the general formula. Because Marx explicitly brackets production ("events... outside the sphere of circulation... do not affect the form of this movement"), he has isolated a form that cannot yet be explained by its own terms. This is the pivot of Chapter 5: if equivalents exchange, how is surplus-value possible? The answer lies outside circulation but only via a commodity whose use-value is the source of value — labor-power. The elegant analytic economy of Chapter 4 is thus a deliberate aporia that structures the entire argument.

Theoretical and philosophical implications are enormous. Methodologically, Marx demonstrates the power of form analysis: the difference between two arrows in an equation (C–M–C vs. M–C–M) is used to generate a theory of surplus-value, of the historical specificity of capitalism, of the capitalist's subjective motive, and of the surface forms of credit and interest. His method is neither empirical induction from isolated facts nor abstract deduction from axioms, but a dialectical exposition of forms in which each form reveals its own immanent contradictions and drives toward its successor.

Historically and politically, the identification of capital with boundless self-valorization connects to Aristotle's chrematistics and to the auri sacra fames of the miser. Where the ancients saw a limit imposed by household need, and where moralists decried the excess of greed, Marx sees a structural unlimitedness that no moral restraint can tame. This has profound implications: capital cannot be reformed by making capitalists more moderate, because moderation contradicts capital's concept. The very form of the movement is a demand for infinite expansion — a category no longer available to pre-capitalist societies.

Philosophically, the chapter stages a critique of the categories of political economy as self-mystifying. The value that "brings forth living offspring" and "enters into a private relationship with itself" is the appearance generated by a social relation (the wage relation) that the economists treat as a natural property of things. The "occult ability" of value is the fetishism of Part One re-emerging at the level of capital. The theological analogy — Father/Son, one single person — is not merely rhetorical ornament: it is Marx's diagnosis that the logic of capital is structurally speculative, a self-positing subject whose apparent self-sufficiency conceals its dependence on living labor. The chapter thus secures the hermeneutic key to the whole of Capital: to read the surface of economic forms as rigorous self-misrecognition, whose correction must be effected by theoretical work penetrating from the self-moving value of circulation to the exploited labor of production.

Finally, the practical implication is stark. Because capital is defined by M–C–M′, every institutional form of capital — merchant, industrial, financial — is a permutation of the same formula. The "abridged form M–M′" of interest-bearing capital, far from being a mere abbreviation, is capital's most confessional moment, laying bare in a single stroke the pretense of productivity without production. Marx's chapter therefore not only frames Part Two but supplies the vocabulary — capital, surplus-value, valorization, personification, the automatic subject — through which the entire anatomy of bourgeois society will be dissected. Its thesis sentence, "M–C–M′ is in fact therefore the general formula for capital, in the form in which it appears directly in the sphere of circulation," is not just a summary but a commission: we are only at the surface — the depths remain to be sounded.
