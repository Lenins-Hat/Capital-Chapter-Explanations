Volume 1 • Chapter 19: The Transformation of the Value (and Respectively the Price) of Labour-Power into Wages

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

This chapter — the opening salvo of Part Six ("Wages") of Capital, Volume 1 — is a surgical intervention into the most self-evident-looking fact of bourgeois society: that the worker sells his labour and is paid a wage for it. Marx's central thesis is that this everyday appearance is a systematic illusion. What the worker actually sells is never labour but labour-power — the capacity to work, embodied in his person — and the wage is the converted form (the money expression) of the value and price of that labour-power, not of labour itself. The category "value of labour" is, Marx insists, an irrational expression, as imaginary as the "value of the earth," yet one that arises necessarily from the surface relations of capitalist production and reproduces itself spontaneously as ordinary common sense.

The intellectual objective is double-edged. First, analytically, Marx must demonstrate that the phrase "value of labour" is not merely imprecise but internally self-destructive: if value is measured by the labour contained in a commodity, then the "value of a 12-hour working day" would be twelve hours — a tautology — and any attempt to have living labour exchange directly against objectified labour would either abolish the law of value or abolish capitalism itself. Second, critically, he must explain why this impossible category nonetheless dominates both bourgeois political economy and the consciousness of workers and capitalists alike, and trace how the wage-form conceals the division of the working day into paid and unpaid labour — a concealment that grounds "all the notions of justice held by both the worker and the capitalist," "all capitalism's illusions about freedom," and "all the apologetic tricks of vulgar economics."

Situated within the broader architecture of Capital, the chapter is the hinge between the theory of surplus-value (developed in Part Three, where Marx already established that surplus-value arises from the use-value of labour-power) and the concrete analysis of wages in subsequent chapters. It performs the crucial ideological work of the whole volume: showing that the mechanisms generating surplus-value are not only economically necessary but socially invisible, and that classical political economy — Ricardo, Smith, the Physiocrats — inadvertently discovered the truth (value of labour-power) while staying trapped in the false category ("value of labour"), thereby handing vulgar economists a "secure base of operations" for worshipping appearances.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Illusory Surface: Wage as the "Price of Labour"

Marx opens not with a refutation but with an act of descriptive fidelity to the surface. "On the surface of bourgeois society the worker's wage appears as the price of labour, as a certain quantity of money that is paid for a certain quantity of labour." This is the form of appearance — the phenomenal level at which everyday economic actors live and reason. From this surface there flow a whole vocabulary: people speak of the "value of labour," call its money expression its "necessary or natural price," and speak of "market prices of labour" oscillating above or below that necessary price. The structure of this vocabulary mirrors exactly the way we speak of any commodity (cotton, corn, iron): a natural price gravitationally anchoring fluctuating market prices. The apparent symmetry is precisely what Marx will demolish.

The demolition begins with a deceptively simple logical probe. What is the value of a commodity? "The objective form of the social labour expended in its production." How is the quantity of that value measured? "By the quantity of the labour contained in it." Now apply this definition to labour itself: how is the value of, say, a 12-hour working day to be determined? "By the 12 working hours contained in a working day of 12 hours, which is an absurd tautology." Here Marx deploys the classical labour theory of value against the classical category "value of labour." If value = socially necessary labour time, then labour has no value in the technical sense; it is the substance of value, the measure of value, but it cannot be measured by itself as a magnitude that it possesses. This is the first crack.

He reinforces this with a second, deeper objection grounded in the temporal structure of sale. "In order to be sold as a commodity in the market, labour must at all events exist before it is sold. But if the worker were able to endow it with an independent existence, he would be selling a commodity, and not labour." This is the pivot of the whole chapter. A commodity must have independent existence before it circulates. Labour, however, is activity; it does not pre-exist its own performance. What is brought to market before the act of working must therefore be something else — a capacity, embedded in the living worker. The footnote (2) sharpens this to a paradox: labour "is created the moment it is brought to market; nay, it is brought to market before it is created." The very ontology of the supposed commodity "labour" is incoherent, because it would have to be sold prior to existing.

Footnote 1 (Bailey on Ricardo) supplies the historical preview: Ricardo "ingeniously enough, avoids a difficulty" by making the value of labour depend on "the quantity of labour required to produce wages." Marx's gloss is that this is "similar to saying, that the value of cloth is estimated, not by the quantity of labour bestowed on its production, but by the quantity of labour bestowed on the production of the silver for which the cloth is exchanged." Ricardo, in other words, tacitly substituted wages (the value of what the worker consumes) for the value of labour — an unwitting step toward the labour-power concept — but without recognizing what he had done.

The Impossibility of Exchange Between Objectified and Living Labour

Marx next frames the problem as a fork in the road with only two possible branches, both fatal to the appearance. "Apart from these contradictions, a direct exchange of money, i.e. of objectified labour, with living labour, would either supersede the law of value, which only begins to develop freely on the basis of capitalist production, or supersede capitalist production itself, which rests directly on wage-labour." This is a compressed statement of the foundational contradiction: capitalism requires that exchanges be governed by value (equivalents), yet also requires that the capitalist extract surplus — i.e., that a non-equivalent exchange occur somewhere. If labour itself is the commodity, no such reconciliation is possible.

The numeric example makes the dilemma concrete. Suppose the working day is 12 hours and is represented in a monetary value of, say, 6 shillings. Then:

  • Alternative A — Equivalents are exchanged. The worker receives 6 shillings for 12 hours of labour; "the price of his labour would be equal to the price of his product." But then "he produces no surplus-value for the buyer of his labour, the 6 shillings are not transformed into capital, and the basis of capitalist production vanishes." The very premise that he sells his labour as wage-labour presupposes a capitalist existence that this alternative destroys.
  • Alternative B — Non-equivalents are exchanged. The worker receives "less than 6 shillings," i.e., 12 hours of labour are exchanged for 10, 6, etc. hours of labour. But this is worse: "to equate unequal quantities in this way does not just do away with the determination of value." A law that declares 12 hours equal to 6 hours is not a law but the negation of law — "a self-destructive contradiction cannot be in any way even enunciated or formulated as a law."

Marx then pre-empts the obvious Hegelian-sounding escape: "It is no use deducing the exchange of more labour against less from the differences in form in each case, one piece of labour being objectified, the other living." That is, one cannot rescue the appearance by arguing that objectified labour is qualitatively different from living labour and therefore commands a premium. This "way out" is "even more absurd," because it presupposes the very thing Marx has just refuted: that the value of a commodity is determined by the objectified form of the labour in it. In fact, the opposite is true — and this is his crucial correction, developed on the following page.

Value Is Determined by Living Labour, Not Its Objectified Form

Here Marx states a decisive principle: "the value of a commodity is determined not by the quantity of labour actually objectified in it, but by the quantity of living labour necessary to produce it." He illustrates with a productivity shock. A commodity "represents, say, 6 working hours. If an invention is made by which it can be produced in 3 hours, the value, even of the commodity already produced, falls by half. It now represents 3 hours of socially necessary labour instead of the 6 formerly required." Note the key phrasing: "even of the commodity already produced." This is the point — the social labour-time required (the socially necessary labour time) is a constantly revalued magnitude, not a fixed quantum crystallized at the moment of production. The determining variable is the living expenditure society must currently make, not the dead labour stored up in the object.

This refutes the "differences in form" escape route definitively: since value is a function of living labour, you cannot appeal to the objectified character of one side of the exchange to justify a permanent quantitative inequality. From here Marx delivers the chapter's clearest formulation of the corrected category: "It is not labour which directly confronts the possessor of money on the commodity-market, but rather the worker. What the worker is selling is his labour-power." And immediately, the temporal argument returns to seal it: "As soon as his labour actually begins, it has already ceased to belong to him; it can therefore no longer be sold by him." Finally, the crispest possible summary: "Labour is the substance, and the immanent measure of value, but it has no value itself." (Footnote 5 cites Hodgskin: "Labour the exclusive standard of value ... the creator of all wealth, no commodity.")

The Inverted Category: "Value of Labour" as Imaginary Expression

Marx now moves from logical impossibility to ideological inversion. "In the expression 'value of labour', the concept of value is not only completely extinguished, but inverted, so that it becomes its contrary. It is an expression as imaginary as the value of the earth." The value of the earth is imaginary because land has no labour embodied in it but nonetheless commands a price — a parallel Marx treats as instructional: both are "imaginary expressions" that nonetheless have real social force. Crucially, he insists (against those who would dismiss him as a pedant) that these expressions "arise, nevertheless, from the relations of production themselves. They are categories for the forms of appearance of essential relations."

This is a philosophical point of great importance: the illusions are not arbitrary errors but necessary appearances. "That in their appearance things are often presented in an inverted way is something fairly familiar in every science, apart from political economy." The analogy is to optics or astronomy — where the sun's apparent motion around the earth is not a mistake we can simply wish away but a phenomenal necessity that science must explain. This rebuts both the naïvely empiricist economist and the radical who thinks the problem is merely a "grammatical ellipsis." In footnote 6 Marx attacks Proudhon precisely for this: Proudhon calls the "value of labour" a "figurative expression," and Marx retorts that in "labour as a commodity, which is a grim reality, he sees nothing but a grammatical ellipsis. Thus the whole of existing society, founded on labour as a commodity, is henceforth founded on a poetic licence." The sarcastic cure — "change the language" and apply to the Académie for "a new edition of its dictionary" — signals Marx's contempt for reforming the names rather than the relations. He adds a jab at J. B. Say's circular definitions ("value is what a thing is worth"), showing how a shallow nominalism collapses into vacuity.

How Classical Political Economy Stumbled: From Market Price to Natural Price to Labour-Power

Marx now narrates the history of the science as a detective story. Classical political economy "borrowed the category 'price of labour' from everyday life without further criticism, and then simply asked the question, how is this price determined?" It then correctly recognized that supply and demand explain "nothing, with regard to the price of labour or any other commodity, except those changes themselves" — the oscillations. Once demand and supply balance, "the oscillation of prices ceases," and by the same token "demand and supply also cease to explain anything." So a third thing — the natural price — must be posited independently of supply and demand.

The classical economists then tried to find this natural price either as the equilibrium point or as the moving average over a period: "a longer period of oscillation in the market price was taken, for example a year, and the oscillations were found to cancel each other out, leaving a mean average quantity, a constant magnitude." This is a genuine methodological achievement — recognizing that price must be determined "otherwise than by its own mutually compensatory variations." The Physiocrats called it the "necessary price"; Adam Smith the "natural price." But both could only conclude that it is "its value expressed in money, as with all other commodities."

The fatal step: "this value was then further determined by the cost of production. But what is the cost of production ... of the worker, i.e. the cost of producing or reproducing the worker himself?" Note Marx's precise wording: the political economists "unconsciously substituted this question for the original one, for the search after the cost of production of labour as such turned in a circle, and did not allow them to get any further forward at all." This is the moment of unconscious discovery. To escape a circular definition (labour determined by the labour producing it), they slid into asking the cost of reproducing the worker — which is exactly the value of labour-power. "Therefore what they called the 'value of labour' is in fact the value of labour-power, as it exists in the personality of the worker, and it is as different from its function, labour, as a machine is from the operations it performs." The machine simile is central: you hire the machine, not its operation, though the hire price appears to attach to the work done.

Yet they never recognized what their analysis had accomplished. Because they focused on "the difference between the market price of labour and its so-called value," and on the relation of that value to profit and to the values of other commodities, "they never discovered that the course of the analysis had led not only from the market prices of labour to its presumed value, but also to the resolution of this value of labour itself into the value of labour-power." Their "unconsciousness of this result of its own analysis" and their "uncritical acceptance of the categories 'value of labour', 'natural price of labour'" led them into "inextricable confusions and contradictions" — and, critically, "offered a secure base of operations to the vulgar economists who, in their shallowness, make it a principle to worship appearances only."

The Conversion Mechanism: How Value of Labour-Power Becomes Wages

Marx now performs the positive analysis, showing the transformation of the hidden essence into its surface form. He sets up the arithmetic: "Assume that the usual working day is 12 hours and the daily value of labour-power 3 shillings, which is the expression in money of a value embodying 6 hours of labour." So 6 hours of the 12-hour day reproduce the value of the labour-power (3 shillings), and 6 hours create surplus value for the capitalist.

Now the crucial sleight-of-hand that the form performs: "If this value of a day's labour-power is now expressed as the value of a day's labour itself, we have the formula: 12 hours of labour has a value of 3 shillings." The value of labour-power has here been projected onto the whole day's labour. Hence: "The value of labour-power thus determines the value of labour, or, expressed in money, its necessary price. If, on the other hand, the price of labour-power differs from its value, the price of labour will similarly differ from its so-called value." This is the mechanism of converted form: the wage is derived from the value of labour-power, but the derivation is transposed onto its phenomenal appearance as the value of the worker's whole day's toil.

The Absurd Result and the Wage-Form's Concealment

From this transformation there follows the chapter's most famous paradox. Because "the value of labour is only an irrational expression for the value of labour-power," it follows "that the value of labour must always be less than its value-product," since "the capitalist always makes labour-power work longer than is necessary for the reproduction of its own value." In the running example: the value of the labour-power functioning through 12 hours is 3 shillings (6 hours to reproduce); yet the value it produces is 6 shillings, because it functions for 12 hours, and "its value-product depends, not on its own value, but on the length of time it is in action." Hence: "labour which creates a value of 6 shillings possesses a value of 3 shillings." Marx flags that this is "at first sight absurd" — but it is exactly the absurdity that would remain invisible under the naturalistic appearance.

The masking is then spelled out with precision: "the value of 3 shillings, which represents the paid portion of the working day, i.e. 6 hours of labour, appears as the value or price of the whole working day of 12 hours, which thus includes 6 hours which have not been paid for." The wage therefore pays, or appears to pay, for unpaid as well as paid labour. "The wage-form thus extinguishes every trace of the division of the working day into necessary labour and surplus labour, into paid labour and unpaid labour. All labour appears as paid labour."

Comparative Forms: Corvée, Slavery, Wage-Labour

To make this concealment vivid, Marx triangulates across three modes of extracting surplus labour:

  • Corvée (serfdom). "There the labour of the serf for himself, and his compulsory labour for the lord of the land, are demarcated very clearly both in space and time." The unpaid component is visible as such.
  • Slavery. "Even the part of the working day in which the slave is only replacing the value of his own means of subsistence, in which he therefore actually works for himself alone, appears as labour for his master. All his labour appears as unpaid labour." Here the opposite distortion occurs: the paid (self-sustaining) portion is hidden inside the general appearance of total servitude.
  • Wage-labour. "Even surplus labour, or unpaid labour, appears as paid."

The structural contrast is drawn with algebraic neatness: "In the one case, the property-relation conceals the slave's labour for himself; in the other case the money-relation conceals the uncompensated labour of the wage-labourer." This symmetry is theoretically important: it shows that both precapitalist and capitalist forms distort the paid/unpaid division, but in opposite directions and by different mechanisms — property versus money. The wage-relation does not merely permit exploitation; it transforms exploitation into its own appearance of freedom and full payment.

Footnote 8 gives a polemical illustration: The Morning Star, a "London free-trade organ which is so naive as to be positively foolish," indignantly claimed during the American Civil War that the Negroes in the "Confederate States" worked "absolutely for nothing." Marx's sardonic rebuttal — the paper "should have compared the daily cost of a Negro in the southern states with that of a free worker in the East End of London" — punctures the moral complacency by suggesting that the "free" East End worker's compensation may be equally illusory once the actual cost of his reproduction is examined.

The Decisive Importance of the Transformation

Marx now states the widest significance of the entire analysis: "All the notions of justice held by both the worker and the capitalist, all the mystifications of the capitalist mode of production, all capitalism's illusions about freedom, all the apologetic tricks of vulgar economics, have as their basis the form of appearance discussed above, which makes the actual relation invisible, and indeed presents to the eye the precise opposite of that relation." This is a strong claim. The chapter's central object is thus not a semantic quibble; it is the ideological cornerstone of capitalism. The wage-form is what makes exploitation appear as exchange, dependence as freedom, and unpaid labour as paid.

Why the Illusion Persists: The Naturalness of the Appearance

Marx — remarkably, given his critical stance — then says "nothing is easier to understand than the necessity, the raison d'être, of this form of appearance." He enumerates four reasons the illusion feels entirely natural:

  1. The mirror of commodity exchange. "The exchange between capital and labour at first presents itself to our perceptions in exactly the same way as the sale and purchase of all other commodities. The buyer gives a certain sum of money, the seller an article which is something other than money." The legal mind sees only "a material difference," captured in the hollow reciprocity of the Roman formulas: Do ut des, do ut facias, facio ut des, facio ut facias ("I give that you may give; I give that you may do; I do that you may give; I do that you may do").
  2. Incommensurability of value and use-value. "Since exchange-value and use-value are in themselves incommensurable magnitudes, the expressions 'value of labour', 'price of labour', do not seem more irrational than the expressions 'value of cotton', 'price of cotton'." The absurdity hides behind the innocuousness of ordinary commodity language.
  3. Money as deferred payment. "The worker is paid after he has given his labour" — so money functions as a means of payment that realizes retroactively the value of the article "supplied." This temporal sequence (work first, pay later) reinforces the belief that the money pays for the work performed.
  4. The use-value delivered is congealed into a specific craft. What the worker actually hands over to the capitalist is, in everyday perception, "a specific form of useful labour, such as tailoring, cobbling, spinning" — not the abstract capacity that is the "universal value-creating element" with properties unlike all other commodities. "That this same labour is ... the universal value-creating element ... is something which falls outside the frame of reference of the everyday consciousness."

The Worker's Perspective and the Capitalist's Perspective

Marx then adopts a dual standpoint, in what is a masterful exercise in critical phenomenology.

From the worker's standpoint: he receives for 12 hours of labour the value-product of 6 hours (Marx here gives 2 shillings, a numerical slip or variant relative to the earlier 3-shilling example, but the logic is identical). His 12 hours are for him the means of buying the 3 shillings he needs. His labour-power's value may fluctuate (3–4 or 3–2 shillings), or its price may rise or fall with demand and supply; but "he always gives 12 hours of labour. Every change in the amount of the equivalent that he receives therefore necessarily appears to him as a change in the value or price of his 12 hours of labour." This is the worker's phenomenological trap. Marx notes explicitly: "This circumstance misled Adam Smith, who treated the working day as a constant quantity, into the opposite assertion that the value of labour is constant" — i.e., Smith held that the value of labour stays fixed even as the value of subsistence goods varies (footnote 9 notes Smith only incidentally alludes to variation of the working day, when discussing piece-wages).

From the capitalist's standpoint: he "wishes to receive as much labour as possible for as little money as possible," and so "the only thing that interests him is the difference between the price of labour-power and the value which its function creates." But he "tries to buy all commodities as cheaply as possible," and thus "his own invariable explanation of his profit is that it is a result of mere sharp practice, of buying under the value and selling over it." Here is the capitalist's counterpart illusion: he doesn't recognize that the very wage-form he operates within is the condition of his profit. "Hence he never comes to see that if such a thing as the value of labour really existed, and he really paid this value, no capital would exist, and his money would never be transformed into capital." The worker and the capitalist are thus locked in asymmetric but mutually reinforcing misrecognitions.

The Two Classes of Phenomena; Appearance vs. Essence; the Limits of Classical Political Economy

Marx closes with an empirical-observational challenge: "the actual movement of wages presents phenomena which seem to prove that it is not the value of labour-power which is paid, but the value of its function, of labour itself." Two classes of these phenomena:

  1. Changes in wages owing to changes in the length of the working day. Longer days bring higher pay, which superficially looks like payment for the quantity of labour performed. Marx replies with the machine analogy: "One might as well conclude that it is not the value of a machine which is paid, but that of its operation, because it costs more to hire a machine for a week than for a day." The higher weekly hire-charge on the machine is entirely compatible with paying for the machine's value rather than its "operation."
  2. Individual wage differences between workers performing the same function. These also occur under slavery, but "there they do not give rise to any illusions, for labour-power is in that case itself sold frankly and openly, without any embellishment." The decisive contrast: "in the slave system, the advantage of a labour-power above the average, and the disadvantage of a labour-power below the average, affects the slave-owner; whereas in the system of wage-labour it affects the worker himself, because his labour-power is, in the one case, sold by himself, in the other, by a third person." The same differential is interpreted as a property of the owner under slavery and of the worker under wage-labour — a perfect illustration of how forms of appearance invert essential relations.

The chapter's final paragraph generalizes into epistemology: what holds for all forms of appearance holds for "'value and price of labour', or 'wages', as contrasted with the essential relation manifested in it, namely the value and price of labour-power. The forms of appearance are reproduced directly and spontaneously, as current and usual modes of thought; the essential relation must first be discovered by science." This is a conciliatory but severe verdict on classical political economy: it "stumbles approximately onto the true state of affairs, but without consciously formulating it." Its limitation is not intellectual capacity but class determination: "It is unable to do this as long as it stays within its bourgeois skin."

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Chapter 19 establishes a fundamental principle with implications far beyond political economy: the most obvious surface facts of a social system can be systematically inverted representations of the mechanisms that sustain it. The wage appears to be payment for labour; it is in reality payment for the capacity to labour, and it simultaneously conceals the unpaid labour that generates surplus-value. This is not a minor terminological correction; it is the epistemological key to the whole of capitalism. To mistake appearance for essence — to speak of the "value of labour" — is not just an economic error but the operating ideology of bourgeois society, shared by worker, capitalist, and economist alike.

The chapter reveals several systemic laws. First, the law of equivalent exchange is preserved at the surface of capitalism precisely by displacing the extraction of surplus-value to the hidden dimension of labour-power's use-value — the market remains a realm of formal equality, while the labor process is a realm of unequal appropriation. Second, the convertibility of essence into appearance is a structural requirement, not an accidental error: because the wage-form must be applied to the entire working day, its very definition extinguishes the trace of the paid/unpaid division. Third, the asymmetry of the worker's and capitalist's illusions — the worker thinks he is paid for his labour, the capitalist thinks his profit comes from buying cheap and selling dear — shows that the ideology of capital is multiply distributed and self-reinforcing across class positions.

Theoretically, the chapter sews up the argument of Volume 1's Part Three by answering the question Marx had posed in A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (footnote 7): how can a regime of exchange-values determined by labour-time produce the result that "the exchange-value of labour is less than the exchange-value of its product"? Answer: because labour is not what is exchanged; labour-power is. Once the category is corrected, the apparent contradiction disappears and the law of value stands intact.

The critique of classical political economy is also carefully calibrated. Marx does not say Ricardo and Smith were stupid; he shows they almost got there — discovered the value of labour-power through the cost-of-reproduction question — but could not name their own discovery because doing so would transcend "bourgeois skin." This produces a striking verdict on the limits of scientific thought under class conditions: even genuine analytic progress can be arrested by the categories one refuses to question. And it explains the rise of "vulgar economics," which does not accidentally worship appearances but makes a principle of it, precisely because classical political economy left the appearance-categories intact.

Philosophically, the chapter's method prefigures the "forms of appearance" logic later elaborated in Volume 3: the task of science is not to abolish appearances but to explain what they conceal. The wage-form is the paradigm case — spontaneous, necessary, socially reproduced, and therefore not refutable by mere linguistic reform. Its practical implication is that any politics built on the wage-form's promises (fair wages, just exchange, freedom of contract) operates within the very terms that hide exploitation — a lesson aimed equally at reformist economists, juridical socialism, and Proudhonian nominalism. The task, for Marx, is not to correct the language of wages but to grasp the relation that the language conceals.
