Volume 1 • Chapter 25: The General Law of Capitalist Accumulation

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem and Its Stakes

Chapter 25 of Capital, Volume 1, constitutes the culminating theoretical payoff of the entire "Process of Accumulation of Capital" section. Its opening sentence states the problem with disarming directness: "In this chapter we shall consider the influence of the growth of capital on the fate of the working class." This is not a narrow technical question. It is the question upon which the moral, political, and theoretical coherence of the entire critique of political economy turns. If capital accumulation — the engine of "wealth of nations" — inevitably and structurally produces the immiseration of the very class that creates that wealth, then the classical economists' claim that capital accumulation is the road to universal prosperity collapses into an ideological illusion.

Marx's intellectual objective is therefore double. First, he must demonstrate the mechanics — the causal dynamics — by which the accumulation of capital, understood as a process driven by the extraction of surplus-value, generates a surplus population of workers that is simultaneously a product and a lever of accumulation. Second, he must demolish the rival explanation: the Malthusian "natural law of population," which holds that poverty arises from the working class's own tendency to reproduce beyond the means of subsistence, and that capital accumulation only benefits workers when population growth is restrained. Marx intends not merely to refute Malthus but to show that the Malthusian law is itself an ideological obfuscation of a law that operates in the opposite direction — that the working population is rendered "redundant" not by its own natural multiplication but by the changing composition of capital.

The Architecture of the Argument

The chapter unfolds in five sections, but its logical engine has three interlocking movements. The first movement (Section 1) isolates the special case in which accumulation proceeds on an unchanged technical basis. Under this abstraction, Marx grants the classical economists their own premise: if the composition of capital remains constant, the demand for labour does indeed rise in proportion to accumulation, and wages may rise. But even here he demonstrates the decisive asymmetry: wages rise only because capital requires more labour, and the rise can never threaten the profit-driven foundation of the system. The rate of accumulation is the independent variable; the wage rate is the dependent variable.

The second movement (Sections 2 and 3) dissolves the abstraction. As accumulation proceeds, it necessarily transforms the technical composition of capital: means of production (constant capital) grow relative to living labour (variable capital). This transformation is not accidental but is driven by the very pursuit of surplus-value through rising productivity. The consequence is that the additional capital formed in each period absorbs proportionally fewer workers, and the old capital, periodically renewed, repels workers it previously employed. The result is the "progressive production of a relative surplus population, or industrial reserve army" — a population superfluous to capital's average valorization requirements. This reserve army, in turn, becomes the regulator of wages, the disciplining mechanism of the employed labour force, and the elastic supply of human material that makes possible the sudden expansions of modern industry.

The third movement (Sections 4 and 5) generalizes and concretizes. Section 4 identifies the three permanent forms of the relative surplus population — the floating, the latent, and the stagnant — and traces pauperism to its place as the "hospital of the active labour-army and the dead weight of the industrial reserve army." It then states the "absolute general law" in its full antagonistic form: "Accumulation of wealth at one pole is, therefore, at the same time accumulation of misery, the torment of labour, slavery, ignorance, brutalization and moral degradation at the opposite pole." Section 5 provides a massive empirical apparatus — English statistics, nutrition studies, housing reports, the gang-system, and the Irish case — to demonstrate the law's operation.

The Theoretical Payoff and Its Place in the Broader Work

This chapter is the pivot of Capital's argument. Book One has traced the logic of value, the extraction of surplus-value, the working day, and the production of relative surplus-value. Chapter 25 now closes the circle: it demonstrates that the accumulation of the capital-relation reproduces that relation on an expanded scale. The worker sells labour-power freely, but the sale reproduces the dependence of the worker on capital; the productive power developed under capital becomes a means of domination over the worker. The chapter thus answers the riddle Marx posed earlier: how the worker, the creator of capital, becomes its slave. And it sets the stage for the historical account of violence, expropriation, and legislation in the chapters on primitive accumulation that follow, as well as the theory of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall developed in Volume 3.

Crucially, Marx insists the law is a tendency: "Like all other laws, it is modified in its working by many circumstances." The chapter is therefore not a mechanical prediction but an analysis of the structural constraints of capitalist production, empirically illustrated but theoretically bounded. Its dialectical structure — the inversion of means of production from serving the producer into a means of exploiting him — is the signature of Marx's method throughout. The "law" is not a natural law but an historical one, specific to a particular mode of production, and thus subject to historical transformation.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

Section 1: A Growing Demand for Labour-Power Accompanies Accumulation If the Composition of Capital Remains the Same

The Twofold Composition of Capital — Foundational Definitions

Marx opens by defining his central analytic variable: the composition of capital, understood in a "twofold sense." As value, capital divides into constant capital (the value of means of production) and variable capital (the value of labour-power, i.e., the sum total of wages). As material, capital divides into means of production and living labour-power; this technical composition is determined by "the relation between the mass of the means of production employed on the one hand, and the mass of labour necessary for their employment on the other."

Here we meet one of the chapter's most important and most frequently misread conceptual distinctions. The value-composition is the ratio of constant to variable capital as expressed in value terms. The technical composition is the ratio in material terms — the actual physical quantity of machinery, raw material, and so forth relative to the number of workers employed. But these two are not identical, and Marx introduces a third, mediating concept: the organic composition. This is the value-composition "in so far as it is determined by its technical composition and mirrors the changes in the latter." In other words, the organic composition is the value-composition understood in its causal dependence on the technical composition. When Marx says "the composition of capital," without qualification, he means this organically determined composition.

Why does this distinction matter? Because the value-composition can change for reasons unrelated to the technical composition (e.g., changes in the prices of raw materials or machinery), while the technical composition can change without a proportional change in value (because rising productivity cheapens means of production). The organic composition is the theoretically relevant variable because it captures the real causal connection between the developing productive forces and the demand for labour.

Marx then introduces an aggregation principle. Individual capitals in a branch of production differ in composition. The average of these individual compositions gives the composition of the total capital in the branch. The average of all branch averages gives the composition of the total social capital of a country — "and it is with this alone that we are concerned here in the final analysis." This move from individual to social capital is essential: surplus population and the wage relation are structural features of the social capital, not reducible to the fortunes of individual firms.

The Basic Mechanism Under Constant Composition

Marx posits accumulation as "growth of capital." This implies growth of its variable constituent — the part invested in labour-power — because "a part of the surplus-value which has been transformed into additional capital must always be re-transformed into variable capital, or additional labour fund." Assume now that the composition remains constant: a definite mass of means of production continues to need the same mass of labour-power. Under this assumption, "the demand for labour, and the fund for the subsistence of the workers, both clearly increase in the same proportion as the capital, and with the same rapidity."

Marx then elaborates a compounding dynamic. Capital produces surplus-value every year; part of this is added to the original capital; this increment itself grows yearly along with the augmentation of capital already functioning. Moreover, under conditions especially liable to stimulate accumulation — the opening of new markets, new spheres of investment from "newly developed social requirements" — the scale of accumulation can be suddenly extended merely by changing the proportion in which surplus-value is divided between capital and revenue. For all these reasons, "the requirements of accumulating capital may exceed the growth in labour-power or in the number of workers; the demand for workers may outstrip the supply, and thus wages may rise."

This is a striking concession to the classical economists. Marx grants that under constant composition, wages can rise. Indeed, he notes historical evidence: complaints were heard in England "during the whole of the fifteenth century, and the first half of the eighteenth." This is the case of capital's expansive demand outrunning the available labour-power. Crucially, however, Marx immediately circumscribes the significance of this: "The more or less favourable circumstances in which the wage-labourers support and multiply themselves in no way alter the fundamental character of capitalist production." Just as simple reproduction reproduces the capital-relation itself (capitalists on one side, wage-labourers on the other), so reproduction on an expanded scale — accumulation — reproduces the capital-relation on an expanded scale, with more or bigger capitalists at one pole and more wage-labourers at the other.

The reproduction of labour-power — which "must incessantly be re-incorporated into capital as its means of valorization, which cannot get free of capital, and whose enslavement to capital is only concealed by the variety of individual capitalists to whom it sells itself" — is "a factor in the reproduction of capital itself." Hence the iconic formulation: "Accumulation of capital is therefore multiplication of the proletariat."

Here Marx inserts a long, historically rich footnote (note 1) that repays close reading. He cites his own Wage Labour and Capital and quotes Colins: "If the masses are all oppressed equally, the more proletarians a country has, the richer it will be." Marx defines "proletarian," economically speaking, as "nothing other than 'wage-labourer,' the man who produces and valorizes 'capital,' and is thrown onto the street as soon as he becomes superfluous to the need for valorization possessed by 'Monsieur Capital.'" He then savagely dismisses Wilhelm Roscher's notion of "the sickly proletarian of the primitive forest": the primitive forester owns the forest and exploits it; he is not exploited by it. "He is not, therefore, a proletarian. This would only be the case if the primitive forest exploited him, instead of being exploited by him." This is a methodological point of considerable importance: the proletarian condition is defined by social relations, not by poverty or health as such.

Classical Political Economy on Accumulation as Proletarian Multiplication

Marx notes that classical political economy grasped this fact "so thoroughly" that Adam Smith and Ricardo inaccurately identified accumulation with the consumption, by productive workers, of the whole capitalized part of the surplus product — i.e., with the transformation of surplus product into additional wage-labourers. Marx then marshals a series of quotable authorities. John Bellers (1696): "if one had a hundred thousand acres of land and as many pounds in money, and as many cattle, without a labourer, what would the rich man be, but a labourer? And as the labourers make men rich, so the more labourers there will be, the more rich men." Bernard de Mandeville (early eighteenth century) achieves a brutally frank formulation: "It would be easier, where property is well secured, to live without money than without poor; for who would do the work?" Mandeville argues the poor "ought to be kept from starving" but "should receive nothing worth saving"; wants are the spur to industry, but "too much will make him insolent and lazy." The "surest wealth consists in a multitude of laborious poor" — they are "the never failing nursery of fleets and armies." And to keep them useful, "it is requisite that great numbers of them should be ignorant as well as poor."

Marx's critical observation: what Mandeville, "an honest man with a clear mind," had not yet grasped is that "the mechanism of the accumulation process itself not only increases the amount of capital but also the mass of the 'labouring poor,' i.e. the wage-labourers, who turn their labour-power into a force for increasing the valorization of the growing capital, and who are thereby compelled to make their relation of dependence on their own product, as personified in the capitalist, into an eternal relation."

Sir F. M. Eden is then quoted at length (The State of the Poor, 1797), drawing the distinction between those who "toil and spin" and those who "can yet command the produce of industry" by virtue of "civil institutions" that have recognized "that individuals may acquire property by various other means besides the exertion of labour." Eden's key sentence: "It is not the possession of land, or of money, but the command of labour which distinguishes the opulent from the labouring part of the community." Marx's marginal comment is devastating: Eden should have asked "whose creatures 'the civil institutions' were." From Eden's standpoint — "that of juridical illusion" — the law is not a product of material relations of production but rather their cause. Marx juxtaposes Linguet, who refuted Montesquieu's "esprit des lois" with the aphorism: "L'esprit des lois, c'est la propriété" ("The spirit of the laws is property").

In a further note (note 6), Marx writes a polemical excursus on Malthus. The Essay on Population (1798), in its first form, is "nothing more than a schoolboyish, superficial plagiarism" of Defoe, Steuart, Townsend, Franklin, and Wallace, "declaimed in the manner of a sermon, but not containing a single original proposition of Malthus himself." Its sensational success was due to its correspondence with "the interests of a particular party": in the context of the French Revolution's defenders in Britain, the "principle of population" was "greeted jubilantly by the English oligarchy as the great destroyer of all hankerings after a progressive development of humanity." Marx notes the irony that Malthus, though an Anglican parson, had taken the "monastic vow of celibacy" (required for a Cambridge fellowship: "Socios collegiorum maritos esse non permittimus..." — "We do not permit the Fellows of the Colleges to be married"). He traces the "principle of population" to Protestant clerics — Bruckner, Wallace, Townsend, Malthus, Chalmers — and notes that earlier political economy was studied by philosophers (Hobbes, Locke, Hume), businessmen, statesmen, and medical men (Petty, Barbon, Mandeville, Quesnay). He also reproduces the orthodox attacks on Adam Smith for his friendship with Hume and his alleged propagation of atheism.

The Nature of the Wage Rise: The "Golden Chain" and the Limits of Concession

Returning to the main argument, Marx notes that under the assumed conditions — the most favourable to workers — their "relation of dependence on capital takes on forms which are endurable or, as Eden says, 'easy and liberal.'" Instead of becoming more intensive, dependence becomes more extensive: the sphere of exploitation extends with the dimensions of capital and with "the number of people subjected to it." A larger part of the worker's own surplus product comes back as means of payment; workers can extend enjoyments, add to consumption, and lay by a small reserve. But: "these things no more abolish the exploitation of the wage-labourer, and his situation of dependence, than do better clothing, food and treatment, and a larger peculium, in the case of the slave." The slave's peculium (the small property allowed a slave) is the precise and mordant analogy. A rise in the price of labour "only means in fact that the length and weight of the golden chain the wage-labourer has already forged for himself allow it to be loosened somewhat."

The essential point generally overlooked, Marx insists, is the differentia specifica of capitalist production. Labour-power is not purchased to satisfy the buyer's personal needs; the aim is "the valorization of his capital, the production of commodities which contain more labour than he paid for, and therefore contain a portion of value which costs him nothing and is nevertheless realized through the sale of those commodities." The production of surplus-value is the "absolute law" of this mode of production. Labour-power can be sold only insofar as it "preserves and maintains the means of production as capital, reproduces its own value as capital, and provides a source of additional capital in the shape of unpaid labour." Wages, by their very nature, imply that the worker will always provide a certain quantity of unpaid labour. Even at the best of times, an increase in wages means "only a quantitative reduction in the amount of unpaid labour the worker has to supply" — a reduction that "can never go so far as to threaten the system itself."

A footnote (note 7) reinforces this from John Wade: the limit to employment "is the possibility of the employer realizing a profit"; if wages reduce the master's gains below average profit, he will cease to employ workers "or he will only employ them on condition of submission to a reduction of wages."

The Two Alternatives and the Mathematical Formulation

Marx then poses the crucial alternatives. Apart from violent conflicts over the wage rate (where Adam Smith already showed the master generally remains the master), a rise in the price of labour resulting from accumulation implies two scenarios:

Either the price of labour keeps rising because its rise does not interfere with accumulation. Adam Smith is quoted: after profits are diminished, "stock may not only continue to increase, but to increase much faster than before... A great stock, though with small profits, generally increases faster than a small stock with great profits." In this case a reduction in unpaid labour "in no way interferes with the extension of the domain of capital."

Or accumulation slackens because the stimulus of gain is blunted. The rate of accumulation lessens; but "this means that the primary cause of that lessening itself vanishes, i.e. the disproportion between capital and exploitable labour-power. The mechanism of the capitalist production process removes the very obstacles it temporarily creates." The price of labour falls again to a level corresponding with capital's valorization requirements.

The decisive theoretical insight: In the first case, it was not a diminished increase in labour-power or population that caused the excess of capital, but the converse — the increase in capital made exploitable labour-power insufficient. In the second case, it was not an increased rate of population growth that made capital insufficient, but the converse — the relative reduction of capital caused exploitable labour-power, or its price, to be in excess. Therefore: "It is these absolute movements of the accumulation of capital which are reflected as relative movements of the mass of exploitable labour-power, and therefore seem produced by the latter's own independent movement."

Marx then gives his famous mathematical formulation: "the rate of accumulation is the independent, not the dependent variable; the rate of wages is the dependent, not the independent variable." This is the pivot of the entire anti-Malthusian argument. The classical economists and Malthusians invert this, treating population as the independent variable driving wages.

Marx illustrates with an analogy to monetary phenomena: when a crisis produces a general fall in commodity prices, this is expressed as a rise in the relative value of money; in prosperity, a general price rise appears as a fall in money's relative value. The Currency School concludes from high prices that too much money circulates, from low prices too little; their ignorance "is worthily paralleled by the economists, who interpret the above phenomena of accumulation by saying that in one case there are too few, and in the other, too many wage-labourers in existence." The methodological critique is that surface phenomena (apparent scarcity or abundance of workers) are the reflection of underlying absolute movements of capital, not their cause.

The "Natural Law of Population" Dissolved

Marx now distills the law of capitalist production that "really lies at the basis of the supposed 'natural law of population'": the relation between capital, accumulation, and the rate of wages "is nothing other than the relation between the unpaid labour which has been transformed into capital and the additional paid labour necessary to set in motion this additional capital." It is not a relation between two mutually independent magnitudes — capital and the number of the working population — but "at bottom, only the relation between the unpaid and the paid labour of the same working population."

The mechanism: if the unpaid labour accumulated by the capitalist class increases so rapidly that its transformation into capital requires "an extraordinary addition of paid labour," then wages rise and unpaid labour diminishes proportionally. "But as soon as this diminution touches the point at which the surplus labour that nourishes capital is no longer supplied in normal quantity, a reaction sets in: a smaller part of revenue is capitalized, accumulation slows down, and the rising movement of wages comes up against an obstacle." Thus the rise of wages is "confined within limits that not only leave intact the foundations of the capitalist system, but also secure its reproduction on an increasing scale."

The law of capitalist accumulation, "mystified by the economists into a supposed law of nature," in fact expresses that "the very nature of accumulation excludes every diminution in the degree of exploitation of labour, and every rise in the price of labour, which could seriously imperil the continual reproduction, on an ever larger scale, of the capital-relation." The final rhetorical flourish: "Just as man is governed, in religion, by the products of his own brain, so, in capitalist production, he is governed by the products of his own hand." A footnote from Von Thünen is quoted, acknowledging the riddle ("How has the worker been able to pass from being the master of capital — as its creator — to being its slave?") and Marx's verdict: "His answer is simply childish."

Section 2: A Relative Diminution of the Variable Part of Capital Occurs in the Course of Further Accumulation and Concentration

The Turn from the Special to the General Case

Section 2 effects the decisive theoretical transition. Marx notes that according to the economists themselves, it is neither the actual extent of social wealth nor the magnitude of capital already acquired that raises wages, but "only the constant growth of accumulation and the degree of rapidity of that growth" (citing Adam Smith, Book I, Chapter 8). "So far, we have considered only one special phase of this process, that in which the increase of capital occurs while the technical composition of capital remains constant. But the process goes beyond this phase."

The new force is the development of the productivity of social labour, which "becomes the most powerful lever of accumulation." Adam Smith is quoted again: "The same cause... which raises the wages of labour, the increase of stock, tends to increase its productive powers, and to make a smaller quantity of labour produce a greater quantity of work."

Productivity, the Means of Production, and Their Double Role

Marx analyzes what determines the level of social productivity. Apart from natural conditions (soil fertility) and the skill of independent producers, "the level of the social productivity of labour is expressed in the relative extent of the means of production that one worker, during a given time, with the same degree of intensity of labour-power, turns into products." The mass of means of production with which the worker functions increases with productivity.

These means play a double role. The increase of some is a consequence of rising productivity (e.g., more raw material worked up in the same time due to the division of labour and machinery); the increase of others is a condition of rising productivity (machinery, beasts of burden, mineral manures, drain-pipes, buildings, furnaces, means of transport). But whether condition or consequence, "the growing extent of the means of production, as compared with the labour-power incorporated into them, is an expression of the growing productivity of labour." The increase of productivity therefore appears "in the diminution of the mass of labour in proportion to the mass of means of production moved by it, or in the diminution of the subjective factor of the labour process as compared with the objective factor."

This is the technical-composition change. It is reflected in the value-composition by "the increase of the constant constituent of capital at the expense of its variable constituent." Marx gives an illustrative example: originally 50 per cent of capital in means of production and 50 per cent in labour-power; later, with developing productivity, 80 per cent in means of production and 20 per cent in labour-power. He asserts that "this law of the progressive growth of the constant part of capital in comparison with the variable part is confirmed at every step... by the comparative analysis of the prices of commodities, whether we compare different economic epochs or different nations in the same epoch." The relative magnitude of the constant part of price is "in direct proportion to the progress of accumulation," while that of the variable part is "in inverse proportion."

The Critical Distinction Between Value and Mass

Marx then introduces a refinement of major theoretical importance: this diminution of the variable part relative to the constant part, "or, in other words, this change in the composition of the value of the capital, provides only an approximate indication of the change in the composition of its material constituents." The reason is that with increasing productivity, the mass of means of production consumed by labour increases, "but their value in comparison with their mass diminishes." The value of means of production rises absolutely, but not in proportion to the increase in their mass. Therefore "the increase of the difference between constant and variable capital is therefore much less than that of the difference between the mass of the means of production into which the constant capital, and the mass of the labour-power into which the variable capital, is converted." The value-difference grows with the mass-difference, but in a smaller degree. This matters because it prevents the erroneous inference that the value-ratio fully captures the real displacement of labour.

Marx then guards against another misunderstanding: the relative diminution of variable capital "by no means thereby excludes the possibility of a rise in its absolute magnitude." He gives a numerical example: a capital-value divided initially 50% constant/50% variable, later 80%/20%. If the original capital of £6,000 has grown to £18,000, its variable constituent has also increased, from £3,000 to £3,600 — a 20% increase. "But whereas formerly an increase of capital by 20 per cent would have sufficed to raise the demand for labour by 20 per cent, now the original capital needs to be tripled to secure an increase of 20 per cent in the demand for labour." This is the quantitative expression of the deteriorating position of labour: the same rate of accumulation yields a diminishing rate of labour absorption.

The Reciprocity of Accumulation and the Capitalist Mode of Production

Marx then recapitulates the argument of Part IV to establish the presuppositions of rising productivity. The development of social productivity presupposes co-operation on a large scale, the division and combination of labour on that basis, economizing of means of production by concentration, instruments usable only in common (systems of machinery), gigantic natural forces pressed into service, and the transformation of the production process into "a process of the technological application of scientific knowledge." Where production is commodity production — means of production privately owned — co-operation on a large scale "can be realized only through the increase of individual capitals, only in proportion as the social means of production and subsistence are transformed into the private property of capitalists." Large-scale production can therefore occur only in capitalist form. A certain accumulation of capital in individual hands is "the necessary pre-condition for a specifically capitalist mode of production." This is called primitive accumulation [ursprüngliche Akkumulation], "because it is the historical basis, instead of the historical result, of specifically capitalist production." How it originates is postponed — but the point is that it forms the starting-point.

The crucial reciprocity: all methods for raising social productivity that grow up on this basis "are at the same time methods for the increased production of surplus-value or surplus product, which is in its turn the formative element of accumulation." They are "methods for the production of capital by capital, or methods for its accelerated accumulation." The continual reconversion of surplus-value into capital appears as the increasing magnitude of capital entering production; this is the basis of an extended scale of production, of methods for raising productivity, and of accelerated surplus-value production. "If, therefore, a certain degree of accumulation of capital appears as a pre-condition for the specifically capitalist mode of production, the latter reacts back to cause an accelerated accumulation of capital." These two factors "bring about, in the compound ratio of the impulses they give to each other," the change in the technical composition by which the variable component becomes smaller and smaller relative to the constant.

Concentration and Centralization — A Crucial Theoretical Distinction

Marx now enters the conceptual heart of Section 2: the distinction between concentration and centralization, which is frequently conflated in secondary literature but is rigorously separated here.

Concentration (or the "concentration which grows directly out of accumulation") refers to the increase in the size of individual capitals as a direct result of accumulation — the growth of each capital as an aliquot part of the growing social capital. Every individual capital is "a larger or smaller concentration of means of production, with a corresponding command over a larger or smaller army of workers." Every accumulation becomes the means of new accumulation. With increasing mass of wealth functioning as capital, accumulation increases the concentration of that wealth in individual hands, widening the basis of large-scale production. Accumulation increases the number of capitalists (through the division of property within capitalist families, offshoots splitting off from original capitals). Two features characterize this concentration: first, it is limited by the degree of increase of social wealth; second, the part of social capital in each sphere is divided among many competing capitalists. Hence accumulation presents itself "on the one hand as increasing concentration of the means of production, and of the command over labour; and on the other hand as repulsion of many individual capitals from one another."

Centralization is a distinct process. It is the "attraction of capitals," which "no longer means the simple concentration of the means of production and the command over labour, which is identical with accumulation." It is "concentration of capitals already formed, destruction of their individual independence, expropriation of capitalist by capitalist, transformation of many small into few large capitals." It presupposes only "a change in the distribution of already available and already functioning capital." Its field of action is therefore not limited by the absolute growth of social wealth. "Capital grows to a huge mass in a single hand in one place, because it has been lost by many in another place." This is centralization proper, "as distinct from accumulation and concentration."

The laws of centralization cannot be fully developed here, but Marx gives "a few brief factual indications." The battle of competition is fought by cheapening commodities; cheapness depends on productivity of labour, which depends on the scale of production. "Therefore the larger capitals beat the smaller." With developing capitalist production, the minimum amount of capital necessary to carry on a business under normal conditions increases. Smaller capitals crowd into spheres large-scale industry has taken only sporadically or incompletely. "Here competition rages in direct proportion to the number, and in inverse proportion to the magnitude, of the rival capitals. It always ends in the ruin of many small capitalists, whose capitals partly pass into the hands of their conquerors, and partly vanish completely."

Then comes a crucial elaboration on the credit system, inserted by Engels in the fourth German edition (replacing a passage written by Marx and retained in the first three German editions, which is reproduced in the footnote). In its first stages, credit "furtively creeps in as the humble assistant of accumulation, drawing into the hands of individual or associated capitalists by invisible threads the money resources which lie scattered... over the surface of society; but it soon becomes a new and terrible weapon in the battle of competition and is finally transformed into an enormous social mechanism for the centralization of capitals."

The older Marxian passage (in the footnote) adds that centralization "shortens and quickens the transformation of separate processes of production into processes socially combined and carried out on a large scale"; the increasing bulk of individual masses of capital "becomes the material basis of an uninterrupted revolution in the mode of production itself." It discusses the "metamorphosis of old capital" — how the change of composition gets a grip on old capital as it reaches the term of its reproduction period. A steam plough is "an incomparably more efficient means of production than an ordinary plough, but the capital-value laid out in it is an incomparably smaller means for employing men than if it were laid out in ordinary ploughs."

Marx then notes that with developing capitalist production there also develops "the two most powerful levers of centralization — competition and credit." The progress of accumulation increases the material amenable to centralization, while the expansion of capitalist production creates both the social need and the technical means for the immense undertakings requiring prior centralization. The advance of centralization "does not depend in any way on a positive growth in the magnitude of social capital," and this distinguishes it from concentration, "the latter being only another name for reproduction on an extended scale." Centralization may result from "a mere change in the distribution of already existing capitals." Its extreme limit in a branch of industry would be the fusion of all individual capitals into a single capital; in society, the entire social capital united in the hands of a single capitalist or company. Engels adds a note (note 10) that "the latest English and American 'trusts' are already striving to attain this goal."

Centralization "supplements the work of accumulation by enabling industrial capitalists to extend the scale of their operations." Whether accomplished by "the violent method of annexation" or "the smoother process of organizing joint-stock companies," the economic effect is the same: the increased scale of establishments is the starting-point for more comprehensive organization of collective labour, for progressive transformation of isolated processes into "socially combined and scientifically arranged processes of production."

Then a vivid illustration: "The world would still be without railways if it had had to wait until accumulation had got a few individual capitals far enough to be adequate for the construction of a railway. Centralization, however, accomplished this in the twinkling of an eye, by means of joint-stock companies." Centralization intensifies and accelerates the effects of accumulation while simultaneously extending and speeding up "those revolutions in the technical composition of capital which raise its constant portion at the expense of its variable portion, thus diminishing the relative demand for labour."

The Renewal of Old Capital and the Dual Reduction of Demand

The additional capitals formed in the normal course of accumulation "serve above all as vehicles for the exploitation of new inventions and discoveries, and industrial improvements in general." But in time the old capital itself "reaches the point where it has to be renewed in all its aspects, a time when it sheds its skin and is reborn like the other capitals in a perfected technical shape, in which a smaller quantity of labour will suffice to set in motion a larger quantity of machinery and raw material." The absolute reduction in demand for labour "is obviously so much the greater, the higher the degree to which the capitals undergoing this process of renewal are already massed together by virtue of the movement towards centralization."

The double result: "On the one hand, therefore, the additional capital formed in the course of further accumulation attracts fewer and fewer workers in proportion to its magnitude. On the other hand, the old capital periodically reproduced with a new composition repels more and more of the workers formerly employed by it." This is the theoretical engine of the industrial reserve army, fully assembled.

Section 3: The Progressive Production of a Relative Surplus Population or Industrial Reserve Army

The Acceleration of Compositional Change

Section 3 opens by recapitulating the movement thus far: accumulation, "which originally appeared only as its quantitative extension, comes to fruition, as we have seen, through a progressive qualitative change in its composition, i.e. through a continuing increase of its constant component at the expense of its variable component." An editorial note by Engels (note 11) preserves a marginal note of Marx: "if the extension is only quantitative, then for a greater and a smaller capital in the same branch of business the profits are as the magnitudes of the capitals advanced. If the quantitative extension induces a qualitative change, then the rate of profit on the larger capital rises at the same time." This anticipates Volume 3's analysis of the profit rate.

Marx emphasizes that the specifically capitalist mode of production, the development of productivity, and the resulting change in organic composition "do not merely keep pace with the progress of accumulation, or the growth of social wealth. They develop at a much quicker rate," because simple accumulation is accompanied by centralization and because the change in composition of additional capital goes hand in hand with a similar change in the composition of the original capital.

The numerical progression: "If it was originally say 1:1, it now becomes successively 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, 5:1, 7:1, etc., so that as the capital grows, instead of 1/2 its total value, only 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, 1/6, 1/8, etc. is turned into labour-power, and, on the other hand, 2/3, 3/4, 4/5, 5/6, 7/8, into means of production." Since the demand for labour is determined "not by the extent of the total capital but by its variable constituent alone, that demand falls progressively with the growth of the total capital, instead of rising in proportion to it, as was previously assumed." It falls relatively to the magnitude of total capital, "and at an accelerated rate, as this magnitude increases."

The Inversion: Relative Diminution Presented as Absolute Increase

Marx then describes the dialectical inversion that is one of the chapter's most famous passages. The intermediate pauses in which accumulation works as simple extension on a given technical basis are shortened. An accelerated accumulation of total capital, "accelerated in a constantly growing progression," is needed to absorb an additional number of workers — or even to keep employed those already functioning (owing to the constant metamorphosis of old capital). This increasing accumulation and centralization becomes "in its turn a source of new changes in the composition of capital, or in other words of an accelerated diminution of the capital's variable component, as compared with its constant one."

Then the inversion: "This accelerated relative diminution of the variable component, which accompanies the accelerated increase of the total capital and moves more rapidly than this increase, takes the inverse form, at the other pole, of an apparently absolute increase in the working population, an increase which always moves more rapidly than that of the variable capital or the means of employment." But in fact "it is capitalist accumulation itself that constantly produces, and produces indeed in direct relation with its own energy and extent, a relatively redundant working population, i.e. a population which is superfluous to capital's average requirements for its own valorization, and is therefore a surplus population."

This is the definition of the relative surplus population: not absolutely superfluous (those workers are needed in other contexts, or would be needed if labour were rationally distributed), but superfluous relative to capital's average valorization requirements at a given moment.

The Differentiation Across Spheres and the Law of Population

Marx then notes that considering the total social capital, accumulation "sometimes causes periodic changes, and at other times distributes various phases simultaneously over the different spheres of production." In some spheres, composition changes without absolute increase (simple concentration); in others, absolute growth is connected with absolute diminution of the variable component; in others, capital grows on its existing technical basis and attracts additional labour-power. "In all spheres, the increase of the variable part of the capital, and therefore of the number of workers employed by it, is always connected with violent fluctuations and the temporary production of a surplus population," whether in the striking form of extrusion of workers or the less evident form of greater difficulty in absorbing additional population.

The scale and interdependence of these movements intensify: with the magnitude of functioning social capital, the extension of production, the mass of workers set in motion, the development of productivity — "there is also an extension of the scale on which greater attraction of workers by capital is accompanied by their greater repulsion." Hence: "The working population therefore produces both the accumulation of capital and the means by which it is itself made relatively superfluous; and it does this to an extent which is always increasing."

This yields a genuinely revolutionary claim: this is "a law of population peculiar to the capitalist mode of production; and in fact every particular historical mode of production has its own special laws of population, which are historically valid within that particular sphere. An abstract law of population exists only for plants and animals, and even then only in the absence of any historical intervention by man." This is the direct refutation of Malthus: there is no universal natural law of population; there are historically specific laws.

To support this empirically, Marx provides a footnote (note 12) with English census data showing falling employment in many branches (agriculture down from 2,011,447 in 1851 to 1,924,110 in 1861; worsted manufacture down from 102,714 to 79,242; silk weaving from 111,940 to 101,678; hat-making from 15,957 to 13,814; comb-making from 2,038 to 1,478) alongside increases in cotton (371,777 to 456,646) and coal-mining (183,389 to 246,613). The census itself notes: "The increase of labourers is generally greatest, since 1851, in those branches of industry in which machinery has not up to the present been employed with success."

Footnote 13 (added by Engels from the French edition) is a rich survey of classical economists who "suspect rather than understand" the law of the progressive diminution of variable capital. John Barton is credited with the greatest merit (though he confuses constant with fixed and variable with circulating capital). Barton: "The demand for labour depends on the increase of circulating, and not of fixed capital... the whole of the annual savings, added to the fixed capital, would have no effect in increasing the demand for labour." Ricardo: "The same cause which may increase the net revenue of the country may at the same time render the population redundant, and deteriorate the condition of the labourer." And with increasing capital, "the demand [for labour] will be in a diminishing ratio." Richard Jones: "Great fluctuations in the amount of employment, and great suffering may become more frequent as capital itself becomes more plentiful." Ramsay: "Every augmentation... in the national stock destined for reproduction, comes, in the progress of society, to have less and less influence upon the condition of the labourer."

The Reserve Army as Lever and Condition of Accumulation

Marx now develops the positive function of the surplus population — its role as a lever of accumulation, not merely a by-product. "It forms a disposable industrial reserve army, which belongs to capital just as absolutely as if the latter had bred it at its own cost." Independently of the actual increase of population, it creates "a mass of human material always ready for exploitation by capital in the interests of capital's own changing valorization requirements."

With accumulation and the development of productivity, capital's "power of sudden expansion also grows." This growing elasticity has multiple sources: the elasticity of already-functioning capital; the expansion of absolute social wealth (of which capital forms an elastic part); credit, which "under every special stimulus, at once places an unusual part of this wealth at the disposal of production in the form of additional capital"; and the technical conditions of production (machinery, means of transport) that "now make possible a very rapid transformation of masses of surplus product into additional means of production." The mass of social wealth "thrusts itself frantically into old branches of production, whose market suddenly expands, or into newly formed branches, such as railways." In all such cases, "there must be the possibility of suddenly throwing great masses of men into the decisive areas without doing any damage to the scale of production in other spheres. The surplus population supplies these masses."

Thus the industrial reserve army is not merely a consequence of accumulation but a condition of the specifically cyclical path of modern industry: "a decennial cycle (interrupted by smaller oscillations) of periods of average activity, production at high pressure, crisis, and stagnation, depends on the constant formation, the greater or less absorption, and the re-formation of the industrial reserve army or surplus population." And conversely: "the varying phases of the industrial cycle recruit the surplus population, and become one of the most energetic agencies for its reproduction."

Marx contrasts this with the infancy of capitalist production, when composition changed only gradually, so that proportional demand for labour roughly corresponded to accumulation. Advance was slow, "yet it came up against a natural barrier in the shape of the exploitable working population; this barrier could only be swept away by the violent means we shall discuss later." The expansion by fits and starts is precondition for sudden contraction; the latter evokes the former; but the former "is impossible without disposable human material, without an increase in the number of workers, which must occur independently of the absolute growth of the population." This increase is effected by "the simple process that constantly 'sets free' a part of the working class; by methods which lessen the number of workers employed in proportion to the increased production." Hence: "Modern industry's whole form of motion therefore depends on the constant transformation of a part of the working population into unemployed or semi-employed 'hands.'"

The Superficiality of Political Economy and the Credit Illusion

Marx then attacks "the superficiality of political economy" for viewing the expansion and contraction of credit as the cause of periodic alternations, "whereas it is a mere symptom of them." He offers an analogy to celestial mechanics: "Just as the heavenly bodies always repeat a certain movement, once they have been flung into it, so also does social production, once it has been flung into this movement of alternate expansion and contraction. Effects become causes in their turn, and the various vicissitudes of the whole process, which always reproduces its own conditions, take on the form of periodicity." Once periodicity is consolidated, even political economy sees "that the production of a relative surplus population... is a necessary condition for modern industry."

The French edition inserts a substantial passage (in the footnote) dating the modern industrial cycle: it required mechanical industry to strike deep roots, foreign trade to predominate, the world market to annex the New World, Asia, and Australia, and a sufficient number of industrial nations to enter the arena. The cycles embrace years and culminate in a general crisis. "Until now the duration of these cycles has been ten or eleven years, but there is no reason to consider this duration as constant. On the contrary, we ought to conclude... that the duration is variable, and that the length of the cycles will gradually diminish."

The Testimony of Apologists — Merivale and Malthus

H. Merivale (former Professor of Political Economy at Oxford, later at the Colonial Office) is quoted: if a nation were to emigrate "some hundreds of thousands of superfluous arms," at the first returning demand for labour there would be a deficiency, since "it takes, at all events, the space of a generation to replace the loss of adult labour." Manufacturers need "hands ready by them," able to increase and slacken activity according to the market. Even Malthus, in his "narrow fashion," recognizes that a surplus population is a necessity of modern industry — though he explains it not by relative superfluity but by "excessive growth." Malthus is quoted: prudential habits might injure a manufacturing country; "a country is always liable to an increase in the quantity of the funds for the maintenance of labour faster than the increase of population." Marx's sarcasm is scathing: political economy, having "declared that the constant production of a relative surplus population of workers is a necessity of capitalist accumulation, very aptly adopts the shape of an old maid" and puts into capital's mouth the words: "We manufacturers do what we can for you, whilst we are increasing that capital on which you must subsist, and you must do the rest by accommodating your numbers to the means of subsistence." Malthus's "beautiful trinity" — over-production, over-population, and over-consumption — are "three very delicate monsters, indeed!"

The Intensive and Extensive Exploitation of Labour-Power

Marx then qualifies the earlier assumption that increase or diminution of variable capital corresponds precisely with increase or diminution of the number of workers employed. "The number of workers under the command of capital may remain the same, or even fall, while the variable capital increases." This occurs if the individual worker provides more labour, so that wages increase although the price of labour remains the same or falls — only more slowly than the mass of labour rises. "Increase of variable capital, in this case, becomes an index of more labour, but not of more workers employed."

Marx identifies a structural motive: "It is the absolute interest of every capitalist to extort a given quantity of labour out of a smaller rather than a greater number of workers, if the cost is about the same." In the latter case, outlay of constant capital increases proportionally to the mass of labour; in the former, the increase is much smaller. "The more extended the scale of production, the more decisive is this motive. Its force increases with the accumulation of capital."

This leads to the analysis of how the same variable capital sets more labour in motion: 1. Through greater extensive or intensive exploitation of each individual labour-power; 2. By buying a greater mass of labour-power through progressively replacing skilled workers by less skilled, mature by immature, male by female, adults by young persons or children.

The triple result: with accumulation, a larger variable capital sets more labour in motion without enlisting more workers; a variable capital of the same magnitude sets more labour in motion with the same mass of labour-power; and a greater number of inferior labour-powers is set in motion by displacement of more skilled labour-powers.

The Acceleration of Surplus Population Production and the "Over-work/Idleness" Dialectic

Marx then draws a striking conclusion: "The production of a relative surplus population, or the setting free of workers, therefore proceeds still more rapidly than the technical transformation of the process of production that accompanies the advance of accumulation and is accelerated by it, and more rapidly than the corresponding diminution of the variable part of capital as compared with the constant."

The mechanism is now fully dialectical: "The over-work of the employed part of the working class swells the ranks of its reserve, while, conversely, the greater pressure that the reserve by its competition exerts on the employed workers forces them to submit to over-work and subjects them to the dictates of capital." Thus: "The condemnation of one part of the working class to enforced idleness by the over-work of the other part, and vice versa, becomes a means of enriching the individual capitalists, and accelerates at the same time the production of the industrial reserve army on a scale corresponding with the progress of social accumulation."

A footnote (note 17) cites a Blackburn pamphlet from the cotton famine of 1863, denouncing over-work resulting from the Factory Act: adult operatives asked to work 12 to 13 hours per day "while there are hundreds who are compelled to be idle who would willingly work partial time." The pamphlet asks whether overtime is "likely to create a good feeling between masters and servants." Marx also quotes the Essay on Trade and Commerce (1770), which grasps "with unerring bourgeois instinct" that the relative surplus population emboldens employed workers: "Whenever from an extraordinary demand for manufactures, labour grows scarce, the labourers feel their own consequence, and will make their masters feel it likewise." Marx's dry comment: "Those fellows were actually asking for a wage-increase!"

The General Movement of Wages and the Refutation of the Dogma

Marx then states the general conclusion: "Taking them as a whole, the general movements of wages are exclusively regulated by the expansion and contraction of the industrial reserve army, and this in turn corresponds to the periodic alternations of the industrial cycle." Wages are "not therefore determined by the variations of the absolute numbers of the working population, but by the varying proportions in which the working class is divided into an active army and a reserve army, by the increase or diminution in the relative amount of the surplus population, by the extent to which it is alternately absorbed and set free." The appropriate law for modern industry is "the law of the regulation of the demand and supply of labour by the alternate expansion and contraction of capital, i.e. by the level of capital's valorization requirements at the relevant moment."

Marx then states the economic-fiction argument in detail. According to the economists, wages rise as a result of accumulation; higher wages stimulate population growth until the labour-market is over-supplied and capital becomes insufficient; wages fall; population is decimated by falling wages; capital again in excess; and so on. "This would indeed be a beautiful form of motion for developed capitalist production! Before the rise in wages could produce any positive increase of the population really fit for work, the deadline would long since have passed within which the industrial campaign would have to have been carried through, and the battle fought to a conclusive finish."

The empirical refutation: Between 1849 and 1859 a rise of wages, practically merely nominal (accompanied by falling corn prices), took place in English agricultural districts. In Wiltshire, weekly wages rose from 7s. to 8s.; in Dorsetshire from 7s. or 8s. to 9s. Marx notes the statistical illusion: "The lower the wage, the higher is the proportion in which even a very insignificant increase is expressed." A 20s. wage rising to 22s. is a 10% rise; a 7s. wage rising to 9s. is 28½% — "which sounds very fine." The Economist prattled about "a general and substantial advance." But what did the farmers do? "No, they introduced more machinery, and in a moment the labourers were 'redundant' again to a degree satisfactory even to the farmers. There was now 'more capital' laid out in agriculture than before, and in a more productive form. With this the demand for labour fell, not only relatively, but absolutely."

Confusion of General Wage Laws with Local Distribution

Marx distinguishes the laws regulating the general movement of wages (the ratio between the total labour-power and the total social capital) from the laws distributing the working population over different spheres. If accumulation is especially active in a special sphere and attracts additional capital, demand for labour rises and wages rise. Higher wages draw a larger part of the working population into the favoured sphere until it is glutted, and wages fall back to average or below. The influx ceases and gives place to an outflow. "Here the political economist thinks he can grasp the situation... But he really sees only the local oscillations of the labour-market in a particular sphere of production."

The Reserve Army as the Context of the Supply-and-Demand Law

Marx now states the decisive conclusion about the labour market: "The industrial reserve army, during the periods of stagnation and average prosperity, weighs down the active army of workers; during the periods of over-production and feverish activity, it puts a curb on their pretensions. The relative surplus population is therefore the background against which the law of the demand and supply of labour does its work. It confines the field of action of this law to the limits absolutely convenient to capital's drive to exploit and dominate the workers."

The "Compensation" Theory Demolished

Marx returns to "one of the great exploits of economic apologetics." When machinery transforms variable into constant capital, "the economic apologist interprets this operation, which 'fixes' capital and by that very act 'sets free' workers, in exactly the opposite way, pretending that capital is thereby set free for the workers." Marx exposes the effrontery: not only are the workers turned out by machines set free, "but so are their future replacements in the rising generation, as well as the additional contingent which, with the usual extension of business on its old basis, would regularly be absorbed." These are all "set free" and every new bit of capital can take advantage of them. "Whether it attracts them or others, the effect on the general demand for labour will be nil, if this capital is just sufficient to take out of the market as many workers as the machines threw into it." The impulse additional capital would otherwise give to the general demand for labour "is therefore in every case neutralized until the supply of workers thrown out of employment by the machine has been exhausted."

Hence: capital acts on both sides at once. If accumulation increases the demand for labour, it also increases the supply of workers by "setting them free," while the pressure of the unemployed compels the employed "to furnish more labour, and therefore makes the supply of labour to a certain extent independent of the supply of workers." The movement of the law of supply and demand "completes the despotism of capital."

The Political Conclusion: Unions, the "Sacred Law," and the Colonies

Marx then reaches the political implications. As soon as workers learn "the secret of why it happens that the more they work, the more alien wealth they produce, and that the more the productivity of their labour increases, the more does their very function as a means for the valorization of capital become precarious"; as soon as they discover that "the degree of intensity of the competition amongst themselves depends wholly on the pressure of the relative surplus population"; as soon as, by setting up trade unions, "they try to organize planned co-operation between the employed and the unemployed in order to obviate or to weaken the ruinous effects of this natural law of capitalist production on their class," — "so soon does capital and its sycophant, political economy, cry out at the infringement of the 'eternal' and so to speak 'sacred' law of supply and demand."

But conversely: "as soon as (in the colonies, for example) adverse circumstances prevent the creation of an industrial reserve army, and with it the absolute dependence of the working class upon the capitalist class, capital, along with its platitudinous Sancho Panza, rebels against the 'sacred' law of supply and demand, and tries to make up for its inadequacies by forcible means." This is a key dialectical point: capital invokes the "law" only when it serves capital's interests; when the reserve army is absent, capital abandons the law and resorts to force. (This anticipates Marx's later analysis of Wakefield's colonization theory, alluded to via the reference to the colonies.)

Section 4: Different Forms of Existence of the Relative Surplus Population — The General Law of Capitalist Accumulation

The Three Permanent Forms

Marx opens Section 4 by noting that "the relative surplus population exists in all kinds of forms. Every worker belongs to it during the time when he is only partially employed or wholly unemployed." Leaving aside the large-scale, periodically recurring forms impressed by the industrial cycle — acute in crises, chronic in slack times — there are three forms it always possesses: the floating, the latent, and the stagnant.

The floating form exists in "the centres of modern industry — factories, workshops, ironworks, mines, etc." Here workers are "sometimes repelled, sometimes attracted again in greater masses, so that the number of those employed increases on the whole, although in a constantly decreasing proportion to the scale of production."

Marx then makes a striking observation about age: in factories and large workshops, "large numbers of male workers are employed up to the age of maturity, but not beyond." Once mature, only a small number continue to find employment; "the majority are regularly dismissed. This majority forms an element of the floating surplus population, which grows with the extension of those branches of industry." Some emigrate, following capital that has itself emigrated. A further consequence: "the female population grows more rapidly than the male — witness England." The contradiction: "That the natural increase of the number of workers does not satisfy the requirements of the accumulation of capital, and yet, at the same time, exceeds those requirements, is a contradiction inherent in capital's very movement. Capital demands more youthful workers, fewer adults."

This contradiction is "no more glaring than the other contradiction, namely that a shortage of 'hands' is complained of, while, at the same time, many thousands are out of work, because the division of labour chains them to a particular branch of industry." A footnote (note 19) cites the Factory Report of 1866: "It does not appear absolutely true to say that demand will always produce supply just at the moment when it is needed. It has not always done so with labour, for much machinery has been idle last year for want of hands." This is a direct empirical refutation of Say's Law as applied to labour.

Marx then discusses the rapid consumption of labour-power: "the worker has already more or less completely lived himself out when he is only half-way through his life. He falls into the ranks of the surplus population, or is thrust down from a higher to a lower step in the scale." The mortality statistics: Dr Lee of Manchester found the average age at death of the Manchester upper middle class was 38 years, of the labouring class 17; at Liverpool, 35 against 15. "It thus appeared that the well-to-do classes had a lease of life which was more than double the value of that which fell to the lot of the less favoured citizens." Under these circumstances, the absolute increase of this section of the proletariat "must take a form which swells their numbers, despite the rapid wastage of their individual elements. Hence, rapid replacement of one generation of workers by another (this law does not hold for the other classes of the population). This social requirement is met by early marriages... and by the premium that the exploitation of the workers' children sets on their production."

The latent form. As soon as capitalist production takes possession of agriculture, "the demand for a rural working population falls absolutely, while the accumulation of the capital employed in agriculture advances, without this repulsion being compensated for by a greater attraction of workers, as is the case in non-agricultural industries." Part of the agricultural population is "constantly on the point of passing over into an urban or manufacturing proletariat, and on the look-out for opportunities to complete this transformation." There is thus "a constant flow from this source of the relative surplus population. But the constant movement towards the towns presupposes, in the countryside itself, a constant latent surplus population." The wages of the agricultural labourer are therefore "reduced to a minimum, and he always stands with one foot already in the swamp of pauperism."

A footnote (note 21) provides census data: in 1861, 781 towns contained 10,960,998 inhabitants, villages and country parishes 9,105,226. Between 1851 and 1861, country population increased half a million, town population by one and a half million (1,554,067). Country parishes grew 6.5%, towns 17.3%. "Three-fourths of the total increase of population has taken place in the towns."

The stagnant form. "This forms a part of the active labour army, but with extremely irregular employment. Hence it offers capital an inexhaustible reservoir of disposable labour-power. Its conditions of life sink below the average normal level of the working class, and it is precisely this which makes it a broad foundation for special branches of capitalist exploitation. It is characterized by a maximum of working time and a minimum of wages." Its chief form is "domestic industry." It is constantly recruited from workers in large-scale industry and agriculture who have become redundant, "and especially from those decaying branches of industry where handicraft is giving way to manufacture, and manufacture to machinery." Its extent grows with accumulation. It "forms at the same time a self-reproducing and self-perpetuating element of the working class, taking a proportionally greater part in the general increase of that class than the other elements." Marx adds a crucial demographic law: "not only the number of births and deaths, but the absolute size of families, stands in inverse proportion to the level of wages, and therefore to the amount of the means of subsistence at the disposal of different categories of worker." This law "would sound absurd to savages, or even to civilized colonists. It calls to mind the boundless reproduction of animals individually weak and constantly hunted down." A footnote (note 22) quotes Adam Smith ("Poverty seems favourable to generation") and Galiani ("God has decreed that the men who carry on the most useful crafts should be born in abundant numbers").

Pauperism: The Lowest Sediment

"Finally, the lowest sediment of the relative surplus population dwells in the sphere of pauperism." Apart from vagabonds, criminals, prostitutes — "the actual lumpenproletariat" — this stratum consists of three categories: 1. Those able to work. The statistics of English pauperism show the quantity of paupers increases with every crisis and diminishes with every revival. 2. Orphans and pauper children. "Candidates for the industrial reserve army," enrolled in times of prosperity. 3. The demoralized, the ragged, and those unable to work — chiefly people who succumb to "incapacity for adaptation" resulting from the division of labour; those who have lived beyond the average life-span; and "the victims of industry, whose number increases with the growth of dangerous machinery, of mines, chemical works, etc., the mutilated, the sickly, the widows, etc."

Marx's formula: "Pauperism is the hospital of the active labour-army and the dead weight of the industrial reserve army." Its production is included in that of the relative surplus population; its necessity is implied by theirs; along with the surplus population, "pauperism forms a condition of capitalist production, and of the capitalist development of wealth." It forms part of the faux frais of capitalist production — "but capital usually knows how to transfer these from its own shoulders to those of the working class and the petty bourgeoisie."

The Absolute General Law Stated

Now Marx states the law in its full form. It is a series of escalating proportions:

  • "The greater the social wealth, the functioning capital, the extent and energy of its growth, and therefore also the greater the absolute mass of the proletariat and the productivity of its labour, the greater is the industrial reserve army."
  • "The same causes which develop the expansive power of capital, also develop the labour-power at its disposal. The relative mass of the industrial reserve army thus increases with the potential energy of wealth."
  • "But the greater this reserve army in proportion to the active labour-army, the greater is the mass of a consolidated surplus population, whose misery is in inverse ratio to the amount of torture it has to undergo in the form of labour."
  • "The more extensive, finally, the pauperized sections of the working class and the industrial reserve army, the greater is official pauperism."

"This is the absolute general law of capitalist accumulation." And immediately: "Like all other laws, it is modified in its working by many circumstances, the analysis of which does not concern us here."

The political conclusion: "We can now understand the foolishness of the economic wisdom which preaches to the workers that they should adapt their numbers to the valorization requirements of capital. The mechanism of capitalist production and accumulation itself constantly effects this adjustment. The first word of this adaptation is the creation of a relative surplus population, or industrial reserve army. Its last word is the misery of constantly expanding strata of the active army of labour, and the dead weight of pauperism."

The Dialectical Inversion Stated

Marx then states the inversion in its most explicit form. On the basis of capitalism — "a system in which the worker does not employ the means of production, but the means of production employ the worker" — the law by which an increasing quantity of means of production may be set in motion by a diminishing expenditure of human power, thanks to rising productivity, "undergoes a complete inversion, and is expressed thus: the higher the productivity of labour, the greater is the pressure of the workers on the means of employment, the more precarious therefore becomes the condition for their existence, namely the sale of their own labour-power for the increase of alien wealth." The fact that means of production and productivity increase more rapidly than the productive population "expresses itself, therefore, under capitalism, in the inverse form that the working population always increases more rapidly than the valorization requirements of capital."

The Dialectical Inversion of All Methods of Production

Marx then recapitulates from Part IV: "within the capitalist system all methods for raising the social productivity of labour are put into effect at the cost of the individual worker; that all means for the development of production undergo a dialectical inversion so that they become means of domination and exploitation of the producers; they distort the worker into a fragment of a man, they degrade him to the level of an appendage of a machine, they destroy the actual content of his labour by turning it into a torment; they alienate [entfremden] from him the intellectual potentialities of the labour process in the same proportion as science is incorporated in it as an independent power; they deform the conditions under which he works, subject him during the labour process to a despotism the more hateful for its meanness; they transform his life-time into working-time, and drag his wife and child beneath the wheels of the juggernaut of capital."

But — and this is the dialectical pivot — "all methods for the production of surplus-value are at the same time methods of accumulation, and every extension of accumulation becomes, conversely, a means for the development of those methods. It follows therefore that in proportion as capital accumulates, the situation of the worker, be his payment high or low, must grow worse."

The Prometheus Metaphor and the Absolute Law

The law of the relative surplus population "rivets the worker to capital more firmly than the wedges of Hephaestus held Prometheus to the rock. It makes an accumulation of misery a necessary condition, corresponding to the accumulation of wealth." And the iconic formulation of the whole chapter: "Accumulation of wealth at one pole is, therefore, at the same time accumulation of misery, the torment of labour, slavery, ignorance, brutalization and moral degradation at the opposite pole, i.e. on the side of the class that produces its own product as capital."

The Antagonistic Character Registered by Political Economy

"This antagonistic character of capitalist accumulation is enunciated in various forms by political economists, although they lump it together with other phenomena which are admittedly to some extent analogous, but nevertheless essentially distinct, since they appear only in pre-capitalist modes of production." Marx then presents a gallery:

  • Ortes, the Venetian monk, regards the antagonism as "a universal natural law of social wealth": "the abundance of wealth with some people is always equal to the lack of wealth with others"; "The great riches of a small number are always accompanied by the absolute deprivation of the essential necessities of life for many others. The wealth of a nation corresponds with its population, and its misery corresponds with its wealth. Diligence in some compels idleness in others." (Ortes also wrote: "Instead of projecting useless systems for achieving the happiness of peoples, I shall limit myself to investigating the reasons for their unhappiness.") The difference from Marx: Ortes treats this as a natural law; Marx as an historical one. And Ortes drew a religious conclusion (justifying charity and monasteries), while the Protestant Townsend drew the opposite.

  • Townsend (the High Church parson, quoted from A Dissertation on the Poor Laws, 1786) glorified misery as a necessary condition of wealth "in a thoroughly brutal way": legal constraint is troublesome, "whereas hunger is not only a peaceable, silent, unremitted pressure, but as the most natural motive to industry and labour, it calls forth the most powerful exertions." Everything depends on making hunger permanent, which is provided for by the "principle of population." Townsend: "It seems to be a law of Nature that the poor should be to a certain degree improvident... that there may always be some to fulfil the most servile, the most sordid, and the most ignoble offices in the community." The Poor Law "tends to destroy the harmony and beauty, the symmetry and order of that system which God and Nature have established in the world." Marx's comment: "If the Venetian monk found in the fatal destiny that makes misery eternal a justification for the existence of Christian charity, celibacy, monasteries and pious foundations, the beneficed Protestant finds in it a pretext for condemning the laws by which the poor possessed a right to a miserable amount of public relief." A footnote notes that Malthus borrowed "the greater part of his doctrine from Sir James Steuart," who wished to multiply needs as a stimulus to labour — but "unlike our fat benefice-holder, he does not conclude from this that the wage-labourer must always go fasting."

  • Storch: "The progress of social wealth begets this useful class of society... which performs the most wearisome, the vilest, the most disgusting functions, which, in a word, takes on its shoulders all that is disagreeable and servile in life, and procures thus for other classes leisure, serenity of mind and conventional dignity of character." Storch asks what the actual advantage of capitalist civilization is, compared with barbarism, and finds only one answer: security.

  • Sismondi: "every worker can produce every day much more than he needs to consume. But at the same time, while his labour produces wealth, that wealth would, were he called on to consume it himself, make him less fit for labour." And: "Exertion today is separated from its recompense; it is not the same man that first works, and then reposes; but it is because the one works that the other rests... The indefinite multiplication of the productive powers of labour can have no other result than the increase of luxury and enjoyment on the part of the idle rich." Sismondi registers the antagonism but laments it as a fall from a prior harmony.

  • Destutt de Tracy, "that fish-blooded bourgeois doctrinaire," makes the point "in the most brutal fashion": "In poor nations the people are comfortable, in rich nations they are generally poor."

Section 5: Illustrations of the General Law of Capitalist Accumulation

(a) England from 1846 to 1866

Marx chooses England as "the classical example" for three reasons: it holds the foremost place in the world market; capitalist production is fully developed only there; and "the introduction of the free-trade millennium since 1846 has cut off the last retreat of vulgar economics." The period is "as favourable for the study of capitalist accumulation" as if "Fortunatus's purse" had been discovered.

Population. Although absolute growth was great, the relative increase fell constantly. Census figures for average annual increase of the population of England and Wales (per cent): 1811–21: 1.533; 1821–31: 1.446; 1831–41: 1.326; 1841–51: 1.216; 1851–61: 1.141.

Wealth. Increase of profits liable to income tax in Great Britain from 1853 to 1864 (farmers and some categories excluded) was 50.47 per cent (annual average 4.58 per cent), while population increased about 12 per cent. Rent of land subject to taxation increased 38 per cent (31½ per cent annually). Categories with greatest increase (percentage excess of annual income 1864 over 1853, and annual percentage increase): Houses 38.60/3.50; Quarries 84.76/7.70; Mines 68.85/6.26; Ironworks 39.92/3.63; Fisheries 57.37/5.21; Gasworks 126.02/11.45; Railways 83.29/7.57.

Rates accelerating: Incomes from profits increased 1853–57 at 1.73% a year; 1857–61 at 2.74%; 1861–64 at 9.30%. Sum of incomes under income tax: 1856: £307,068,898; 1859: £328,127,416; 1862: £351,745,241; 1863: £359,142,897; 1864: £362,462,279; 1865: £385,530,020. A footnote notes these figures are "false" absolutely — some £100,000,000 of income is not declared every year, with systematic fraud documented by the Inland Revenue Commissioners.

Concentration and centralization. Between 1851 and 1861, farms of less than 100 acres fell from 31,583 to 26,597 (5,016 thrown together into larger farms). From 1815 to 1825 no personal estate over £1,000,000 came under succession duty; from 1825 to 1855, eight did; from 1856 to June 1859, four did. Income Tax Schedule D (profits) analysis for 1864 and 1865: taxable incomes amounted in 1864 to £95,844,222 (308,416 persons out of 23,891,009), in 1865 to £105,435,579 (332,431 out of 24,127,003). Distribution table shows concentration: the top categories (over £X) grew.

Production. Coal: 1855: 61,543,079 tons, value £16,113,167; 1864: 92,787,873 tons, value £23,197,968. Pig-iron: 1855: 3,218,154 tons, value £8,045,385; 1864: 4,767,951 tons, value £11,919,877. Railways: 1854: 8,054 miles, paid-up capital £286,068,794; 1864: 12,789 miles, capital £425,719,613. Total imports and exports: 1854: £268,210,145; 1865: £489,923,285. Exports: 1846: £58,842,377; 1849: £63,596,052; 1856: £115,826,948; 1860: £135,842,817; 1865: £165,862,402; 1866: £188,917,563. The Registrar-General: "Rapidly as the population has increased, it has not kept pace with the progress of industry and wealth."

Gladstone. Marx juxtaposes two Gladstone speeches. In 1843 Gladstone said: "It is one of the most melancholy features in the social state of this country, that we see... while there is at this moment a decrease in the consuming powers of the people, an increase of the pressure of privations and distress... there is at the same time a constant accumulation of wealth in the upper classes, an increase of the luxuriousness of their habits." Twenty years later, in his Budget speech of 16 April 1863: "From 1842 to 1852 the taxable income of the country increased by 6 per cent... In the eight years from 1853 to 1861 it had increased from the basis taken in 1853 by 20 per cent! The fact is so astonishing as to be almost incredible... this intoxicating augmentation of wealth and power... entirely confined to classes of property... must be of indirect benefit to the labouring population, because it cheapens commodities of general consumption. While the rich have been growing richer, the poor have been growing less poor. At any rate, whether the extremes of poverty are less, I do not presume to say." Marx's critique: "How lame an anticlimax! If the working class has remained 'poor', only 'less poor' in proportion as it produces for the wealthy class 'an intoxicating augmentation of wealth and power', then it has remained relatively just as poor. If the extremes of poverty have not lessened, they have increased, because the extremes of wealth have."

Marx demonstrates the "cheapening" claim is false: official statistics (e.g., London Orphan Asylum accounts) show an increase in price of 20 per cent over the last ten years (comparing 1860–62 with 1851–53), and 1863–65 saw a progressive rise in the price of meat, butter, milk, sugar, salt, coal, and other necessaries.

Gladstone's next Budget speech (7 April 1864) is a "Pindaric dithyramb": he speaks of masses "on the border of pauperism," of branches of trade in which "wages have not increased," and sums up the happiness of the working class: "human life is but, in nine cases out of ten, a struggle for existence." A footnote (note 40) quotes Boileau to characterize Gladstone's contradictions: "Voilà l'homme en effet. Il va du blanc au noir..." ("Such is the man: he goes from black to white...")

Fawcett. Professor Fawcett declares roundly: "I do not, of course, deny that money wages have been augmented by this increase of capital... but this apparent advantage is to a great extent lost, because many of the necessaries of life are becoming dearer... the rich grow rapidly richer, whilst there is no perceptible advance in the comfort enjoyed by the industrial classes... They become almost the slaves of the tradesman, to whom they owe money."

Pauperism. Official list of paupers in England: 1855: 851,369; 1856: 877,767; 1865: 971,433. As a result of the cotton famine: 1863: 1,079,382; 1864: 1,014,978. The crisis of 1866 (London most severely hit) created an increase of pauperism of 19.5% in 1866 over 1865, and 24.4% over 1864, with a still greater increase in early 1867. Two points: the rise and fall of paupers reflects the industrial cycle; and official statistics "become more and more misleading... in proportion as, with the accumulation of capital, the class struggle develops, and hence the class-consciousness of the workers as well." Marx notes the growing horror in which workers hold the workhouse, "that place of punishment for poverty."

(b) The Badly Paid Strata of the British Industrial Working Class

Marx draws on Dr Edward Smith's investigations for the Privy Council during the cotton famine of 1862–63. Smith concluded that "to avert starvation diseases" the daily food of an average woman ought to contain at least 3,900 grains of carbon and 180 grains of nitrogen; the daily food of an average man at least 4,300 grains of carbon and 200 grains of nitrogen. For the weekly average of adult men and women, at least 28,600 grains of carbon and 1,330 grains of nitrogen. The cotton workers' consumption in December 1862 was 29,211 grains of carbon and 1,295 grains of nitrogen — the nitrogen below the minimum.

In 1863 the Privy Council ordered an investigation into the worst-nourished part of the English working class. Dr Simon (Privy Council medical officer) chose Dr Smith, whose inquiry covered agricultural labourers, silk-weavers, needlewomen, kid-glovers, stocking-weavers, glove-weavers, and shoemakers. It selected in each category the most healthy families and those in comparatively the best circumstances. Result: "in only one of the examined classes of indoor operatives did the average nitrogen supply just exceed, while in another it nearly reached, the estimated standard of bare sufficiency... and in two classes there was defect — in one, a very large defect — of both nitrogen and carbon." Among agricultural families, more than a fifth had less than the estimated sufficiency of carbonaceous food, more than a third less than the sufficiency of nitrogenous food; in Berkshire, Oxfordshire, and Somersetshire, insufficiency of nitrogenous food was the average local diet.

Just under one-half (6½) of the categories had no beer; 28 per cent no milk. Liquid nourishment varied from 7 ounces (needlewomen) to 24½ (stocking-makers). Bread: 7½ lb (needlewomen) to 11½ lb (shoemakers); average 9.9 lb per adult weekly. Sugar (treacle etc.): 4 ounces (kid-glovers) to 11 (stocking-makers); average 8 ounces per adult per week. Butter (fat etc.): average 5 ounces per adult. Meat (bacon etc.): 7½ ounces (silk-weavers) to 18½ (kid-glovers); average 13.6 ounces. Weekly cost of food per adult: silk-weavers 2s. 2½d., needlewomen 2s. 7d., kid-glovers 2s. 9½d., shoemakers 2s. 7½d., stocking-weavers 2s. 6½d.; for silk-weavers of Macclesfield only 1s. 8½d.

Dr Simon's General Health Report is quoted at length on the "sanitary context": privation of food is reluctantly borne; "long before insufficiency of diet is a matter of hygienic concern... the household will have been utterly destitute of material comfort; clothing and fuel will have been even scantier than food." The home will be where shelter can be cheapest bought — "in quarters where commonly there is least fruit of sanitary supervision, least drainage, least scavenging, least suppression of public nuisances." And crucially: "the poverty to which they advert is not the deserved poverty of idleness. In all cases it is the poverty of working populations." Simon concludes: "on a very large scale the nominal self-support can be only a circuit, longer or shorter, to pauperism."

Housing. Marx turns to the housing situation, where "every unprejudiced observer sees that the greater the centralization of the means of production, the greater is the corresponding concentration of workers within a given space; and therefore the more quickly capitalist accumulation takes place, the more miserable the housing situation of the working class." Town "improvements" (demolition of poorly built districts, erection of palaces for banks and warehouses, widening of streets for business traffic, luxury carriages, tramways) "obviously drive the poor away into even worse and more crowded corners." House prices stand in inverse ratio to quality; "the mines of misery are exploited by house speculators with more profit and at less cost than the mines of Potosi were ever exploited." The antagonistic character of accumulation is "here so evident that even the official English reports... teem with heterodox onslaughts on 'property and its rights.'" From 1847 to 1864, no less than ten Acts of Parliament on sanitation were passed. Dr Simon's 1865 report: "Speaking generally, it may be said that the evils are uncontrolled in England." A footnote (note 50) quotes S. Laing: "In no particular have the rights of persons been so avowedly and shamefully sacrificed to the rights of property as in regard to the lodging of the labouring class."

Dr Simon's remark on the moral consequences of overcrowding is quoted: "In its higher degrees it almost necessarily involves such negation of all delicacy, such unclean confusion of bodies and bodily functions, such exposure of animal and sexual nakedness, as is rather bestial than human... To children who are born under its curse, it must often be a very baptism into infamy."

Dr Hunter (in the Seventh and Eighth Reports on Public Health, 1865 and 1866): "there are about twenty large colonies in London, of about 10,000 persons each, whose miserable condition exceeds almost anything I have seen elsewhere in England." "It is not too much to say that life in parts of London and Newcastle is infernal." Rents have become so heavy that few labouring men can afford more than one room. The land price in London is always very high relative to yearly revenue, so buyers speculate on selling at a "jury price" or pocketing an increase from proximity to large-scale undertakings. "There is a regular trade in the purchase of 'fag-ends of leases.'" As a result of railway construction, "the spectacle has lately been seen in the East of London of a number of families wandering about some Saturday night with their scanty worldly goods on their backs, without any resting place but the workhouse." Displaced workers try to stay near their workshops, "parting their two-room tenements into single rooms, and crowding even those." In one Strand parish, the Public Health Officer reckoned 581 persons per acre (including half the width of the Thames).

Marx's ironic comment on "improvements": the owner of land and houses, when expropriated by improvements, "does not just receive full compensation. He must also be comforted, both according to human law and divine law, by receiving a substantial profit in return for his compulsory 'abstinence'. The worker, with his wife and child and chattels, is thrown out into the street, and, if he crowds in too large numbers near districts where the local authority insists on decency, he is prosecuted in the name of public health!"

At the beginning of the nineteenth century, no English town (except London) had more than 100,000 inhabitants; only five had more than 50,000. Now there are twenty-eight towns with more than 50,000. The result is that "the old close-packed little towns are now centres built round on every side, open nowhere to air." The houses pass to the poor, and "a population, for which the houses were not intended and quite unfit, has been created, whose surroundings are truly degrading to the adults and ruinous to the children. The more rapidly capital accumulates in an industrial or commercial town, the more rapidly flows the stream of exploitable human material, the more miserable are the improvised dwellings of the workers."

Newcastle-on-Tyne. Not less than 34,000 persons live there in single rooms. Houses have been pulled down as dangerous, but building of new houses progresses slowly while business progresses quickly. Dr Embleton (Newcastle Fever Hospital): "There can be little doubt that the great cause of the continuance and spread of the typhus has been the over-crowding of human beings, and the uncleanliness of their dwellings." Rooms in confined and unwholesome yards; men, women, and children "lie at night huddled together"; night-shift succeeds day-shift "in unbroken series"; "the whole house badly supplied with water and worse with privies; dirty, unventilated, and pestiferous." Lodgings from 8d. to 3s. a week. Dr Hunter: "The town of Newcastle-on-Tyne contains a sample of the finest tribe of our countrymen, often sunk by external circumstances of house and street into an almost savage degradation."

The fluctuation of housing conditions. The state of dwellings may be tolerable today, frightful tomorrow, as capital and labour ebb and flow. Bradford: in 1861 there were 1,751 uninhabited houses; with the revival of trade, an overflow from the wages of the reserve army occurred. Dr Hunter obtained from an insurance agent a list of "frightful cellar habitations and rooms" inhabited largely by well-paid workers who declared they would willingly pay for better dwellings if they existed. Dr Bell's report (5 September 1865) describes one small cellar measuring 1,500 cubic feet with ten persons; 223 houses with 1,450 inhabitants, 453 beds, and 36 privies; beds averaging 3.3 persons, many with 5 or 6; some sleeping in their clothes on the bare boards — "young men and women, married and unmarried, all together." These are "the centres from which disease and death are distributed amongst those in better circumstances, who have allowed them thus to fester in our midst." The footnote (note 61) lists individual houses and cellars with occupancy (e.g., 16 persons in one room on Vulcan Street; 26 in two cellar rooms on Salt Pie Street).

Bristol takes third place after London in the misery of its dwellings: "Bristol, where the blankest poverty and domestic misery abound in the wealthiest town of Europe."

(c) The Nomadic Population

Marx turns to "the light infantry of capital, thrown from one point to another according to its present needs. When they are not on the march they 'camp'." Nomadic labour is used for building and draining works, brick-making, lime-burning, railway-making, etc. "A flying column of pestilence, it carries smallpox, typhus, cholera and scarlet fever into the places in whose neighbourhood it pitches its camp." In undertakings with large capital outlay, the contractor provides wooden huts — "improvising villages which lack all sanitary arrangements, are outside the control of the local authorities, and are very profitable to the gentleman who is doing the contracting, for he exploits his workers in two directions at once — as soldiers of industry, and as tenants." Depending on whether the hut has one, two, or three holes, the inhabitant pays 2, 3, or 4 shillings a week.

A long quotation from the Chairman of the Nuisances Removal Committee of Sevenoaks to Sir George Grey (Home Secretary), September 1864, details smallpox outbreaks in railway workers' huts built by contractor Mr Jay: no ventilation or drainage, overcrowded, each occupant had to accommodate lodgers, two rooms per tenement. "In the night-time these poor people were compelled to endure all the horror of suffocation to avoid the pestiferous smells." Mr Jay promised to appropriate a hut for contagious cases but took no steps; several cases of smallpox and two deaths occurred. One family lost five children to smallpox and fever. A footnote (note 66) adds a report from Chapel-en-le-Frith Union: labourers living in "small excavations... made into a large hillock of lime ashes," small and damp, with "not the slightest means of ventilation except up a hole pushed through the top," where smallpox had been raging.

Miners. "Workers in coal and other mines belong to the best paid categories of the British proletariat." The mine exploiter builds cottages for his "hands," who receive cottages and coal for firing "for nothing" — these form part of their wages, paid in kind. Those unhoused receive £4 per annum compensation. Ground rent is high where population is dense; the mining employer tries to put up, within the smallest space, "exactly the number of cottages necessary to pack together his workers and their families." Dr Hunter: the lodging of pitmen in Northumberland and Durham is "perhaps, on the whole, the worst and the dearest of which any large specimens can be found in England." The extreme badness is in the high number of men in one room, small ground-plots, want of water, absence of privies, houses placed on top of one another. "The lessee acts as if the whole colony were encamped, not resident." Dr Stevens: with very few exceptions, the statement that no means are taken to secure health "would be true of all of them." Colliers are "bound" to the colliery lessee or owner for twelve months; if they express discontent, their names are marked, and they are turned off at the annual "binding." Stevens: "no part of the 'truck system' could be worse than what obtains in these densely-populated districts. The collier is bound to take as part of his hiring a house surrounded with pestiferous influences; he cannot help himself... he is, to all intents and purposes, a serf." The proprietor first consults his balance-sheet.

Dr Simon's official report offers capital's "apology": mines are commonly worked on lease; the lessee's interest (commonly 21 years) is too short to justify good accommodation; the landlord would fix an exorbitant ground-rent for the "privilege" of having a decent village on the surface. Dr Simon notes: "Claims of landlordship are being so used as to do great public wrong. The landlord in his capacity of mine-owner invites an industrial colony to labour on his estate, and then in his capacity of surface-owner makes it impossible that the labourers whom he collects should find proper lodging where they must live." The lessee has no pecuniary motive to resist, knowing "that his labourers on whom they fall have not education enough to know the value of their sanitary rights, that neither obscenest lodging nor foulest drinking water will be appreciable inducements towards a 'strike'."

(d) Effect of Crises on the Best Paid Section of the Working Class

Marx examines the crisis of 1866 ("its next crisis was due in 1866"; the year 1857 brought one of the "gigantic crises"). The 1866 crisis was "predominantly financial." Its outbreak in May 1866 was signalled by the failure of a giant London bank, followed by the collapse of countless swindling companies. Iron shipbuilding was one of the industries involved: the magnates had over-produced and entered enormous contracts on speculative assumptions about credit. A terrible reaction set in.

Marx quotes a detailed report from the Morning Star (7 January 1867): in East End districts (Poplar, Millwall, Greenwich, Deptford, Limehouse, Canning Town), at least 15,000 workmen and their families were in "utter destitution," and 3,000 skilled mechanics were breaking stones in the workhouse yard after distress of over half a year. Marx describes the workhouse scene in vivid detail: a hungry crowd besieging the door; men breaking paving-stones into macadam, "each man had a big paving-stone for a seat, and he chipped away at the rime-covered granite until he had broken up, and think! five bushels of it," for threepence and an allowance of food. In another part of the yard, men huddled together for warmth, picking oakum and disputing "which could work the longest on a given quantity of food — for endurance was the point of honour." Seven thousand in one workhouse were recipients of relief; many had earned the highest wages paid to artisans six or eight months before.

Marx narrates several visits: an ironworker out of work for 27 weeks, his family picking oakum, his dinner "a couple of slices of bread and dripping, and a cup of milkless tea"; a middle-aged woman with her family staring at a dying fire, her boys idle 26 weeks, showing a bank-book documenting their savings melting from £20 to nothing; an iron labourer's wife ill from want of food, lying on a mattress in her clothes, just covered with a strip of carpet, all bedding pawned, children "looking as much in need of nursing as their mother"; a young fellow with "a young wife, two pretty children, a cluster of pawn-tickets, and a bare room."

A Standard extract (5 April 1867) describes "a frightful spectacle" — 40,000 starving people in the East End, "packed — next door to the most enormous accumulation of wealth the world ever saw — cheek by jowl with this are 40,000 helpless, starving people." The local ratepayers themselves are "driven by the parochial charges to the verge of pauperism." A footnote quotes Reynolds' Newspaper (20 January 1867): placards announcing "Fat oxen! Starving men! The fat oxen from their palace of glass have gone to feed the rich in their luxurious abode, while the starving men are left to rot and die in their wretched dens."

Belgium. Marx then examines Belgium, which English capitalists cite as "the workers' paradise" because "freedom of labour" (or "freedom of capital") is there limited neither by trade unions nor Factory Acts. He draws on M. Ducpetiaux's Budgets économiques des classes ouvrières de la Belgique (Brussels, 1855). Ducpetiaux's normal Belgian worker's family consists of father, mother, and four children; four of the six may be usefully employed year-round; no sick person, no expenses for religious, moral, or intellectual purposes except a small sum for church pews; no savings or benefit society contributions; no luxury or improvidence. The father and eldest son allow themselves tobacco and on Sundays go to the ale-house (86 centimes a week). Highest average daily wage: 1 franc 56 centimes for men, 89 for women, 56 for boys, 55 for girls. Calculated at 300 working days, family resources at maximum: 1,068 francs a year (father 468, mother 267, boy 168, girl 165).

Ducpetiaux notes that assigning wages to the mother removes the household from her management: "who will look after the house and the young children?... This is the dilemma presented every day to the workers." Annual expenditure yields deficits depending on food: sailor in the fleet 1,828 francs (deficit 760); soldier 1,473 (deficit 405); prisoner 1,112 (deficit 44). "We see that few workers' families can reach... even that of the prisoner." The average cost per prisoner (1847–49) was 63 centimes; compared with daily maintenance of the worker, a difference of 13 centimes. And the prisoner does not pay for lodgings. Marx asks: "How does it happen, then, that a great number, we might say the great majority of workers, live even more economically than prisoners?" Because they adopt expedients whose secrets only workers know: they reduce rations, substitute rye-bread for wheat, eat less or no meat, content themselves with one or two rooms crammed together, "where boys and girls sleep side by side, often on the same mattress," economize on clothing, washing, decency, and give up Sunday diversions. "Once this extreme limit has been reached, the least rise in the price of food, the shortest stoppage of work, the slightest illness, increases the worker's distress and brings him to complete disaster."

Marx adds: "in this 'paradise for capitalists', the smallest change in the prices of the most essential means of subsistence is followed by a change in the number of deaths and crimes!" And the class distribution: of 930,000 Belgian families, 90,000 are wealthy and on the voters' list (450,000 persons); 390,000 families of the lower middle class (1,950,000 persons); 450,000 working-class families (2,250,000 persons). "Of the 450,000 working-class families, over 200,000 are on the pauper list!"

(e) The British Agricultural Proletariat

Marx states: "Nowhere does the antagonistic character of capitalist production and accumulation assert itself more brutally than in the progress of English agriculture (including cattle-breeding) and the retrogression of the English agricultural labourer." He gives a historical survey.

Arthur Young's statements about the agricultural labourer of 1771 show him "a very pitiable role" compared with his predecessor of the end of the fourteenth century, "when the labourer... could live in plenty, and accumulate wealth" — not to speak of the fifteenth century, "the golden age of the English labourer in town and country." A 1777 book: "The great farmer is nearly mounted to a level with him [the gentleman]; while the poor labourer is depressed almost to the earth." Real agricultural wages fell by nearly ¼ (25 per cent) between 1737 and 1777. Dr Richard Price: "Modern policy is, indeed, more favourable to the higher classes of people; and the consequences may in time prove that the whole kingdom will consist of only gentry and beggars, or of grandees and slaves." The agricultural labourer's average wage expressed in pints of wheat: 1770–71: 90 pints; Eden's time (1797): 65; 1808: 60.

The Poor Law and the wage-pauper combination. During the Anti-Jacobin War, nominal wages rose, partly from banknote depreciation and partly from rising prices of primary means of subsistence. The movement can be demonstrated simply: the Poor Law was administered the same way in 1795 and 1814 — the parish made up the nominal wage to the level needed for simple vegetation. The ratio between wage paid by the farmer and the wage-deficit made good by the parish shows: first, wages had fallen below their minimum; second, the degree to which the labourer was "a combination of wage-labourer and pauper, or the degree to which he had been turned into a serf of his parish." Northamptonshire example: 1795, weekly wage 7s. 6d.; yearly expenditure of a family of six £36 12s. 5d.; income £29 18s.; deficit made good by parish £6 14s. 5d. In 1814: weekly wage 12s. 2d.; expenditure of family of five £54 18s. 4d.; income £36 2s.; deficit £18 6s. 4d. "In 1795 the deficit was less than a quarter of the wage, in 1814 it was more than a half." Hence the labourer, the instrumentum vocale ("speaking implement"), became "the most oppressed, the worst nourished, the most brutally treated" of all the animals kept by the farmer.

The Swing riots of 1830 revealed that "misery and black mutinous discontent smouldered quite as fiercely under the surface of agricultural as of manufacturing England." Sadler christened the agricultural labourers "white slaves"; a bishop echoed the epithet. E. G. Wakefield: "The peasant of the South of England... is not a freeman, nor is he a slave; he is a pauper."

The Corn Law repeal period. Before repeal, the middle-class agitators wanted to prove how little the Corn Laws protected actual producers; the industrial bourgeoisie was angry at the landed aristocracy's denunciations of the factory system. "When thieves fall out, honest men come into their own." The Morning Chronicle sent special commissioners (1844–45) into agricultural districts, publishing names of families and landlords. Marx includes a table of wages paid in three villages near Blandford, Wimborne, and Poole, owned by Mr G. Bankes and the Earl of Shaftesbury. Post-repeal agricultural revolution. The repeal gave "a marvellous impulse" to English agriculture: drainage on the most extensive scale, new methods of stall-feeding and artificial cultivation of green crops, mechanical manuring apparatus, new treatment of clay soils, increased use of mineral manures, employment of the steam-engine and all kinds of new machinery, more intensive cultivation. Mr Pusey (Chairman of the Royal Agricultural Society) declares the relative expenses of farming have been reduced nearly 50 per cent by the introduction of new machinery. Greater outlay of capital per acre and more rapid concentration of farms were essential conditions. Area under cultivation increased from 1846 to 1856 by 464,119 acres. Yet the number of actual agricultural labourers of both sexes and all ages fell from 1,241,396 in 1851 to 1,163,217 in 1861. A footnote (note 83) shows how medium-sized farmers declined (the category "Farmer's son, grandson, brother..." fell from 216,851 in 1851 to 176,151 in 1861; farms under 20 acres fell by more than 900; 50–75 acre farms fell from 8,253 to 6,370; large farms (300–500 acres) rose from 7,771 to 8,410; over 500 acres from 2,755 to 3,914; over 1,000 acres from 492 to 582). The Registrar General: "The increase of farmers and farm-labourers, since 1801, bears no kind of proportion... to the increase of agricultural produce."

Professor Rogers concludes that the situation of the English agricultural labourer of his day, compared with 1770–80 (not to speak of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries), has changed for the worse "to an extraordinary extent," that "the peasant has again become a serf," and a serf worse fed and worse clothed. Dr Hunter: "The cost of the hind is fixed at the lowest possible amount on which he can live... the supplies of wages and shelter are not calculated on the profit to be derived from him. He is a zero in farming calculations." And: "As to any further reduction of his income, he may say, nihil habeo nihil curo. He has no fears for the future, because he has now only the spare supply necessary to keep him. He has reached the zero from which are dated the calculations of the farmer. Come what will, he has no share either in prosperity or adversity."

Housing of the agricultural labourer. Dr Simon: "almost every page of Dr Hunter's report bears testimony" to insufficient and miserable housing, which has been deteriorating, "house-room being now greatly more difficult for him to find, and, when found, greatly less suitable to his needs than, perhaps, for centuries had been the case." Whether the labourer finds house-room "depends on the use which others may see fit to make of their 'right to do as they will with their own'." No law requires a proportion of decent labourers' dwellings on a farm, nor reserves any right in the soil to the labourer. The Poor Law (settlement and chargeability provisions) gives each parish a pecuniary interest in minimizing resident labourers. "Large proprietors... have but to resolve that there shall be no labourers' dwellings on their estates, and their estates will thenceforth be virtually free from half their responsibility for the poor." A footnote (note 99) explains "close villages" (owned by one or two big landowners) and "open villages" (soil belonging to many small proprietors), where building speculators can build cottages.

Dr Hunter's finding: destruction of houses had been in progress in 821 parishes, which received in 1861 (compared with 1851) a population 5½ per cent greater, into house-room 4½ per cent less. The result is a "show-village" where cottages are reduced to a few, inhabited only by shepherds, gardeners, gamekeepers. "Regular servants who receive the good treatment usual to their class." Labourers come from a neighbouring open village, "perhaps three miles off." A footnote describes the "show-village" as unreal as those Catherine II saw in the Crimea; near Market Harborough, a 500-acre sheep-farm employs only one man, who receives a "thirteenth shilling a week for lodgings" and must find them far away. The labourer must walk six or eight miles daily to work, and the open villages cram the labourers into the cheapest hovels.

Dr Simon's further report: even on "princely estates" the cottage "may be of the meanest description"; "There are landlords who deem any stye good enough for their labourer and his family, and who yet do not disdain to drive with him the hardest possible bargain for rent." The Nuisances Removal Acts are "a mere dead letter" where the cottage-owner is the one from whom the hovel is rented. "Lamentable indeed, must be the case, when... it is the common conclusion of competent observers that even the general badness of dwellings is an evil infinitely less urgent than their mere numerical insufficiency." Overcrowding favours the extension of contagious disease and "the origination of disease which is not contagious"; and it outrages decency: "adult persons of both sexes, married and unmarried, are huddled together in single small sleeping rooms." Dr Ord's report on a fever outbreak at Wing (Buckinghamshire): a young man with fever "slept in a room with nine other persons"; five out of the nine caught fever, one died. Dr Harvey's report: ten persons in one room, including a young woman with fever, her parents, her bastard child, two brothers, two sisters each with a bastard child; "A few weeks ago thirteen persons slept in it."

The dialectic of depopulation and surplus population. Marx draws the theoretical conclusion: "The continual emigration to the towns, the continual formation of a surplus population in the countryside through the concentration of farms, the conversion of arable land into pasture, the introduction of machinery, etc., are things which go hand in hand with the continual eviction of the agricultural population by the destruction of their cottages." The more empty the district, the greater its "relative surplus population"; the greater their pressure on means of employment, the greater the absolute excess over housing, and the greater the "pestilential herding together." "The creation of dense knots of humanity in scattered little villages and small country towns corresponds to the forcible draining of men from the surface of the land." The continuous conversion of agricultural labourers into a surplus population, "in spite of their diminishing number and the increasing mass of their products, is the cradle of pauperism. The pauperism of the agricultural labourers is ultimately a motive for their eviction; it is also the chief source of their miserable housing, which breaks down their last power of resistance, and makes them mere slaves of the landed proprietors and the farmers." Thus the minimum of wages becomes "a law of nature" for them.

Yet the land, "in spite of its constant 'relative surplus population', is at the same time under-populated." There are always too many labourers for ordinary needs and too few for exceptional and temporary requirements. Hence "contradictory complaints from the same places of a simultaneous deficiency and excess of labour." A temporary and local shortage of labour "does not bring about a rise in wages, but rather forces the women and children into the fields, and constantly lowers the age at which exploitation begins." As soon as the exploitation of women and children takes place on a large scale, "it becomes in turn a new means of making the male agricultural labourer 'redundant' and keeping down his wage." The finest fruit of this vicious circle is the gang-system in eastern England.

The gang-system. It obtains almost exclusively in Lincolnshire, Huntingdonshire, Cambridgeshire, Norfolk, Suffolk, and Nottinghamshire, and sporadically in Northamptonshire, Bedfordshire, and Rutland. Lincolnshire serves as example: much of it is new land, formerly marsh or won from the sea. The steam-engine has worked wonders in drainage; what were fens now bear "a luxuriant sea of corn, and very high ground rents." No new cottages were built; as new farms arose, old cottages were demolished, and labour had to come from "open villages" miles away. Labourers living on 1,000-acre farms ("confined labourers") do only permanent, difficult work requiring horses; for every 100 acres there is scarcely one cottage. A fenland farmer testified: "I farm 320 acres, all arable land. I have not one cottage on my farm. I have only one labourer on my farm now. I have four horsemen lodging about. We get light work done by gangs."

The gang consists of from ten to forty or fifty persons: women, young persons of both sexes (13–18, boys mostly eliminated at 13), and children of both sexes (6–13). At the head is the gang-master, "always an ordinary agricultural labourer, and usually what is called a bad lot, a rake, unsteady, drunken, but with a dash of enterprise and savoir faire." He recruits the gang and negotiates with farmers over piece-work; his income depends on extracting maximum labour in minimum time. Farmers have discovered that women work steadily only under male direction, but that women and children, once set going, "spend their vital forces impetuously — as Fourier already knew in his time — whereas the adult male worker is shrewd enough to economize on his strength as much as he can." The gang-master employs his gang six to eight months a year, making employment more lucrative and certain than through individual farmers; his influence in the open villages is thus riveted.

The "drawbacks": over-working of children and young persons, enormous daily marches (five, six, sometimes seven miles), and demoralization. The gang-master, armed with a long stick, seldom uses it; he is "a democratic emperor, or a kind of Pied Piper of Hamelin." He binds his subjects "by the charms of the gipsy life." "Coarse freedom, noisy jollity and the obscenest kind of impertinence give attractions to the gang." He pays up in a public house, then returns home at the head of a drunken procession. "On the return journey what Fourier calls 'phanerogamie' is the order of the day." Girls of 13 and 14 are commonly made pregnant by male companions of the same age. The open villages "become Sodoms and Gomorrahs," with twice as high a rate of illegitimacy as the rest of the kingdom. The private gangs are made up similarly but count fewer members and work under an old farm servant; "the gipsy fun has vanished... but the payment and treatment of the children is worse."

The gang-system "clearly does not exist for the sake of the gang-master. It exists for the enrichment of the large-scale farmers and indirectly for the landowners." For the farmer, "there is no more ingenious method of keeping his labourers well below the normal level, and yet of always having an extra hand ready for extra work, of extracting the greatest possible amount of labour with the least possible expenditure of money, and of making adult male labour 'redundant'." Hence the apparent paradox: a greater or lesser lack of employment for the agricultural labourer is admitted, while the gang-system is declared "necessary" on account of the shortage of adult male labour and its migration to the towns. "The cleanly weeded land and the unclean human weeds of Lincolnshire are pole and counterpole of capitalist production."

A footnote (note 17) notes that the Public Health Report treating the gang-system "remains unknown to the press," while the Children's Employment Commission's report "afforded the press sensational and welcome copy." The Liberal press asked how the fine gentlemen and ladies of Lincolnshire, who send missions to the South Sea Islands "for the improvement of the morals of South Sea Islanders," could allow such a system. Marx's dry observation: "Under the accursed conditions to which these 'delicate' people condemn the agricultural labourer, it would not be surprising if he ate his own children. What is really wonderful is the healthy integrity of character he has largely retained." The official reports prove that parents, even in gang districts, loathe the gang-system.

(f) Ireland

Marx's analysis. England, "a pre-eminently industrial country with fully developed capitalist production, would have bled to death under such a population drain as Ireland has suffered. But Ireland is at present merely an agricultural district of England." The depopulation threw much land out of cultivation, diminished produce, and brought about an absolute decline in some branches of cattle breeding. Yet "the rents of the land and the profits of the farmers increased along with the fall in the population." Why? Because (i) with the throwing together of smallholdings and the change from arable to pasture, a larger part of the total product was transformed into surplus product — the surplus product increased although the total product decreased; (ii) the monetary value of the surplus product increased still more rapidly than its quantity, owing to the rise in the price of meat, wool, etc. on the English market.

Marx states a fundamental theoretical point: "The scattered means of production that serve the producers themselves as means of employment and subsistence, without valorizing themselves through the incorporation of the labour of others, are no more capital than a product consumed by its producer is a commodity. If the mass of the means of production employed in agriculture diminished along with the mass of the population, the mass of the capital employed in agriculture increased, because a part of the means of production that were formerly scattered was turned into capital." This is the centralization of agricultural capital through the dispossession of smallholders.

Total capital outside agriculture accumulated slowly, with fluctuations; but "the concentration of its individual constituents" developed rapidly, and "its relative growth, in proportion to the diminishing population, was tremendous."

The Malthusian experiment. Marx frames the Irish case as an experiment: "Here then, under our own eyes, and on a large scale, there emerges a process which perfectly corresponds to the requirements of orthodox economics for the confirmation of its dogma, the dogma that misery springs from an absolute surplus of population, and that equilibrium is re-established by depopulation. This is a far more important experiment than the mid-fourteenth-century plague so celebrated by the Malthusians." He notes that the fourteenth-century plague in England led to the "enfranchisement and enrichment of the agricultural population," whereas in France it led to "a greater degree of enslavement and an increase in misery" — a difference the Malthusians overlook. A footnote cites Thomas Sadler's Ireland: Its Evils, and Their Remedies (1829), which proved by comparing provinces and counties that misery there is not in proportion to population level, but in inverse ratio to it.

The Irish famine of 1846 killed more than 1,000,000 people, "but it killed poor devils only. It did not do the slightest damage to the wealth of the country." The exodus did not decimate the means of production. "The Irish genius discovered an altogether new way of spiriting a poor people thousands of miles away from the scene of its misery." The exiles send money home as travelling expenses for those left behind; every emigrating troop draws another after it. "Thus, instead of costing Ireland anything, emigration forms one of the most lucrative branches of its export trade." It is a systematic process, "which does not simply make a passing gap in the population, but sucks out of it every year more people than are replaced by births." A footnote (note 25) notes total emigrants 1851–1874: 2,325,922.

Consequences for those left behind. "The relative surplus population is as great today as it was before 1846; wages are just as low; the oppression of the labourers has increased; misery is forcing the country towards a new crisis." The reasons: the revolution in agriculture kept pace with emigration; "The production of a relative surplus population has more than kept pace with the absolute depopulation." The change from arable to pasture worked more acutely in Ireland than in England: in England, green crops increase with cattle-breeding; in Ireland they decrease. Smaller and medium farmers (those not cultivating more than 100 acres) still make up about 1/8 of the whole number (a footnote, note 26, says 94.6% of farms are smaller than 100 acres, while 5.4% exceed that amount). They are crushed by the competition of capital-managed agriculture and "continually furnish new recruits to the class of wage-labourers." The linen industry requires relatively few adult men. The 'domestic industry' system renders workers 'redundant' through under-payment and over-work.

Wages. The Reports of the Irish Poor Law Inspectors (1870) state wages rose by 50 to 60 per cent in twenty years, now averaging 6s. to 9s. a week. But this "apparent rise hides an actual fall in wages," because it does not cancel the rise in the price of necessaries. The labourers were formerly indistinguishable from the smallest small farmers; only since 1846 have they begun to form "a section of the class of pure wage-labourers, a special estate which is now connected with its masters only by monetary relations."

Housing. Their living conditions have grown worse. Some day-labourers continue to live on farmers' holdings in "overcrowded huts whose hideousness far surpasses the worst examples the agricultural districts of England can offer." An inspector: "The agricultural labourers' huts are a disgrace to the Christianity and to the civilization of this country." To make the holes more attractive, the pieces of land that belonged to them are systematically confiscated. "The mere sense that they exist subject to this species of ban... has given birth in the minds of the labourers to corresponding sentiments of antagonism and dissatisfaction towards those by whom they are thus led to regard themselves as being treated as... a proscribed race." The first act of the agricultural revolution was to sweep away the huts at the place of work; labourers were compelled into villages and towns, "thrown like refuse into garrets, holes, cellars and corners, in the worst slum districts." Thousands of Irish families "notable for their rare attachment to the domestic hearth, for the gaiety and the purity of their home life, suddenly found themselves transplanted into hotbeds of vice." Men now seek work daily from neighbouring farmers, "under the most precarious form of wage," walking long distances, getting wet, "not infrequently ending in sickness, disease and want."

"The towns have had to receive from year to year what was deemed to be the surplus-labour of the rural division" — and then people wonder that "there is still a surplus of labour in the towns and villages, and either a scarcity or a threatened scarcity in some of the country divisions." The scarcity only appears "in harvest-time, or during spring"; at other periods many hands are idle; "from the digging out of the main crop of potatoes in October until the early spring following... there is no employment for them"; and during active times they are "subject to broken days and to all kinds of interruptions."

The model landlords aggravate matters: instead of spending rents abroad, they live on their demesnes and draw "labour-supply... chiefly from their small tenants, who are obliged to attend when required to do the landlords' work, at rates of wages, in many cases, considerably under the current rates paid to ordinary labourers, and without regard to the inconvenience or loss to the tenant of being obliged to neglect his own business at critical periods of sowing or reaping."

Marx's summary comparison. "The uncertainty and irregularity of employment, the constant return and long duration of gluts of labour, are all symptoms of a relative surplus population." The difference between England and Ireland: "in England, an industrial country, the industrial reserve is recruited from the countryside, whereas in Ireland, an agricultural country, the agricultural reserve is recruited from the towns, the places of refuge of the agricultural labourers who have been driven from the land. In England, the surplus rural labourers are transformed into factory workers; in Ireland, those forced into the towns remain agricultural labourers even while they exert a downward pressure on urban wages, and are constantly sent back to the countryside in search of work."

The inspectors describe the material condition: "Though living with the strictest frugality, his own wages are barely sufficient to provide food for an ordinary family and pay his rent, and he depends upon other sources for the means of clothing himself, his wife, and his children... The atmosphere of these cabins, combined with the other privations they are subjected to, has made this class particularly susceptible to typhus and consumption." "It is no wonder that, according to the unanimous testimony of the inspectors, a sombre discontent runs through the ranks of this class, that they long for the return of the past, loathe the present, despair of the future, give themselves up 'to the evil influence of agitators', and have only one fixed idea, to emigrate to America. This is the land of Cockaigne, into which depopulation, the great Malthusian panacea, has transformed green Erin!"

The Irish factory worker. Marx quotes Robert Baker's evidence verbatim from "Johnson, a beetler" (embossing fabrics under rollers): works from 6 in the morning till 11 at night, Monday to Friday, Saturday leaving at 6 p.m., with three hours off for meals; five children; earns 10s. 6d. a week; wife works, gets 5s. The oldest girl, 12, minds the house and is "cook, and all the servant we have." The family gets nothing to eat before work; tea once a week; otherwise stirabout of oatmeal or Indian meal; sometimes a little milk. "So we go on from day to day, Sunday and week day, always the same the year round. I am always very much tired when I have done at night. We may see a bit of flesh meat sometimes, but very seldom." Three children attend school at 1d. a week each. Rent 9d. a week; peat for firing 1s. 6d. a fortnight at the lowest. "Such are Irish wages, such is Irish life!"

Lord Dufferin and the Malthusian finale. At the end of 1866 and beginning of 1867, Lord Dufferin, an Irish land magnate, set about solving the problem in The Times. Marx's German exclamation from Goethe's Faust: "Wie menschlich von solch' grossem Herrn!" ("What humanity from such a great lord!"). Marx recapitulates the concentration from Table E: the three money-grubbers, the 26, 28, 121, 186, 1,131, 1,194. "But the lion's share of the yearly national rental which an inconceivably small number of land magnates in England, Scotland and Ireland swallow up is so monstrous that English statesmanship finds it inappropriate to afford the same statistical materials about the distribution of rents as about the distribution of profits. Lord Dufferin is one of those land magnates."

Dufferin declares Ireland is still over-populated, that depopulation benefits the landlords and therefore the soil, and hence the people. He demands a new blood-letting of one-third of a million. Marx cites the "school of Sangrado" (from Gil Blas), who ordered blood-letting after blood-letting until the patient died of the cure. Dufferin demands 300,000, "instead of about two millions."

A footnote (note 41) quotes Nassau W. Senior's posthumous Journals, Conversations, and Essays Relating to Ireland (1868): "'Well,' said Dr G., 'we have got our Poor Law and it is a great instrument for giving the victory to the landlords. Another, and a still more powerful instrument is emigration... No friend to Ireland can wish the war to be prolonged... The sooner it is over — the sooner Ireland becomes a grazing country, with the comparatively thin population which a grazing country requires, the better for all classes'." The footnote explains the historical context: the English Corn Laws of 1815 secured to Ireland a monopoly of free corn importation into Great Britain, artificially encouraging corn cultivation; their abolition in 1846 removed this monopoly and gave "a great impulse to the conversion of Irish arable land into pasture, to the concentration of farms, and to the eviction of small-scale cultivators." Having praised the fruitfulness of the Irish soil between 1815 and 1846 as destined by nature for wheat, "English agronomists, economists and politicians suddenly discovered that it was good for nothing but the production of forage."

The final dialectical twist. Marx closes with an irony: "Like all good things in the world, this profitable mode of proceeding has its drawbacks. The accumulation of the Irish in America keeps pace with the accumulation of rents in Ireland. The Irishman, banished by the sheep and the ox, re-appears on the other side of the ocean as a Fenian. And there a young but gigantic republic rises, more and more threateningly, to face the old queen of the waves." He quotes Horace: "Acerba fata Romanos agunt / Scelusque fraternae necis" ("A cruel fate torments the Romans, and the crime of fratricide"). The suggestion is clear: England's colonial policy in Ireland generates the Irish-American population that will return as a political threat — a threat linked thematically to the "fratricide" of the class and national division.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principle: The Subject-Object Inversion

The organising principle of the entire chapter is the dialectical inversion of the means of production into means of domination. Marx's crucial formulation in Section 4 — "a system in which the worker does not employ the means of production, but the means of production employ the worker" — is the philosophical fulcrum. Under capitalism, the productive forces developed by labour become an independent power that confronts and dominates labour. Every increase in the productivity of labour, every expansion of the means of production, every advance in science and technology, instead of liberating the producer, renders the producer's position more precarious. The chapter thus delivers the theoretical core of Marx's theory of alienation in its specifically economic form: the worker creates capital, and capital enslaves the worker.

This yields a fundamental "systemic law": accumulation is structurally antagonistic. The accumulation of wealth at one pole is the accumulation of misery at the other — not as two separate facts linked by chance, but as two expressions of the same relation. The "absolute general law" is therefore not a moral indictment appended to an economic analysis; the moral indictment is the economic analysis. The relative surplus population is simultaneously product, lever, and condition of accumulation. Wages are regulated by the reserve army, not by population. And the working population produces "the means by which it is itself made relatively superfluous."

The Refutation of the "Natural Law of Population"

The chapter's most important polemical achievement is its dissolution of the Malthusian "natural law of population." Marx's move is threefold. First, he identifies the independent variable: the rate of accumulation, not the rate of population growth, drives the wage rate. Second, he demonstrates the historical specificity of population laws: "every particular historical mode of production has its own special laws of population." Third, he furnishes the empirical counterexample of Ireland, where a massive population decrease did not improve the condition of the remaining workers but instead consolidated the relative surplus population and worsened their condition of dependence. The Malthusian "experiment" was performed on a grand scale, and it refuted rather than confirmed the doctrine. The modern parallel will be immediate to any reader: the chapter demonstrates that poverty is not produced by the reproductive behaviour of the poor but by the structural requirements of capital.

The Distinction Between Concentration and Centralization

The rigorous separation of concentration (growth of individual capitals through accumulation, limited by social wealth) from centralization (attraction of already-formed capitals, not limited by accumulation) is a theoretical achievement with enduring significance. Centralization is the more rapid and potent process: it can accomplish "in the twinkling of an eye" what accumulation would take generations to do (the railway example). It works through competition and credit, and its limit is the fusion of all capital into a single hand. This anticipates later developments in finance capital, the credit system, and the analysis of trusts and monopolies (noted by Engels). It is the theoretical bridge from the logic of individual capitals to the logic of the social capital as a whole.

The Industrial Reserve Army as the Regulator of Wages

The concept of the industrial reserve army is the chapter's most enduring contribution to labor economics. Its lesson is that the labour market is not a self-equilibrating system of independent supply and demand, but a mechanism through which capital acts on both sides at once. Capital determines both the demand for labour (through the variable component of its capital) and the supply of labour (through the "setting free" of workers and the over-work of the employed). The reserve army is the "background" against which the law of supply and demand does its work, "confining the field of action of this law to the limits absolutely convenient to capital." The corollary is that the "sacred law of supply and demand" is invoked by capital only as a rhetorical weapon — and abandoned in the colonies when it does not serve capital's interest.

The Dialectic of Over-work and Idleness

One of the chapter's most striking insights is the dialectic whereby "the condemnation of one part of the working class to enforced idleness by the over-work of the other part, and vice versa, becomes a means of enriching the individual capitalists." This anticipates the modern analysis of precarious employment, zero-hours contracts, and the intensification of labour. The over-work of the employed increases the reserve army; the competition of the reserve army forces the employed to submit to over-work. The mechanism is self-reinforcing and makes the condition of the employed worker (as Marx says in his most general formulation) grow worse "be his payment high or low."

The Method: Theory and Empirical Illustration

The chapter models Marx's mature method. Section 1–4 develop the theory at successive levels of abstraction; Section 5 provides a massive empirical apparatus. The statistics are not decorative but essential: they demonstrate the law's operation and refute its rivals. The nutrition tables, the housing reports, the Belgian budget, the convict-diet comparison, the Irish tables — all serve to show that the theory is not a deduction from abstract premises but a comprehension of historical reality. The interspersed commentary (Gladstone, Fawcett, the Morning Star, the Standard, the Poor Law inspectors) gives voice to the historical actors and demonstrates the ideological obfuscation the theory dissolves.

The Theory of Relative Impoverishment

The chapter establishes the position — later made canonical in Marx's own correspondence and in the Marxist tradition — that the condition of the worker "must grow worse" in relative terms even when it improves in absolute terms. The "golden chain" metaphor captures this precisely: the wage-labourer may be better fed, better clothed, and better treated than the slave, but the measure of dependence is the relation, not the level of material welfare. The worker's improvement is a "quantitative reduction in the amount of unpaid labour" that can never threaten the system. This distinction between absolute and relative condition is the theoretical foundation of the entire critique of reformist optimism within capitalism.

Broader Historical Implications

The chapter's final section on Ireland establishes the colonial dimension of accumulation. The Irish case demonstrates that the "relative surplus population" cannot be understood within a single national economy; emigration, colonial land policy, the world market, and the extraction of surplus across borders are integral to the process. The Irish "fratricide" quotation points to the political consequences: the export of the Irish as "surplus" population generates a new political force (the Fenians, and more broadly the Irish diaspora in the United States) that returns as a threat. This is a prototype of Marx's later analyses of colonialism and world market relations, developed in Capital Volume 3 and in the writings on India, China, and Ireland. The lesson: capitalist accumulation is a world-historical process, and the reserve army is a global phenomenon.

The Promise and the Failure of Classical Political Economy

Marx's engagement with Smith, Ricardo, Barton, Jones, Ramsay, Sismondi, Storch, and Ortes reveals a nuanced position. He credits them with having "suspected rather than understood" the law of the progressive diminution of variable capital. He treats Ricardo and Barton as having approached the truth, and Sismondi and Storch as having registered the antagonism — while remaining trapped in the assumption that it is either a natural law (Ortes) or a lamentable fall from a prior harmony (Sismondi). Marx's advance is to show that the antagonism is specific to a particular mode of production and therefore historically transformable. The chapter's final lesson is thus a revolutionary one: because the law is not natural but historical, it can be superseded — but only by a movement that understands its mechanism and its contradictions. The "absolute general law of capitalist accumulation" is not a fatalistic prophecy but an analysis of the structural limits of a historically determinate system, and therefore an invitation to its transformation.
