Volume 1 • Chapter 21: Piece-Wages

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 21 is the third and shortest of the three chapters that Marx devotes to the form of wages in Volume I of Capital, and it performs what is, in the architecture of the book, an exercise in systematic demystification. Its opening sentence states the thesis with lapidary economy: "The piece-wage is nothing but a converted form of the time-wage, just as the time-wage is a converted form of the value or price of labour-power." That is, the chapter's intellectual objective is to strip away a specific, historically durable illusion — the illusion, endemic to piece-work, that the capitalist buys and pays for labour embodied in a product rather than labour-power as a capacity. Marx's central problem is not to add a new category to political economy but to demonstrate that an apparently distinct and even "more rational" payment system is at bottom the same relation of exploitation, only transposed into a different phenomenal guise. The stakes are exactly the stakes of Part VI as a whole: to show that wage-forms, whatever their variety, systematically conceal the division of the working day into paid and unpaid labour, and thereby conceal the extraction of surplus-value itself.

The chapter therefore has two halves, a conceptual and an operational one. The conceptual half (roughly pp. 692–694) dissolves the piece-wage into the time-wage by showing that the two are mutually convertible via a fixed equation — the value of a day's labour equals the daily value of labour-power — and by demonstrating, through a worked numerical example, that a per-piece price is simply a translated hourly price. The operational half (pp. 694–700) examines the characteristic peculiarities that this form of wage produces when it becomes general: it polices the quality of labour through the product itself; it supplies an exact measure of labour-intensity; it renders direct superintendence largely superfluous; it thereby provides the material basis for "domestic labour," for subletting (the "sweating system"), and for a stratified system in which one worker supervises and exploits another; it aligns the worker's immediate private interest with over-exertion and over-long hours; and it individualises wages in a way that simultaneously rewards the strong and depresses the average. It concludes with a historical claim of considerable weight: that piece-wages are "the form of wage most appropriate to the capitalist mode of production," whose great proving-ground was the period of manufacture and whose most devastating deployment came in the "stormy youth of large-scale industry," 1797–1815.

In relation to the broader theoretical framework, this chapter sits between the analysis of time-wages, whose "hour-system" piece-wages are said to support, and the analysis of national differences in wages, which presupposes precisely the concept here analysed — the wage as the converted price of labour-power. It closes a circuit of appearance versus essence: it takes the most "self-evidently" product-based form of payment, one that even sympathetic observers mistook for a form of artisan independence, and shows it to be an intensification, not a mitigation, of the wage-relation. It is also, as the chapter's closing pages make plain, a political chapter: its object is not merely a category but a site of struggle, "constant struggles between the capitalist and the worker" over the meaning of the piece-rate.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Illusion at First Sight

Marx begins by specifying the false appearance that piece-wages generate. In time-wages, however mystified, the purchased object at least appears to be a duration of living labour. In piece-wages, the appearance shifts: it seems "at first sight as if the use-value bought from the worker is not his labour-power as it actually functions, living labour, but labour already objectified in the product." The distinction is precisely the distinction between labour-power (the capacity to labour, sold for a period) and labour objectified (labour congealed in a commodity, already realised as value). The second half of the illusion follows from the first: it also seems "as if the price of this labour is determined not … by the daily value of labour-power / working day of a given number of hours, but by the producer's capacity for work" — that is, not by the value of the commodity labour-power (which resolves into the socially necessary means of subsistence for its reproduction) but by the worker's individual efficiency, his dexterity, strength, and application.

This appearance has consequences that extend well beyond academic confusion. Marx cites, in the footnote appended to this passage, the work of John Watts (Trade Societies and Strikes, Machinery and Co-operative Societies, Manchester, 1865, pp. 52–3), who asserts that "the system of piece-work illustrates an epoch in the history of the working-man; it is halfway between the position of the mere day-labourer depending upon the will of the capitalist and the co-operative artisan, who in the not distant future promises to combine the artisan and the capitalist in his own person." For Watts, the piece-worker is his own master, "even whilst working upon the capital of the employer." Marx's treatment of this is brutal and dismissive: he quotes the book "because it is a veritable gutter full of long-decayed and apologetic commonplaces," and he convicts its author of a reactionary volte-face, noting that Watts had previously published, in 1842, a pamphlet (Facts and Fictions of Political Economy) in which "property is robbery" was declared — "but that is already in the distant past." The lesson here is a methodological one about how to read the phenomenal form of a social relation: the piece-wage's appearance of independence is not a neutral fact but an ideological resource that apologetics seize upon.

The Empirical Shock: Both Forms Coexist Simultaneously

The first solvent Marx pours onto the illusion is empirical. The claim that piece-wages reflect a different purchase — labour objectified rather than labour-power — cannot survive the fact that "both forms of wages exist side by side, at the same time, in the same branches of industry." He supplies concrete, documented examples: the compositors of London "as a general rule, work by the piece, time-work being the exception," whereas the compositors of the countryside "work by the day, the exception being work by the piece." The shipwrights of the port of London work by the job or piece, "while those of all other ports work by the day" (cited from T. J. Dunning, Trades' Unions and Strikes, London, 1860, p. 22). In the saddlery shops of London, "often for the same work," Frenchmen are paid piece-wages while Englishmen are paid time-wages. Even inside factories, where piece-wages are the general rule, "certain specific operations have to be excepted from this form of evaluation on technical grounds," and these are paid by time-wages. If the two forms were expressions of genuinely distinct purchase-contracts — embodied labour versus living labour — such arbitrary and geographically contingent substitutions would be unintelligible. Their very interchangeability proves that the difference is one of form of payment and not substance of exchange.

The coexistence, however, is not merely a logical embarrassment to the illusion; it is, Marx shows, an instrument of fraud. The footnote to this passage cites the Reports of the Inspectors of Factories … 31 October 1860, p. 9, to the effect that a factory employing 400 people, half paid by the piece and half by the day, is so arranged that the piece-workers have "a direct interest in working longer hours," while the other 200, "paid by the day, work equally long with the others, and get no more money for their overtime." The inspectors calculate: "The work of these 200 people for half an hour a day is equal to one person's work for 50 hours, or 6/100 of one person's labour in a week, and is a positive gain to the employer." The mixed system thus converts the piece-workers into the unpaid instruments by which the day-workers' labour-time is extended without compensation. Leonard Horner, in the Reports … 30 April 1859, pp. 8–9, is quoted to the same purpose: over-working "to a very considerable extent still prevails; and, in most instances, with that security against detection and punishment which the law itself affords." Marx's conclusion is though measured: "it is in itself obvious that the difference of form in the payment of wages in no way alters their essential nature, although the one form may be more favourable to the development of capitalist production than the other." That last clause is the hinge of the whole chapter — the two forms are identical in essence, but not in consequence.

The Numerical Anatomy: Twelve Hours, Twenty-Four Pieces, Six Shillings

The decisive proof that piece-wages are a converted time-wage is arithmetical, and Marx is at pains to make the conversion explicit and transparent. He stipulates: an ordinary working day of 12 hours, of which 6 are paid and 6 unpaid; the value-product of the day is 6 shillings; therefore the value-product of one hour of labour is 6d. He assumes a worker of average intensity and skill, working only the "amount of labour-time socially necessary," produces in 12 hours "twenty-four pieces, either distinct products or measurable parts of some integral construction." The value of those 24 pieces, once the constant capital consumed in them is deducted, is 6 shillings — so the value of a single piece is 3d. The worker, however, receives 1½d per piece, "and thus earns 3 shillings in 12 hours."

Two structural equivalences then fall out. First, at the level of the working day: with time-wages it is a matter of indifference whether we say the worker works 6 hours for himself and 6 for the capitalist, or works the first half of every hour for himself and the second half for the capitalist; the partition is conventional and distributive. Exactly the same indifference applies here: "it does not matter whether we say that each individual piece is half paid for, and half unpaid for, or that the price of only twelve of the pieces is the equivalent of the value of the labour-power, while in the other twelve pieces surplus-value is incorporated." Twelve pieces at 3d apiece equal 3 shillings — the value of labour-power — and the remaining twelve pieces carry pure surplus-value. Surplus labour is thus distributed across every unit of output rather than confined to a block of time, but the rate of exploitation (here 100 per cent) is unaltered.

The Irrationality of the Form and the Inversion of Measurement

Marx then makes his sharpest conceptual move. Having established the equivalence, he declares: "The form of piece-wages is just as irrational as that of time-wages." The irrationality is this — in the example, two pieces, after subtracting the value of the means of production consumed in them, are worth 6d, being the product of one hour of labour, yet the worker receives for them a price of 3d. This is the same rent-like concealment as in time-wages: the wage-form presents the price of the whole hour as though it were the price of labour, whereas half of it is unpaid. From this it follows that "Piece-wages are not in fact a direct expression of any relation of value. It is not, therefore, a question of measuring the value of the piece by the labour-time incorporated in it. It is rather the reverse: the labour the worker has expended must be measured by the number of pieces he has produced."

This is the inversion of measurement, and it is the theoretical core of the chapter. In time-wages, labour is measured by its immediate duration. In piece-wages, labour is measured by the quantity of products in which it has become embodied during a given time. But because the price of labour-time is itself finally fixed by the equation value of a day of labour = daily value of labour-power, the product-measure is simply an indirect route back to the same quantity: hours. The measurement runs backwards through the commodity, but it terminates at the same place. Marx cites in support a 1796 work, the Abrégé élémentaire des principes de l'économie politique (Paris, p. 32), by G. Garnier: "Wages can be measured in two ways: either by the duration of the labour, or by its product." Hence the chapter's conclusion: "The piece-wage is therefore only a modified form of the time-wage."

Peculiarity One — The Quality of Labour Policed by the Work Itself

There follows a taxonomic survey of what Marx calls "the characteristic peculiarities of piece-wages." The first is that "the quality of the labour is here controlled by the work itself, which must be of good average quality if the piece-price is to be paid in full." The product becomes its own inspector. Where the worker's output is defective, the penalty falls on him — the piece-price is docked, or the goods are rejected. Marx cites Andrew Ure's Philosophy of Manufactures (pp. 316–17) for the classic illustration from spinning: "So much weight of cotton is delivered to him [the spinner] and he has to return by a certain time, in lieu of it, a given weight of twist or yarn, of a certain degree of fineness, and he is paid so much per pound for all that he so returns. If his work is defective in quality, the penalty falls on him; if less in quantity than the minimum fixed for a given time, he is dismissed and an abler operative procured." Two consequences are thus fused in a single mechanism: quality assurance is shifted from paid supervision to unpaid worker self-discipline, and the threshold of "acceptable" output becomes a moving frontier that can be used to dismiss the weak. Marx's verdict is unsparing: "Piece-wages become, from this point of view, the most fruitful source of reductions in wages, and of frauds committed by the capitalists."

Peculiarity Two — Intensity Measured with Precision

The same mechanism which polices quality also, and more importantly, calibrates intensity. Because the piece-price presumes a definite quantity of output per unit of time, "only the labour-time which is embodied in a quantity of commodities laid down in advance and fixed by experience counts as socially necessary labour-time and is paid as such." Marx gives a fascinating ethnographic detail from the London tailoring trade: "a certain piece of work, a waistcoat for instance, is called an hour, or half an hour, the hour being valued at 6d." That is, the unit of account itself becomes a unit of product — the "hour" is a garment. Practice, not the clock, determines "the size of the average product of one hour." And because the standard is conventional rather than natural, every change in fashion or design opens a contest: "a contest arises between the employer and the worker as to whether a particular piece of work is one hour, and so on, until here also experience decides." The same practice is documented for London furniture workshops. The sanction attached to failing the standard is dismissal: the worker who "cannot provide labour of an average degree of efficiency, and if he cannot therefore supply a certain minimum of work per day," is let go. Intensity, in short, is not merely measured; it is enforced by the wage-form itself.

Peculiarity Three — The Euthanasia of Supervision, Subletting, and Hierarchical Exploitation

From the first two peculiarities Marx draws the third, which is the chapter's most structurally significant claim. "Since the quality and intensity of the work are here controlled by the very form of the wage, superintendence of labour becomes to a great extent superfluous." The capitalist no longer needs to stand over the worker; the piece-rate does it. This has two far-reaching consequences.

First, piece-wages become "the basis for the modern 'domestic labour'" described in an earlier chapter of Capital — the system in which the worker, nominally independent, works in his own dwelling on materials supplied by a merchant-capitalist, and in which the wage-relation is concealed beneath the appearance of a simple purchase of finished goods. Because no supervision is required and overhead is externalised onto the worker's own household, this "domestic" form becomes viable and widespread.

Second, piece-wages become the basis for "a hierarchically organized system of exploitation and oppression," of which Marx identifies two fundamental forms. The first is subletting: piece-wages "make it easier for parasites to interpose themselves between the capitalist and the wage-labourer," and "the profits of these middlemen come entirely from the difference between the price of labour which the capitalist pays, and the part of that price they actually allow the worker to receive." In England, Marx notes, this is "characteristically" called the sweating system. The Children's Employment Commission, Second Report (p. lxx, n. 424) is cited: "It is when work passes through several hands, each of which is to take its share of profits, while only the last does the work, that the pay which reaches the workwoman is miserably disproportioned." The chain of subcontractors is a chain of deductions, each link living off the gap between what capital pays and what the actual producer receives.

The second form is the group-contracting or "butty" arrangement: "piece-wages allow the capitalist to make a contract for so much per piece with the most important worker — in manufacture, with the chief of some group, in mines with the extractor of the coal, in the factory with the actual machine-worker — at a price for which this man himself undertakes the enlisting and the payment of his assistants." Here the essential relation is not merely concealed; it is doubled. "Here the exploitation of the worker by capital takes place through the medium of the exploitation of one worker by another." The foreman, the hewer, the senior operative becomes a sub-capitalist, whose own interests lie in squeezing his fellows. Even the apologist Watts is forced to concede, as Marx notes with some relish: "It would be a great improvement to the system of piece-work, if all the men employed on a job were partners in the contract, each according to his abilities, instead of one man being interested in over-working his fellows for his own benefit." Marx adds, from the Children's Employment Commission, Third Report, the phrase "the vile nature of the piece-work system" as the considered judgment of the commissioners themselves.

Peculiarity Four — Self-Exploitation and the Lengthening of the Working Day

The fourth peculiarity concerns the alignment of the worker's immediate private interest with the most exhausting rhythms of capital. "Given the system of piece-wages, it is naturally in the personal interest of the worker that he should strain his labour-power as intensely as possible; this in turn enables the capitalist to raise the normal degree of intensity of labour more easily." The worker, paid per unit, discovers that more units mean more money within the same hours — so he polices his own effort. Marx is careful to note that this spontaneous tendency is often artificially cultivated: in the London engineering trade, the customary trick is "the selecting of a man who possesses superior physical strength and quickness, as the principal of several workmen, and paying him an additional rate," with the explicit understanding that he is "to exert himself to the utmost to induce the others, who are only paid the ordinary wages, to keep up to him." Dunning, the trade-union secretary from whom Marx takes this passage, notes that "without any comment this will go far to explain many of the complaints of stinting the action, superior skill, and working-power, made by the employers against the men" — and Marx observes that even though Dunning is a worker and union official, and might therefore be suspected of exaggeration, the same technique is recommended to farmers as an approved one in the "highly respectable" Cyclopaedia of Agriculture edited by J. C. Morton.

The same logic extends the working day itself. "The lengthening of the working day is now in the personal interest of the worker, since with it his daily or weekly wages rise." The inspectors document the willingness: "All those who are paid by piece-work … profit by the transgression of the legal limits of work." The Reports of the Inspectors of Factories … 30 April 1858, p. 9, singles out women employed as weavers and reelers as especially prone to this self-overworking. The Children's Employment Commission, First Report, p. xiii, records that the piece-work system "tends directly to encourage the young potter greatly to over-work himself during the four or five years during which he is employed in the piece-work system, but at low wages," and identifies this as "another great cause to which the bad constitutions of the potters are to be attributed."

But Marx immediately notes a reaction, "like that already described in time-wages." As hours lengthen and workers compete, the piece-rate is pressured downward, so the increased effort purchases no permanent increase in the daily wage. And he adds the crucial structural point, which is independent of any such downward drift: "quite apart from the fact that the prolongation of the working day, even if the piece-wage remains constant, includes of necessity a fall in the price of the labour." The reason is definitional — a fixed piece-price for a longer day means the same daily value of labour-power is purchased with more labour, so the price of labour (the wage per unit of labour-time) falls. The worker's apparent self-advancement is a self-undermining.

Peculiarity Five — Individualisation, the Sense of Independence, and the Lowering of the Average

The final peculiarity is the most dialectically interesting. "In time-wages, with few exceptions, the same wage is paid for the same function, while in piece-wages, although the price of the labour-time is measured by a definite quantity of the product, the daily or weekly wage will vary with the individual differences between the workers," one supplying only the minimum, another the average, a third more than the average. There is therefore "great variety among the individual workers, according to their different degrees of skill, strength, energy and staying-power" — a point Dunning also makes (op. cit., p. 17): in piece-work "wages may very materially differ in amount," whereas in day-work there is generally "an uniform rate … recognized by both employer and employed as the standard of wages for the general run of workmen in the trade."

Marx then insists — twice — that this variation "does not alter the general relation between capital and wage-labour," and he gives two reasons. First, individual differences cancel out in the aggregate: "the individual differences cancel each other out in the workshop as a whole, which thus supplies the average product within a given period of labour, and the total wages paid will be the average wage of that particular branch of industry." The capitalist pays, in total, the average; the dispersion is internal to the wage-bill. Second, and more fundamentally, "the proportion between wages and surplus-value remains unaltered, since the mass of surplus labour supplied by each particular worker corresponds with the wage he receives." The man who produces more is paid more, and for that very reason also generates more surplus-value; the rate of exploitation is invariant across the distribution.

Nevertheless, the form has a dynamic effect on that distribution, and here Marx identifies one of the most consequential tendencies of piece-work. "The wider scope that piece-wages give to individuality tends to develop both that individuality, and with it the worker's sense of liberty, independence and self-control, and also the competition of workers with each other." This is a genuinely two-sided observation. Piece-work produces a subjective figure — the self-reliant, competitive, self-managing worker, proud of his output — and it is precisely this subjectivity that Watts and others mistook for genuine independence. But the objective consequence is the reverse of what it feels like: "The piece-wage therefore has a tendency, while raising the wages of individuals above the average, to lower this average itself." The exemplar raises the collective standard; the collective standard then becomes the new normal against which everyone is measured; and the reward for the exemplar is converted into a uniform intensification. The illusion of freedom is the mechanism of its own dissolution.

Marx notes, with a telling historical illustration, that where this downward adjustment has been blocked by custom, capital turns to outright compulsion: where "a particular rate of piece-wage has for a long time been a fixed tradition, and its lowering, therefore, has presented especial difficulties, in such exceptional cases the masters have sometimes had recourse to the forcible transformation of piece-wages into time-wages." In 1860 this action set off a big strike among the ribbon-weavers of Coventry. The direction of the manoeuvre is significant: time-wages, normally the older and less "advanced" form, can be imposed as a weapon precisely because they permit a de facto rate reduction without the appearance of altering an established price. And finally, "the piece-wage is one of the chief supports of the hour-system described in the preceding chapter" — the practice whereby employers engage workers only when work is available, so that idle time is at the workers' expense. The Les Typographes devant le tribunal correctionnel de Bruxelles (Brussels, 1865, p. 9), by H. Grégoir, is quoted on this: employers take on "many more workers than were needed actually to do the work" in the expectation of uncertain or "even completely imaginary" work, and since they are paid piece-wages, "the employers say to themselves that they run no risk, because any loss of working time will be at the expense of the workers who are unoccupied." The piece-form transfers the entire risk of market fluctuation onto the workforce.

Historical Vindication and the Cantillon Excursus

At this point Marx turns to history, and the argument acquires a developmental dimension. "From what has been shown so far, it is apparent that the piece-wage is the form of wage most appropriate to the capitalist mode of production." He is careful, however, to deny novelty to the form: "Although by no means new — it figures officially side by side with time-wages in the French and English labour statutes of the fourteenth century — it only conquered a larger field of action during the period of manufacture properly so-called." Its great trauma came later: "In the stormy youth of large-scale industry, and particularly from 1797 to 1815, it served as a lever for the lengthening of the working day and the lowering of wages."

Marx then adduces what he calls "very important material bearing on the movement of wages during that period," drawn from two Blue Books: the Report and Evidence from the Select Committee on Petitions Respecting the Corn Laws (Parliamentary Session 1813–14), and the Report from the Lords' Committee, on the State of the Growth, Commerce, and Consumption of Grain (Session 1814–15). These contain "documentary evidence of the constant lowering of the price of labour from the beginning of the Anti-Jacobin War." In weaving, "piece-wages had fallen so low that in spite of the very great lengthening of the working day, the daily wage was then lower than it had been before." A contemporaneous pamphlet (Remarks on the Commercial Policy of Great Britain, London, 1815, p. 48) is quoted: "The real earnings of the cotton weaver are now far less than they were; his superiority over the common labourer, which at first was very great, has now almost entirely ceased. Indeed … the difference in the wages of skilful and common labour is far less now than at any former period." Two things are happening at once: the absolute wage is falling even as hours lengthen, and the wage differential between skilled and common labour is being compressed — a direct consequence, in Marx's framework, of the intensification and homogenisation of labour that generalised piece-work produces.

For agriculture, Marx quotes a pamphlet written in defence of the landlords and farmers (A Defence of the Landowners and Farmers of Great Britain, London, 1814, pp. 4–5): "By far the greater part of agricultural operations is done by people, who are hired for the day or on piece-work. Their weekly wages are about 12 shillings, and although it may be assumed that a man earns on piece-work under the greater stimulus to labour, 1 shilling, or perhaps 2 shillings more than on weekly wages, yet it is found, on calculating his total income, that his loss of employment, during the year, outweighs this gain." This is the risk-transfer point made empirically: the piece-premium is more than cancelled by the unpaid idle time that the piece-form permits. The pamphlet continues, in a passage Marx quotes without comment: wages "bear a certain proportion to the price of the necessary means of subsistence, so that a man with two children is able to bring up his family without recourse to parish relief" — a statement which, in Marx's framework, is simply the classical confirmation that the wage hovers around the value of labour-power, no more.

The chapter's historical section concludes with a quotation from Malthus's Inquiry into the Nature and Progress of Rent (London, 1815, p. 49, note): "I confess that I see, with misgiving, the great extension of the practice of piece-wage. Really hard work during 12 or 14 hours of the day, or for any longer time, is too much for any human being." Marx's deployment of the arch-defender of population doctrine as a witness against the piece-wage is a characteristic rhetorical move: the system is condemned even by those whose economic convictions lie wholly on the other side.

Productivity, the Falling Piece-Rate, and the Contradiction Made Visible

The final analytic section examines what happens to the piece-rate when the productivity of labour changes. Marx's reasoning is rooted in the labour theory of value: "Changes in the productivity of labour mean that the same quantity of a given product represents an amount of labour-time which varies. Therefore, the piece-wage also varies, for it is the expression of the price of a definite amount of labour-time."

He returns to his example. Twenty-four pieces in 12 hours; value-product of 12 hours 6 shillings; daily value of labour-power 3 shillings; price of an hour of labour 3d; wage per piece 1½d; half an hour of labour absorbed in one piece. Now suppose productivity doubles, so that the same working day yields 48 pieces instead of 24, "all other circumstances remaining unchanged." Then "the piece-wage falls from 1½d. to ¾d., as every piece now only represents ¼ instead of ½ a working hour. 24 × 1½d. = 3s., and, similarly, 48 × ¾d. = 3s." The arithmetic is exact and the implication is total: the daily wage is unchanged at 3 shillings, the value-product is unchanged at 6 shillings (since value is fixed by labour-time and not by output), and the entire gain from increased productivity accrues to capital in the form of cheaper unit costs. The piece-wage, as Marx says, "is lowered in the same proportion as the number of pieces produced in the same time rises, and therefore in the same proportion as the amount of labour-time employed on the same piece falls."

This is where Marx engages Ure most directly, and the engagement is a study in how to read apologetics. Ure writes (Philosophy of Manufactures, p. 317): "The productive power of his spinning-machine is accurately measured, and the rate of pay for work done with it decreases with, though not as, the increase of its productive power." Marx notes two things. First, Ure himself later contradicts the apologetic qualification: he admits that lengthening the mule increases the quantity of labour required, so that "the amount of labour does not, therefore, diminish in the same ratio as its productivity increases." Second, Ure's own further evidence shows the spinner's position deteriorating even where his money earnings nominally rise. Marx quotes (p. 321): "By this increase the productive power of the machine will be augmented one-fifth. When this event happens the spinner will not be paid at the same rate for work done as he was before, but as that rate will not be diminished in the ratio of one-fifth, the improvement will augment his money earnings for any given number of hours of work" — but, Ure immediately adds, "the foregoing statement requires a certain modification … The spinner has to pay something additional for juvenile aid out of his additional sixpence." And Ure also concedes that improvements in machinery "displace a portion of adults," which, Marx notes dryly, "certainly does not tend to raise wages." The pattern is revealing: the apologist's own casuistry is undone by the apologist's own data.

The Final Struggle: The Worker's "Gross Error" and the Capitalist's Rejoinder

The chapter closes on the terrain of class struggle. The change in the piece-wage, though "so far purely nominal" (since the daily wage is unchanged when productivity rises), "leads to constant struggles between the capitalist and the worker." Marx identifies three distinct grounds of friction. First, the capitalist "uses it as a pretext for actually lowering the price of labour" — the nominal adjustment is opportunistically extended into a real one. Second, an increase in the productivity of labour is "accompanied by an increase in its intensity," so the worker's real expenditure of effort rises without commensurate compensation. Third, and most revealingly, "the worker takes the outward appearance of piece-wages seriously, i.e. he thinks his product is being paid for and not his labour-power, and he therefore resists any reduction of wages which is not accompanied by a reduction in the selling price of the commodity." This is the practical consequence of the theoretical illusion with which the chapter began. The worker's own tacit theory of the wage — that he is paid for the product — leads him to demand a share in any increase in the product's value in the market. H. Fawcett's The Economic Position of the British Labourer (Cambridge and London, 1865, p. 178) is cited for the workers' practice: "The operatives … carefully watch the price of the raw material and the price of manufactured goods, and are thus enabled to form an accurate estimate of their master's profits." From the appearance that the product is being paid for, it is a short step to the claim that worsening sale conditions, or improving ones, ought to be reflected in the rate — a type of claim that shades into profit-sharing.

Marx's comment on the capitalist's response is one of the most ironic passages in the chapter: "The capitalist rightly rejects such pretensions as being gross errors as to the nature of wage-labour. He cries out against this presumptuous attempt to lay taxes on the progress of industry, and declares roundly that the productivity of labour does not concern the worker in the least." The word "rightly" is doing enormous work. From the standpoint of the concept of wage-labour — the purchase of a definite labour-power for a definite price — the capitalist is correct: the productivity of labour is none of the worker's business, because the worker is not selling a product but a capacity. The worker's error is not moral but conceptual, and it is an error manufactured by the wage-form itself, which presents the product as the object of exchange. The capitalist's advantage consists precisely in being able to switch between the two readings as convenience dictates: the form says "you are paid for your product," but the substance says "your product is not your concern."

This duplicity is documented in the chapter's final footnotes. The London Standard of 26 October 1861 (note 19) reports proceedings brought by the firm of John Bright and Co. before the Rochdale magistrates "to prosecute for intimidation the agents of the Carpet Weavers' Trades' Union." Bright's partners had installed new machinery "which would turn out 240 yards of carpet in the time and with the labour (!) previously required to produce 160 yards." The law and the capitalists held that "the workmen had no claim whatever to share in the profits made by the investment of their employer's capital in mechanical improvements." Accordingly Bright proposed to lower the rate of pay from 1½d per yard to 1d — precisely the productivity-proportional reduction Marx's arithmetic predicted — "leaving the earnings of the men exactly the same as before for the same labour. But there was a nominal reduction, of which the operatives, it is asserted, had not fair warning beforehand." The struggle is thus not over the level of the wage but over whether the piece-rate is a price of a product (subject to renegotiation when the product becomes cheaper to make) or a price of labour-power (fixed by the cost of reproducing the worker). The capitalists answered: the latter — while the workers, taking the form at its word, answered: the former. And the final note (note 20), from On Combinations of Trades (new edn, London, 1834, p. 42), gives the definitive statement of the capitalist position: "'Trades' Unions, in their desire to maintain wages, endeavour to share in the benefits of improved machinery!' (Quelle horreur!) 'The demanding higher wages, because labour is abbreviated, is in other words the endeavour to establish a duty on mechanical improvements.'" With that, the chapter closes: the wage-form's illusion, the worker's resistance to its dissolution, and the capitalist's insistence on the harshest version of the truth.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The most fundamental lesson of Chapter 21 is a principle of form-analysis as such: the phenomenal form of an economic relation is not a neutral wrapper around its content but a historically active mechanism. Marx's entire procedure here consists in distinguishing essence from appearance without ever treating the distinction as inert. Piece-wages are time-wages in essence — the same 3 shillings, the same 6 shillings of value-product, the same 100 per cent rate of exploitation. But the form is not therefore epiphenomenal. It changes the distribution of surplus-value among capitals (via subletting), the organisation of the labour process (supervision becomes "superfluous," domestic labour becomes viable, internal hierarchies emerge), the risk-bearing of production (idle time falls on the worker, the "hour-system" is sustained), the subjective disposition of the worker (independence, competition, self-exploitation), and the dynamic path of the wage (the average is lowered even as individuals rise above it). Essence without form is a mere abstraction; form without essence is ideology.

The second lesson is that this chapter supplies the missing link in the theory of surplus-value's concealment. Value theory explains how surplus-value is produced; the wage-form chapters explain how it is rendered invisible. Time-wages conceal the division of the day by presenting a price for "labour." Piece-wages conceal it twice over, because they additionally present a price for the product, thereby transferring the relation into the seemingly neutral and voluntarist domain of market exchange — the worker sells goods, and gets paid for them in proportion to his efficiency. The worker's own spontaneous consciousness, as Marx notes, is formed by this appearance; his resistance to a rate cut takes the form of a claim about the product's price, and he is thereby placed in the position of someone making a "gross error" about his own condition. The form of wages is thus not merely an economic category but an ideological one: it generates the very "trade-union consciousness" that capital can then denounce as ignorance of economics.

The third lesson is historical and developmental. Piece-wages are the form "most appropriate to the capitalist mode of production" — not because they are exploitative in a merely local sense, but because they are the form in which capital's drive to intensify and extend labour is transferred from external coercion to the worker's own self-interest, and because they are uniquely suited to the period of manufacture's subcontracting hierarchies, to the prolongation of the working day in 1797–1815, and, under the Factory Acts, to capital's need to increase output "only by intensifying labour," since hours are legally capped. The empirical dossier from the Blue Books of 1813–15 corroborates the theoretical prediction: falling real wages for weavers, compression of the skilled-unskilled differential, an agricultural proletariat whose piece-work premium is cancelled by unemployment. Marx's structural claim — that piece-wages give capital a leverage for lengthening the working day and lowering wages — is therefore not a rhetorical flourish but a stated and partly documented historical thesis.

The broader implications extend in two directions. For political economy, the chapter exposes the apologetic use of the illusion, exemplified by Watts and unmasked by the exposure of his earlier position — a case study in how an intellectual converts the appearance of worker independence into a defence of the piece-work system. For workers' practice, the chapter explains why the piece-rate is an inevitable and perpetual site of conflict: it is a price attached to a product whose value is not the worker's, and both parties can play the double character of the form against each other. Marx's closing irony — that the capitalist "rightly" rejects the workers' claim — is a precise statement that within the wage-relation as legally defined, the worker's moral conviction is a category mistake, and that the only remedy lies not in correcting the category but in transcending the relation that generates it.
