Volume 1 • Chapter 20: Time-Wages

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 20, "Time-Wages," is the opening chapter of Part VI of Capital Volume One and the first of two chapters (the second being "Piece-Wages") devoted to the forms of appearance of the wage. Its core problem is a problem of form. Marx begins by asserting that "wages themselves again take many forms," and that this plurality is invisible to "the ordinary economic treatises, which, in their crude obsession with the material side [Stoff], ignore all differences of form." The neglect of form is not a minor taxonomic failing; it is precisely through form that the essential relation — the extraction of unpaid labour — is concealed, and, more importantly for this chapter, it is through form that new mechanisms of exploitation become possible.

The chapter's central thesis is that in time-wages, the price of labour and the amount of the wage are two distinct quantities that can move independently, and even in opposite directions. Because the sale of labour-power "always takes place for definite periods of time," its value is necessarily presented as day-wages, week-wages — "time-wages." From this it follows that the same daily or weekly sum can represent radically different prices per hour of labour, and that wages may stay constant while the price of labour falls, or rise while the price of labour stays constant or falls. Marx's intellectual objective is to demonstrate that this formal slippage is not an academic curiosity but the heartbeat of a cumulative spiral: a low price of labour compels longer hours; longer hours lower the price of labour further; competition among workers and among capitalists transmits and normalizes the result, until "an abnormally low selling price of the commodity ... becomes the constant basis of a miserable wage for excessive hours of work."

The chapter's placement is deliberate. It functions as the hinge between the abstract laws of Chapter 17 (on changes in the relative magnitudes of the price of labour-power and surplus-value) and the historical material of the working-day analysis and the 1860s factory reports. Marx states explicitly that Chapter 17's laws "can be transformed, by a simple alteration in their form, into laws of wages," and that the difference between the exchange-value of labour-power and the sum of means of subsistence into which it converts now "appears as the distinction between nominal and real wages." He refuses to repeat the essential analysis at the level of appearance — a methodological signature of the whole work — and restricts himself to "a few points which characterize time-wages." What those few points reveal is a form of payment that, left unregulated, converts the wage contract into an instrument of unlimited and self-reinforcing intensification of labour.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Formal Problem: Why Wages Require a Theory of Form

Marx opens by conceding a limitation of scope: an exposition of all wage forms belongs to "the special study of wage-labour, and not, therefore, to this work." He will nonetheless treat "the two fundamental forms" — time-wages and, in the following chapter, piece-wages. The methodological target is announced immediately: the "ordinary economic treatises," in their "crude obsession with the material side [Stoff]," ignore "all differences of form." The bracketed German term Stoff signals the Stoff/Form distinction that organizes Marx's critical method: the classical economists treat wages as a quantity of stuff (a sum of money, a basket of goods) and therefore miss that the form in which that stuff is paid — by the hour, by the day, by the piece — has its own causal powers. Form is not a wrapper around an invariant content; it is a determinant of the content.

This is the reason the chapter cannot be read as mere description. If the form of payment is causally efficacious, then the same "value of labour-power" can support wildly different lived outcomes and wildly different rates of exploitation depending on the temporal unit in which it is contracted.

From Laws of Value to Laws of Wages: Nominal and Real Wages

The transition into the substantive argument is compressed into a few sentences. Because "the sale of labour-power ... always takes place for definite periods of time," the converted form in which the daily or weekly value of labour-power is "directly presented" is time-wages — "therefore day-wages, etc." Two transformations follow. First, the laws of Chapter 17, governing changes in the relative magnitudes of the price of labour-power and surplus-value, can be re-expressed "by a simple alteration in their form" as laws of wages. Second, the distinction worked out at the level of essence — between the exchange-value of labour-power and the sum of means of subsistence into which that value is converted — now reappears at the level of appearance as the distinction between nominal wages and real wages.

Here a definition is embedded rather than announced: nominal wages are "the sum of money which the worker receives for his daily or weekly labour," i.e., "his wages estimated in value"; real wages are the sum of means of subsistence into which that money converts. Marx adds a founding assumption in footnote 1: "Here we always assume that the value of money itself remains constant." Without that assumption, every comparison below would be contaminated by monetary variation.

Crucially, Marx explicitly declines to repeat the essential analysis: "It would be useless to repeat here, when dealing with the form of appearance, what we have already worked out in relation to the essential form." This is a discipline of exposition, not an evasion. The chapter's whole force depends on the reader already knowing that wages are the converted form of the value of labour-power, and that the "price of labour" is an irrational expression concealing unpaid labour.

The Unit of Measurement: Price of Labour as Daily Value Divided by Hours

Because the same daily wage can represent very different prices of labour, Marx must distinguish "between the sum total of the daily or weekly wages ... and the price of labour." He defines the latter with the help of Sir Edward West (footnote 2): "The price of labour is the sum paid for a given quantity of labour" (Price of Corn and Wages of Labour, London, 1826, p. 67). Marx names West's earlier anonymous 1815 Essay on the Application of Capital to Land an "epoch-making work in the history of political economy," yet in a later footnote accuses him of dismissing the central question — "How is the price of labour determined?" — "with mere banalities."

Marx supplies the answer West failed to give. The average price of labour is the average daily value of labour-power divided by the average number of hours in the working day. His worked example: if the daily value of labour-power is 3 shillings, which is "the value-product of 6 working hours," and the working day is 12 hours, then the price of one working hour is 3/12 of a shilling = 3d. This hourly rate "serves as the unit measure for the price of labour." The formula is thus:

price of labour (per hour) = daily value of labour-power ÷ hours in the working day.

Two structural features are already latent here. The numerator is a value (the reproduction cost of a day's labour-power); the denominator is a duration. The ratio therefore behaves strangely under change: it is not a market price equilibrating supply and demand, but an arithmetic quotient whose components can be manipulated by the capitalist's control over hours.

Two-Way Divergence: Constant Wages, Falling Price of Labour — and the Reverse

Marx now works both directions of the divergence.

(a) Wages constant, price of labour falling. Suppose the usual working day is 10 hours and the daily value of labour-power is 3 shillings (= 36d). Then the hourly price is 3.6d (3 3/5 d). If the working day rises to 12 hours, the hourly price falls to 3d; at 15 hours it falls to 2.4d (2 2/5 d). "Despite all this, daily or weekly wages remain unchanged." The worker receives the same 3 shillings for a longer working day.

(b) Wages rising, price of labour constant or falling. With a 10-hour day and 3 shillings daily value, the hourly price is 3 3/5 d. If the worker, "owing to an increase in the number of orders," works 12 hours and the price of labour remains the same, his daily wage rises to 3s 7 1/5 d (12 × 3.6d = 43.2d) "without any variation having taken place in the price of labour." The same result follows if "instead of the extensive magnitude of labour, its intensive magnitude increased" — that is, if the worker works harder rather than longer.

Marx draws out two consequences. First, "the rise of nominal daily or weekly wages may therefore be unaccompanied by any change in the price of labour, or may even be accompanied by a fall in the latter." Second, the same logic applies to family income: when "the quantity of labour provided by the head of the family is augmented by the labour of the members of his family," the household's total money income can rise while the price per hour of labour falls. The conclusion: "There are therefore methods of lowering the price of labour which are independent of any reduction in the nominal daily or weekly wage."

This is where the nuance of rival positions enters. Footnote 3 quotes West himself recognizing the phenomenon — "An increase in the wages of labour does not necessarily imply an enhancement of the price of labour. From fuller employment, and greater exertions, the wages of labour may be considerably increased, while the price of labour may continue the same" — yet Marx immediately undercuts him: West "dismisses the main question, 'How is the price of labour determined?', with mere banalities." Footnote 4 adds two more witnesses. The anonymous author of the Essay on Trade and Commerce — "the most fanatical representative of the eighteenth-century industrial bourgeoisie" — saw it, confusedly: "It is the quantity of labour and not the price of it ... that is determined by the price of provisions ... Master-manufacturers know that there are various ways of raising and falling the price of labour, besides that of altering its nominal amount." And N. W. Senior, in Three Lectures on the Rate of Wages (1830), which "uses West's work without mentioning it," writes: "The labourer is principally interested in the amount of wages." Marx's rejoinder is acid: Senior means "the worker is principally interested in what he receives, the nominal sum of his wages, not in what he gives, the quantity of labour!"

The General Law of Time-Wages, and Under-Employment

Marx then states the general law in two halves:

  1. Given the amount of daily/weekly labour, the daily/weekly wage depends on the price of labour, which varies either with the value of labour-power or with the divergences between its price and its value.
  2. Given the price of labour, the daily/weekly wage depends on the quantity of labour expended daily or weekly.

From here Marx derives a phenomenon distinct from overwork: under-employment. Repeating the unit of measurement (12-hour day, 3 shillings daily value, 3d per hour, 6d of value produced per hour), he notes that if the worker is employed for less than 12 hours — "for instance only for 6 or 8 hours" — he receives, at the same 3d rate, only 1s 6d or 2s a day. The arithmetic is decisive: to produce a day's wage equal to the value of his labour-power, he must work on average 6 hours; and since he works "only half of every hour for himself, and half for the capitalist," he cannot obtain the value-product of 6 hours unless he is employed for the full 12. "In previous chapters we saw the destructive consequences of over-work; but here we come upon the origin of the sufferings which arise for the worker out of his being insufficiently employed."

Footnote 5 sharpens the nuance by contrasting abnormal under-employment with a legally reduced working day. The former "has nothing to do with the absolute length of the working day, and may just as well occur in a working day of 15 hours as in one of 6." The normal hourly price is calculated on a 15-hour basis in one case and a 6-hour basis in the other, and "the result is therefore the same if the worker is employed in the one case for only 7 1/2 hours, and in the other case for only 3 hours" — in both instances he receives only 1s 6d, half the daily value. The absolute length of the normal day is irrelevant to the proportional shortfall.

Hour-Wages at the Capitalist's Will: Destruction of the Paid/Unpaid Nexus

The chapter now reaches its theoretical pivot. If the hour's wage is fixed so that "the capitalist does not bind himself to pay a day's or a week's wage, but only to pay wages for the hours during which he chooses to employ the worker," then the capitalist can employ the worker for less time than the average that served as the basis for computing the hourly rate. Since the unit of measurement is "determined by the ratio of the daily value of labour-power to the working day of a given number of hours," it "naturally loses all meaning as soon as the working day ceases to contain a definite number of hours." The consequence is stated in a phrase of great formal precision: "The connection between the paid and the unpaid labour is destroyed."

What this means is that the legal form of the wage contract no longer guarantees the worker even the equivalent of his subsistence, let alone a determinate proportion of paid to unpaid time. The capitalist can "wring from the worker a certain quantity of surplus labour without allowing him the labour-time necessary for his own subsistence." He can "annihilate all regularity of employment" and, "according to his own convenience, caprice, and the interest of the moment, make the most frightful over-work alternate with relative or absolute cessation of work." He can abnormally lengthen the day "without giving the worker any corresponding compensation, under the pretence of paying 'the normal price of labour.'"

This is why Marx calls the London building workers' resistance "the perfectly rational revolt" — the strike of 1859–60 (the footnote corrects the text: it began in July 1859 and ended in February 1860) was a revolt against the hourly-wage form as such. Marx also marks the limits of reform: "The legal limitation of the working day puts an end to mischievous acts of this kind, though it does not of course end the diminution of employment caused by the competition of machinery, by changes in the quality of the workers employed, and partial or general crises."

Prolongation Without Compensation: The Rising Numerator and the Fractional "Normal Day"

A second, subtler mechanism follows. Even when the daily or weekly wage increases, the price of labour may remain nominally constant "and yet fall below its normal level" — this happens "every time the working day is prolonged beyond its customary length, while the price of labour (reckoned per working hour) remains constant." Marx's reasoning is expressed in the terms of the ratio:

daily value of labour-power ÷ working day

If the denominator increases, "the numerator increases still more rapidly. The amount of deterioration in labour-power, and therefore its value, increases with the duration of its functioning, and to a more rapid degree than the increase of that duration." In other words, the longer the day, the greater the wear and tear that must be repaired for labour-power to reproduce itself; the reproduction cost rises faster than the hours. A constant hourly rate therefore understates the true value of the labour actually expended. This is the theoretical justification for overtime premia — and simultaneously the demonstration that customary premia are systematically inadequate.

Marx documents the spontaneous emergence of a normative division of the day in industries without legal limits. A habit grows up "of regarding the working day as normal only up to a point in time, for instance up to the expiration of the tenth hour ('normal working day', 'the day's work', 'the regular hours of work')." Beyond that point, time becomes "overtime," paid at a "better hourly rate ('extra pay'), although often in a proportion which is ridiculously small." Footnote 6 records that in lace-making, overtime paid from 1/2 d. to 2d. per hour stands "in painful contrast to the amount of injury produced to the health and stamina of the workpeople," and that the small sum earned "is also often obliged to be spent in extra nourishment." The sting is structural: "The normal working day exists here as a fraction of the actual working day, and over the year as a whole the latter is often more common than the former." Footnote 7 gives the paper-staining case: "the day's work of 10 hours is finished by 4.30 p.m., and all after that is overtime, and we seldom leave off working before 6 p.m., so that we are really working overtime the whole year round."

The mechanism is perverse: the low price of labour during the so-called normal time "compels the worker to work during the better paid overtime, if he wishes to obtain a sufficient wage at all." Footnote 8's Scottish bleaching-works illustrate: ten hours a day at a nominal 1s 2d, plus 3 or 4 hours of daily overtime at 3d per hour — roughly double the regular hourly rate. The effect, in the inspector's words, was that "a man could not earn more than 8s. per week when working the ordinary hours ... without overtime they could not earn a fair day's wages." The same footnote records that "The higher wages, for getting adult males to work longer hours, are a temptation too strong to be resisted," and describes London book-binders' indentured girls of 14–15 working until 10, 11, 12 or 1 o'clock in the last week of each month, "tempted" by extra pay and supper in neighbouring public houses — a "great debauchery among these 'young immortals' " whose compensation Marx dryly notes lies partly in binding "many Bibles and other edifying books." Again: legal limitation "puts an end to this pastime."

The Empirical Regularity: Longer Hours, Lower Wages

Having built the mechanism, Marx now appeals to observation: "It is a generally known fact that the longer the working day in a branch of industry, the lower the wages are." Footnote 10 supplies the inspectors' generalization — "where long hours are the rule, small wages are also so" (31 October 1863, p. 9), and "The work which obtains the scanty pittance of food is, for the most part, excessively prolonged" (Public Health, Sixth Report, 1864, p. 15). The decisive evidence is comparative and quantitative: the factory inspector Alexander Redgrave, in a comparative review of the twenty years from 1839 to 1859, showed that "wages rose in the factories under the Ten Hours' Act, while they fell in the factories where the work went on for 14 and 15 hours every day" (footnote 11). This is a natural-experiment style comparison: same country, same period, same industry, differing legal regimes — and the shorter day coincides with the higher wage.

The Cumulative Spiral of Low Wages and Overwork

From the second half of the general law — price of labour given, the wage depends on the quantity of labour — Marx deduces a feedback loop in two steps. Step one: "the lower the price of labour, the greater must be the quantity of labour, or the longer must be the working day, for the worker to secure even a miserable average wage. The low level of the price of labour acts here as a stimulus to the extension of the labour-time." Footnote 12 documents the English hand nail-makers, forced to work 15 hours a day (6 a.m. to 8 p.m.) at low prices in order to hammer out a wretched weekly wage: to earn 11d. or 1s. a day, the worker must work hard all the time, and from that must be deducted "the wear of the tools, the cost of firing, and something for waste iron ... which takes off altogether 2 1/2 d. or 3d." The women, working the same length of time, earn a weekly wage of only 5 shillings.

Step two: "the extension of the period of labour produces in its turn a fall in the price of labour, and with this a fall in the daily or the weekly wage." The circle closes: low price → long hours → lower price. And note the asymmetry Marx identifies in the ratio: "the determination of the price of labour by use of the ratio ... shows that a mere prolongation of the working day lowers the price of labour, if no compensatory factor enters. But the same circumstances which allow the capitalist in the long run to prolong the working day also allow him at first, and compel him finally, to reduce the price of labour nominally as well until the total price of the increased number of hours goes down, and therefore the daily or weekly wage falls."

The Two Competitive Mechanisms and the Underselling of Commodities

Marx then specifies "two kinds of circumstance" through which the nominal price is driven down.

First, competition among workers. "If one man does the work of 1 1/2 or 2 men, the supply of labour increases, although the supply of labour-power on the market remains constant." Here is a delicate distinction: the headcount of workers is unchanged, but the quantity of labour supplied rises with the intensity and duration of each worker's effort — so the effective supply of labour services increases. "The competition thus created between the workers allows the capitalist to force down the price of labour, while the fall in the price of labour allows him, on the other hand, to force up the hours of work still further." Footnote 13 quotes the inspectors: a factory worker who refused the customary long hours "would very shortly be replaced by somebody who would work any length of time, and thus be thrown out of employment." Senior, despite himself, concurs: "If one man performs the work of two ... the rate of profits will generally be raised ... in consequence of the additional supply of labour having diminished its price."

Second, competition among capitalists. Command over abnormal quantities of unpaid labour — "quantities in excess of the average social amount" — becomes a weapon. Since "a part of the price of the commodity consists of the price of labour," and since "the unpaid part of the price of labour does not need to be reckoned as part of the price of the commodity," that unpaid portion "may be given to the buyer as a present." The first step under the impulse of competition is to donate the unpaid surplus to the purchaser. The second step, "also compelled by competition, is the exclusion from the selling price of the commodity of at least a part of the abnormal surplus-value created by the extension of the working day." Thus "an abnormally low selling price of the commodity arises, at first sporadically, and becomes fixed by degrees; this lower selling price henceforward becomes the constant basis of a miserable wage for excessive hours of work, just as originally it was the product of those very circumstances." The exception becomes the norm.

Marx flags the methodological boundary: "This movement is simply indicated here, as the analysis of competition does not belong to this part of the investigation." He then "lets the capitalist speak." The Birmingham manufacturer complains: "there is so much competition of masters one against another, that many are obliged to do things as employers that they would otherwise be ashamed of; and yet no more money is made, but only the public gets the benefit." (Footnote 14.)

The Bakers' Jeremiad: The Capitalist's Semblance-Consciousness

The London bakers supply the chapter's richest case. Two sorts of bakers existed — the "full-priced," selling bread at its full price, and the "undersellers," selling below its normal price (cross-referenced to the earlier discussion of the bakers' night-work). The full-priced denounced their rivals before the Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry: the undersellers "only exist now by first defrauding the public, and next getting 18 hours' work out of their men for 12 hours' wages." The unpaid labour of the men "was made ... the source whereby the competition was carried on, and continues so to this day." Competition among the master-bakers "is the cause of the difficulty in getting rid of night-work," since an underseller "must make it up by getting more out of the labour of the men": "If I got only 12 hours' work out of my men, and my neighbour got 18 or 20, he must beat me in the selling price. If the men could insist on payment for overwork, this would be set right." A large number of the undersellers' employees "are foreigners and youths, who are obliged to accept almost any wages they can obtain." (Footnote 15.) Marx adds, from the same testimony, that the full-priced bakers themselves — as their spokesman Bennett admits — start their men at 11 p.m. and run them through the following day until as late as 7 in the evening.

Marx's interpretation is the theoretical heart of the passage. This "jeremiad" is interesting because "it shows how it is only the semblance of the relations of production which is reflected by the brain of the capitalist." The capitalist "does not know that the normal price of labour also includes a definite quantity of unpaid labour, and that this very unpaid labour is the normal source of his profits. The category of surplus labour-time does not exist at all for him, since it is included in the normal working day, which he thinks he has paid for in the day's wages." But overtime — prolongation beyond the limits corresponding to the usual price of labour — "certainly does exist for him," and against an underselling competitor he even insists on extra pay for it. Yet "he does not know that this extra pay also includes unpaid labour, just as much as the price of the customary hour of labour does."

This is not hypocrisy but a structural form of consciousness: the wage form itself, by presenting the value of labour-power as the price of a full day's labour, makes the unpaid portion invisible. The capitalist is not lying when he believes he has paid for the whole day; he is describing the semblance faithfully.

The Final Arithmetic: One-Half versus One-Third

The chapter closes with a numerical demonstration of the point that even "extra pay" is a wage for unpaid labour. Take a 12-hour day whose hourly price is 3d. Since the daily value of labour-power is 3 shillings and it is the value-product of 6 hours, one working hour produces 6d of new value. The paid 3d is therefore "the value-product of half a working hour," and "the capitalist appropriates one-half of the working hour" without payment. Now suppose overtime is paid at 4d per hour — "the value-product of 2/3 of a working hour." Here the capitalist "appropriates one-third" of the hour, again "without making any payment in return."

The structure of the argument is exact. Under the normal hour, the rate of exploitation is 100% (3d unpaid against 3d paid). Under the overtime hour, the rate of exploitation falls to 50% (2d unpaid against 4d paid) — yet the absolute mass of unpaid labour still rises, because an additional hour is worked and because the total day is extended. The premium is real but insufficient, and it never reaches the point of compensating the increased depreciation of labour-power that Marx identified earlier in the rising numerator. The worker trades a longer life for a slightly better-paid hour; the capitalist pockets the difference in both directions.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The fundamental lesson of Chapter 20 is that the wage is not a thing but a relation expressed in a temporal form, and that this form has autonomous causal force. Three systemic principles emerge.

First, the two-quantity principle: the price of labour (money per unit of time) and the amount of the wage (money per day or week) are distinct variables capable of opposite movement. This dissolves the ordinary intuition that higher wages mean better-paid labour, and it dismantles the standard of "a fair day's wage" as anything self-evident — as Senior's complacent formula ("the labourer is principally interested in the amount of wages") shows, the ideology of the wage form is precisely to fix attention on the amount while the price is quietly eroded.

Second, the reflexivity principle: the determinants of wages are mutually reinforcing, not equilibrating. Low hourly price drives longer hours; longer hours drive the hourly price lower, and increase labour-power's own reproduction cost faster than the hours increase; competition among workers and competition among capitalists then convert a sporadic advantage into a settled norm — the abnormally low selling price becomes "the constant basis of a miserable wage for excessive hours of work." There is no tendency to a natural wage floor within this form; the spiral runs downward until an external limit is imposed.

Third, the juridical-indeterminacy principle: when the contract fixes an hourly rate without fixing the number of hours, "the connection between the paid and the unpaid labour is destroyed." The worker loses the guarantee of even subsistence, and the capitalist acquires the power to alternate "the most frightful over-work" with "relative or absolute cessation of work." This is why the London building workers of 1859–60 demanded, as conditions for accepting hourly wages, (1) a legally defined normal day of 9 or 10 hours, with the hourly price for the 10-hour day set higher than for the 9-hour day, and (2) proportional extra pay for every hour beyond the normal day. Their two demands are the exact political translation of the chapter's two theoretical findings.

The broader implications are epistemological and political. Epistemologically, Marx shows that the capitalist's consciousness is a faithful mirror of "the semblance of the relations of production" — the employer genuinely does not perceive the unpaid labour embedded in the normal wage, because the wage form itself performs that concealment. Ideology here is a structural effect of the form of appearance, not a delusion to be corrected by argument alone. Politically, the chapter establishes the necessity and the limits of the legal limitation of the working day: it ends the specific mischiefs of hourly wage-by-caprice but cannot end unemployment generated by machinery, changes in the quality of workers employed, or crises. Above all, it establishes that wage struggles are never merely about the amount of money; they are struggles over the form of payment, the length of the day, and the rate at which the worker's life is exchanged for a price.
