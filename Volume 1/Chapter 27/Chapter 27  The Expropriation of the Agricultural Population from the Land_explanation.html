Volume 1 • Chapter 27: The Expropriation of the Agricultural Population from the Land

An Exhaustive Deconstruction and Analysis

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 27 is the opening movement of the celebrated Part Eight of Capital, Volume One, the section Marx himself labels "So-Called Primitive Accumulation." Its title is deceptively narrow. On the surface, the chapter narrates how English and Scottish peasants lost their land between roughly the late fourteenth century and the middle of the nineteenth. Beneath that surface, however, the chapter is doing something far more ambitious: it is dismantling the founding myth of political economy. Classical economists treated the emergence of capital and wage-labour as a natural, almost pre-historical fact — a world in which some people happened to possess money and means of production while others happened to possess nothing but their capacity to work, and in which the first group "saved" and the second group was "lazy" or "improvident." Marx's counter-thesis is stark and uniform across the chapter: the separation of the producer from the means of production — the precondition of the wage relation — was not natural, not gradual, and not consensual. It was the product of systematic, state-supported violence, fraud, and legal theft.

The chapter's central problem, therefore, is genetic rather than distributive. It is not asking who owns what now but asking how the ownership pattern that capital presupposes ever came into being. Marx answers that question through a single, disciplined historical narrative. He begins with a portrait of fifteenth-century England as a society of free peasant proprietors and village communities whose wealth lay precisely in a multiplicity of small holders and in broad, shared, common lands. He then traces four great expropriating instruments, which accumulate and reinforce each other across four centuries:

  • The dissolution of the great feudal households, which ejected a mass of "free" retainers onto the labour market.
  • The Tudor-era conversion of arable land into sheep-walks, driven by the wool manufacture of Flanders and enforced by the new landed nobility that began to value money above people.
  • The Reformation and the spoliation of church lands, which destroyed the religious bulwark of the older forms of landed property and threw thousands of sub-tenants and ecclesiastical dependents into destitution.
  • The theft of the state domains and the communal lands, first by open force and then — after the "Glorious Revolution" of 1688 — by Act of Parliament, until the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries had perfected the "clearing of estates" in which human beings were literally swept off the soil.

The intellectual objective is to demonstrate two things simultaneously: first, that the historical process that created capital is the same process that created the modern proletariat; and second, that this process was carried through by an identifiable coalition of classes — the new landed aristocracy, the new bankocracy, high finance, and the manufacturers dependent on protective duties — using the law as the "instrument" rather than the obstacle of expropriation. By the time the chapter ends, the reader is presented with a single, cold balance sheet: "They conquered the field for capitalist agriculture, incorporated the soil into capital, and created for the urban industries the necessary supplies of free and rightless proletarians."

Fitted into the broader architecture of the book, Chapter 27 supplies the historical proof for the theoretical claims advanced earlier. The first volume of Capital had already established, in the analysis of the labour process and of surplus value, that capital requires the separation of the worker from the conditions of labour as its precondition. Chapter 27 shows how that precondition was manufactured. It thus closes the conceptual circle opened at the beginning of the volume: capital is not a thing but a social relation, and the chapter's function is to demonstrate the bloodstained genesis of that relation.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

1. The Baseline: England's Fifteenth-Century World of Free Peasant Proprietors

Marx opens with a deliberate and provocative historical claim: "In England, serfdom had disappeared in practice by the last part of the fourteenth century." The phrase "in practice" is doing significant work. He does not deny that "feudal trappings" survived and might, on a formal reading, have continued to "disguise" the underlying reality. But what matters for the analysis is the effective character of property and labour. The immense majority of the population consisted, by the fifteenth century, of free peasant proprietors — smallholders whose legal title might be embedded in the vocabulary of a lord's domain but whose actual control over their plots was absolute.

Several supporting structures were dissolved by this baseline fact. In the larger manorial domains, the old bailiff, himself a serf, had been displaced by the free farmer — an early hint that even within the seigneurial economy, a relationship based on money was beginning to replace one based on status. The agricultural wage-labourers, Marx explains, were of two kinds: some were themselves peasants who worked on large estates only in their leisure time, and some were an independent class of specialised wage-labourers. But even the latter were "in practice" peasants, because their wages were supplemented by an allotment of arable land "to the extent of four or more acres" attached to their cottages. In addition — and this is the lynchpin of the entire chapter — they, "like the other peasants," enjoyed the right to exploit the common land, which "gave pasture to their cattle, and furnished them with timber, fire-wood, turf, etc."

The footnote material deepens this portrait rather than merely ornamenting it. Marx quotes Macaulay, "a systematic falsifier of history" who "minimizes facts of this kind as much as possible," and yet even Macaulay concedes that not fewer than 160,000 proprietors — who with their families made up more than a seventh of the whole population — derived their subsistence from little freehold estates, with average incomes estimated at £60 to £70 a year, and that the number of persons tilling their own land exceeded "the number of those who farmed the land of others." By the last third of the seventeenth century, four-fifths of the English people were still agriculturalists. Marx's rhetorical strategy is clear: he does not want to be accused of romanticising the medieval past. He chooses as his witness a historian whose ideological biases run in exactly the opposite direction, and then demonstrates that even the hostile witness confirms the substance of the claim.

A second footnote — on the serf — sharpens a conceptual distinction that will become crucial later in the chapter. Even the serf, Marx insists, was "not only the owner of the piece of land attached to his house… but also a co-proprietor of the common land." He quotes Mirabeau on Silesia, where serfs possess common lands, and contrasts the Silesian case with the Neumark, where partition of the commons had been implemented "with very great success." The purpose of this footnote is to establish that common land ownership was not exotic or marginal but deeply embedded across European feudalism, surviving even in the most degraded forms of personal dependence. A third footnote annexes Japan as an illustrative case: because its landed property remained "purely feudal" and its small-scale agriculture developed, Japan "gives a much truer picture of the European Middle Ages than all our history books." The aside also contains a methodological jab: "It is far too easy to be 'liberal' at the expense of the Middle Ages."

2. The Structural Logic of Feudal Power and Why It Ruled Out Capital

The chapter then sets out a general proposition about feudalism — not merely as an English phenomenon but as a European one. "In all countries of Europe, feudal production is characterized by division of the soil amongst the greatest possible number of sub-feudatories." The reason is political and military rather than economic. The power of the feudal lord, "like that of the sovereign," depended not on the length of his rent-roll — not on the magnitude of the money he could extract — but on the number of his subjects, and the number of his subjects in turn depended on the number of peasant proprietors. A lord with a hundred small tenants was stronger, in the medieval sense of the word, than a lord with two large estates.

This produces the apparent paradox Marx then explains. After the Norman Conquest, England's soil was carved into "gigantic baronies," one of which might contain some 900 of the old Anglo-Saxon lordships. Yet the country was "strewn with small peasant properties," merely interspersed here and there with great seigniorial domains. The baronial map was a patchwork; behind every patch stood a multiplicity of smallholders. And it was this very multiplicity, together with "the urban prosperity so characteristic of the fifteenth century," that made possible the popular well-being Chancellor Fortescue celebrated in his De laudibus legum Angliae. But — and this is the hinge on which the entire chapter turns — the same conditions that produced popular wealth "ruled out wealth in the form of capital." Peasant prosperity and capitalist accumulation are here presented as structurally incompatible. Capital requires the concentration of means of production in few hands and the severing of producers from those means. A society of 160,000 small proprietors sharing the commons was, precisely because it was comfortable, incapable of generating capital.

3. The Prelude (c. 1470–1530): Retainers, Wool, and the Slogan of Sheep-Walks

Against this baseline, the "revolution that laid the foundation of the capitalist mode of production" is dated with precision to "the last third of the fifteenth century and the first few decades of the sixteenth." The first instrument was the dissolution of the bands of feudal retainers — the armed households of the nobility who, in Sir James Steuart's vivid phrase, "everywhere uselessly filled house and castle." Marx is careful to distribute causal credit here. The royal power, itself a product of bourgeois development, "forcibly hastened the dissolution of these bands of retainers in its striving for absolute sovereignty," but it was "by no means the sole cause of it." The deeper cause lay with the great feudal lords themselves, who, in "their defiant opposition to the king and Parliament, created an incomparably larger proletariat by forcibly driving the peasantry from the land, to which the latter had the same feudal title as the lords themselves, and by usurpation of the common lands."

The juridical point embedded here deserves emphasis: the peasantry possessed "the same feudal title" to the land as the lords did. There was no clean legal hierarchy in which the lord was owner and the peasant a mere guest. Both held derivative titles; the difference was one of force, not of law. What the lords accomplished was the conversion of their de facto superiority into de jure private ownership — a usurpation summarised in this single sentence.

The immediate economic trigger — "the direct impulse for these evictions" — was the rapid expansion of wool manufacture in Flanders and the corresponding rise in the price of wool in England. The new nobility, "the child of its time, for which money was the power of all powers," saw its opportunity and adopted as its slogan the "Transformation of arable land into sheep-walks." The old nobility had been destroyed by the great feudal wars; the new one was a commercial animal. Wool was worth more than wheat, and depopulation was the arithmetic consequence.

4. The Chroniclers' Testimony and Marx's Methodological Caution

Marx then summons contemporary witnesses. Harrison, in his Description of England, describes how the "great incroachers" razed the dwellings of peasants and the cottages of labourers, "or doomed to decay." The statutes of manors reveal "seventeene, eighteene, or twentie houses… shrunk," and "England was neuer less furnished with people than at the present." Harrison complains of "cities and townes either utterly decaied or more than a quarter or half diminished, though some one be a little increased here or there; of townes pulled downe for sheepe-walks, and no more but the lordships now standing in them."

Immediately after quoting Harrison, Marx inserts a methodological caveat that is characteristic of his best historical writing: "The complaints of these old chroniclers are always exaggerated, but they faithfully reflect the impression made on contemporaries by the revolution in the relations of production." He is not asking the reader to accept chronicle hyperbole at face value. He is asking the reader to accept that the experience of dispossession was catastrophic enough to shape the entire rhetorical register of contemporary complaint. To reinforce the point he contrasts Fortescue's fifteenth-century panegyric on England's laws with Thomas More's sixteenth-century Utopia — the "gulf between the fifteenth and the sixteenth centuries" — and cites Thornton's epigram that "the English working class was precipitated without any transitional stages from its golden age to its iron age."

5. Statutory Resistance and Its Systematic Failure: The Tudor Legislation

The chapter then turns to the legal reaction — and to its total failure. Marx first situates that failure ideologically. Legislation's impotence was not merely technical; it reflected the fact that the state had not yet reached "that high level of civilization where the 'wealth of the nation' (i.e. the formation of capital and the reckless exploitation and impoverishment of the mass of the people) figures as the ultima Thule of all statecraft." In the Tudor period, the state was still capable of measuring wealth in people rather than in money — and that is precisely why its legislation was doomed.

Bacon's Life of Henry VII supplies the historical narrative. Inclosures "began to be more frequent" after 1489; arable land, "which could not be manured without people and families," was turned into pasture, "which was easily rid by a few herdsmen"; tenancies for years, lives, and at will — "whereupon much of the yeomanry lived" — were "turned into demesnes." The consequence was "a decay of people, and, by consequence, a decay of towns, churches, tithes, and the like." Against this, the "king's wisdom was admirable, and the parliament's at that time," which took course "to take away depopulating inclosures, and depopulating pasturage."

The statutory record is then itemised with precision. Henry VII's Act of 1489 (c. 19) forbade the destruction of "houses of husbandry" possessing twenty acres. 25 Henry VIII (c. 13) renewed it, reciting that "many farms and large flocks of cattle, especially of sheep, are concentrated in the hands of a few men, whereby the rent of land has much risen, and tillage has fallen off, churches and houses have been pulled down, and marvellous numbers of people have been deprived of the means wherewith to maintain themselves and their families." The Act ordained the rebuilding of decayed farmsteads, fixed a proportion between corn land and pasture, and — an index of the sheer scale of the concentration — limited the number of sheep any single owner might keep to 2,000, even while reciting that some owners already possessed 24,000.

The verdict is blunt: "The cries of the people and the legislation directed, for 150 years after Henry VII, against the expropriation of the small farmers and peasants, were both equally fruitless."

6. Bacon's "Device" and the Military-Economic Argument for a Peasantry

Bacon, "without knowing it," reveals the secret of this failure. In his twenty-ninth Essay, Civil and Moral, he describes Henry VII's policy as "profound and admirable, in making farms and houses of husbandry of a standard; that is, maintained with such a proportion of land unto them as may breed a subject to live in convenient plenty and no servile condition, and to keep the plough in the hands of the owners and not mere hirelings." Marx immediately juxtaposes Bacon's ideal against capital's requirement: "What the capitalist system demanded was the reverse of this: a degraded and almost servile condition of the mass of the people, their transformation into mercenaries, and the transformation of their means of labour into capital."

The accompanying footnote elaborates Bacon's reasoning, and it is analytically important because it shows that even a seventeenth-century statesman understood the military — and therefore political — significance of peasant property. Bacon argues that a free, well-to-do peasantry "did in effect amortise a great part of the lands of the kingdom unto the hold and occupation of the yeomanry or middle people," and that "the principal strength of an army consisteth in the infantry or foot." Good infantry "requireth men bred, not in a servile or indigent fashion, but in some free and plentiful manner." Where a state "run most to noblemen and gentlemen," and the husbandmen are mere workfolk, labourers, or cottagers "which are but hous'd beggars," the result is "a good cavalry, but never good stable bands of foot." France and Italy, he observes, are forced to hire Swiss mercenaries. The footnote thus supplies an implicit counter-argument to the enclosure movement from within the strategic logic of the state — and demonstrates that the case against expropriation was not merely humanitarian. It was also, in its own terms, military. Marx does not resolve this tension; he simply notes that the capitalist system demanded the opposite, and that capital won.

7. The Four-Acre Cottage: A Dying Guarantee

A short but revealing section traces legislation's last-ditch attempt to preserve the material basis of the agricultural labourer's independence: the four acres attached to the cottage. Parliament "strove to retain the four acres of land by the cottage of the agricultural wage-labourer, and forbade him to take lodgers into his cottage." The cases are almost comic in their specificity. In 1627, under Charles I, Roger Crocker of Fontmill was condemned for having built a cottage on the manor of Fontmill without four acres of land attached "in perpetuity." In 1638, in the same reign, a royal commission was appointed to enforce the old laws, "especially the law referring to the four acres of land." Even Cromwell forbade the building of a house within four miles of London unless it was endowed with four acres. As late as the first half of the eighteenth century, complaints were still being made if the cottage of the agricultural labourer did not possess "an adjunct of one or two acres." The trajectory from four acres to one or two, and from one or two to "a small garden" or "a few roods of land at a great distance from his cottage," measures the collapse of peasant-independence in miniature.

The section closes with a quotation that is arguably the single most important sentence in the chapter's economic argument. Dr Hunter: "Landlords and farmers work here hand in hand. A few acres to the cottage would make the labourers too independent." Here the logic of capital is laid bare without ornament. The expropriation of the last acre was not incidental to the creation of the wage-labourer. It was the mechanism. A labourer with four acres, a cow, and common rights was not fully dependent on wages. To make him a reliable wage-earner, he had to be stripped of the material means of partial self-sufficiency. Marx's accompanying footnote (George Roberts) notes the shift in perception: the old allocation "would now be judged too great for labourers, and rather as likely to convert them into small farmers." The same piece of land that was once the guarantee of independence had become, in the ideology of the new order, an obstacle to be removed.

8. The Reformation as a Second Great Impulse to Expropriation

The process received, in the sixteenth century, "a new and terrible impulse" from the Reformation and the "consequent colossal spoliation of church property." The Catholic Church was, at the time of the Reformation, "the feudal proprietor of a great part of the soil of England." The dissolution of the monasteries "hurled their inmates into the proletariat." The estates of the church were "to a large extent given away to rapacious royal favourites, or sold at a nominal price to speculating farmers and townsmen, who drove out the old-established hereditary sub-tenants in great numbers, and threw their holdings together." The legally guaranteed property of the poorer folk in a part of the church's tithes was "quietly confiscated."

Marx then frames the strategic significance of this spoliation with a single sentence that ties the section back to his general theory of property: "The property of the church formed the religious bulwark of the old conditions of landed property. With its fall, these conditions could no longer maintain their existence." The point is not merely that church lands changed hands. It is that the church, as a landlord with a different logic than the commercial nobility — one invested in the stability of the older tenurial arrangements — had functioned as a systemic obstacle to the generalisation of private property. Remove it, and the entire architecture of peasant tenure was exposed to the market.

9. The Poor Law and the Criminalization of the Dispossessed

The Reformation coincides with the first official recognition of pauperism. Queen Elizabeth, after a journey through England, cries out the Ovidian tag "Pauper ubique jacet" — "the poor man is everywhere in subjection." In the forty-third year of her reign, it "finally proved necessary to recognize pauperism officially by the introduction of the poor-rate." Marx quotes Cobbett's observation that the authors of this law "seem to have been ashamed to state the grounds of it," since, contrary to traditional usage, "it has no preamble whatever." The poor-rate was declared perpetual by 16 Charles I, c. 4, and "in fact only in 1834 did it take a new and severer form."

Two substantive footnotes extend this discussion in directions that matter. Footnote 9 reproduces the extraordinary "nine questions" submitted by landed proprietors and wealthy farmers to Sergeant Snigge, later a judge under James I, asking whether the Poor Law would permit them to erect a prison in the parish and then farm out the poor to the lowest sealed bidder — a proposal whose logic is the transformation of relief into a punitive labour-contract. It also cites Fletcher of Saltoun's 1698 declaration in the Scottish Parliament that "the number of beggars in Scotland is reckoned at not less than 200,000," and that "the only remedy that I, a republican on principle, can suggest, is to restore the old state of serfdom, to make slaves of all those who are unable to provide for their own subsistence." Meanwhile, Eden — whose State of the Poor Marx will later pillory as "crafty special pleading" — writes that "the decrease of villeinage seems necessarily to have been the era of the origin of the poor. Manufactures and commerce are the two parents of our national poor." Marx's response is surgical: Eden is "only wrong on this point: not the abolition of villeinage, but the abolition of the property of the agricultural labourer in the soil made him a proletarian, and eventually a pauper."

This is one of the chapter's most important conceptual distinctions. The conventional bourgeois account attributes poverty to the emancipation of the serf — i.e., to freedom. Marx inverts the causality. Freedom from personal dependence was not the cause of pauperism; the loss of property in the soil was. The serf, though personally unfree, had secure access to land. The "free" wage-labourer had neither land nor security. In this inversion, the entire ideological narrative of progress is exposed. France, Marx notes in the same footnote, followed a different path: the Ordinance of Moulins (1571) and the Edict of 1656 correspond to the English Poor Laws.

Footnote 10 adds a telling detail: Mr Rogers, "although he was at the time Professor of Political Economy in the University of Oxford, the very centre of Protestant orthodoxy," emphasized the pauperization of the mass of the people by the Reformation in his preface to the History of Agriculture. The ideological bind is again drawn tight: even the institutional voice of the orthodox economics of the day could not entirely suppress the fact.

10. The Disappearance of the Yeomanry

A brief but pivotal section summarises the social result of these four centuries of pressure. "Even in the last few decades of the seventeenth century, the yeomanry, the class of independent peasants, were more numerous than the class of farmers." They had "formed the backbone of Cromwell's strength," and even Macaulay "admits" that they "stood in favourable contrast to the drunken squires and their servants, the country clergy, who had to marry their masters' cast-off mistresses." Macaulay's concession functions here as more than colour. It establishes that the independent peasantry was a morally and militarily significant social stratum, not a marginal curiosity. Then the chronology tightens: "By about 1750 the yeomanry had disappeared, and so, by the last decade of the eighteenth century, had the last trace of the common land of the agricultural labourer."

Marx then states his method explicitly: "We leave on one side here the purely economic driving forces behind the agricultural revolution. We deal only with the violent means employed." This is a crucial disclaimer. Marx is not claiming that the agricultural revolution — new crops, new techniques, higher yields — was insignificant. He is bracketing those forces in order to isolate and expose a different variable: coercion. The chapter is a study of the violent means through which a class was severed from its means of production.

11. The Restoration Settlement: Legal Usurpation and the Laws of Settlement

After the Restoration of the Stuarts, "the landed proprietors carried out, by legal means, an act of usurpation which was effected everywhere on the Continent without any legal formality." Three operations are distinguished: they abolished the feudal tenure of land (ridding it of all obligations to the state); they "indemnified" the state by imposing taxes on the peasantry and the rest of the people; and they established for themselves "the rights of modern private property in estates to which they had only a feudal title." Finally, they "passed those laws of settlement which had the same effect on the English agricultural labourer, mutatis mutandis, as the edict of the Tartar Boris Godunov had on the Russian peasantry." The footnote provides the operative content of that comparison: the Edict of 1597, by which peasants who had fled from their lords could be pursued for five years and forcibly returned when caught. The laws of settlement played the same functional role in England: they bound the newly-created proletariat to the parish, preventing free movement and thereby ensuring a captive supply of labour.

The significance is conceptual as much as historical. The state, in this phase, does not merely tolerate expropriation. It completes it by criminalising mobility. The labourer who has lost his land is not allowed to leave until the new regime has fully absorbed him into the wage relation.

12. The "Glorious Revolution" and the Theft of State Lands

The "Glorious Revolution" brought into power "along with William of Orange, the landed and capitalist profit-grubbers." They "inaugurated the new era by practising on a colossal scale the thefts of state lands which had hitherto been managed more modestly." Estates were "given away, sold at ridiculous prices, or even annexed to private estates by direct seizure," and all this "without the slightest observance of legal etiquette." The Crown lands thus fraudulently appropriated, "together with the stolen Church estates, in so far as these were not lost again during the republican revolution, form the basis of the present princely domains of the English oligarchy."

A series of footnotes supplies the evidentiary scaffolding — and shows Marx's reliance on hostile or establishment sources. Footnote 12 quotes from the Sloane Manuscript (No. 4224) on William's private morality, noting that the large Irish grant to Lady Orkney in 1695 was "a public instance of the king's affection, and the lady's influence," and that her "endearing offices" were described as foeda labiorum ministeria — "base services performed with the lips." Footnote 13 cites F. W. Newman's judgment that the illegal alienation of the Crown Estates, "partly by sale and partly by gift, is a scandalous chapter in English history… a gigantic fraud on the nation." The Engels addition to the fourth German edition points readers to Our Old Nobility, by "Noblesse Oblige," for details on how the present large landed proprietors came into their possessions. Footnote 14 directs the reader to Burke's 1796 pamphlet against the ducal house of Bedford — the family of Lord John Russell, "the 'tomtit of liberalism.'"

The argument then ascends to the level of class strategy. "The bourgeois capitalists favoured the operation, with the intention, among other things, of converting the land into a merely commercial commodity, extending the area of large-scale agricultural production, and increasing the supply of free and rightless proletarians driven from their land." The "new landed aristocracy was the natural ally of the new bankocracy, of newly hatched high finance, and of the large manufacturers, at that time dependent on protective duties." The chapter's comparative instinct then provides a decisive contrast: "The English bourgeoisie acted quite as wisely in its own interest as the Swedish burghers, who did the opposite: hand in hand with the bulwark of their economic strength, the peasantry, they helped the kings in their forcible resumption of crown lands from the oligarchy, in the years after 1604 and later on under Charles X and Charles XI." The point is that expropriation is not an iron law of "development." It is a choice — made by identifiable class coalitions under identifiable historical conditions — and Sweden made a different choice.

13. Communal Property and the Parliamentary Coup d'État of Enclosure

The chapter now isolates a distinct object: communal property. Marx is emphatic that it is "entirely distinct from the state property we have just been considering." It was "an old Teutonic institution which lived on under the cover of feudalism." Its forcible usurpation, "generally accompanied by the turning of arable into pasture land," begins at the end of the fifteenth century and extends through the sixteenth. But here a decisive qualitative shift occurs: at first the process was "carried on by means of individual acts of violence against which legislation, for a hundred and fifty years, fought in vain." Then, "the advance made by the eighteenth century shows itself in this, that the law itself now becomes the instrument by which the people's land is stolen, although the big farmers made use of their little independent methods as well." The Parliamentary form is that of "Bills for Inclosure of Commons" — "in other words, decrees by which the landowners grant themselves the people's land as private property, decrees of expropriation of the people."

Here, Sir F. M. Eden is unmasked. He tries to represent communal property as the private property of the great landlords who had taken the place of the feudal lords; but "he himself demands a 'general Act of Parliament for the enclosure of Commons' (thereby admitting that a parliamentary coup d'état is necessary for their transformation into private property), and moreover calls on the legislature to indemnify the expropriated poor." Eden's own argument thus contains its refutation: if communal property were already the private property of the landlords, no coup d'état would be needed. The demand for legislation is the admission of usurpation.

14. "Capital Farms" and the "Setting Free" of the Proletariat

The social consequence is again summarised: the place of the independent yeoman was taken by tenants at will, small farmers on yearly leases, "a servile rabble dependent on the arbitrary will of the landlords." Meanwhile, the "systematic theft of communal property was of great assistance, alongside the theft of the state domains, in swelling those large farms which were called in the eighteenth century capital farms, or merchant farms, and in 'setting free' the agricultural population as a proletariat for the needs of industry."

The terminology matters. "Capital farms" (from Two Letters on the Flour Trade, 1767) and "merchant farms" (from Forster's An Enquiry into the Causes of the Present High Price of Provisions, 1767) show that contemporaries recognised what was happening. They saw that the enclosure movement was not primarily an efficiency reform but the consolidation of small holdings into units organised around capital. And the scare quotes around "setting free" carry the whole ironic argument of the chapter. What is called "freeing" the population was, in fact, the severing of a population from the means of subsistence. The word "free" in "free wage-labourer" is a euphemism for "freed from everything but the necessity to sell one's labour-power."

15. The Eighteenth-Century Polemic: Price, Addington, Wright

Marx acknowledges that "the eighteenth century did not yet recognize as fully as the nineteenth the identity between the wealth of the nation and the poverty of the people. Hence the very vigorous polemic, in the economic literature of that time, on the 'enclosure of commons'." From "the mass of material that lies before me," he selects extracts "for the strong light they throw on the circumstances of the time."

  • Thomas Wright (1779): "In several parishes of Hertfordshire, twenty-four farms, numbering on the average 50 to 150 acres, have been melted up into three farms."
  • Rev. Addington (1772): In Northamptonshire and Leicestershire, the enclosure of common lands took place on a very large scale, and most of the new lordships were turned into pasturage, "in consequence of which many lordships have not now 50 acres ploughed yearly, in which 1,500 were ploughed formerly." The ruins of former dwelling-houses, barns, and stables are the sole traces of the former inhabitants. "An hundred houses and families have in some open field villages… dwindled to eight or ten." The landholders in most parishes that have been enclosed for fifteen or twenty years are "very few in comparison of the numbers who occupied them in their open-field state." "It is no uncommon thing for four or five wealthy graziers to engross a large enclosed lordship which was before in the hands of twenty or thirty farmers, and as many smaller tenants and proprietors."
  • Dr Price: He notes that it was "not only land that lay waste, but often also land that was still under cultivation" that was annexed under pretext of enclosure. "Even the enclosure of waste lands (as now carried on) bears hard on the poor, by depriving them of a part of their subsistence, and only goes towards increasing farms already too large." His central prediction is the mechanism of proletarianisation stated in its clearest form: "When this land gets into the hands of a few great farmers, the consequence must be that the little farmers… will be converted into a body of men who earn their subsistence by working for others, and who will be under a necessity of going to market for all they want… There will, perhaps, be more labour, because there will be more compulsion to it… Towns and manufactures will increase, because more will be driven to them in quest of places and employment." And his summary: "Upon the whole, the circumstances of the lower ranks of men are altered in almost every respect for the worse. From little occupiers of land, they are reduced to the state of day-labourers and hirelings; and, at the same time, their subsistence in that state has become more difficult."

The Poverty was not slow in coming. Marx notes, "even according to Eden," wages fell below the minimum between 1765 and 1780 and had to be supplemented by official Poor Law relief. Their wages, Eden says, "were not more than enough for the absolute necessaries of life."

Footnote 23 supplies the classical parallel: Appian on ancient Rome, where the wealthy bought poor neighbours' land with their acquiescence and seized the rest by force, cultivating wide domains with slaves while the free population declined through poverty, taxation, and military service. The Licinian Law (367 B.C.) — limiting any holder to 500 jugera of public land, 100 cattle, 500 sheep — was the Roman analogue of Henry VII's statutes: an attempt to check concentration that ultimately failed. Marx also notes that Charlemagne's reliance on military service functioned "as in a forcing-house" to convert free German peasants into serfs and bondsmen. The implication: the English story is not unique. It is a variant of a recurrent pattern in class societies.

16. The Apologists: Arbuthnot, Seeley, and the Arithmetic of Improvement

Against these critics, Marx sets the defenders of enclosure. The principal apologist — [J. Arbuthnot], An Inquiry into the Connection between the Present Price of Provisions, etc. — argues: "Nor is it a consequence that there must be depopulation, because men are not seen wasting their labour in the open field." If converting the little farmers into a body of men who must work for others produces more labour, "it is an advantage which the nation… should wish for." The nation here is, of course, that "to which the people who have been 'converted' do not belong." The produce being greater when their joint labours are employed on one farm, "there will be a surplus for manufactures, and by this means manufactures, one of the mines of the nation, will increase, in proportion to the quantity of corn produced." Footnote 24 pairs this with a similar argument from a different moral register: Seeley's The Perils of the Nation (1843): "Working-men are driven from their cottages and forced into the towns to seek for employment; but then a larger surplus is obtained, and thus capital is augmented."

The importance of this pairing is that it exposes the underlying identity of two otherwise opposed positions. The "apologist" and the "philanthropist" arrive at the same conclusion from opposite moral registers. Both accept that the dispossession of the peasantry is a precondition of national wealth, and both treat the resulting human cost as either irrelevant or providential. Marx's point is not that one is hypocritical and the other honest. It is that the entire economic discourse of the period — including its "reforming" and "philanthropic" wings — was structured around a conception of the nation that excluded the dispossessed from membership.

17. Eden's Stoicism and the Arable-Pasture Ledger

Sir F. M. Eden, "Tory and 'philanthropic' in his political colouring," provides the chapter's most chilling specimen of the economist's mentality. Having surveyed "the whole series of thefts, outrages and popular misery" that accompanied the expropriation from the last third of the fifteenth century to the end of the eighteenth, Eden arrives at this "comfortable" reflection:

"The due proportion between arable land and pasture had to be established. During the whole of the fourteenth and the greater part of the fifteenth century, there was 1 acre of pasture to 2, 3, and even 4 of arable land. About the middle of the sixteenth century the proportion was changed to 2 acres of pasture to 2, later on, to 2 acres of pasture to 1 of arable, until at last the just proportion of 3 acres of pasture to 1 of arable land was attained."

"The stoical peace of mind with which the political economist regards the most shameless violation of the 'sacred rights of property' and the grossest acts of violence against persons, as soon as they are necessary in order to lay the foundations of the capitalist mode of production" is here put on display in Eden's own words. Human families, villages, entire regional economies are subsumed under a ratio of pasture to arable. The "just proportion" of three to one is the mathematical form of depopulation. Marx's irony — the word "comfortable" — is the chapter's most concentrated act of critique: it does not deny Eden's data; it merely juxtaposes his data against his tone.

18. The Nineteenth-Century Balance Sheet: 3,511,770 Acres

"By the nineteenth century, the very memory of the connection between the agricultural labourer and communal property had, of course, vanished." To make the loss quantitative, Marx asks a single, devastating rhetorical question: "To say nothing of more recent times — have the agricultural population received a farthing's compensation for the 3,511,770 acres of common land which between 1801 and 1831 were stolen from them and presented to the landlords by the landlords, through the agency of Parliament?" This figure is the chapter's numerical summation. It converts the four-century narrative into an auditable quantity. The phrase "presented to the landlords by the landlords, through the agency of Parliament" is an almost complete theory of the state in one clause: the legislature is described as the instrument by which a class grants itself what it has already seized in fact.

19. The Highland Clearances: Clan Property as the Peculiar Form

The final great process is "the so-called 'clearing of estates', i.e. the sweeping of human beings off them." Marx introduces it by noting that all the English methods "culminated in 'clearing'." As established in an earlier chapter, once no more independent peasants remain to be expelled, the clearing of cottages begins — the agricultural labourer no longer finds on the soil even the necessary space for his own housing. But the full meaning of the practice is only visible in the Scottish Highlands, "the promised land of modern romantic novels."

The Highland case is distinguished by three features: (1) its systematic character; (2) the magnitude of the scale on which it was carried out at one blow — in Ireland landlords had gone as far as sweeping away several villages at once, but in the Highlands "areas as large as German principalities are dealt with"; and (3) "the peculiar form of property under which the embezzled lands were held."

That peculiar form is the clan. "The Highland Celts were organized in clans, each of which was the owner of the land on which it was settled." The chief or "great man" was "only the titular owner of this property, just as the Queen of England is the titular owner of all the national soil." When the English government suppressed the intestine wars of these great men and their incursions into the Lowlands, "the chiefs of the clans by no means gave up their time-honoured trade as robbers; they merely changed its form." On their own authority, they "transformed their nominal right to the land into a right of private property," and when this came up against resistance from their clansmen, "they resolved to drive them out openly and by force." Professor Newman's line — "A king of England might as well claim to drive his subjects into the sea" — measures the enormity of the act. The revolution began in Scotland after the last rising of the followers of the Pretender (1745–6) and can be traced in the writings of Sir James Steuart and James Anderson. Steuart, in a footnote, observes that if you compare the rent of Highland lands with the numbers fed upon the farm, "you will find that an estate in the Highlands maintains, perhaps, ten times as many people as another of the same value in a good and fertile province."

In the eighteenth century, the Gaels were "both driven from the land and forbidden to emigrate, with a view to driving them forcibly to Glasgow and other manufacturing towns." Footnote 28 records that in 1860 some of the people expropriated by force were "exported to Canada under false pretences," while others fled to the mountains and neighbouring islands, "were followed by the police, came to blows with them and escaped." Two forms of coercion here combine: expulsion from the land, and prohibition of departure. The expropriated population is not permitted to disperse; it is compelled to become a labour reservoir for industrial Glasgow.

20. The Duchess of Sutherland and the Architecture of Eviction

The chapter's most famous illustrative passage concerns the Duchess of Sutherland, "well instructed in economics," who "resolved, when she succeeded to the headship of the clan, to undertake a radical economic cure, and to turn the whole county of Sutherland, the population of which had already been reduced to 15,000 by similar processes, into a sheep-walk." Between 1814 and 1820, these 15,000 inhabitants — about 3,000 families — were "systematically hunted and rooted out." All their villages were destroyed and burnt, all their fields turned into pasturage. British soldiers enforced the mass of evictions and came to blows with the inhabitants. One old woman was burnt to death in the flames of the hut she refused to leave.

The arithmetic is then laid out. The Duchess appropriated 794,000 acres of land that had belonged to the clan "from time immemorial." She assigned the expelled inhabitants 6,000 acres on the sea-shore — two acres per family. The 6,000 acres had "until this time lain waste, and brought in no income to their owners." Then the detail that completes the indictment: "The Duchess, in the nobility of her heart, actually went so far as to let these waste lands at an average rent of 2s. 6d. per acre to the clansmen, who for centuries had shed their blood for her family." She divided the stolen land into twenty-nine huge sheep farms, each inhabited by a single family, "for the most part imported English farm-servants." By 1825, the 15,000 Gaels had been replaced by 131,000 sheep.

The remnant, flung onto the sea-shore, "tried to live by catching fish. They became amphibious, and lived, as an English writer says, half on land and half on water, and withal only half on both." Footnote 30 records the exchange with Mrs Beecher Stowe, author of Uncle Tom's Cabin, whom the Duchess entertained with great magnificence in London to show her sympathy for the slaves of the American republic — "a sympathy she prudently forgot, along with her fellow-aristocrats, during the Civil War, when every 'noble' English heart beat for the slave-owners." Marx notes that he published the facts about the Sutherland slaves in the New York Tribune, that the article was reprinted in a Scottish newspaper, and that it "called forth a nice polemic between that newspaper and the sycophants of the Sutherlands." The Sutherland case is thus made to carry a broader comparative argument: the rhetoric of antislavery in one hemisphere coexisted, in the same class, with indifference or active enthusiasm for a form of domestic expropriation that was, in Marx's account, structurally parallel.

21. From Sheep to Sea-Shore: The Second and Third Expulsions

The narrative then moves through two further waves of dispossession, each driven by a more profitable use of the same land. First, the fish. "The smell of their fish rose to the noses of the great men. They scented some profit in it, and let the sea-shore to the big London fishmongers. For the second time the Gaels were driven out." Footnote 31 notes that details on this fish trade appear in David Urquhart's Portfolio, and that Nassau W. Senior described the Sutherland proceedings as "one of the most beneficent clearings since the memory of man" — a phrase whose perverse charity Marx lets stand without comment, as it needs none.

Second, the deer. "Finally, however, part of the sheep-walks were turned into deer preserves." Marx's aside on this is characteristically sardonic: "Everyone knows that there are no true forests in England. The deer in the parks of the great are demure domestic cattle, as fat as London aldermen. Scotland is therefore the last refuge of the 'noble passion'." He then quotes Robert Somers (1848): "In the Highlands new forests are springing up like mushrooms." Glenfeshie, Ardverikie, the Black Mount, Loch Archaig, Glengarry, Glenmoriston — a "continuous line of forests." Sheep were introduced into glens that had been the seats of communities of small farmers; the farmers were driven to "coarser and more sterile tracts of soil." Now deer are supplanting sheep; "and these are once more dispossessing the small tenants, who will necessarily be driven down upon still coarser land and to more grinding penury." Somers's conclusion: "Deer-forests and the people cannot co-exist. One or other of the two must yield."

The reason is economic, not sentimental: "a mountain range laid out in forest is, in many cases, more profitable to the proprietor than when let as a sheep-walk." And the moral register of Somers's report rises: "Sufferings have been inflicted in the Highlands scarcely less severe than those occasioned by the policy of the Norman kings. Deer have received extended ranges, while men have been hunted within a narrower and still narrower circle… One after one the liberties of the people have been cloven down… The clearance and dispersion of the people is pursued by the proprietors as a settled principle, as an agricultural necessity, just as trees and brushwood are cleared from the wastes of America or Australia; and the operation goes on in a quiet, business-like way."

Footnote 32 is a sharp terminological incision: "The deer-forests of Scotland do not contain a single tree. The sheep are driven from, and then the deer driven to, the naked hills, and this is then called a deer-forest." The euphemism "forest" is exposed as a linguistic mask for depopulated waste. Footnote 33 expands the frame. It records that "the English economists of course explained the famine of the Gaels in 1847 by referring to — over-population," a doctrine of which Marx is transparently contemptuous: "At all events, they were 'pressing' on their food supply." It then notes the German analogue, Bauernlegen ("peasant-throwing-off"), which took hold in Germany especially after the Thirty Years' War and as late as 1790 led to peasant revolts in Electoral Saxony, and was particularly prevalent in eastern Germany. Frederick II secured property rights for the peasants in most Prussian provinces after the conquest of Silesia, forced landowners to rebuild huts and provide cattle and implements — "He wanted soldiers for his army, and taxpayers for his treasury." Mirabeau's description of the North German peasant is then quoted at length: the peasant crushed by direct taxes, forced labour, obligations, indirect taxes, and restrictions on selling his produce, spinning flax at his wheel as "a resource against misery and not a means towards well-being," sleeping from midnight to five or six in winter, ageing faster in the country than in the town. The Fredrician "reform," in other words, was not philanthropy but the fiscal-military logic of a state that needed bodies.

The footnote also cites Professor Leone Levi's 1866 lecture before the Society of Arts on the transformation of sheep-walks into deer-forests, depicting "the further progress in the devastation of the Scottish Highlands." Levi notes that "depopulation and transformation into sheep-walks were the most convenient means for getting an income without expenditure." One could walk from the Earl of Dalhousie's estates in Forfarshire to John o' Groats "without ever leaving forest land." Immense tracts described in the statistical account as having pasturage "of very superior description" were "shut out from all cultivation and improvement, and… solely devoted to the sport of a few persons for a very brief period of the year." The London Economist of 2 June 1866 is then quoted on a sheep farm in Sutherlandshire yielding £1,200 a year in rent, which was to be converted into a deer-forest on expiry of the lease — "Here we see the modern instincts of feudalism… operating pretty much as they did when the Norman Conqueror… destroyed thirty-six villages to create the New Forest." Two million acres "totally laid waste"; the natural grass of Glen Tilt among the most nutritive in Perthshire; Ben Aulder the best grazing ground in Badenoch; a part of the Black Mount forest "the best pasture for black-faced sheep in Scotland." The ground of Ben Aulder alone would pasture 15,000 sheep, and it was "not more than one-thirtieth part of the whole forest ground in Scotland." The land "might just as well have been submerged under the waters of the North Sea." The passage closes with the Economist's appeal: "Such extemporized wildernesses or deserts ought to be put down by the decided interference of the Legislature."

22. The Recapitulation: "Idyllic Methods of Primitive Accumulation"

The chapter's final paragraph is a controlled, almost liturgical summation. "The spoliation of the Church's property, the fraudulent alienation of the state domains, the theft of the common lands, the usurpation of feudal and clan property and its transformation into modern private property under circumstances of ruthless terrorism, all these things were just so many idyllic methods of primitive accumulation." The word "idyllic" is the master-irony of the chapter: it is the term bourgeois political economy uses to characterise the pre-history of capital, and Marx appropriates it to describe what he has just documented — burnt villages, evicted families, mass dispossession, state-sponsored theft. The paragraph then states the outcome in the language of capital itself: "They conquered the field for capitalist agriculture, incorporated the soil into capital, and created for the urban industries the necessary supplies of free and rightless proletarians." Three achievements, each indispensable to the capitalist mode of production: the land is converted into an input of capital; the producer is separated from it; and a labour supply is manufactured to work it.

23. Some Critical Nuances and Counter-Arguments

Several layers of nuance deserve explicit notice, because they shape how the chapter should be read.

First, Marx's own concession that the chroniclers' complaints are "always exaggerated" means that the chapter is not to be read as a literal statistical record of every eviction. The exaggeration is admitted; what is claimed is that the complaints "faithfully reflect the impression made on contemporaries by the revolution in the relations of production." The evidentiary standard is therefore impressionistic where it must be, and quantitative where it can be (the 3,511,770 acres; the 160,000 proprietors; the 15,000 Gaels and 131,000 sheep; the parkland acreages). Marx is careful to mix the two registers.

Second, Marx's own methodological bracketing — "We leave on one side here the purely economic driving forces behind the agricultural revolution. We deal only with the violent means employed" — is a deliberate self-limitation. It acknowledges that there were economic forces at work, and that the enclosure movement also responded to changes in prices, techniques, and markets. The chapter's argument is not that violence replaced economics but that violence was the form through which economic transformation was accomplished. This is a subtler claim than simple expropriation theory, and it protects Marx from the accusation that his account is monocausal.

Third, the comparative material, especially the Swedish case, functions as a standing refutation of any claim that expropriation was historically inevitable. The English bourgeoisie and the Swedish burghers faced comparable circumstances and made opposite choices: the English allied with a landed aristocracy that was expelling peasants; the Swedes allied with their peasantry and helped the crown reclaim lands from the oligarchy. Marx's point is not that the Swedish route was morally superior in the abstract but that the English route was chosen, not fated.

Fourth, the treatment of Eden and the apologists is itself a form of argument. Marx does not rebut their economics as such. He exposes their tone. He shows that the same processes labelled "depopulating inclosures" by Tudor legislation were labelled "improvement" by eighteenth-century agronomy, and "clearing of estates" by nineteenth-century estate management. The euphemism is part of the mechanism. To call a burn-out "clearing," or a depopulated waste a "deer-forest," or a compulsory dispossession a "necessary agricultural adjustment," is not merely rhetorical — it participates in the legitimation of the violence.

Fifth, the chapter acknowledges that the phenomena are European, not merely English. It notes counterparts in France (the Ordinance of Moulins, the Edict of 1656, the Continental pattern of legal usurpation without legal formality), in Germany (Bauernlegen, Frederick II's counter-measures, Silesia), in Russia (Boris Godunov's edict and the Edict of 1597), in Scotland (the clan-based clearances), in Ireland (village-scale clearances), in ancient Rome (Appian, the Licinian Law), and in Japan (the persistence of feudal landed property). This comparative reach is important, because it positions English expropriation not as a peculiarity but as the classic case of a general process. England was the model because it completed the process most thoroughly — and because its completion provided the template that other capitalist economies would later approximate.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The fundamental principle this chapter establishes is that the capital relation has a historical and violent genesis, and that this genesis is inseparable from the formation of the modern state. Capital cannot be understood as a self-generating fact of nature. It presupposes the separation of the producer from the means of production, and that separation had to be produced. The chapter's narrative — stretching across some four centuries — shows the successive instruments through which this separation was manufactured: the dissolution of feudal households, the sheep-walk conversion, the spoliation of church property, the theft of crown lands, the parliamentary enclosure of the commons, and finally the clearing of estates. Each of these instruments built on the previous one, and each was carried through by a specific class coalition — the new landed aristocracy, the bankocracy, high finance, the protectionist manufacturers.

Several systemic laws follow. First, property at the origin of capitalism is not the product of peaceful accumulation but of violence, fraud, and legal fiat. The "sacred rights of property" that liberal discourse presents as the foundation of order were themselves created by the systematic violation of precisely those rights — a point made with savage clarity by Marx's use of Eden's "just proportion of 3 acres of pasture to 1 of arable." Second, the state is revealed as an active participant in expropriation, not an impartial arbiter. The shift from "individual acts of violence" to "Bills for Inclosure of Commons" is not a moral progress; it is the enlargement of the state's role in making private property out of communal usage. The 3,511,770 acres "presented to the landlords by the landlords, through the agency of Parliament" is the chapter's canonical illustration. Third, the same process that produced capital also produced the modern proletariat. The two cannot be separated; they are the two sides of a single operation. To "free" land into capital is to "free" people into wage-labour. Marx's phrase "free and rightless proletarians" captures this exactly: they are free in the double sense of being released from personal dependence and being released from any property in the means of subsistence. Fourth, expropriation is comparative and contested, not inevitable. Sweden chose a different path; Frederick II, however ruthless his motives, restored peasant property to secure recruits and taxpayers. The English outcome was a victory in a class struggle, not a stage in an unfolding teleology. Fifth, ideology is part of the apparatus of expropriation. The vocabulary shifts — "depopulating enclosures" → "improvement" → "clearing" → "agricultural necessity" — and each shift is a small act of legitimation. The economist's "stoical peace of mind" is not incidental to the process; it is the intellectual form in which the process becomes acceptable.

The broader implications run in several directions. For economic theory, the chapter dissolves the myth of "primitive accumulation" as a peaceful prior accumulation of savings and virtue. Marx's demonstration that the "original accumulation" was nothing but an extended campaign of dispossession undermines the foundational apologies of liberal political economy — and, by extension, any theory that treats current property distributions as legitimate because they are current. For historical method, the chapter illustrates Marx's capacity to argue through the testimony of his opponents: Macaulay, Bacon, Eden, the Economist, Nassau Senior, Sergeant Snigge. He does not need sympathetic witnesses; he assembles a hostile archive and lets it self-incriminate. For political theory, the chapter offers a theory of the state as an instrument of class consolidation: laws of settlement, poor laws, enclosure acts, and military enforcement are all shown to operate as the legal chassis of accumulation. And for moral and philosophical reflection, the chapter raises a question that retains its force in any time or place: what is the status of the person who is "free" in the double sense — free from the land, free from the means of labour, and therefore free to sell the only commodity he still possesses? The Marx of Chapter 27 answers darkly. What liberal doctrine calls freedom, the chapter suggests, is often the historical name for a completed dispossession.

What remains is the image with which the chapter effectively closes: the clan remnant "flung onto the sea-shore," becoming "amphibious," living "half on land and half on water, and withal only half on both." It is the image of a population that has lost the ground under its feet and has not yet been given the factory floor to replace it — the transitional body that is neither peasant nor proletarian, suspended between the world that capital destroyed and the world that capital is building. Chapter 27 makes the reader watch as that body is created — and in watching, see that capital's own prehistory is neither idyllic nor accidental. It was made. And it was made by force.
