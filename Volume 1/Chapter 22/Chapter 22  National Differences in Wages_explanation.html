Volume 1 • Chapter 22: National Differences in Wages

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Core Thesis. Chapter 22 of Capital, Volume I, is a short but extraordinarily dense hinge-chapter that performs a conceptual translation. Its central proposition is that the very laws which Chapter 17 established concerning the value of labour-power — the manifold combinations that raise or lower that value, whether measured absolutely or relatively against surplus-value — do not vanish when we step from one country to another. Instead, they reappear "as a set of simultaneous differences in national wage-levels." What is a temporal succession of varying combinations inside a single nation becomes a spatial array of coexisting national wage structures. Marx's task is therefore to specify precisely what an honest international comparison of wages would require, why the naive comparison (comparing money wages directly) is fraudulent, and what the true relationship is between the poverty or wealth of a nation's workers and the apparent height of their wages.

The chapter's decisive conclusion, stated early and defended empirically and polemically thereafter, is a double one. First, nominal wages are typically higher in the more developed capitalist nation than in the less developed one, but this "by no means proves that the same can be said of real wages." Second — and more explosively — the relative price of labour (the price of labour measured against surplus-value and against the value of the product) frequently stands higher in the poorer, less-developed nation than in the richer one. In other words, labour can be dear to the capitalist precisely where the worker is paid least, and cheap to the capitalist where the worker's money wage is highest. This is the theoretical bombshell the chapter exists to deliver.

The Central Problem and Intellectual Objective. The problem Marx confronts is that the category "wages" is, as he established in the immediately preceding chapters, an exoteric form — an outward appearance that conceals the underlying value-relation of labour-power. Because the wage-form hides the value of labour-power, comparing wages across nations on the basis of their surface appearance is guaranteed to mislead. The chapter's objective is therefore twofold:

  1. Methodological: to establish the conditions under which a valid international comparison is even possible — specifically, the reduction of average daily wages in the same trades to a uniform working day, followed by the translation of the time-wage into the piece-wage, "as only the latter can be a measure both of the productivity and of the intensity of labour."
  2. Theoretical and polemical: to show how the law of value is modified — not abolished — in its international application, and to demolish the optimistic doctrine of Henry Charles Carey that national wage differences are directly proportional to differences in the productivity of labour, a doctrine Marx exposes as both logically absurd (given his own analysis of surplus-value) and empirically falsified by the evidence of factory inspectors.

Fit within the Broader Framework. The chapter sits at the culmination of Part VI ("Wages") of Capital Volume I. Having analysed in Chapter 17 the transformation of the value (and price) of labour-power into wages, and in Chapters 19–21 the forms of wages (time-wage, piece-wage) and their national peculiarities, Marx now pushes the analysis onto the world market. In doing so he foreshadows the later, more fully developed treatment of the world market and international exchange, and anticipates themes that would become central to theories of unequal exchange and uneven development. Structurally, the chapter functions as a bridge: it connects the micro-logic of the wage-form to the macro-logic of international economic hierarchy, and it simultaneously serves as a platform for ideological combat against the "harmonious wisdom" of the free-trade optimists. It is at once a technical chapter on comparative wage measurement and a political intervention into the ideological self-understanding of capitalist political economy.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Point of Departure — Chapter 17 and the Transformation of Value into Wages

The chapter opens by explicitly recalling its own foundation. In Chapter 17, Marx had been concerned with "the manifold combinations which may bring about a change in the magnitude of the value of labour-power," considering that magnitude "either absolutely or relatively (i.e. as compared with surplus-value)." The phrase "value of labour-power" is the load-bearing concept here and must be held firmly: it is not the same as wages. The value of labour-power is the value of the means of subsistence required to produce, maintain, and reproduce the worker — determined, like all value, by the socially necessary labour-time embodied in those means of subsistence, but conditioned by what Marx elsewhere calls the "natural and historical" elements of a given society's standard of life.

Marx then reminds us of a subtle finding from Chapter 17: the quantity of the means of subsistence in which the price of labour-power is realized "might again undergo fluctuations independent of, or different from, the changes in this price." This is the wedge that opens the entire chapter. The price of labour-power (its money expression) and the mass of use-values that price can command are two distinct variables that can move in different directions. This non-identity is precisely what makes cross-national comparison treacherous.

The pivot of the whole chapter is stated in one sentence: "the simple translation of the value, or respectively of the price, of labour-power, into the exoteric form of wages transforms all these laws into laws governing the movement of wages." The term exoteric form is critical — it means the outward, surface, publicly visible form, as opposed to the inner essential relation. Wages are the phenomenal form in which the value of labour-power presents itself on the market. Because that surface form is what everyone sees, the underlying laws of value "appear" (erscheinen) as laws of wage movement. When we compare nations, this translation effect produces the illusion that we are comparing like with like, when in fact we are comparing differently-constituted surface appearances of different underlying value-relations.

Hence the methodological pivot from time to space: what "appears within the movement of wages as a series of varying combinations may appear for different countries as a set of simultaneous differences in national wage-levels." A single nation runs through a sequence; the international arena displays the same variety as a simultaneous array. This insight reframes the comparative problem entirely: the differences between nations are not exotic anomalies but the same laws playing out under different conditions.

2.2 The Comparative Method — Uniform Working Day and the Primacy of Piece-Wage

From the reformulation of the problem, Marx immediately derives the conditions of any valid comparison. To compare wages in different nations, "we must therefore take into account all the factors that determine changes in the amount of the value of labour-power." He enumerates them with precision:

  • the price and the extent of the prime necessities of life in their natural and historical development — that is, both the cost of the subsistence basket and the historically formed standard of what counts as necessary;
  • the cost of training the workers — the reproduction cost of skilled labour;
  • the part played by the labour of women and children — the composition of the labouring family unit whose combined wage reproduces the working class;
  • the productivity of labour — output per unit of labour-time;
  • its extensive and intensive magnitude — the length of the working day (extensive) and the density/concentration of effort per unit of time (intensive).

Then comes the first technical prescription: "Even the most superficial comparison requires the prior reduction of the average daily wage for the same trades, in different countries, to a uniform working day." This is indispensable because comparing, say, a 10-hour English day with a 14.5-hour Continental day at face value conflates the price per day with the price per hour of labour. Marx had already established in the analysis of the time-wage that the daily wage is the product of the price of labour-power and the length of the working day; a longer day at the same daily wage conceals a lower hourly price. Without normalization, the comparison is meaningless.

But even this reduction is insufficient. After wages are reduced to a common time-basis, "the time-wage must again be translated into the piece-wage, as only the latter can be a measure both of the productivity and of the intensity of labour." This is a profound methodological claim. The piece-wage (payment per unit produced) is, in Marx's analysis, the form of wages that most directly registers both how much is produced (productivity) and how hard labour is expended (intensity). A time-wage hides these differences because it pays for time spent rather than work done; a piece-wage, by contrast, is a direct index of output, and therefore a comparative instrument capable of capturing the very variables — productivity and intensity — that make national wage levels incommensurable on the surface.

2.3 The National Average Intensity of Labour and the World Market

The next beat introduces the pivotal concept of national average intensity of labour. Within any given country, Marx explains, "there is a certain average intensity of labour, below which the labour for the production of a commodity requires more than the time socially necessary, and therefore does not count as labour of normal quality." This is a crucial refinement of the theory of socially necessary labour-time: intensity is not merely a psychological or physiological matter but is socially standardized. In a given country, "only a degree of intensity which is above the national average alters the measurement of value by the mere duration of labour-time." In other words, inside a nation, the average intensity is tacitly treated as the norm, and only deviations above the norm create more value per unit of time.

But "it is otherwise on the world market, whose integral parts are the individual countries." Here the critical move is made: "The average intensity of labour changes from country to country; here it is greater, there less. These national averages form a scale whose unit of measurement is the average unit of universal labour." The concept of universal labour (or abstract, general human labour) supplies the measuring unit for the international scale. This means that the different national intensities are not private facts sealed within borders but are ranked on a single comparative scale established by the world market. The consequence is stated without qualification: "The more intense national labour, therefore, as compared with the less intense, produces in the same time more value, which expresses itself in more money."

Thus the labor-time that is "socially necessary" economically is, at the international level, a weighted magnitude. A day of labour in a high-intensity nation counts for more than a day of labour in a low-intensity nation, because the world market imposes a single metric onto the national averages. This is the first major theoretical platform for the chapter's conclusion: nations with more intense labour create more value per hour and hence would command higher money returns, independently of any difference in workers' real standard of living.

2.4 Productivity as Intensity — The International Modification of the Law of Value

Marx then deepens the argument by folding productivity into the same logic: "the law of value is yet more modified in its international application by the fact that, on the world market, national labour which is more productive also counts as more intensive, as long as the more productive nation is not compelled by competition to lower the selling price of its commodities to the level of their value." This is a subtle and qualified claim. The qualifier — the conditional clause — is essential: the more productive nation's labour counts as more intensive only if competition does not force it to sell at value. If competitive pressure forces the price down to the value of the commodity, the productivity advantage is surrendered to consumers rather than retained as higher value per unit of labour. The conditionality here is Marx's recognition that the world market is a contested terrain, not a mechanically fixed hierarchy.

The argument proceeds with a key proposition: "In proportion as capitalist production is developed in a country, so, in the same proportion, do the national intensity and productivity of labour there rise above the international level." This ties the international scale directly to the degree of capitalist development. Development and intensity/productivity are posited as rising in lockstep. From this it follows that "the different quantities of commodities of the same kind, produced in different countries in the same working time, have, therefore, unequal international values, which are expressed in different prices, i.e. in sums of money varying according to international values."

We can render the implicit relation the text is working with in a compact reconstruction:

  • Let $v_i$ be the international value produced by one unit of working time in nation $i$.
  • Comparing two nations, $A$ (more developed) and $B$ (less developed): $v_A > v_B$, because the labour of $A$ counts as both more intense and more productive.
  • Hence the money expression of one unit of working time is larger in $A$: $M_A > M_B$, where $M$ is the money sum representing that value.

This formalization should be read strictly as a reconstruction of the text's stated logic, not as an equation supplied by Marx; the chapter contains no algebraic apparatus.

The crucial conclusion follows: "The relative value of money will therefore be less in the nation with a more developed capitalist mode of production than in the nation with a less developed capitalism." Because the labour of the developed nation embodies more value per unit of time, the commodities it produces confront money as being of higher value, so a unit of money represents a smaller quantum of value — the relative value of money (its value against commodities) is lower. The mechanism Marx is driving at is that a higher money wage can be nothing but the necessary expression of a lower value of money, not evidence of greater worker welfare. Therefore, "nominal wages, the equivalent of labour-power expressed in money, will also be higher in the first nation than in the second; but this by no means proves that the same can be said of real wages, i.e. the means of subsistence placed at the disposal of the worker."

This is the pivot on which the chapter's critical punch rests. Nominal wages are money wages — the sum of money the worker receives. Real wages are the mass of means of subsistence (use-values) that money commands. The two can diverge systematically across nations because the value of money itself is nation-specific. The reader should note the deliberate caution of Marx's footnote 2, where he defers the fuller investigation: "We shall inquire, in another place, what circumstances in relation to productivity may modify this law for individual branches of production." This is an admission that the argument is stated at the level of national averages and that branch-specific complications remain to be treated.

2.5 The Relative Price of Labour — The Decisive Category

The chapter now introduces and insists upon the category that carries its most important message: the relative price of labour, defined as "the price of labour as compared both with surplus-value and with the value of the product." This is labour's cost to capital, its share in the value produced. Marx states the thesis flatly: "even apart from these relative differences in the value of money in different countries, it will frequently be found that the daily or weekly wage in the first nation is higher than in the second while the relative price of labour ... stands higher in the second than in the first."

In other words, the "cheap labour" nation is, from capital's standpoint, dear, and the "expensive labour" nation is cheap. This seeming paradox is the theoretical heart of the chapter. It is not a curiosity but a structural tendency. The reason is that the high-productivity, high-wage nation produces so much more value per worker that the worker's share of that value (the relative price of labour) can be smaller even when the absolute money paid is larger. Conversely, in the low-wage nation each worker produces little, so the small absolute wage can represent a large fraction of what the worker produces.

Here Marx draws support from a historical authority in footnote 3: James Anderson, in a polemic against Adam Smith, distinguished the apparent price of labour (the daily wage) from the real price — "that which a certain quantity of work performed actually costs the employer." Anderson argued that "labour is in almost all cases cheaper in rich countries than in those that are poorer," and that "Labour by the piece is generally cheaper in England." Anderson even notes the reciprocal process: "a low level of wages produces, in its turn, dearness of labour," citing the observation that "Labour being dearer in Ireland than it is in England ... because the wages are so much lower." This gives Marx an eighteenth-century authority for a central contention, and it also introduces the crucial concept of cost per unit of work performed as distinct from wage per day — precisely the distinction footnoted again in footnote 1, where David Buchanan observes that it is inaccurate to say wages rise merely because the same money "purchases more of a cheaper article."

2.6 The Empirical Evidence — Cowell and Redgrave on British vs. Continental Labour

Marx now substantiates the theory with the empirical investigations of official British factory inspectors — a hallmark of his method in this part of Capital. He cites J. W. Cowell, a member of the Factory Commission of 1833, who concluded after careful investigation of the spinning trade that "in England wages are virtually lower to the capitalist, though higher to the operative than on the Continent of Europe." This single sentence crystallizes the chapter's thesis: the wage is simultaneously higher for the worker (in nominal terms) and lower for the capitalist (in relative terms). The two perspectives do not contradict; they describe different measures of the same fact.

Marx then introduces Alexander Redgrave, an English factory inspector, whose Report of 31 October 1866 yields the chapter's richest empirical material. Redgrave "proves, by comparing the statistics of Continental states, that in spite of lower wages and much longer hours of work, Continental labour is, in proportion to the product, dearer than English." The structure of the demonstration is important: lower wages and longer hours do not make Continental labour cheaper to capital; when measured against output, it is dearer. This is the empirical vindication of the "relative price of labour" category.

The mechanism is revealed through a concrete case. The English manager of a cotton factory in Oldenburg declared "that the working hours there were from 5.30 a.m. to 8 p.m., Saturdays included" — an extraordinarily long day (14.5 hours, six days a week) — "and that the workers, when under English overseers, did not supply during this time quite so much of the product as the English in 10 hours, while they supplied much less when under German overseers." Here intensity and productivity are isolated with striking clarity: even with the same supervision (English overseers), the Continental workers could not match English output per hour; under local supervision, they fell further behind. Wages are "much lower than in England, in many cases lower by 50 per cent," yet the number of "hands" in proportion to the machinery is much greater — "in certain departments in the proportion of 5 : 3." To operate the same machinery, more than one and a half times as many workers were required. The low-wage nation thus employs more labour-power per unit of capital, another dimension of labour's relative dearness.

The next case, Russia, sharpens the point. Redgrave relayed precise details on Russian cotton factories from an English manager until recently employed there. On Russian soil, "so fruitful of all infamies, the old horrors of the early days of English factories are in full swing." The managers are English, since "the native Russian capitalist is incapable of handling factory business." Despite over-work "which continues throughout the day and the night" and "the most shameful under-payment of the workers, Russian manufacturing only manages to vegetate thanks to the prohibition of foreign competition" — i.e., protective tariffs. This is doubly significant: it shows that a regime of the very lowest wages and the very longest hours still fails to be competitive on its own terms, and it introduces the theme of state intervention (protection) that Marx will deploy against Carey. Cheap labour, far from guaranteeing capitalist viability, correlates with an inability to survive international competition without tariff shelter.

2.7 The Spindle Statistics — Quantifying Productivity and Intensity Differentials

Redgrave's comparative tables provide the chapter's most quantitative evidence. The tables report two measures: average number of spindles per factory and average number of spindles per person across European countries.

Average number of spindles per factory: England 12,600; France 1,500; Prussia 1,500; Belgium 4,000; Saxony 4,500; Austria 7,000; Switzerland 8,000.

Average number of spindles per person (one person to): France 14; Russia 28; Prussia 37; Bavaria 46; Austria 49; Belgium 50; Saxony 50; Switzerland 55; Smaller German states 55; Great Britain 74.

These figures quantify two dimensions of the productivity/intensity differential. Spindles per factory measure the scale and concentration of capital; spindles per worker measure the technical productivity of labour — how much machinery each worker operates. Great Britain, the most developed capitalist nation, has both by far the largest factories (12,600 spindles, dwarfing France's and Prussia's 1,500) and the highest number of spindles per worker (74, against France's 14). Redgrave is careful about the reliability of the data: the figures "were collected a few years ago, and ... since that time the size of the factories and the number of spindles per worker in England has increased," but he assumes "an approximately equal advance has been made in the Continental countries," so the comparison retains its value.

Redgrave adds a crucial caveat about the direction of bias: the comparison is "yet more unfavourable to Great Britain, inasmuch as there is so large a number of factories in which weaving by power is carried on in conjunction with spinning" — while, in the table, the weavers are not deducted, and "the factories abroad are chiefly spinning factories." Since the British figures include weaving as well as spinning labour, the true British advantage in spinning productivity is understated. To drive this home, Redgrave offers a concrete illustration: "many cotton spinning factories in my district in which mules containing 2,200 spindles are minded by one man (the 'minder') and two assistants only, turning off daily 220 lb. of yarn, measuring 400 miles in length." This single statistic — three workers, 2,200 spindles, 220 pounds and 400 miles of yarn daily — gives the productivity differential a tangible form and grounds the abstract category of intensity in measurable industrial practice.

2.8 Railway Construction and the Inverse Relationship Confirmed

The chapter extends its empirical base beyond cotton. Marx notes that "in Eastern Europe, as well as in Asia, English companies have undertaken the construction of railways, and have, in making them, employed a certain number of English workers side by side with the locals." This created a natural experiment in which English and local labour worked on the same projects. "Practical necessity has thus compelled them to take into account the national differences in the intensity of labour, but this has not brought them any loss." The capitalists had to adjust their expectations and wage offers to the differing intensities of the national labour forces, and doing so proved profitable.

The lesson is stated conceptually: "Their experience shows that even if the level of wages more or less corresponds with the average intensity of labour, the relative price of labour (i.e. the price of labour in relation to the product) generally varies in the inverse direction." Wages may track intensity roughly, but the relative price of labour moves inversely — the higher the intensity (and wage), the lower labour's relative cost; the lower the intensity (and wage), the higher labour's relative cost. The railway example thus generalizes the finding beyond textiles and across continents.

2.9 The Critique of Carey — From False Premise to Ideological Apologetics

The final movement of the chapter turns to open polemic against H. C. Carey and his Essay on the Rate of Wages (Philadelphia, 1835) — footnote 4 gives the full, revealing title: Essay on the Rate of Wages: With an Examination of the Causes of the Differences in the Condition of the Labouring Population throughout the World. Carey "tries to prove that differences in national wage-levels are directly proportional to the degree of productivity of the working day of each nation, in order to draw from this international ratio the deduction that wages everywhere rise and fall in proportion to the productivity of labour." This is the doctrine Marx must demolish, because it underwrites the free-trade optimist's claim that the market harmonizes the interests of capital and labour: high productivity produces high wages automatically.

Marx's refutation operates on three levels: logical, evidentiary, and ideological.

Logical. "The whole of our analysis of the production of surplus-value shows that this deduction would be absurd even if Carey himself had demonstrated his premises." The reduction is fatal: even granting Carey his premises, the conclusion does not follow. This is because the analysis of surplus-value establishes that higher productivity lowers the value of labour-power and raises surplus-value; it does not mechanically raise wages in proportion. The direct proportionality thesis is therefore internally void.

Evidentiary. Marx accuses Carey of "shuffling a confused mass of statistical material to and fro in his usual uncritical and superficial manner." Carey had not demonstrated his premises; he had merely assembled statistics without method. The comparison with Marx's own careful use of Cowell, Redgrave, and the spindle tables is implicit but pointed: Marx weighs his sources, distinguishes like from like, flags biases; Carey does none of this.

Ideological. Marx then exposes Carey's confession-within-the-claim. Carey "does not assert that things actually are as they ought to be according to his theory. For state intervention has falsified the natural economic relation." Carey must explain away reality by appeal to an external distorting force: protective tariffs (state intervention) have prevented the natural proportionality from materializing. He therefore proposes that "the different national wages must therefore be calculated on the assumption that the part of them that goes to the state in the form of taxes was received by the worker himself" — a hypothetical readjustment that removes the taxes and restores the imagined harmony. Marx's rejoinder is a single devastating question: "Ought not Mr Carey to consider further whether these 'state expenses' are not the 'natural fruits' of capitalist development?" If protection and state expenditure are products of capitalist development itself, then the "natural" harmony Carey posits is a fiction, and the intervention he deplores is internal to the system, not an external accident.

2.10 The Polemic Climax — Carey, Bastiat, and the Free-Trade Optimists

The chapter closes with a sustained, almost invective passage that escalates the critique into an ideological unmasking. Marx constructs a portrait of Carey as a thinker whose contradictions reveal the general bankruptcy of harmony economics. Carey first "declared that capitalist relations of production were eternal laws of nature and reason, whose free and harmonious working was only disturbed by the intervention of the state." He then "discovered afterwards that state intervention, i.e. the defence of those laws of nature and reason by the state, alias the system of protection, was necessitated by the diabolical influence of England on the world market" — an influence which, absurdly, "does not spring from the natural laws of capitalist production."

The escalation continues: Carey "discovered, further, that the theorems of Ricardo and others, in which existing social antagonisms and contradictions are formulated, are not the ideal product of the real economic movement, but, on the contrary, that the real antagonisms of capitalist production in England and elsewhere are the result of the theories of Ricardo and others!" This is the inversion Marx finds laughable — as if reality conformed to theory rather than the reverse, as if the class antagonisms of capitalism were caused by an economist's writings. Finally, Carey "discovered that it is, in the last resort, trade which destroys the inborn beauties and harmonies of the capitalist mode of production."

Marx's sarcasm peaks: "A step further, and he will perhaps discover that the one evil in capitalist production is capital itself." The sentence is a trap: Carey's logic, if pursued consistently, would dissolve into the recognition that capitalism's contradictions are intrinsic — the very opposite of the harmony he preaches. Marx concludes that "only a man with such an atrocious lack of the critical faculty, and such spurious erudition, deserved, in spite of his Protectionist heresy, to become the secret source of the harmonious wisdom of a Bastiat, and of all the other free-trade optimists of the present day." Bastiat stands here as the emblem of harmony economics — the doctrine that capitalism's free operation produces universal benefit. Marx's verdict is that Carey, despite being a protectionist (a "heresy" from the free-trade standpoint), nevertheless supplied the ideological raw material for the free-traders' "harmonious wisdom." The polemic thus binds the technical argument to its political stakes: the illusion of harmonious international wage relations is not a neutral error but a class instrument.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Fundamental Principles Revealed. The chapter yields several enduring systemic principles. First, the wage-form is a concealment, and any comparison built on surface appearance is necessarily distorted. The exoteric form of wages hides the value of labour-power, so the analyst must penetrate beneath nominal money sums. Second, the law of value is not transcended but modified on the world market: national averages of labour intensity and productivity are ranked on a single international scale measured in universal labour, so that the same working time represents unequal quantities of value across nations. Third, and most importantly, labour's cheapness is measured by its relative price, not its absolute wage. The high-wage, high-productivity nation can have cheap labour in the capitalist sense (a small share of a large product), while the low-wage nation can have dear labour (a large share of a small product). This inverts the commonsense equation of low wages with cheap labour — a reversal that Marx attributes in the older literature to James Anderson, and which he vindicates with the factory statistics of Cowell and Redgrave.

The Inverse Relation and the "Dearness of Cheap Labour." A subsidiary law repeatedly surfaces: a low level of wages itself produces the "dearness of labour" (Anderson), because low wages are bound up with low intensity, low productivity, and consequently high cost per unit of output. The Oldenburg and Russian cases demonstrate this concretely: longer hours and 50-percent-lower wages coexist with a greater number of hands per machine and a higher relative cost of labour to the capitalist. This reciprocal mechanism is a precursor of later analyses of the self-reinforcing character of underdevelopment.

The Ideological Lesson. The critique of Carey is not ornamental — it is the payoff. Carey's proportionality thesis supplies the ideological justification for free-trade optimism: if wages rise proportionally with productivity, then the international market harmonizes interests and the worker's poverty in a less-developed nation is merely a productivity problem to be solved automatically by development. Marx's demolition shows this to be both logically void (surplus-value analysis refutes it a priori) and empirically false (the inspectors' data refute it a posteriori). Equally significant is the exposure of the move by which harmony theorists explain away contrary evidence: they invoke state intervention as an external distortion of an otherwise harmonious system. Marx's counter — that state expenses and protection are themselves "natural fruits" of capitalist development — refuses the externalization and relocates the contradiction inside the system. Reality does not deviate from a natural harmony; the "harmony" was a fiction all along.

Broader Implications. Practically, the chapter warns against reading international wage statistics naively — a caution that remains potent in modern debates about global labour standards, offshoring, and "cheap labour" competition. Theoretically, it lays groundwork for later theories of unequal exchange and uneven and combined development: the international scale of value means that exchanges between nations of unequal productivity and intensity can systematically transfer value, and the "cheapness" of labour in the periphery is a relational, not an absolute, fact. Philosophically, the chapter enacts Marx's method at its most characteristic: it takes an apparently simple empirical question (why do wages differ between countries?) and reveals beneath it a structure of concealed value relations, contested categories, and class-serving ideologies. The lesson is that economic appearances are not merely imperfect reflections of reality to be corrected by better statistics — they are systematically produced forms that invert the underlying relations. To understand the wages of nations, one must first understand the value of labour-power, and one must refuse to mistake the visible measure for the hidden magnitude.
