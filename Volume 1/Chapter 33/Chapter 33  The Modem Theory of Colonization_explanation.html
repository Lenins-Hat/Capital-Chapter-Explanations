Volume 1 • Chapter 33: The Modem Theory of Colonization

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

This chapter's central problem is a paradox of visibility. In Western Europe, the homeland of political economy, the process of primitive accumulation is historically accomplished — the separation of the producer from the means of production is complete, and the resulting social order is so thoroughly naturalized that the economist can drape a pre-capitalist vocabulary of "law" and "property" over it without apparent contradiction. In the colonies, by contrast, this accomplished fact does not yet exist: the producer still owns his own conditions of labour and employs them to enrich himself rather than a capitalist. The contradiction between these two diametrically opposed economic systems therefore becomes practical, manifesting itself as an open struggle — and it is precisely this colonial openness that allows Marx to see what the mother country conceals.

Marx's intellectual objective is therefore not ethnographic. He states explicitly at the close that "we are not concerned here with the condition of the colonies"; the colonies matter only as an experimental terrain in which the presuppositions of capital become legible. The chapter's central thesis is that the fundamental condition of capitalist private property — and thus of the capitalist mode of production and accumulation as a whole — is "the annihilation of that private property which rests on the labour of the individual himself; in other words, the expropriation of the worker."

The chapter's vehicle is Edward Gibbon Wakefield, whom Marx credits with the "great merit" of having discovered in the colonies the truth about capitalist relations in the mother country. Wakefield's "systematic colonization" — the artificial pricing of virgin land plus a government emigration fund — is treated as a confessed, half-honest plan to manufacture wage-labourers where they do not spontaneously appear. Wakefield thus serves as a hostile witness whose testimony dismantles the apologetics of his own class. The intellectual payoff is a demolition of the fiction of the "free contract" between capitalist and worker, exposed as a relation of absolute dependence that must, in the colonies, be created by force and fraud.

Within the broader architecture of Capital, this is the dénouement. After eight chapters tracing the bloody genesis of the capital relation, Marx ends not with England but with a mirror held up in the New World — and thereby returns the analysis to its foundation: capital is not a thing but a social relation, and that relation must be continuously made.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Colonial Optic: Two Kinds of Private Property and the Truth-Telling Function of the Periphery

Marx opens with a methodological indictment: political economy "confuses, on principle, two different kinds of private property." The first rests on the labour of the producer himself; the second rests on the exploitation of the labour of others. The confusion is not accidental but structural — the discipline "forgets" that the second is not merely different from the first but its direct antithesis, and that it "grows on the former's tomb and nowhere else." This metaphorical formulation is doing precise analytical work: exploitation-based property is not an independent species of property that could have arisen on its own; it is parasitical, requiring the prior destruction of producer-property as its soil.

The reason this confusion can be sustained in Europe is that the destruction is already complete. Capitalist relations have either directly subordinated the whole of national production or, where economic development lags, exercise indirect control over decaying pre-capitalist strata that survive beside it. The political economist then applies "the notions of law and of property inherited from a pre-capitalist world" to this ready-made capitalist world — and the more loudly the facts cry out against his ideology, the more "anxious zeal" and "unction" he brings to the task. Marx is diagnosing a specific apologetic mechanism: the ideological vocabulary is anachronistic, and its fervour is proportionate to its falsity.

It is otherwise in the colonies. There the capitalist "constantly comes up against the obstacle presented by the producer" who owns his own conditions of labour. The contradiction becomes a struggle, and where the capitalist has the power of the mother country behind him, he "tries to use force to clear out of the way" the modes of production resting on personal labour. Marx notes that the same interest which in the mother country compels the economist to declare the capitalist mode of production "theoretically its own opposite" now drives him, in the colonies, "to make a clean breast of it." This is the chapter's crucial rhetorical pivot: the colonies produce confession. The apologist's armour, "piece by piece, like rotten touchwood," crumbles off. What he confesses is that the development of social productivity — co-operation, division of labour, large-scale machinery — is "impossible without the expropriation of the workers"; and that in the interest of the "so-called wealth of the nation," he seeks "artificial means to ensure the poverty of the people." A footnote clarifies the colonial definition: Marx means true colonies — virgin soil colonized by free immigrants — with the United States still, economically speaking, a colony of Europe; old plantation societies where abolition revolutionized earlier relations also qualify.

Wakefield's Discovery: Capital as a Social Relation, Not a Thing

Marx introduces E. G. Wakefield with a precise compliment: his merit lies not in discovering something new about the colonies but in discovering, in the colonies, the truth about capitalist relations in the mother country. The analogy is to protectionism, which originally aimed at manufacturing capitalists artificially at home; Wakefield's theory of colonization, which England for a time attempted to enforce by Act of Parliament, aims at manufacturing wage-labourers in the colonies. This is what he calls "systematic colonization." A footnote traces the insight's genealogy: Wakefield's few genuine perceptions were "fully anticipated" by Mirabeau père, the Physiocrat, in L'Ami des hommes (1756), and even earlier by English economists — Marx is careful to deny Wakefield originality of concept while granting him originality of exposure.

Wakefield's first discovery is the chapter's theoretical keystone: in the colonies, property in money, means of subsistence, machines and other means of production does not as yet stamp a man as a capitalist if the essential complement is missing — "the wage-labourer, the other man, who is compelled to sell himself of his own free will." From this follows the definition: capital is not a thing, but a social relation between persons which is mediated through things. Marx enforces this with an extended footnote quoting his own 1849 essay Wage Labour and Capital: "A negro is a negro. In certain relations he becomes a slave. A mule is a machine for spinning cotton. Only in certain relations does it become capital. Outside these circumstances, it is no more capital than gold is intrinsically money, or sugar is the price of sugar... Capital is a social relation of production. It is a historical relation of production." The "formula" of the chapter is thus this relational definition; it is the analytical lens through which every subsequent fact is read.

The empirical illustration is the celebrated reductio of Mr Peel. Peel transported to the Swan River district of Western Australia £50,000 in means of subsistence and production, plus — with foresight — 3,000 persons of the working class, men, women and children. On arrival, "Mr Peel was left without a servant to make his bed or fetch him water from the river." Marx's mock-elegy is exact: "Unhappy Mr Peel, who provided for everything except the export of English relations of production to Swan River!" The point is not that Peel was foolish but that capital cannot be exported as a thing; the wage-relation must be exported as a set of enforceable social constraints. Remove the constraints, and £50,000 of "capital" reverts to a heap of useful objects and a crowd of independent settlers.

Two Preliminary Admonitions: The Category Error and the Feudal Jurist Analogy

Before expounding Wakefield's further discoveries, Marx makes two preliminary remarks. First, we know that means of production and subsistence, while they remain the property of the immediate producer, are not capital; they become capital only under circumstances in which they serve simultaneously as means of exploitation of and domination over the worker. But this "capitalist soul" is so intimately wedded, in the political economist's mind, to their material substance, that he "christens them capital under all circumstances, even where they are its exact opposite." This is fetishism in its crudest form — the attribution of a social property to a physical thing. Second, and consequently, Wakefield describes the splitting-up of the means of production into the individual property of many mutually independent and self-employed workers as "equal division of capital." Marx's simile: the political economist is like the feudal jurist who "used to attach the labels supplied by feudal law even to relationships which were purely monetary." The error is one of mislabeling, but its effects are material — it converts a denial of the capital relation into an appearance of its universal presence.

The Social Contract Fiction and the Slave Alternative

Wakefield's own reasoning is then quoted: if all members of society possess equal portions of capital, "no man would have a motive for accumulating more capital than he could use with his own hands. This is to some extent the case in new American settlements, where a passion for owning land prevents the existence of a class of labourers for hire." Marx draws the decisive inference: so long as the worker can accumulate for himself — which he can do so long as he retains possession of his means of production — capitalist accumulation and the capitalist mode of production are impossible. The class of wage-labourers essential to them is simply lacking. This yields the analytical question to which the rest of the chapter is an answer: how, in old Europe, was the expropriation of the worker from his conditions of labour accomplished?

Wakefield's answer is a "social contract of a quite original kind": mankind adopted a "simple contrivance for promoting the accumulation of capital" — "they have divided themselves into owners of capital and owners of labour... This division was the result of concert and combination." Marx's paraphrase, dripping with irony, is that "the mass of mankind expropriated itself in honour of the 'accumulation of capital.'" The sarcasm exposes the absurdity of a voluntarist account of expropriation. And the reductio is immediate: if this instinct of self-denying fanaticism existed, it would "run riot in the colonies," where the conditions exist for turning the social contract from dream into reality. Yet it does not. Why else would "systematic colonization" be required to replace spontaneous, unregulated colonization? Wakefield's own data answer him: in the Northern States of the American Union, not one-tenth of the people would fall under the description of hired labourers, while in England "the labouring class compose the bulk of the people." Indeed, the drive to self-expropriation is so feeble that slavery, by Wakefield's own admission, is "the sole natural basis of colonial wealth"; his systematic colonization is "a mere makeshift, resulting from the fact that he has free men, not slaves, to deal with." Wakefield's Swan River example confirms it: the first Spanish settlers of Santo Domingo did not import labourers from Spain, and without labourers "their capital must have perished"; so it did in the last English-founded colony, where masses of seed, implements and cattle perished "for want of labourers to use it," and no settler preserved much more capital than he could work with his own hands.

The Free Colony's Secret: Public Land as Prosperity and as Cancer

Marx now consolidates the structural analysis. Expropriation of the mass of the people from the soil is the basis of the capitalist mode of production; the essence of a free colony is the opposite — "the bulk of the soil is still public property," so that every settler can convert part of it into private property and individual means of production without preventing later settlers from doing the same. This is, in one phrase, "the secret both of the prosperity of the colonies and of their cancerous affliction — their resistance to the establishment of capital." Prosperity and pathology are the same fact viewed from different sides. A footnote (footnote 10, quoting Wakefield) sharpens the point: "Land, to be an element of colonization, must not only be waste, but it must be public property, liable to be converted into private property." Wakefield's own formulation of the resulting scarcity is quoted: "Where land is very cheap and all men are free, where every one who so pleases can easily obtain a piece of land for himself, not only is labour very dear, as respects the labourer's share of the produce, but the difficulty is to obtain combined labour at any price."

The Missing Home Market and the Vanishing Wage-Labourer

Marx develops the consequences in two directions. First, the market problem: in the colonies the separation of the worker from the conditions of labour and from the soil "does not yet exist, or only sporadically, or on too limited a scale." Hence agriculture is not separated from industry, and the domestic industries of the countryside have not been destroyed. Whence, then, "the home market for capital"? Wakefield's own enumeration of the American settler is adduced as evidence: no part of the population is exclusively agricultural except slaves and their employers; free Americans who cultivate the soil follow many other occupations, make their own furniture and tools, frequently build their own houses, and carry to market the produce of their own industry — "They are spinners and weavers, they make soap and candles, as well as, in many cases, shoes and clothes for their own use. In America the cultivation of land is often the secondary pursuit of a blacksmith, a miller or a shopkeeper." Marx's question is lethal: "Where, among such curious characters, is the 'field of abstinence' for the capitalists?" — the phrase "abstinence" being the vulgar economists' term for the capitalist's supposed sacrifice in not consuming his capital.

Second, the labour-market problem, which Marx unfolds with a doctrinal statement of the normal capitalist mechanism. The "great beauty" of capitalist production is that it not only constantly reproduces the wage-labourer as wage-labourer but always produces a relative surplus population in proportion to the accumulation of capital. This surplus keeps the law of supply and demand in relation to labour "on the right lines," confines the oscillation of wages within limits satisfactory to capitalist exploitation, and secures the indispensable social dependence of the worker on the capitalist. In the mother country, the economist's "smug deceitfulness" can convert this relation of absolute dependence into a "free contract between buyer and seller," between equally independent owners of commodities — capital on one side, labour on the other.

In the colonies this illusion "is torn aside." Population increases faster because many workers enter colonial society as ready-made adults, and yet the labour-market is "always understocked." The law of supply and demand for labour "collapses completely." The old world throws in capital "thirsting after exploitation and 'abstinence'"; the regular reproduction of the wage-labourer meets "insuperable" obstacles. And the reproduction of redundant wage-labourers? "Today's wage-labourer is tomorrow's independent peasant or artisan, working for himself. He vanishes from the labour-market — but not into the workhouse." This constant transformation of wage-labourers into independent producers reacts "very adversely" on the labour market: the degree of exploitation remains "indecently low," and the worker "loses, along with the relation of dependence, the feeling of dependence" on the abstemious capitalist — "Hence all the inconveniences depicted so honestly, so eloquently and so movingly by our friend E. G. Wakefield."

Wakefield's Lament: The Understocked Market and the Imported Competitor

Marx now lets Wakefield speak, and the quotations are chosen for their involuntary candour. The supply of labour "is always, not only small, but uncertain." "Though the produce divided between the capitalist and the labourer be large, the labourer takes so great a share that he soon becomes a capitalist... Few, even of those whose lives are unusually long, can accumulate great masses of wealth." The workers "most emphatically refuse to let the capitalist abstain from paying for the greater part of their labour" — an ironic reversal of the abstinence doctrine, since it is the worker who refuses abstinence to the capitalist. Worse: importing wage-labourers from Europe with the capitalist's own capital only produces competitors, since the imported workers soon "cease... to be labourers for hire; they... become independent landowners, if not competitors with their former masters in the labour-market." Marx's commentary is satirically accelerated: "Horror of horrors! The excellent capitalist has imported bodily from Europe, with his own good money, his own competitors! The end of the world has come!" Wakefield's lament over the absence of relations and feelings of dependence is then crowned by his disciple Merivale, who observes that on account of high wages there is in the colonies an urgent desire for cheaper and more subservient workers, "for a class of people to whom the capitalist may dictate his terms, instead of having his terms dictated by them" — and who states the general law baldly: "In the old civilized countries the worker, although free, is by a law of nature dependent on the capitalist; in colonies this dependence must be created by artificial means." This single sentence is the chapter's most damning exhibit: the "law of nature" is exposed as a historical product that elsewhere requires manufacture.

Merivale, Molinari, and the Scandal of Inverted Exploitation

A long critical footnote introduces the vulgar free-trader Molinari as a still more revealing witness. In colonies where slavery was abolished without compulsory labour being replaced by an equivalent quantity of free labour, "the opposite of what happens every day before our eyes" occurred: "Simple workers have been seen to exploit in their turn the industrial entrepreneurs, demanding from them wages which bear absolutely no relation to the legitimate share in the product which they ought to receive." Planters were ruined or closed their businesses; and Molinari concludes that "it is doubtless better that these accumulations of capital should be destroyed than that generations of men should perish" — to which Marx interjects "(how generous of M. Molinari)" — "but would it not be better if both survived?"

Marx's rejoinder is a masterpiece of immanent critique. What becomes, he asks, of the Ten Commandments, of Moses and the Prophets, of the law of supply and demand, "if in Europe the 'entrepreneur' can cut down the worker's 'legitimate share' and in the West Indies the workers can cut down the entrepreneur's?" And what is this "legitimate share" which, "according to your own admission, the capitalist in Europe daily neglects to pay"? The revelation is that Molinari's free-trade orthodoxy licenses exploitation in one direction and demands police intervention when the vector reverses: where the workers are so "simple" as to exploit the capitalist, Molinari "feels a powerful itch to use police methods to set on the right road that law of supply and demand which works automatically everywhere else." The "law" is thus revealed as a normative preference masquerading as a natural mechanism.

The "Barbarizing Tendency of Dispersion" and the Problem of Long-Term Capital

Wakefield's diagnosis of the colonial condition is that dispersion of producers and of national wealth has a "barbarizing tendency." The fragmentation of the means of production among innumerable self-employed owners "annihilates, along with the centralization of capital, all the foundations of combined labour." Every lengthy undertaking extending over several years and demanding fixed capital is prevented. In Europe capital "does not hesitate for a moment," because the working class forms its living appendage, "always present in excess, always at its disposal" — but not in the colonies. Wakefield's anecdote is then recounted: speaking with capitalists of Canada and the state of New York, where the wave of immigration often deposits a sediment of "redundant" workers, one of his characters explains that their capital "was ready for many operations which require a considerable period of time for their completion; but we could not begin such operations with labour which, we knew, would soon leave us." Even a temporary engagement would have been acceptable "provided we had been sure of a fresh supply whenever we might need it." This is the core capitalist requirement — not merely labour, but reproducible, replaceable labour.

Marx then notes a dialectical reversal in Wakefield's own text: after contrasting English capitalist agriculture and its "combined" labour with scattered American cultivation, Wakefield "unwittingly shows us the obverse of the medal." He depicts the mass of the American people as "well-to-do, independent, enterprising and comparatively cultured," whereas "the English agricultural labourer is a miserable wretch, a pauper." Wakefield's own words: "In what country, except North America and some new colonies, do the wages of free labour employed in agriculture much exceed a bare subsistence for the labourer?... Undoubtedly, farm-horses in England, being a valuable property, are better fed than English peasants." Marx's gloss — that the wealth of the nation is once again, "by its very nature, identical with the misery of the people" — ties the colonial evidence back to the general law of capitalist accumulation.

The Cure: Artificial Land Price and the "Sufficient Price" as Ransom

The policy prescription follows. How can the anti-capitalist cancer be healed? To convert all land from public into private property "at one blow" would destroy the root of the evil — but also the colony. The trick "to kill two birds with one stone" is for the government to set an artificial price on virgin soil, "a price independent of the law of supply and demand," compelling the immigrant to work a long time for wages before he can buy land and become an independent farmer. The fund arising from land sales — "this fund of money extorted from the wages of labour by a violation of the sacred law of supply and demand" — is then to be applied by government "in proportion to its growth, to the importation of paupers from Europe into the colonies, so as to keep the wage-labour market full for the capitalists." Under these circumstances, "everything will be for the best in the best of all possible worlds" — a Panglossian quotation that frames the scheme as theodicy.

Wakefield's own triumphant formulation is then quoted: the supply of labour "must be constant and regular, because, first, as no labourer would be able to procure land until he had worked for money, all immigrant labourers, working for a time for wages and in combination, would produce capital for the employment of more labourers; secondly, because every labourer who left off working for wages and became a landowner, would, by purchasing land, provide a fund for bringing fresh labour to the colony." The land price must be "sufficient" — high enough "to prevent the labourers from becoming independent landowners until others had followed to take their place." Marx's interpretive translation is the chapter's sharpest conceptual compression: this "sufficient price for the land" is "nothing but a euphemistic circumlocution for the ransom which the worker must pay to the capitalist in return for permission to retire from the wage-labour market to the land." The transaction has two stages: first the worker must create for the capitalist the "capital" that enables him to exploit more workers; then, at his own expense, he must put a "substitute" in the labour-market, dispatched across the sea by the government, again at the worker's expense, for his old master. A footnote (the Colins extract) generalizes the principle from the other side: "it is thanks to the individual appropriation of the soil that there exist men who only possess the strength of their arms... When you put a man in a vacuum, you rob him of the air. You do the same, when you take away the soil from him... for you are putting him in a space void of wealth, so as to leave him no way of living except according to your wishes."

The Fiasco: Peel's Bank Act, the Diverted Emigration, and the American Aftermath

Marx notes, "extremely characteristic," that the English government for years practised this method of primitive accumulation prescribed by Wakefield expressly for the colonies. The resulting fiasco was "as ignominious as the fate of Peel's Bank Act" — explained in a footnote as Sir Robert Peel's Bank Act of 1844, suspended in November 1857 with the onset of that year's commercial crisis. The scheme failed on its own terms: the stream of emigration was "simply diverted from the English colonies to the United States." Meanwhile, the advance of capitalist production in Europe, with increasing government pressure, rendered Wakefield's recipe superfluous. Marx then documents the closing of the American escape route in three movements: first, the enormous continuous flood of humanity driven onto American shores leaves "a stationary sediment in the East of the United States," since European immigration throws men onto the eastern labour-market faster than westward migration can wash them away; second, the American Civil War brings a colossal national debt, a heavy tax burden, the creation of "a finance aristocracy of the vilest type," and the granting of immense tracts of public land to speculative companies for railways and mines — in short, "a very rapid centralization of capital." The republic has therefore "ceased to be the promised land for emigrating workers," though wages and dependence have not yet reached the normal European level. Finally, Australia: the shameless squandering of uncultivated colonial land on aristocrats and capitalists by the English government — denounced even by Wakefield — combined with the gold-rush immigration and competition from imported English commodities affecting "everyone down to the smallest artisan," produced "an ample 'relative surplus population of workers,'" so that almost every mail-boat brings news of a "glut of the Australian labour-market," and prostitution flourishes "as exuberantly as in the Haymarket in London." A footnote on the Land Law of Victoria (1862), by the Hon. C. G. Duffy, notes that the first objective of the new land act was to give "increased facilities for the settlement of the people," but that the squandering already accomplished by the English government "stands in the way" — reform arrives too late to undo the primitive accumulation already effected.

The Final Revelation

The chapter closes by withdrawing its own ethnographic interest. "However, we are not concerned here with the condition of the colonies." What interests Marx is "the secret discovered in the New World by the political economy of the Old World, and loudly proclaimed by it": that the capitalist mode of production and accumulation, and therefore capitalist private property as well, have for their fundamental condition the annihilation of that private property which rests on the labour of the individual himself — "in other words, the expropriation of the worker." The circle is closed: the chapter began by distinguishing producer-property from exploitation-property and claiming that the latter grows "on the former's tomb and nowhere else"; it ends by establishing this as a universal law confirmed at the point where its operation is most visible.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Four fundamental principles emerge. First, capital is a social relation mediated through things, not a collection of things — the Peel anecdote is the most economical proof in the Marxist canon that means of production become capital only when accompanied by a class compelled to sell its labour-power. Property is therefore a bundle of enforceable social relations, not a physical inventory.

Second, the "free contract" of the labour market is the ideological mask of a manufactured dependence. Marx's decisive analytic move is to show that the relation the mother-country economist calls a "law of nature" must, in the colonies, be "created by artificial means," including state price-fixing, taxation through land sales, and government-sponsored emigration — all violations of the very supply-and-demand law invoked to naturalize it. The normal case and the colonial exception are revealed as the same case, differing only in the visibility of the coercion.

Third, the colonies function as a control experiment in historical materialism. By stripping away the completed structures of European society, the colonial setting exposes the presupposition of capital: the prior expropriation of the producer. The periphery does not merely illustrate the metropole; it demonstrates it. This methodological device — understanding the developed form through its undeveloped instantiation — is one Marx uses elsewhere, but here it carries the burden of the entire argument's climax.

Fourth, the "sufficient price" as ransom crystallizes the political economy of settler colonialism: the worker is made to finance the production of his own replacement and the expansion of the capital that exploits him. This is exploitation raised to a second power — the worker pays for the reproduction of the class relation that subordinates him.

The broader implications are twofold. Historically, Marx's epilogue shows that the closure of the frontier is not incidental but integral to capitalist development: the American Civil War, railway land grants, the tax burden and finance aristocracy, the Australian land squandering, all transformed the escapes from wage-labour into further reservoirs of it. The "promised land" was closed not by accident but by accumulation. Philosophically, the chapter refutes any contractarian or voluntarist account of class society — the "social contract" of capital is a fiction whose real analogue is force, and whose measure is the ruin it leaves in the producer's soil. The essential takeaway, stated in the chapter's final clause, is that capitalist private property is the negation of the property that labour creates, and that it can exist only where that prior, laborious property has first been annihilated.
