Volume 1 • Chapter 7: The Labour Process and the Valorization Process

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 7 of Capital, Volume One opens Part Three, "The Production of Absolute Surplus-Value," and constitutes one of the most consequential pivots in Marx's entire theoretical architecture. Its central problem is deceptively simple but devastating in its implications: How is it possible for money to be transformed into capital in a way that obeys, rather than violates, the laws of commodity exchange? The chapter's core thesis is that this apparent paradox — the secret of profit under conditions of fair exchange — is resolvable only by recognizing that the commodity the capitalist purchases, labour-power, has a use-value qualitatively unlike any other: its consumption creates more value than it costs to reproduce. Everything else in the chapter is the rigorous working-out of this discovery.

The chapter performs its argument in two tightly bound movements. The first movement (Section 1, "The Labour Process") abstracts entirely from social form to deliver a trans-historical account of what labour is — the "everlasting nature-imposed condition of human existence" through which humanity metabolizes with nature. Marx deliberately strips away all historically specific determinations (slave-owner, serf-master, capitalist) to isolate the pure anatomy of purposeful activity, object of labour, and instrument of labour. This abstraction is not ornamental: it establishes the conceptual baseline against which the specifically capitalist distortions of the labour process — supervision, property in the product, and the extraction of surplus — can later be measured as departures from, and appropriations of, a universal human activity.

The second movement (Section 2, "The Valorization Process") reintroduces capital and shows how the same material process, viewed under the aspect of value, becomes the engine of self-expanding wealth. Marx produces a meticulous numerical demonstration — the famous cotton-spinning example — that exhausts every "moral" and "service"-based defence of profit and lands on the sole remaining explanation: the difference between the value of labour-power and the value that labour-power can be made to create in a day's work. This gap is the seed of all surplus-value, and Part Three of Capital (and ultimately the entire critique of political economy) unfolds from it.

Intellectually, this chapter belongs to the deepest level of Marx's critical project. Having established in Chapter 1 the dual nature of the commodity (use-value and exchange-value), and in Chapters 4–6 the laws of exchange and the circulation of capital (the elusive M–C–M′ formula whose "+" Marx had promised to explain), Marx now locates the site where that mysterious increment is actually produced. The chapter thereby links the abstract theory of value to the concrete labour process of the workshop, dissolving the false separation between "circulation" and "production" that both vulgar economics and, in different ways, the Physiocrats and Ricardo had maintained. It is also where Marx deploys his most sustained philosophical anthropology (the architect-versus-bee argument), his clearest statement of productive consumption, and the definitive distinction between labour as creator of use-values and labour as creator of value — a distinction, he says, that "resolves itself into a distinction between two aspects of the production process."

The chapter's rhetorical strategy is as important as its logic. It is a drama in three acts: an apparently serene technical account of production, a psychological collapse of the capitalist ("stares in astonishment") when his calculations yield no surplus, and a triumphant resolution in which the capitalist "laughs" because he has all along understood — like the practical man he is — what the professors of political economy are paid to obscure. The chapter is thus simultaneously an economics treatise, a piece of materialist philosophy, and a polemic against apologetics. Its objective is nothing less than to make visible, in plain daylight and without appeal to fraud, theft, or unequal exchange, the mechanism by which capital feeds on living labour.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

Framing: Part Three and the Absolute Form of Surplus-Value

The chapter is placed under the banner of Part Three, "The Production of Absolute Surplus-Value." The adjective absolute is the key to the entire section's logic. Surplus-value, Marx will argue, can be increased in two fundamental ways: by lengthening the working day beyond the time needed to reproduce the worker (absolute surplus-value), or by shortening that necessary time itself through increased productivity (relative surplus-value). Chapter 7 lays the conceptual groundwork for the absolute form because it isolates, for the first time, the pure mechanics of how any surplus arises at all — the length of the working day versus the value of labour-power. Everything about relative surplus-value (developed later, in Parts Four and beyond) presupposes this baseline. The chapter is therefore foundational not merely chronologically but structurally.

2.1 The Labour Process in its Simple, Abstract Elements

The Use of Labour-Power as Labour Itself

Marx opens by completing a transaction begun in the preceding chapters. The capitalist has purchased labour-power on the market. But a purchased commodity must be consumed to be of use, and the consumption of labour-power is labour itself. "The purchaser of labour-power consumes it by setting the seller of it to work." In working, the seller becomes "in actuality what previously he only was potentially, namely labour-power in action, a worker." This is a crucial dialectical move: potentiality (the capacity to work, purchased as a commodity) is actualized only through the act of working. To embody labour in commodities, the worker must first embody it in use-values — because value cannot exist except as the bearer of some use-value. Hence the capitalist sets the worker to produce a specific article. Crucially, Marx adds that the fact that this production is carried on "under the control of a capitalist and on his behalf does not alter the general character of that production." This is the justification for his abstraction: we can study the labour process "independently of any specific social formation."

Labour as Metabolism Between Man and Nature

Marx then delivers his celebrated definition of labour: "Labour is, first of all, a process between man and nature, a process by which man, through his own actions, mediates, regulates and controls the metabolism between himself and nature." The term metabolism (in German, Stoffwechsel, literally "material exchange") is central. Man "confronts the materials of nature as a force of nature," setting in motion "the natural forces which belong to his own body, his arms, legs, head and hands." By acting on external nature and changing it, he "simultaneously changes his own nature." Labour is thus not a one-way extraction from nature but a reciprocal transformation: humanity develops "the potentialities slumbering within nature" while subjecting "the play of its forces to his own sovereign power."

This passage is deeply grounded in a materialist anthropology. Marx explicitly excludes "those first instinctive forms of labour which remain on the animal level." He notes the "immense interval of time" separating the fully human labour presupposed here from humanity's earliest instinctive forms — a historical-developmental claim. The labour he analyzes is "an exclusively human characteristic."

The Architect and the Bee: Purpose and Ideality

Here Marx deploys his most famous illustration of what distinguishes human from animal production. A spider performs operations resembling a weaver's; a bee puts many a human architect to shame in building its honeycomb. "But what distinguishes the worst architect from the best of bees is that the architect builds the cell in his mind before he constructs it in wax." At the end of every labour process, a result emerges that "had already been conceived by the worker at the beginning, hence already existed ideally." The German verwirklichen (realize) is literally "to make real" — the worker realizes his own purpose in the material. This purpose is conscious, determining "the mode of his activity with the rigidity of a law," and the worker "must subordinate his will to it." This subordination is not momentary; it requires purpose, will, and "close attention" for the whole duration of the work. Marx adds a psychological-register observation: the less the worker is attracted to the work and the less he "enjoys it as the free play of his own physical and mental powers, the closer his attention is forced to be." This is a subtle foreshadowing of alienation — where labour is not intrinsically enjoyable, attention becomes a disciplinary imposition rather than spontaneous absorption.

The Three Simple Elements of the Labour Process

Marx reduces the labour process to three "simple elements": (1) purposeful activity, that is work itself; (2) the object on which that work is performed; and (3) the instruments of that work. This tripartite scheme is the chapter's structural skeleton for everything that follows.

The Object of Labour and the Definition of Raw Material

The object of labour is whatever human activity works upon. Marx begins with the most basic case: the land (economically including water) in its original state, which supplies "necessaries or means of subsistence ready to hand... as the universal material for human labour." Things that labour "merely separates from immediate connection with their environment" are objects of labour "spontaneously provided by nature" — fish caught and separated from water, timber felled in virgin forests, ores extracted from veins. Marx's footnote here quotes James Steuart to the effect that nature's spontaneous productions are small in quantity and independent of man, appearing "as it were, to be furnished by Nature, in the same way as a small sum is given to a young man, in order to put him in a way of industry, and of making his fortune."

But if the object of labour has "been filtered through previous labour," Marx calls it raw material (a technical use of the term, narrower than standard English, as the editors note). His precise formulation: "All raw material is an object of labour, but not every object of labour is raw material; the object of labour counts as raw material only when it has already undergone some alteration by means of labour." The logic is a matter of position in the process, not intrinsic property.

The Instrument of Labour

The instrument of labour is "a thing, or a complex of things, which the worker interposes between himself and the object of his labour and which serves as a conductor, directing his activity onto that object." The worker uses the mechanical, physical, and chemical properties of some substances to set them to work on other substances "as instruments of his power, and in accordance with his purposes." Marx here cites Hegel's Logic on "cunning" (the List der Vernunft): cunning lies in the "intermediative action which, while it permits the objects to follow their own bent and act upon one another till they waste away, and does not itself directly interfere in the process, is nevertheless only working out its own aims." This is an extraordinary philosophical resource: Marx borrows from Hegel the idea that the tool is a form of mediated power, wherein the worker's purpose is realized indirectly through the tool's own natural propensities.

Marx distinguishes between ready-made means of subsistence (like fruit, gathered by bodily organs alone — the body itself as instrument) and the general case, where the object the worker directly takes possession of is "not the object of labour but its instrument." Nature thus becomes "one of the organs of his activity," which he annexes to his bodily organs, "adding stature to himself in spite of the Bible." The famous image: "As the earth is his original larder, so too it is his original tool house." The earth supplies stones for throwing, grinding, pressing, cutting. The earth itself is an instrument of labour (as in agriculture), but using it in this way "presupposes a whole series of other instruments and a comparatively high stage of development of labour-power." Marx cites Ganilh (in his otherwise "miserable work") for enumerating the long series of labour processes that agriculture presupposes, and footnotes the Physiocrats' error that the productivity of labour appeared as a "gift of nature," so that surplus-value appeared as a "gift of nature."

Even at the smallest stage of development, the labour process requires "specially prepared instruments" — stone implements and weapons in the oldest caves. In earliest human history, domesticated animals (themselves modified by labour) play the chief role as instruments, alongside worked stones, wood, bones, and shells. Marx cites Turgot on the importance of domesticated animals for the beginnings of civilization. The use and construction of instruments of labour, though present "in germ" among certain animals, is "characteristic of the specifically human labour process," prompting Benjamin Franklin's definition of man as "a tool-making animal."

Instruments as Indices of Social Epochs

Marx now makes a sweeping theoretical claim of major importance: "Relics of bygone instruments of labour possess the same importance for the investigation of extinct economic formations of society as do fossil bones for the determination of extinct species of animals." His argument: "It is not what is made but how, and by what instruments of labour, that distinguishes different economic epochs." Instruments of labour not only measure the development of human labour but "indicate the social relations within which men work." This is a proto-archaeological, materialist methodology — the basis for what later becomes the mode-of-production thesis. He subdivides instruments into mechanical ones ("the bones and muscles of production"), which offer "much more decisive evidence of the character of a given social epoch," versus those that merely hold materials (pipes, tubs, baskets, jars) — the "vascular system of production," which first becomes important in the chemical industries. His footnote laments that historians have paid "very little attention to the development of material production, which is the basis of all social life, and therefore of all real history." He notes that prehistory is classified by tools (Stone Age, Bronze Age, Iron Age) rather than by "so-called historical research."

Marx extends the definition of instruments of labour "in a wider sense" to include "all the objective conditions necessary for carrying on the labour process," which do not directly enter but without which the process is impossible or partial. The earth is again the universal instrument of this kind (the "ground beneath his feet" and a "field of employment"). Mediated versions of such conditions include "workshops, canals, roads, etc."

The Product: Objectification of Labour

Marx now completes the circuit: "In the labour process, therefore, man's activity, via the instruments of labour, effects an alteration in the object of labour which was intended from the outset. The process is extinguished in the product." The product is a use-value, "a piece of natural material adapted to human needs by means of a change in its form." Crucially: "Labour has become bound up in its object: labour has been objectified, the object has been worked on." Marx stages this as a transformation from Unruhe (unrest, the restless activity of living labour) to Sein (being, the fixed, immobile characteristic of the finished product). "The worker has spun, and the product is a spinning." This is the philosophical grammar of objectification — later the hinge of alienation theory, though Marx does not use that word here.

Means of Production and Productive Labour

Viewing the process from its result (the product), "both the instruments and the object of labour are means of production" and "the labour itself is productive labour." Marx has a footnote cautioning that "this method of determining what is productive labour, from the standpoint of the simple labour process, is by no means sufficient to cover the capitalist process of production." This is a critical caveat: what counts as "productive" in the abstract labour process is not yet what counts as productive for capital, a distinction that will matter enormously later.

Marx notes that although a use-value emerges as product, other use-values (products of previous labour) enter as means of production. "The same use-value is both the product of a previous process, and a means of production in a later process."

Extractive vs Transformative Industries

With the exception of the extractive industries (mining, hunting, fishing, and agriculture only insofar as it "starts by breaking up virgin soil"), where the material comes directly from nature, all branches of industry deal with raw material — an object of labour "already filtered through labour." Even animals and plants we consider "products of nature" may be, in their present form, the result of "a gradual transformation continued through many generations under human control." As for instruments of labour, they "show traces of the labour of past ages, even to the most superficial observer."

Principal Substance and Accessory Materials

Raw material may form a product's principal substance or enter only as an accessory. Accessories may be consumed by the instruments (coal by a steam-engine, oil by a wheel, hay by draft-horses); added to the raw material to produce a physical modification (chlorine to unbleached linen, coal to iron, dye to wool); or help accomplish the work itself (materials for heating and lighting). The distinction between principal substance and accessory "vanishes in the chemical industries proper," where none of the raw material reappears in its original composition in the product. Marx cites Storch (who distinguishes matière from materiaux) and Cherbuliez (who calls accessories matières instrumentales).

The Relativity of Functional Categories

Marx insists that any object can shift roles. Corn is raw material for millers, starch-manufacturers, distillers, cattle-breeders; it also enters its own production as seed. Coal emerges from mining as product and enters as means of production. An animal fattening for beef is simultaneously raw material and instrument (producing manure). A product ready for consumption can become raw material (grapes into wine) or be released only as raw material (cotton, thread, yarn — semi-manufactured, better described as "manufactured up to a certain level"). The decisive principle: "whether a use-value is to be regarded as raw material, as instrument of labour or as product is determined entirely by its specific function in the labour process, by the position it occupies there: as its position changes, so do its determining characteristics." These categories are therefore relational, not substantial — a point of great methodological importance.

The Extinguishing of Past Labour in Successful Production

When products enter as means of production into new labour processes, "they lose their character of being products and function only as objective factors contributing to living labour." A spinner treats spindles only as means for spinning, flax as material. In the process itself, the fact that they are products of past labour is "as irrelevant as, in the case of the digestive process, the fact that bread is the product of the previous labour of the farmer, the miller and the baker." On the contrary, it is "by their imperfections" that means of production reveal their past-labour character: "A knife which fails to cut, a piece of thread which keeps on snapping, forcibly remind us of Mr A, the cutler, or Mr B, the spinner. In a successful product, the role played by past labour in mediating its useful properties has been extinguished." This is a remarkable epistemic point: success conceals labour's history; failure reveals it.

Living Labour as Vivifier

A machine not active in the labour process is useless and falls prey to natural destruction: "Iron rusts; wood rots. Yarn with which we neither weave nor knit is cotton wasted." Living labour must "seize on these things, awaken them from the dead, change them from merely possible into real and effective use-values." Marx's near-lyrical language: "Bathed in the fire of labour, appropriated as part of its organism, and infused with vital energy for the performance of the functions appropriate to their concept and to their vocation in the process, they are indeed consumed, but to some purpose." Finished products thus are both results and conditions of the labour process; their contact with living labour is "the sole means by which they can be made to retain their character of use-values, and be realized."

Productive vs Individual Consumption

Labour "uses up its material elements... consumes them, and is therefore a process of consumption." Marx distinguishes productive consumption (using up products as means of subsistence for labour) from individual consumption (using up products as means of subsistence for the living individual). The product of individual consumption is the consumer himself; the result of productive consumption is "a product distinct from the consumer." Labour consumes products to create products — turning one set of products into means of production for another. And even now, production still employs many means provided directly by nature, "not representing any combination of natural substances with human labour."

The Labour Process as Universal Condition

Marx summarizes his abstraction: the labour process, in its "simple and abstract elements," is "purposeful activity aimed at the production of use-values... an appropriation of what exists in nature for the requirements of man. It is the universal condition for the metabolic interaction between man and nature, the everlasting nature-imposed condition of human existence, and it is therefore independent of every form of that existence, or rather it is common to all forms of society in which human beings live." This is the theoretical payoff of the abstraction. He then explicitly notes what he has not introduced: the worker's relationship to other workers. It sufficed to present "man and his labour on one side, nature and its materials on the other." His memorable illustration: "The taste of porridge does not tell us who grew the oats, and the process we have presented does not reveal the conditions under which it takes place, whether it is happening under the slave-owner's brutal lash or the anxious eye of the capitalist, whether Cincinnatus undertakes it in tilling his couple of acres, or a savage, when he lays low a wild beast with a stone."

This last example invites Marx's delicious polemical footnote targeting Colonel Torrens, who claimed to find "the origin of capital" in the first stone the savage flings at his prey, or the first stick used to strike down fruit. Marx ironizes: "No doubt this 'first stick' [Stock] would also explain why 'stock' in English is synonymous with capital." This is the chapter's first direct assault on the capitalist apologetics that treat capital as a natural, trans-historical category rather than a social relation.

The Capitalist's Two Characteristic Phenomena

Marx now "returns to our would-be capitalist," who has purchased all the factors of production. He has selected them "with the keen eye of an expert." He proceeds to consume labour-power by causing the worker to consume the means of production. Marx reiterates that "the general character of the labour process is evidently not changed" by the fact that the worker works for the capitalist. The methods of bootmaking or spinning are "not immediately altered by the intervention of the capitalist." He must "begin by taking the labour-power as he finds it in the market" — labour of a kind that arose "in a period when there were as yet no capitalists." Marx signals that the transformation of the mode of production itself (via subordination to capital) "can only occur later on."

The labour process, when it becomes the capitalist's consumption of labour-power, exhibits two characteristic phenomena:

  1. The worker works under the control of the capitalist, to whom his labour belongs. The capitalist ensures the work is done properly, that means of production are applied directly to purpose, raw material is not wasted, and instruments are spared (worn only as necessary).
  2. The product is the property of the capitalist, not the worker. By paying for a day's labour-power, the capitalist has the right to use it for a day — "just as much as the right to use any other commodity, such as a horse he had hired for the day." From the moment the worker "steps into the workshop," the use-value of his labour-power — and therefore its use, which is labour — belongs to the capitalist. The capitalist incorporates labour "as a living agent of fermentation" into the lifeless constituents of the product, which also belong to him. The product belongs to him "just as much as the wine which is the product of the process of fermentation going on in his cellar."

Marx supports this with a battery of citations: Cherbuliez ("Products are appropriated before they are transformed into capital"), the same author on the law of appropriation whose "fundamental principle was the exact opposite, namely that every worker has an exclusive right to the ownership of what he produces," and James Mill (the term "capital" as commonly used absurdly "includes labour and capital both"). These citations establish that the appropriation of the product by the capitalist follows rigorously from the law of commodity exchange — not from theft — even though it inverts the classical labour-theory-of-property principle.

2.2 The Valorization Process

Two Objectives of the Capitalist

Marx now shifts register. The product — the capitalist's property — is a use-value (yarn or boots). "But although boots are, to some extent, the basis of social progress... he does not manufacture boots for their own sake. Use-value is certainly not la chose qu'on aime pour lui-même in the production of commodities." Use-values are produced "only because and in so far as they form the material substratum of exchange-value." The capitalist has two objectives: (1) to produce a use-value that has exchange-value, i.e., an article destined for sale, a commodity; and (2) to produce a commodity greater in value than the sum of the values of the commodities used to produce it (means of production plus labour-power). "His aim is to produce not only a use-value, but a commodity; not only use-value, but value; and not just value, but also surplus-value."

The Unity of Labour Process and Value-Creation

Marx signals the structure of the coming analysis: "Just as the commodity itself is a unity formed of use-value and value, so the process of production must be a unity, composed of the labour process and the process of creating value [Wertbildungsprozess]." Thus the same process has two aspects, mirroring the twofold nature of the commodity established in Chapter 1.

The Value Calculation: The Spinning Example

Marx now performs his meticulous numerical demonstration. He assumes the production of yarn, with these figures:

  • Raw material: 10 lb. of cotton, bought at its full value = 10 shillings (this price already expresses the labour needed to produce the cotton in terms of average social labour).
  • Instrument of labour: wear and tear of the spindle = 2 shillings.
  • Labour-time necessary to produce the gold representing 12 shillings: 24 hours (two working days).

From these, the value of the yarn: two days of labour are objectified in 12 shillings' worth of constant capital (cotton + spindle). The value of the product must include both the raw material and the worn portion of the spindle, because "the labour-time required for the production of the cotton... is part of the labour necessary to produce the yarn, and is therefore contained in the yarn," and "the same applies to the labour embodied in the spindle." Marx cites Ricardo's authority: "Not only the labour applied immediately to commodities affects their value, but the labour also which is bestowed on the implements, tools, and buildings with which such labour is assisted."

The Transmigration of Value into the Product

Marx insists that despite the transformation of forms (cotton into yarn, spindle worn away and disappeared), the value is unaffected: "The spindle and cotton, instead of resting quietly side by side, join together in the process, their forms are altered, and they are turned into yarn; but their value is no more affected by this fact than it would be if they had been simply exchanged for their equivalent in yarn." This is a crucial conservation-of-value principle: value does not originate in the labour process as such, but is transferred — the means of production preserve their value by reappearing in the product. Using an elegant grammatical metaphor, Marx notes that all the labour contained in the yarn is past labour; the labour producing the constituent elements lies "further back in the past" than the spinning, standing "as it were, in the pluperfect, the latter in the perfect tense, but this does not matter." His house example: if thirty days of labour are needed to build a house, the total labour incorporated in it is not altered by the fact that the work of the last day was done 29 days later than that of the first. "Therefore the labour contained in the raw material and instruments of labour can be treated just as if it were labour expended in an earlier stage of the spinning process, before the labour finally added in the form of actual spinning."

The Two Conditions for Value Transfer

Marx adds two conditions for the means of production to transfer value properly:

  1. The means of production must genuinely have served to produce a use-value — they must become yarn. "Value is independent of the particular use-value by which it is borne, but a use-value of some kind has to act as its bearer." If they do not enter into a real use-value, their value is not transferred.
  2. The labour-time expended must not exceed what is socially necessary under given social conditions of production. If no more than 1 lb. of cotton is needed to spin 1 lb. of yarn, no more may be consumed. If the capitalist has "a foible for using golden spindles instead of steel ones, the only labour that counts for anything in the value of the yarn remains that which would be required to produce a steel spindle, because no more is necessary under the given social conditions."

Labour as Value-Creating vs Labour as Useful Activity

Marx now distinguishes two standpoints on the spinner's labour. In the labour process, we viewed it as "the activity which has the purpose of changing cotton into yarn" — a specifically qualitative activity, differing from other kinds of productive labour, revealed subjectively (purpose of spinning) and objectively (special operations, special means of production, special use-value of product). Cotton and spindles are necessities for spinning but useless for making rifled cannon.

In the value-creating process, on the contrary, the spinner's labour differs "in no respect from the labour of the man who bores cannon, or... from the labour of the cotton-planter and the spindle-maker." It is only by reason of this identity (abstract, homogeneous human labour) that cotton-planting, spindle-making, and spinning can form the component parts of one whole — the value of the yarn. Here we are "no longer concerned with the quality, the character and the content of the labour, but merely with its quantity."

The Transformation of Labour from Unrest to Being

During the labour process, the worker's labour "constantly undergoes a transformation, from the form of unrest [Unruhe] into that of being [Sein], from the form of motion [Bewegung] into that of objectivity [Gegenständlichkeit]." At the end of one hour, "the spinning motion is represented in a certain quantity of yarn; in other words, a definite quantity of labour, namely that of one hour, has been objectified in the cotton." Note the terminology: labour, not spinning labour, because spinning counts here only as "the expenditure of labour-power in general."

The Socially Necessary Condition Maintained

Again, Marx stresses the socially necessary condition: no more time may be consumed than is necessary under given social conditions. If x pounds of cotton are made into y pounds of yarn in one hour, then a day's labour does not count as 12 hours' labour unless 12x lb. of cotton have been made into 12y lb. of yarn; "for only socially necessary labour-time counts towards the creation of value."

Raw Material and Product in a New Light

Under the value-creating aspect, raw material "merely serves to absorb a definite quantity of labour"; by being soaked in labour, it is changed into yarn. The product (yarn) is "now nothing more than a measure of the labour absorbed by the cotton." If 1⅔ lb. of cotton can be spun into 1⅔ lb. of yarn per hour, then 10 lb. of yarn indicates the absorption of 6 hours of labour. Definite quantities of product now represent "nothing but definite quantities of labour, definite masses of crystallized labour-time" — "the material shape taken by a given number of hours or days of social labour." Marx notes the irrelevance of the specific character (spinning, cotton, yarn): if the worker were in a coal-mine, a definite quantity of coal would represent a definite quantity of absorbed labour.

The Value of Labour-Power Assumed

Marx now assumes the value of a day's labour-power is 3 shillings, and that 6 hours of labour are incorporated in that sum (the amount needed to produce the worker's average daily means of subsistence). If the spinner converts 1⅔ lb. of cotton per hour, then in 6 hours he converts 10 lb. of cotton into 10 lb. of yarn. During the spinning process, the cotton absorbs 6 hours of labour. The same quantity of labour is embodied in a piece of gold worth 3 shillings. "A value of 3 shillings, therefore, is added to the cotton by the labour of spinning."

The Total Value of the Product — and the Absence of Surplus

Now: the total value of 10 lb. of yarn. Two and a half days of labour are objectified in it: two days contained in the cotton and worn spindle, half a day absorbed during spinning. Two and a half days = 15 shillings. So 15 shillings is an adequate price for 10 lb. of yarn, and the price per pound is 1s. 6d.

At this point Marx returns to the capitalist — and here the drama peaks. "Our capitalist stares in astonishment. The value of the product is equal to the value of the capital advanced. The value advanced has not been valorized, no surplus-value has been created, and consequently money has not been transformed into capital." The 15 shillings spent (10 for cotton, 2 for spindle wear, 3 for labour-power) equal the 15 shillings' worth produced. "The swollen value of the yarn is of no avail, for it is merely the sum of the values formerly existing in the cotton, the spindle and the labour-power: out of such a simple addition of existing values, no surplus-value can possibly arise." These values are concentrated in one thing, but so they were "in the sum of 15 shillings, before it was split up into three parts by the purchase of the commodities."

Marx gives a mundane illustration: "whether a man buys his house ready built, or has it built for him, neither of these operations will increase the amount of money laid out on the house." He also footnotes the Physiocrats, who held that all non-agricultural labour is unproductive — a doctrine this result would seem to confirm ("For the professional economist it is irrefutable"), quoting Mercier de la Rivière: addition is not multiplication. This is a dialectical setup: Marx demonstrates the Physiocrat conclusion holds if we assume the value of labour-power equals the value it creates — the very assumption he is about to shatter.

The Capitalist's Apologetic Interlude

Marx now has the capitalist resort to every "moral" and apologetic argument, satirizing them all:

  • "Intention": The capitalist says he intended to make more money. Marx comments: "The road to hell is paved with good intentions, and he might just as well have intended to make money without producing at all." He threatens to buy commodities in the market instead of manufacturing them — but "if all his brother capitalists were to do the same, where would he find his commodities?" And "he cannot eat his money." Footnote: capitalists have withdrawn capital from production to gamble (railways 1844–47, Liverpool cotton exchange during the American Civil War) — the practical refutation of the "intention" argument.
  • "Abstinence": The capitalist recites the catechism "Consider my abstinence. I might have squandered the 15 shillings, but instead I consumed it productively and made yarn with it." Marx mocks: "Very true; and as a reward he is now in possession of good yarn instead of a bad conscience." He adds: "whatever the merits of his abstinence there is no money there to recompense him, because the value of the product is merely the sum of the values thrown into the process... Let him therefore console himself with the reflection that virtue is its own reward." Marx jibes at MacCulloch, whose "proven remedy" for over-production was to tell capitalists to produce only what they need themselves.
  • "Service": The capitalist protests that he provided materials, instruments, and the worker's means of subsistence, so surely he deserves a return. Marx rebuts: has the worker not performed an equivalent service by turning cotton and spindle into yarn? "In any case, here the question of service does not arise. A service is nothing other than the useful effect of a use-value." He footnotes Luther's blistering satire on "service" (adulterers do each other great service; a horseman serves a robber; "the devil himself does his servants a great, inestimable service"). The productive-contribution argument collapses: the capitalist paid 3 shillings and received an exact equivalent of 3 shillings in value added.
  • "Superintendence labour": The capitalist asks, "Have I myself not worked?... does not this labour, too, create value?" His own overseer and manager "shrug their shoulders." Marx notes drolly: "with a hearty laugh, he recovers his composure. The whole litany he has just recited was simply meant to pull the wool over our eyes... He leaves this and all similar subterfuges and conjuring tricks to the professors of political economy, who are paid for it. He himself is a practical man, and although he does not always consider what he says outside his business, within his business he knows what he is doing."

The Real Solution: The Value of Labour-Power vs the Value it Creates

Now Marx delivers the decisive argument. The value of a day's labour-power is 3 shillings, because "half a day's labour is objectified in that quantity of labour-power" — the means of subsistence required daily for producing labour-power cost half a day's labour. But: "the past labour embodied in the labour-power and the living labour it can perform, and the daily cost of maintaining labour-power and its daily expenditure in work, are two totally different things. The former determines the exchange-value of the labour-power, the latter is its use-value." The fact that half a day's labour keeps the worker alive for 24 hours "does not in any way prevent him from working a whole day." Therefore "the value of labour-power, and the value which that labour-power valorizes [verwertet] in the labour-process, are two entirely different magnitudes; and this difference was what the capitalist had in mind when he was purchasing the labour-power."

This is the kernel of the entire chapter. The useful quality of labour-power (that it makes yarn or boots) was to the capitalist "merely the necessary condition for his activity." What was really decisive was "the specific use-value which this commodity possesses of being a source not only of value, but of more value than it has itself. This is the specific service the capitalist expects from labour-power, and in this transaction he acts in accordance with the eternal laws of commodity-exchange." The seller of labour-power "realizes its exchange-value, and alienates its use-value." He cannot take the one without giving the other. The use-value of labour-power belongs as little to its seller as the use-value of sold oil belongs to the dealer. The buyer has paid for a day's labour-power and therefore has the use of it for a day. "On the one hand the daily sustenance of labour-power costs only half a day's labour, while on the other hand the very same labour-power can remain effective, can work, during a whole day, and consequently the value which its use during one day creates is double what the capitalist pays for that use; this circumstance is a piece of good luck for the buyer, but by no means an injustice towards the seller." — This last clause is the chapter's most explosive ideological move: the exploitation of labour is not a violation of market justice but its perfect realization.

The Extended Labour Process

"Our capitalist foresaw this situation, and that was the cause of his laughter." The worker now finds "the means of production necessary for working not just 6 but 12 hours." If 10 lb. of cotton absorbed 6 hours, now 20 lb. absorbs 12 hours and becomes 20 lb. of yarn. Now five days of labour are objectified in the 20 lb. of yarn: four days due to cotton and the lost steel of the spindle, the remaining day absorbed during spinning. Expressed in gold, five days = 30 shillings. That is the price of 20 lb. of yarn (still 1s. 6d. per lb.). But the sum of values of the commodities thrown into the process = 27 shillings. The value of the yarn = 30 shillings. "Therefore the value of the product is one-ninth greater than the value advanced to produce it; 27 shillings have turned into 30 shillings; a surplus-value of 3 shillings has been precipitated. The trick has at last worked: money has been transformed into capital."

The Theoretical Reconciliation

Marx savors the elegance: "Every condition of the problem is satisfied, while the laws governing the exchange of commodities have not been violated in any way. Equivalent has been exchanged for equivalent." The capitalist paid full value for cotton, spindle, and labour-power. He then did what every purchaser does: consumed the use-value. The process yielded 20 lb. of yarn worth 30 shillings. The capitalist, "formerly a buyer, now returns to the market as a seller," sells at its exact value — and yet withdraws 3 shillings more than he put in. "This whole course of events, the transformation of money into capital, both takes place and does not take place in the sphere of circulation." It takes place through circulation (because conditioned by the purchase of labour-power in the market); it does not take place in circulation (because what happens there is only an introduction to the valorization process, which is entirely confined to the sphere of production). "And so 'everything is for the best in the best of all possible worlds.'" (A Voltairean allusion to Candide — Marx's irony at the expense of Panglossian optimism about the market.)

Value as a Self-Expanding Subject

Marx summarizes: by turning money into commodities that serve as building materials for a new product and as factors in the labour process, "by incorporating living labour into their lifeless objectivity, the capitalist simultaneously transforms value, i.e. past labour in its objectified and lifeless form, into capital, value which can perform its own valorization process, an animated monster which begins to 'work', 'as if its body were by love possessed.'" (The Goethe quotation from Faust.) This is a key philosophical culmination: value itself becomes an active subject, a self-moving, self-valorizing entity — capital as "M–C–M′" personified. It also carries a strong Burkean-Gothic flavor: capital is an "animated monster" — an undead force driven by a vampiric compulsion.

Value-Creation vs Valorization

Marx now clarifies the relation between the two processes: "If we now compare the process of creating value with the process of valorization, we see that the latter is nothing but the continuation of the former beyond a definite point. If the process is not carried beyond the point where the value paid by the capitalist for the labour-power is replaced by an exact equivalent, it is simply a process of creating value; but if it is continued beyond that point, it becomes a process of valorization." So the two are not distinct processes but phases of one process, separated by a threshold — the point at which the value equivalent to labour-power has been reproduced.

Value-Creation vs Labour Process

Then: "If we proceed further, and compare the process of creating value with the labour process, we find that the latter consists in the useful labour which produces use-values. Here the movement of production is viewed qualitatively... But if it is viewed as a value-creating process the same labour process appears only quantitatively." In the value-creating view, commodities no longer count as functionally determined and material elements on which labour-power acts with a purpose; "they count merely as definite quantities of objectified labour." Whether the labour was already in the means of production or has just been added, "that labour counts only according to its duration. It amounts to so many hours, or days, etc."

The Socially Necessary Conditions Restated

Marx reiterates: "the time spent in production counts only in so far as it is socially necessary for the production of a use-value." This has several consequences:

  1. Normal objective conditions: Labour-power must be functioning under normal conditions. If a self-acting mule is the socially predominant instrument, it is "impermissible to supply the spinner with a spinning-wheel." The cotton must be of suitable quality. Otherwise the spinner spends more than socially necessary time, and the excess "would create neither value nor money." Whether the objective factors are normal "does not depend on the worker, but rather on the capitalist."
  2. Normal subjective conditions: Labour-power must itself be of normal effectiveness — average skill, dexterity, and speed for that trade; capitalist took care to buy it so. It must be expended with average exertion and usual intensity. The capitalist is "as careful to see that this is done, as he is to ensure that his workmen are not idle for a single moment. He has bought the use of the labour-power for a definite period, and he insists on his rights. He has no intention of being robbed."
  3. No wasteful consumption: All wasteful consumption of raw material or instruments is strictly forbidden — "our friend has a penal code of his own" — because what is wasted represents "a superfluous expenditure of quantities of objectified labour, labour that does not count in the product or enter into its value." Marx footnotes the case of slavery: the slave distinguishes himself as instrumentum vocale, an animal as instrumentum semi-vocale, and a lifeless implement as instrumentum mutum (Varro). Under slavery, only the "rudest and heaviest implements" are used because slaves damage them deliberately; this is confirmed by Cairnes (The Slave Power) and Olmsted (A Journey in the Seaboard Slave States), who describes the clumsy tools and the mule-over-horse preference (mules "will bear cudgelling... and they do not take cold or get sick, if neglected or overworked").

The Two Unities

Marx draws the synthesis: "The difference between labour, considered on the one hand as producing utilities, and on the other hand as creating value, a difference which we discovered by our analysis of a commodity, resolves itself into a distinction between two aspects of the production process." He gives two definitive definitions:

"The production process, considered as the unity of the labour process and the process of creating value, is the process of production of commodities; considered as the unity of the labour process and the process of valorization, it is the capitalist process of production, or the capitalist form of the production of commodities."

This is the chapter's structural climax — the pairing maps perfectly onto the commodity's dual unity, closing the loop opened in Chapter 1.

Skilled vs Simple Labour — the Final Nuance

The chapter closes with an important qualification. Marx earlier assumed the labour appropriated was simple, average social labour. But the argument holds equally for more complex labour. Higher, more complicated labour is "expenditure of labour-power of a more costly kind, labour-power whose production has cost more time and labour... and which therefore has a higher value." Being of higher value, it expresses itself in higher sorts of labour, and so objectifies in proportionally higher values during equal time. "Whatever difference in skill there may be between the labour of a spinner and that of a jeweller, the portion of his labour by which the jeweller merely replaces the value of his own labour-power does not in any way differ in quality from the additional portion by which he creates surplus-value. In both cases, the surplus-value results only from a quantitative excess of labour, from a lengthening of one and the same labour-process."

An extensive footnote unpacks why "skilled" and "unskilled" are shaky categories: partly "pure illusion" or "traditional convention," partly the product of the "helpless condition of some sections of the working class." Accidental circumstances cause the two forms sometimes to change places. Where the working class's physique has deteriorated (as in highly developed capitalist countries), heavy-muscle labour is considered "higher" and delicate labour sinks to "simple" (bricklaying vs damask-weaving; the fustian-cutter's labour counts as simple despite greater exertion). Marx cites Laing's estimate that in England and Wales, 11,300,000 people lived on unskilled labour (out of 18 million), with Laing's numbers criticized for including better-paid factory workers and middle-class categories to swell the "skilled" side. James Mill: "The great class who have nothing to give for food but ordinary labour, are the great bulk of the people."

The final technical point: "in every process of creating value the reduction of the higher type of labour to average social labour, for instance one day of the former to x days of the latter, is unavoidable." Marx therefore "saves himself a superfluous operation" by assuming the worker's labour is average simple labour. His closing footnote cites Cazenove: "Where reference is made to labour as a measure of value, it necessarily implies labour of one particular kind... the proportion which the other kinds bear to it being easily ascertained." The reduction of complex labour to simple is thus treated as a technical operation of value-theory, not a claim about the dignity or difficulty of work — a subtle but necessary component of the value-creating analysis.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter yields a set of principles that operate at every level of Marx's system. First, the principle of the two aspects: the same labour process is simultaneously qualitative (creating use-values) and quantitative (creating value). This resolves the apparent contradiction of a production process that both transfers old value and creates new value — the value of the means of production is preserved in the product, while the living labour adds a new quantum. Second, the principle of the threshold: value-creation becomes valorization the moment labour is extended beyond the point at which it has reproduced the equivalent of its own price. Surplus-value is thus not a discrete "thing" produced at some point in the day, but a quantitative excess that accrues continuously after the threshold is crossed. Third, the principle of the conservation and transfer of value — Marx's answer to the Physiocrats: production adds nothing to the value of the means of production except what living labour adds; the rest is transferred, not created. Fourth, the principle of exchange-conformity: capital's exploitation of labour does not require breaking the laws of the market. It is realised through fair exchange, not despite it.

The broader philosophical implication is Marx's materialist anthropology: labour is the "everlasting nature-imposed condition of human existence," the universal metabolism between humanity and nature. This trans-historical baseline allows Marx to make a devastating critique of apologetic political economy, which takes the specific historical form of the labour process (capitalist control, property in product, surplus extraction) and presents it as natural and universal. By contrast, Marx shows that the form of the labour process under capital — supervision, discipline, product appropriation, the extraction of surplus beyond the value paid for labour-power — is a historically specific shape imposed on a universal activity. The "practical man" of the chapter understands what the "professors of political economy" are paid to obscure.

The chapter also establishes the ideological mechanism of capitalism. The capitalist's apologies (intention, abstinence, service, superintendence labour) are all shown to be incoherent, yet they survive because the operation of the market presents exploitation as fair exchange. The "good luck" of the buyer is not an injustice to the seller — and it is precisely this legal-ethical shield that makes the extraction of surplus-value appear natural, indeed just. The vampire image — capital as an "animated monster" driven by a self-expanding compulsion — announces the central theme of the rest of Capital: the self-valorization of value as a social force that subordinates living labour to its own dead logic.

Practically, the chapter's conclusions reverberate through the entire political economy that follows. The length of the working day becomes a contested terrain (Parts Three and Eight, on absolute surplus-value and the factory legislation), because the only way to increase surplus without raising productivity is to lengthen the day. The value of labour-power becomes the pivot of class struggle, because any cheapening of the worker's subsistence (or any intensification of work without compensation) shifts the threshold in capital's favor. And the socially necessary labour-time standard becomes the normative yardstick against which both productivity and exploitation are measured. In short, Chapter 7 is where Marx converts the abstract logic of value into a living conflict: between the capitalist, who buys labour-power to consume it for a day, and the worker, who sells it but retains the human capacity to resist its full expenditure. The chapter that begins with the architect's ideal plan and the bee's instinctive construction ends with the discovery that the very same human purposiveness that distinguishes labour is what capital captures, commercializes, and turns against the labourer. That paradox is the engine of the rest of Marx's critique.
