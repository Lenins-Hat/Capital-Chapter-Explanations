Volume 1 • Chapter 8: Constant Capital and Variable Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 8 of Capital, Volume One marks the precise moment where Marx's discovery of the twofold character of labour — laid out in Chapter 7 — crystallizes into the analytical apparatus that will govern the rest of the entire work: the distinction between constant capital and variable capital. The chapter's central problem is deceptively simple but conceptually treacherous: how is it that the same act of labour both adds new value to a product and simultaneously preserves the old value of the means of production consumed in making it?

Marx opens by observing that the various factors of the labour process "play different parts in forming the value of the product." The worker's living labour adds fresh value; the means of production (raw cotton, spindles, machinery) have their values preserved and transferred into the product. The intellectual challenge the chapter solves is explaining this twofold result without invoking two separate acts of labour — the worker, Marx insists, "does not perform two pieces of work simultaneously." The unifying solution is that labour possesses two properties inhering in a single indivisible act: as abstract social labour it creates new value; as concrete, useful labour (spinning, weaving, forging) it transfers and preserves the value of the means of production.

This distinction has a two-tier architecture. First, Marx rigorously demonstrates the mechanics of value transfer: means of production never transfer more value than they themselves possess or lose; free gifts of nature (land, wind, water, virgin timber) transfer nothing because they embody no labour. He introduces several celebrated analytical devices — the "metempsychosis" of value, the difference between entering a process "as a whole" versus "piece by piece," the "'devil's dust'" of cotton waste — to show how the labour process (production of use-values) and the valorization process (production of value) can diverge within the identical act.

Second, he translates these functional distinctions into the categorical vocabulary of capital itself. That portion of capital embodied in means of production — raw material, auxiliary material, instruments of labour — "does not undergo any quantitative alteration of value in the process of production," and is therefore named constant capital. That portion laid out in labour-power does alter its value: it reproduces its own equivalent and produces a surplus, "which may itself vary." Hence variable capital — the part "continually being transformed from a constant into a variable magnitude."

The chapter's intellectual objective is thus to give capitalism's value-generating mechanism a scientific anatomy. It fits into the broader architecture of Capital as the hinge between the abstract theory of value (Parts I–II) and the concrete analysis of surplus-value extraction, the working day, and the composition of capital. Critically, Marx pre-emptively wards off misreadings: the term "constant" does not deny that the prices of means of production may change; it only asserts that such changes originate outside the production process in which they serve. The chapter closes by insisting that even a vast revolution in the technical ratio of constant to variable capital "has not in the least degree affected the essential difference between the two" — a forward-looking gesture toward the organic composition of capital and the falling rate of profit. The whole argument is repeatedly sharpened against bourgeois apologists — J. B. Say and Wilhelm Roscher — who attempt to derive surplus-value from the "productive services" of nature and materials, a mystification Marx treats with withering sarcasm.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Problem: Two Results from One Labour

Marx begins (p. 307) with an observation that is simultaneously economic and philosophical: the "various factors of the labour process play different parts in forming the value of the product." On the one hand the worker — spinner, weaver, smith — "adds fresh value to the material of his labour by expending on it a given amount of additional labour," irrespective of the specific content and purpose of that labour. On the other hand, the values of the means of production — cotton and spindle — "re-appear again in the value of the yarn."

This is the phenomenon of value transfer: "The value of the means of production is therefore preserved by being transferred to the product." Marx specifies that this transfer occurs during the conversion of means into product — that is, during the labour process — and that it is "mediated through labour." The word mediated is doing heavy lifting here: it flags that labour is not merely the cause of transfer but the vehicle through which dead past labour (crystallized in materials) re-enters living circulation.

The immediate analytical puzzle: the worker does not perform two separate acts. He does not spin to add value and then separately work "to preserve the value of the means of production." And yet two entirely distinct results emerge. The conclusion Marx draws is that "this twofold nature of the result can be explained only by the twofold nature of his labour; it must at the same time create value through one of its properties and preserve or transfer value through another." This is the load-bearing move of the chapter — a direct application of the twofold-labour analysis established in Chapter 7. Concrete useful labour (the specific, purposeful form — spinning, weaving, forging) is the property that transfers and preserves value; abstract social labour (expenditure of human labour-power in general, measured by duration) is the property that creates new value.

The Mechanics of Transfer: Productive Form as the Condition

On p. 308, Marx makes the mechanism explicit. How does any worker add fresh labour-time and fresh value? "Evidently, only by working productively in a particular way." The spinner adds value by spinning, the weaver by weaving, the smith by forging. These operations "add labour as such, and therefore new values" — but here comes the crucial qualification: it is "only through the agency of labour directed to a particular purpose" that cotton, yarn, iron, etc., "become constituent elements of the product, of a new use-value." The old form of use-value disappears but is "taken up again in a new form of use-value."

Marx then invokes the conclusion of the earlier analysis of value-creation: if a use-value is effectively consumed in producing a new use-value, then the quantity of labour needed to produce the consumed article forms part of the labour necessary to produce the new use-value; "this portion is therefore labour transferred from the means of production to the new product." The decisive formulation follows: the worker "preserves the values of the already consumed means of production or transfers them to the product as portions of its value, not by virtue of his additional labour as such, but by virtue of the particular useful character of that labour, by virtue of its specific productive form."

This is the chapter's conceptual hinge, and Marx reinforces it with a vivid image: concrete, purposeful labour "raises the means of production from the dead merely by entering into contact with them, infuses them with life so that they become factors of the labour process, and combines with them to form new products." The counterfactual proof is elegant: "If the specific productive labour of the worker were not spinning, he could not convert the cotton into yarn, and therefore he could not transfer the values of the cotton and spindle to the yarn." A spinner who switched to joinery would still add value by a day's labour — but he would no longer transfer the value of cotton into yarn, because he would no longer be spinning. The concrete form is what determines which use-values get transformed and therefore which values get transferred; the abstract form is what determines how much new value is added, since the value added depends only on the duration of the labour, not its content.

Marx crystallizes this in the summary passage: "it is by virtue of its general character as expenditure of human labour-power in the abstract that spinning adds new value...; and on the other hand, it is by virtue of its special character as a concrete, useful process that the same labour of spinning both transfers the values of the means of production to the product and preserves them in the product." Footnote 1 cites an 1821 tract: "Labour gives a new creation for one extinguished" — a neat pre-Marxian intuition of the phenomenon.

Four Thought-Experiments in Value and Productivity

Marx then conducts four controlled variations to prove that the two properties of labour — preserving and creating value — are "different in their very essence" (p. 309).

Experiment 1 — Rising productivity. Suppose an invention allows the spinner to spin in 6 hours as much cotton as before in 36 hours. His labour is now six times as effective as useful productive activity. The product rises sixfold, from 6 lb. to 36 lb. of yarn. But the new labour absorbed by each pound of cotton falls to one-sixth of the former amount, so the value added per pound is one-sixth of what it was. Conversely, the value transferred from the cotton to the product is six times as great, because six times as much cotton has been worked up. Thus the same 6 hours of spinning, within one indivisible process, transfers six times as much preserved value while adding one-sixth as much new value per pound. The two functions move in opposite directions — proof of their distinct essence. Marx states the general law: the longer the time needed to spin a given weight of cotton, the greater the fresh value added; the greater the weight spun in a given time, the greater the value preserved by transfer.

Experiment 2 — Changing value of raw material. Productivity is held constant; the spinner still needs the same time per pound; but the exchange-value of cotton rises sixfold or falls to one-sixth. The spinner puts the same quantity of labour into the pound and adds the same value. Yet the value transferred from the cotton to the yarn is now either six times or one-sixth as much. The same result holds if the value of the instruments of labour rises or falls "while their usefulness in the labour process remains unaltered."

Experiment 3 — Unchanged conditions. If technical conditions and the values of means of production are constant, the spinner consumes in equal working-times equal quantities of material and machinery of unvarying value. Then "the value preserved in the product is directly proportional to the new value added to the product." In two weeks he adds twice as much value as in one and preserves twice as much. But Marx immediately qualifies: "this does not happen because he adds new value, but because the addition of new value takes place under unvaried conditions which are independent of his own labour." This is an important anti-teleological caveat — the correlation between added and preserved value is not causal but contingent on unchanged external conditions.

Experiment 4 — Labour-time proportionality. Despite all variation, Marx concedes a relative sense in which the worker "always preserves old value in proportion to the added quantity of new value": whether cotton's value rises to two shillings or falls to sixpence, the worker preserves half as much value in one hour as in two. The proportionality is to labour-time, not to productivity or value levels.

Use-Value as the Substratum of Value

Before proceeding, Marx inserts a philosophical reminder (pp. 310–11): "Value exists only in use-values, in things, if we leave aside its purely symbolic representation in tokens." Even man, "viewed merely as the physical existence of labour-power," is "a natural object, a thing, although a living, conscious thing." He notes the German phrase dingliche Äusserung — the physical, thing-like manifestation of labour-power. The consequence: if an article loses its use-value, it also loses its value. Means of production escape this fate because they "lose in the labour process the original form of their use-value only to assume in the product the form of a new use-value." Yet what particular object serves as value's bearer "is still a matter of complete indifference." The general law: "in the labour process the means of production transfer their value to the product only in so far as they lose their exchange-value along with their independent use-value. They only give up to the product the value they themselves lose as means of production."

Divergent Behaviours: Auxiliary Materials vs. Instruments

But the objective factors "do not all behave in the same way" (p. 311). Marx distinguishes three types:

  • Coal and lubricating oil: vanish entirely, leaving no trace.
  • Dyestuffs and auxiliary substances: vanish as independent entities but re-appear in the properties of the product.
  • Raw material: forms the substance of the product, but only after a change of form.

All three — raw material and auxiliary substances — lose the independent form with which they entered the labour process.

Instruments of labour behave differently. Tools, machines, factory buildings, containers are useful "only as long as they keep their original shape." During their lifetime they retain their shape independently of the product, and so too after death: "The mortal remains of machines, tools, workshops etc., always continue to lead an existence distinct from that of the product they helped to turn out." Over the whole period of service, the instrument's use-value is completely consumed and its exchange-value completely transferred. A spinning machine lasting ten years transfers its total value gradually to the product of ten years. Its lifetime is "spent in the repetition of a greater or lesser number of similar operations." The famous analogy: "The instrument suffers the same fate as the man." Every day brings a man twenty-four hours nearer the grave, yet life insurance companies use the theory of averages to draw "very accurate, and what is more, very profitable conclusions" — so too with machines.

Marx operationalizes this (p. 312): if a machine's use-value lasts six days, it loses one-sixth per day and parts with one-sixth of its value to each day's product. "The deterioration of all instruments, their daily loss of use-value, and the corresponding quantity of value they part with to the product, are accordingly calculated on this basis." This is the conceptual foundation of depreciation.

The Iron Ceiling on Value Transfer

From this Marx extracts a general law: "means of production never transfer more value to the product than they themselves lose during the labour process by the destruction of their own use-value." The corollary is decisive for the critique of capitalism: if an instrument "has no value to lose, i.e. if it is not the product of human labour, it transfers no value to the product." It helps create use-value without contributing to exchange-value. This applies to "all those means of production supplied by nature without human assistance, such as land, wind, water, metals in the form of ore, and timber in virgin forests."

The Two Divergences: Whole vs. Piece, Piece vs. Whole

Marx introduces "another interesting phenomenon" (p. 312). A machine worth £1,000 wears out in 1,000 days; one-thousandth of its value is transferred daily, yet the machine as a whole "continues to take part in the labour process, though with diminishing vitality." This gives the signature structural insight:

"one factor of the labour process, a means of production, continually enters as a whole into that process, while it only enters in parts into the valorization process. The distinction between the labour process and the valorization process is reflected here in their objective factors, in that one and the same means of production, in one and the same process of production, counts in its totality as an element in the labour process, but only piece by piece as an element in the creation of value."

Footnote 2 clarifies that repairs are excluded — a machine under repair "is no longer an instrument of labour, but its material"; work is done upon it, not with it. Marx permits assuming repair labour counts as part of original production labour, but the text deals only with "that kind of wear which cannot be repaired from time to time," wear that "little by little brings about death," quoting an anonymous writer on the knife that "is not worth a new blade." This same footnote contains a polemical aside against the author of Observations on Certain Verbal Disputes in Political Economy, who mocked Ricardo by arguing that the whole labour of the engineer making stocking-machines is contained in each pair of stockings. Marx concedes the author's confusion is justified only to the extent that "neither Ricardo nor any other economist before or since has accurately distinguished the two aspects of labour."

The Inverse Case: Devil's Dust

On p. 313 Marx presents the mirror image: a means of production "may enter as a whole into the valorization process, although it enters only piece by piece into the labour process." Example: in spinning, 15 lb. out of every 115 lb. of cotton becomes "'devil's dust'" — not yarn, but unavoidable waste. Yet the value of the 15 lb. is transferred to the yarn just as surely as that of the 100 lb. that become yarn substance. The reason is strictly functional: "The use-value of 15 lb. of cotton must vanish into dust before 100 lb. of yarn can be made. The destruction of this cotton is therefore a necessary condition for the production of the yarn. And because it is a necessary condition, and for no other reason, the value of that cotton is transferred to the product." The same applies to refuse generally; Marx notes Manchester machine-building factories where mountains of iron turnings are carted to the foundry at night, only to re-appear the next morning as solid iron.

The Quantitative Ceiling and the "Metempsychosis" of Value

Marx re-states the limit (p. 314): the maximum value loss is limited by the original value with which means entered, or "the labour-time required to produce them." Hence a machine costing £150 (or 500 days of labour) "cannot under any circumstances add more than £150 to the value of the product." Its value "is determined not by the labour process into which it enters as a means of production, but by that out of which it has issued as a product."

Footnote 3 is a substantial polemic. Marx demolishes J. B. Say's doctrine that surplus-value (interest, profit, rent) derives from the "services productifs" rendered by means of production (land, instruments, raw material) via their use-values. He mocks Roscher's citation of Say's oil-mill example: "The oil produced by the oil mill is indeed something very different from the labour expended in constructing the mill!" — a deliberate expose of Roscher's confusion between value and stuff (oil). Marx ridicules Roscher's "nature" as resembling "the foolish virgin who admitted she had had a child, but 'only a very little one.'" He then quotes Roscher's defence of the capitalist's "abstinence," describing it as an "anatomico-physiological method of political economy, which converts a mere 'demand' into a source of value!" This is a crucial theoretical boundary: nature and materials produce use-values but no exchange-value — a point that will become the axis of the critique of "productivity-of-capital" theories.

The chapter's key metaphor appears here: while productive labour changes means of production into constituents of a new product, "their value undergoes a metempsychosis. It deserts the consumed body to occupy the newly created one. But this transmigration takes place, as it were, behind the back of the actual labour in progress." The Pythagorean image is deliberate: value appears to migrate on its own, invisibly, while the living labour is busy doing something else.

The Gift of Nature and the Revelatory Function of Crisis

On p. 315 Marx draws out the consequence: the worker cannot add new labour without simultaneously preserving old values, because his labour must be of a useful kind, and useful labour requires employing products as means of production. Therefore:

"The property which labour-power in action, living labour, possesses of preserving value, at the same time that it adds it, is a gift of nature which costs the worker nothing, but is very advantageous to the capitalist since it preserves the existing value of his capital."

The phrase "gift of nature" is critical — value-preservation is a gratuitous service that capital appropriates without payment. And its concealed character is revealed by crisis:

"As long as trade is good, the capitalist is too absorbed in making profits to take notice of this gratuitous gift of labour. Violent interruptions of the labour process, crises, make him painfully aware of it."

Footnote 5 (The Times, 26 November 1862) gives a concrete indictment: a manufacturer with 800 workers complains of £6,000 annual overhead when the mill is idle, including £150 for coal and £1,200 depreciation, because "the weather and the natural principles of decay do not suspend their operations because the steam-engine ceases to revolve." Here Marx shows the living-labour dependence of capital's self-preservation: without labour's preservation function, capital decays.

Consumed Use-Value vs. Preserved Value

Marx refines the vocabulary (p. 315): as regards means of production, what is really consumed is use-value; labour's consumption of that use-value results in the product. There is "in fact no consumption of their value," so "it would therefore be inaccurate to say that it is reproduced. It is rather preserved." It is preserved not through any operation the means itself undergoes, but because the use-value in which it existed "vanishes (although when it vanishes, it does so into another use-value)." Footnote 6 cites S. P. Newman's Productive Consumption, confirming: "In these instances there is no consumption of value."

Then the key sentence (p. 316): "the value of the means of production re-appears in the value of the product, but it is not strictly speaking reproduced in that value. What is produced is a new use-value in which the old exchange-value re-appears." This terminology distinguishes value reproduction (genuine creation of new value) from value re-appearance (mere preservation by transfer).

The Subjective Factor: Labile Reproduction

"It is otherwise with the subjective factor of the labour process, labour-power, which sets itself in motion independently." While concrete labour preserves value, "at the same time, throughout every instant it is in motion, it is creating an additional value, a new value." Marx gives a concrete numeric illustration: suppose the process stops when the worker has produced an equivalent for his own labour-power — say six hours of labour adding three shillings. This three shillings is "the excess of the total value of the product over the portion of its value contributed by the means of production. It is the only original value formed during this process, the only portion of the value of the product created by the process itself." Marx concedes: this new value only replaces the money advanced for labour-power — the money the worker spends on subsistence — so it appears as mere reproduction. "Nevertheless, it is a real reproduction, and not, as in the case of the value of the means of production, simply an apparent one. The replacement of one value by another is here brought about by the creation of new value."

Footnote 7 lampoons F. Wayland's American compendium, whose slogan "'It matters not in what form capital re-appears'" leads to the absurd claim that the value of food and clothing "re-appears in that new vigour imparted to his body and mind, forming fresh capital." Marx retorts: what re-appears in the man's vigour is not the bread's price but its body-building substance; what re-appears in the value of the vigour is not the substance but the value. Cheap bread at half price would build just as much muscle — but not muscle of the same value. "This confusion of 'value' and 'vigour'... conceals an attempt, an inevitably vain attempt, to squeeze an explanation of surplus-value out of the mere re-appearance of pre-existing values." Here Marx intercepts a specifically crude variant of the same error he attacked in Say and Roscher: substituting physical productivity for value creation.

Surplus-Value and the Naming of the Categories

On p. 317 Marx shows why the labour process continues beyond the reproduction-equivalent. Six hours suffice to reproduce labour-power's value; but the process lasts twelve. "The activity of labour-power, therefore, not only reproduces its own value, but produces value over and above this. This surplus-value is the difference between the value of the product and the value of the elements consumed in the formation of the product, in other words the means of production and the labour-power."

And now the categorical consolidation — the moment the chapter names its subject:

"That part of capital, therefore, which is turned into means of production, i.e. the raw material, the auxiliary material and the instruments of labour, does not undergo any quantitative alteration of value in the process of production. For this reason, I call it the constant part of capital, or more briefly, constant capital."

"On the other hand, that part of capital which is turned into labour-power does undergo an alteration of value in the process of production. It both reproduces the equivalent of its own value and produces an excess, a surplus-value, which may itself vary, and be more or less according to circumstances. This part of capital is continually being transformed from a constant into a variable magnitude. I therefore call it the variable part of capital, or more briefly, variable capital."

The parallel structure is underscored: the same elements distinguishable as objective/subjective in the labour process are distinguishable as constant/variable in the valorization process. This is the taxonomy — c and v — that will structure the analysis of the rate of surplus-value (s/v) and organic composition (c/v) in later chapters.

The Value-Change Caveat

Marx immediately guards against a pervasive misreading (pp. 317–18): "The definition of constant capital given above by no means excludes the possibility of a change of value in its elements." Example: cotton rises from sixpence to a shilling a pound due to crop failure. Each pound bought at sixpence and worked up after the rise transfers a shilling's worth; cotton already spun and circulating as yarn similarly transfers twice its original value. "It is plain, however, that these changes of value are independent of the valorization of the cotton in the spinning process itself." If the old cotton had never been spun, it could have been resold at a shilling.

This isolates the source of change: the value change "originates not in the process in which the cotton plays the part of a means of production, and in which it therefore functions as constant capital, but in the process in which the cotton itself is produced." Marx leverages the point for an empirical aside: speculators, when sudden revaluations occur, "speculate in the raw material in its least worked-up form" — in cotton rather than yarn, yarn rather than cloth.

This grounds a crucial meta-claim about value theory: "The value of a commodity is certainly determined by the quantity of labour contained in it, but this quantity is itself socially determined." If the labour-time socially necessary for production alters — after a bad harvest a given weight of cotton represents more labour — this "reacts back on all the old commodities of the same type, because they are only individuals of the same species." Their value is measured by the labour socially necessary "under the social conditions existing at the time." Footnote 8 cites Le Trosne: "all products of the same kind form a single mass, and their price is determined in general and without regard to particular circumstances." This is the law of socially necessary labour-time restated in dynamic terms: capital's book-values are continuously revalued by changing social productivity — a harbinger of Marx's later treatment of moral depreciation and technical obsolescence.

Parallel reasoning applies to instruments of labour. If a new invention allows machinery of a given kind to be produced with less labour, old machinery "undergoes a certain amount of depreciation" and transfers proportionately less value. "But here too the change in value originates outside the process in which the machine is acting as a means of production. Once engaged in this process the machine cannot transfer more value than it possesses independently of the process."

Composition Changes Do Not Alter Functional Distinctions

The chapter's closing move (p. 319) anticipates a potential objection: does a change in the ratio of constant to variable capital compromise the distinction? Marx shows it does not. The technical conditions may be revolutionized: where "ten men using ten implements of small value worked up a relatively small quantity of raw material, one man may now, with the aid of one expensive machine, work up one hundred times as much raw material." This produces "an enormous increase in the constant capital... and at the same time a great reduction in the variable part." But: "This change however alters only the quantitative relation between the constant and the variable capital, or the proportion in which the total capital is split up into its constant and variable constituents; it has not in the least degree affected the essential difference between the two."

This final sentence is programmatic. The qualitative distinction between c and v — between value-preserving dead labour and value-creating living labour — is invariant under any change in their quantitative proportion. This is the analytical bedrock for the later concepts of the organic composition of capital and the tendency of the rate of profit to fall.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The fundamental principle unveiled by Chapter 8 is that value can only be created by living labour, but it can be transferred only through the concrete form of that labour's productive activity. This is not a metaphysical claim but a functional one, and it yields several systemic laws. First, the conservation-and-creation law: means of production never transfer more value than they possess or lose; they are value-conserving while living labour alone is value-generating. Second, the free-gift law: the preservation of capital's value is a gratuitous by-product of labour's concrete usefulness, appropriated by capital without compensation — a hidden subsidy whose existence is typically revealed only when crisis interrupts production and decay proceeds unmitigated. Third, the composition-invariance law: however drastic the technical re-organization of production, the qualitative distinction between constant and variable capital persists.

The broader theoretical implications are enormous. This chapter is the birthplace of the c/v schema that anchors the entire subsequent analysis of Capital. Without it, the rate of surplus-value (s/v) would have no denominator, the organic composition of capital (c/v) no numerator, and the theory of the falling rate of profit no foundation. The distinction also resolves a foundational paradox of classical political economy: how capital can be "productive" without being a source of value. Marx's answer — capital's apparent productivity is the loan of living labour's creative and preserving power — directly repudiates Adam Smith's resolution of value into wages, profit, and rent into cost components, and demolishes Say's and Roscher's attempts to derive surplus-value from nature's bounty or capital's "abstinence."

Philosophically, the chapter's insistence that "value exists only in use-values, in things" and that its transference is a "metempsychosis" occurring "behind the back of the actual labour in progress" articulates a powerful critique of fetishism in motion: value appears self-moving, migrating from material to material, but this self-motion is an illusion sustained by the unacknowledged labor-power that animates dead matter. The analogy between machines and human mortality — "The instrument suffers the same fate as the man" — alongside the actuarial reference, underscores that depreciation, like life, is a social accounting over natural decay.

Practically, the chapter implies that crises of profitability during production halts are not moral failures of businessmen but structural exposures of the capital-form's reliance on continuously flowing living labour. Theoretically, it supplies the analytical tool by which Marx shows that capitalism's growing technological power — increasing constant capital and shrinking variable capital in proportion — does not liberate capital from its dependence on living labour but deepens it. The distinction drawn here between apparent and real reproduction of value thus carries the entire theoretical weight of surplus-value: only value produced by labour-power constitutes genuine new value; the "re-appearance" of material value is a passive preservation. In this single chapter, Marx thereby establishes the closed circuit — dead labour preserves, living labour creates, capital appropriates — that drives the whole machinery of the capitalist mode of production.
