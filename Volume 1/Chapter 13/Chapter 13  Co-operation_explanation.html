Volume 1 • Chapter 13: Co-operation

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 13, "Co-operation," is the opening salvo of Part Four of Capital, Volume I — the section devoted to the production of relative surplus-value. Its placement is deliberate and structural. The preceding part (on absolute surplus-value) had analyzed how capital lengthens the working day to extract surplus labour. But Marx here pivots from the duration of labour to its social organization. The chapter's central problem is deceptively simple: what happens when capital gathers many workers under one roof and sets them to work together? Marx's answer is that nothing changes quantitatively in the production of value — yet everything changes qualitatively in the production of use-values and in the social relations of production. This dialectical tension between the quantitative equivalence of aggregated value-production and the qualitative novelty of combined labour is the chapter's beating heart.

The core thesis is threefold. First, simple co-operation — the simultaneous employment of numerous wage-labourers by a single capital on the same labour process — is the historically and conceptually primordial form of capitalist production. It is "the starting-point of capitalist production," the moment "the birth of capital itself." Manufacture, at its earliest, is nothing but an enlargement of the guild master's workshop; the initial difference is "purely quantitative."

Second, and crucially, this quantitative expansion secretly generates a new qualitative power. When workers co-operate in a planned way, they produce a social productive power of labour that is not the mere sum of individual powers. This collective force — arising from the fusion of many forces, from the division of a complex operation into simultaneous phases, from the temporal compression enabled by many hands, from the stimulation of rivalry and "animal spirits," and from the joint economization of the means of production — is a free gift to capital. It costs capital nothing, and because it only materializes once labour has been subsumed into capital, it appears as a power inherent in capital itself.

Third, the chapter anatomizes the political and coercive form that this co-operation assumes. Because co-operation brings workers into contact and hence into potential resistance, and because the means of production confront the wage-labourer as "another's property" (fremdes Eigentum), capital's direction of the labour process is twofold in content (a technical function of social labour plus a function of exploitation) but purely despotic in form. The scale of co-operation necessitates a specialized hierarchy of managers and overseers — an "industrial army" with its officers and NCOs — who exercise supervision in the name of capital. Marx's decisive inversion of bourgeois ideology is crystallized here: "It is not because he is a leader of industry that a man is a capitalist; on the contrary, he is a leader of industry because he is a capitalist."

Intellectually, the chapter sits at the hinge of Marx's entire architectonic. It prepares the ground for the division of labour in manufacture (Chapter 14) and machinery and modern industry (Chapter 15) by establishing simple co-operation as the "fundamental form of the capitalist mode of production" that persists beneath all its more developed incarnations. It also advances the theory of relative surplus-value by showing how capital raises productive power without intensifying exploitation one worker at a time, and it deepens the theory of formal versus real subsumption of labour under capital — the shift from capital command as a mere "formal result" of the wage-relation to capital command as a "real condition of production," as indispensable as a general's command on a battlefield. Finally, the chapter situates capitalist co-operation in a materialist history of labour's social forms, contrasting it with the communal co-operation of tribal and Indian village life (grounded in common ownership) and with the slave-based co-operation of ancient empires and plantations (grounded in relations of domination and servitude). The historical specificity is the point: co-operation is not inherently capitalist, but capital appropriates and transforms it, so that from the standpoint of the peasant and artisan, co-operation itself appears as the distinctive, epochal form of the capitalist process of production.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Quantitative Threshold: Large-Scale Simultaneous Employment as the Genesis of Capitalist Production

Marx opens by recalling an established conclusion: "Capitalist production only really begins... when each individual capital simultaneously employs a comparatively large number of workers," such that the labour-process is carried on "on an extensive scale" and yields "relatively large quantities of products." The precise definition he supplies is worth pausing over, because every subsequent argument depends on it. A large number of workers working together, at the same time, in one place (or in the same field of labour), in order to produce the same sort of commodity, under the command of the same capitalist — this constitutes the starting-point of capitalist production. The three operative criteria are simultaneity, spatial co-presence, and unity of command (one capital, one capitalist). All further distinctions — of technique, division of labour, machinery — are, at this threshold, absent.

The historical-conceptual claim is emphasized: this starting-point is true "both historically and conceptually." Historically, because manufacture (Manufaktur), in its earliest stages, "can hardly be distinguished" from the handicraft trades (Handwerksindustrie) of the guilds except by the greater number of workers simultaneously employed by the same individual capital. Conceptually, because the logical category of capitalist production presupposes the aggregation of labour-powers under one command before any qualitative transformation of the labour process can be theorized. The workshop of the guild master, "merely an enlargement," is thus the chrysalis of the factory. "At first, then, the difference is purely quantitative."

The Value-Theoretic Puzzle: Why Numbers Don't Change Value — And How They Do

Here Marx inserts a crucial recapitulation from the theory of value, and it functions as the chapter's provocation. "We have shown that the surplus-value produced by a given capital is equal to the surplus-value produced by each worker multiplied by the number of workers simultaneously employed." In formal terms, if s is the surplus-value produced by one worker and n is the number of workers, the total surplus-value S = s × n. The immediate implication is that the number of workers "does not in itself affect either the rate of surplus-value [s/v] or the degree of exploitation of labour-power." The rate is a ratio internal to each labour-power; multiplying the workers multiplies the numerator and denominator alike, leaving the rate untouched.

Marx illustrates with a value-production calculation. "If a working day of 12 hours is objectified in 6 shillings, 1,200 working days of 12 hours will be objectified in 1,200 times 6 shillings." Whether we express this as 12 × 1,200 working-hours or as twelve working-hours multiplied 1,200-fold, the aggregate value produced is identical. "In the production of value a number of workers merely rank as so many individual workers," he concludes, "and it therefore makes no difference in the value produced whether the 1,200 men work separately or united under the command of one capitalist." This is the abstract labour side of the twofold nature of labour: value is congealed homogeneous labour-time, indifferent to how it is socially packaged. It is essential to grasp that Marx is not backtracking here; he is setting up a contrast. The identity holds for value — but not for use-value, and not for the social relations of production.

The Emergence of Average Social Labour: The Statistical Logic of the Collective Working Day

The moment of transition is signaled by the phrase "Nevertheless, within certain limits, a modification does take place." Marx's argument turns on a statistical insight: "The labour objectified in value is labour of an average social quality, it is an expression of average labour-power." But an average is "merely the average of a number of separate magnitudes all of one kind, but differing in quantity." In every industry, each individual worker deviates from the average. These individual differences are "errors" in the mathematical sense — deviations that "compensate each other and vanish whenever a certain minimum number of workers are employed together." Marx invokes Edmund Burke, "that famous sophist and sycophant," who from his practical observations as a farmer asserted that in a platoon as small as five farm labourers, "all individual differences in the labour vanish," so that any five adult farm labourers will do as much work as any other five. Marx neither endorses the precise number nor dismisses the principle; "however that may be," he grants the statistical logic.

The mechanism is then formalized through the collective working day. Let each individual work 12 hours. The collective working day of twelve simultaneously employed workers therefore consists of 144 hours. Although each man's labour may diverge from average social labour — each requiring a different amount of time for the same operation — the working day of each "possesses the qualities of an average social working day, because it forms one-twelfth of the collective working day of 144 hours." Each individual day is an aliquot part of the collective day. And this holds "no matter whether the twelve men help each other in their work, or whether the connection between their operations consists merely in the fact that the men are all working for the same capitalist." This is a critical nuance: the averaging effect does not require technical interaction. Mere aggregation of labour-powers under one capital, at a certain minimum scale, suffices to render the labour socially average. The footnote to Quetelet and his "average man" situates Marx's argument within contemporaneous statistical theory — Marx is appropriating the mathematics of probabilities to explain a social result of combined labour.

The Counterfactual of the Six Small Masters: How Co-operation Secures the Law of Valorization

The argument is then sharpened by a thought-experiment. Suppose the twelve men are employed in six pairs by six different "small masters." It becomes "entirely a matter of chance" whether each master produces the same value, and hence whether he secures the general rate of surplus-value. Divergences occur in individual cases. If one worker requires considerably more time than is socially necessary, "the duration of the necessary labour-time would, in his case, diverge significantly from the labour-time socially necessary," and his labour "would therefore not count as average labour." His labour-power "would not count as average labour-power"; it would be unsaleable, or saleable only below the average value of labour-power. Marx notes that capitalist production supplies the means of fixing this minimum of efficiency — foreshadowing the disciplinary apparatus examined later — yet "this minimum diverges from the average," even as the capitalist pays the average value of labour-power. The consequence for the six small masters: one "would squeeze out more than the average rate of surplus-value, another less." The inequalities "would cancel out for the society as a whole, but not for the individual masters."

The theoretical payoff is stated with precision: "The law of valorization therefore comes fully into its own for the individual producer only when he produces as a capitalist and employs a number of workers simultaneously, i.e. when from the outset he sets in motion labour of a socially average character." This is a profound result. The law of value — that commodities exchange at their socially necessary labour-time — is realized at the level of the individual enterprise only through the scale of co-operation. Competition among small masters distributes the gains randomly; the collective working day, by internalizing the average, makes the general rate of surplus-value a reliable result. Co-operation thus converts the statistical average into an organizational reality for the individual capitalist.

The Revolution in the Objective Conditions: Economy in the Means of Production

A second, independent consequence is then introduced: "Even without an alteration in the method of work, the simultaneous employment of a large number of workers produces a revolution in the objective conditions of the labour process." The focus shifts from labour-power to constant capital. Buildings, store-houses for raw material, implements and utensils used simultaneously or in turns — "a portion of the means of production" — are now "consumed jointly."

Marx makes a two-sided argument about the exchange-value of these means. On the one hand, their exchange-value is not increased, "for the exchange-value of a commodity is not raised by any increase in the exploitation of its use-value." On the other hand, they are "used in common, and therefore on a larger scale." A room where twenty weavers work at twenty looms must be larger than that of a single weaver with two assistants; but "it costs less labour to build one workshop for twenty persons than to build ten to accommodate two weavers each." Hence the value of concentrated means of production "does not increase in direct proportion to their extent and useful effect."

This produces an economy in constant capital with a determinate effect on commodity value. When means of production are consumed in common, they give up a smaller part of their value to each single product — for two reasons: (i) the total value they part with is spread over a greater number of products; (ii) their value, though greater in absolute terms, is relatively less "from the point of view of their sphere of action" than the value of separate means. Consequently "the value of a part of the constant capital falls, and, in proportion to the size of this fall, the total value of the commodity also falls." The effect is "the same as if the means of production had cost less." Marx emphasizes that this economy "arises entirely out of their joint consumption in the labour process by many workers," and — importantly — this social character attaches to the instruments of labour "even when the numerous workers assembled together do not assist each other but merely work side by side." He notes: "A portion of the instruments of labour acquires this social character before the labour process itself does so."

Marx then distinguishes two points of view on this economy. Firstly, "in so far as it cheapens commodities, and thereby brings about a fall in the value of labour-power" — this directly feeds the theory of relative surplus-value, since a cheaper wage-good lowers the value of labour-power and thus raises surplus-value without lengthening the working day. Secondly, "in so far as it alters the ratio of surplus-value to the total capital advanced," that is, the ratio s/(c+v), which is the rate of profit. The footnote clarifies this: s/(c+v) is the rate of profit (since c+v = C), as opposed to the rate of surplus-value s/v. Marx explicitly defers this second aspect to the first section of Volume 3, Chapter 2 ("The Rate of Profit"), conceding that the analysis "forces this tearing apart of the object under investigation." He adds an ideological observation: this fragmentation "corresponds also to the spirit of capitalist production," because the worker encounters the instruments of labour as another man's property, so "economy in their use appears, from his standpoint, to be a separate operation," one disconnected from the methods that raise his own productivity.

The Definition of Co-operation and the Emergence of a New Collective Productive Power

Marx now delivers the formal definition: "When numerous workers work together side by side in accordance with a plan, whether in the same process, or in different but connected processes, this form of labour is called co-operation." Two criteria are embedded: plan (conscious direction) and simultaneity (side-by-side labour), across either a single or several connected processes.

The definition is immediately followed by the chapter's most famous claim — the emergence of a new productive power. Marx deploys a military analogy: "Just as the offensive power of a squadron of cavalry, or the defensive power of an infantry regiment, is essentially different from the sum of the offensive or defensive powers of the individual soldiers taken separately, so the sum total of the mechanical forces exerted by isolated workers differs from the social force that is developed when many hands co-operate in the same undivided operation." Examples include raising a heavy weight, turning a winch, clearing an obstacle. In such cases "the effect of the combined labour could either not be produced at all by isolated individual labour, or it could be produced only by a great expenditure of time, or on a very dwarf-like scale." The claim is emphatic: "Not only do we have here an increase in the productive power of the individual, by means of co-operation, but the creation of a new productive power, which is intrinsically a collective one." This is the qualitative leap — the whole genuinely greater than the sum of its parts, a social force that has no individual analogue.

The Mechanical and Psychological Augmentation of Force: The Social Animal and "Animal Spirits"

The argument then broadens from the mechanical to the psychological. "Apart from the new power that arises from the fusion of many forces into a single force, mere social contact begets in most industries a rivalry and a stimulation of the 'animal spirits', which heightens the efficiency of each individual worker." Hence a dozen people working together produce "far more" in a 144-hour collective day than twelve isolated men each working 12 hours, and "far more than one man who works 12 days in succession." The source: "man, if not as Aristotle thought a political animal, is at all events a social animal." Marx's footnote corrects Aristotle: the real meaning of the definition is that man is by nature a citizen of a town (polis), characteristic of classical antiquity — just as Franklin's "tool-making animal" is characteristic of Yankeedom. This is a small but telling materialist-historical aside: definitions of human nature themselves bear the stamp of their epoch.

Temporal Compression: Simultaneity, Phases, and Critical Moments

Next, Marx develops the temporal dimension of co-operation's productivity. Even when workers perform the same task, that labour "may correspond to a distinct phase of the labour process," and "as a result of the system of co-operation, the object of labour passes through the phases of the process more quickly than before." His illustration: a dozen masons in a row passing stones from the foot of a ladder to its summit. Each does the same thing, "and yet their separate acts form connected parts of one total operation; these acts are particular phases which each stone must go through, and the stones are thus carried up more quickly by the twenty-four hands of the row of men than they could be if each man went separately up and down the ladder with his load." The object of labour "is carried over the same distance in a shorter time." (The Skarbek footnote confirms this is a "partial division of labour" even under identity of task.)

The spatial-temporal point is reinforced: a building taken in hand "on different sides simultaneously" means co-operating masons do the same work, yet twelve masons in 144 collective hours "make much more progress with the building than one mason could make working for 12 days, or 144 hours." The reason: "a body of men working together have hands and eyes both in front and behind, and can be said to be to a certain extent omnipresent. The various parts of the product come to fruition simultaneously." Marx stresses that this elementary identity of work "plays a great part in co-operation, even at its most fully developed stage." And crucially, when the labour process is complicated, "the sheer number of the co-operators permits the apportionment of various operations to different hands, and consequently their simultaneous performance," shortening the total time (with a Destutt de Tracy footnote on fishing — one rows, one holds the rudder, a third casts the net).

Marx then introduces the concept of critical moments — "periods of time determined by the nature of the labour process itself, during which certain definite results must be obtained." Examples: sheep-shearing, wheat harvest, herring fishing. Here "the quantity and quality of the product depend on the initiation and the completion of the work at certain definite points in time," and "the time the labour process may take is laid down in advance." The arithmetic of temporal compulsion is stark: "A single person cannot carve a working day of more than, say, 12 hours out of the natural day, but 100 men co-operating can extend the working day to 1,200 hours." The "shortness of the time allowed for the work is compensated for by the large mass of labour thrown into the field of production at the decisive moment." Completion within the deadline "depends on the simultaneous application of numerous combined working days"; useful effect "depends on the number of workers"; and this number "is always smaller than the number of isolated workers that would be required to do the same amount of work in the same period." The empirical cost of its absence is given: corn wasted annually in the western United States, and cotton in eastern India "where English rule has destroyed the old communities" — a pointed historical-materialist footnote about the destruction of pre-capitalist co-operation.

Spatial Dialectics: Extension of the Arena and Contraction of the Field (Faux Frais)

Marx turns to the spatial dimension, framed as a dialectic. On the one hand, co-operation "allows work to be carried on over a large area; for certain labour processes, therefore, it is required simply by the physical constitution of the object of labour." Examples: draining marshes, constructing dykes, irrigation, canals, roads, railways. On the other hand, "while extending the scale of production it renders possible a relative contraction of its arena." This seemingly paradoxical "simultaneous restriction of space and extension of effectiveness" allows "a large number of incidental expenses (faux frais) to be spared," and results "from the massing together of workers and of various labour processes, and from the concentration of the means of production." The R. Jones footnote supplies the historical illustration: capital and labour that once "loosely occupied 500 acres" are "concentrated for the more complete tillage of 100" — space is concentrated, yet the sphere of production is enlarged relative to that of a single independent agent. The gloss on faux frais explains it as a technical French expression (Garnier, Say) for costs not directly incurred in production, originating in Adam Smith's distinction between productive and unproductive labour — a small lexical gesture that ties the analysis to the political-economy tradition Marx is both using and criticizing.

The Consolidated Theorem: The Social Productive Power of Labour

Marx now gathers every previously enumerated cause into a single synthesis. The combined working day produces "a greater quantity of use-values than an equal sum of isolated working days," and "consequently diminishes the labour-time necessary for the production of a given useful effect." He lists the causes of this increased productivity: whether the combined day (i) heightens mechanical force, (ii) extends the sphere of action over a greater space, (iii) contracts the field of production relative to scale, (iv) sets large masses of labour to work at the critical moment, (v) excites rivalry and raises animal spirits, (vi) impresses on similar operations "the stamp of continuity and many-sidedness," (vii) performs different operations simultaneously, (viii) economizes the means of production by use in common, or (ix) lends individual labour "the character of average social labour." The exhaustive catalogue matters because Marx insists: "whichever of these is the cause of the increase, the special productive power of the combined working day is, under all circumstances, the social productive power of labour, or the productive power of social labour. This power arises from co-operation itself." The chapter's philosophical summit follows: "When the worker co-operates in a planned way with others, he strips off the fetters of his individuality, and develops the capabilities of his species." Here Marx smuggles in an almost Feuerbachian-humanist formulation — co-operation as species-being (Gattungswesen) realized — which coexists uneasily with, and is immediately subordinated to, the appropriation argument that follows.

The Capitalist Condition: Concentration of Capital as Material Presupposition

From the social nature of co-operation, Marx derives its dependence on capital. "As a general rule, workers cannot co-operate without being brought together: their assembly in one place is a necessary condition for their co-operation." Hence "wage-labourers cannot co-operate unless they are employed simultaneously by the same capital, the same capitalist, and therefore unless their labour-powers are bought simultaneously by him." The total value of these labour-powers, the day's or week's wages, "must be ready in the pocket of the capitalist before the workers themselves are ready to start the process of production." This establishes a scale threshold: "The payment of 300 workers at once, even though only for one day, requires a greater outlay of capital than the payment of a smaller number of men, week by week, during a whole year." Therefore "the number of the workers that co-operate, or the scale of co-operation, depends in the first instance on the amount of capital that the individual capitalist can spare for the purchase of labour-power" — i.e., on "the extent to which a single capitalist has command over the means of subsistence of a number of workers."

The same logic applies to constant capital. "For example, the outlay on raw material is thirty times as great for the capitalist who employs 300 men as it is for each of the thirty capitalists who employ ten men." The value and quantity of instruments used in common "does not, it is true, increase at the same rate as the number of workers, but it does increase very considerably." Marx's conclusion: "Hence concentration of large masses of the means of production in the hands of individual capitalists is a material condition for the co-operation of wage-labourers, and the extent of co-operation, or the scale of production, depends on the extent of this concentration." He links this back to an earlier finding: a minimum capital is needed so that the number of workers, and the surplus-value produced, "might suffice to liberate the employer himself from manual labour, to convert him from a small master into a capitalist, and thus formally to establish the capital-relation." Now that minimum is shown to be "a material condition for the conversion of numerous isolated and independent processes into one combined social process."

Formal Subsumption Becomes Real Command: The Genesis of the Directing Authority

A pivotal theoretical distinction is now advanced. Earlier, "the subjection of labour to capital was only a formal result of the fact that the worker, instead of working for himself, works for, and consequently under, the capitalist." But "through the co-operation of numerous wage-labourers, the command of capital develops into a requirement for carrying on the labour process itself, into a real condition of production." This is the transition from formal to real subsumption in embryo: capital's command ceases to be an external legal relation superimposed on a self-sufficient labour process and becomes technically woven into production itself. "That a capitalist should command in the field of production is now as indispensable as that a general should command on the field of battle."

Marx then offers a general theory of direction. "All directly social or communal labour on a large scale requires, to a greater or lesser degree, a directing authority, in order to secure the harmonious co-operation of the activities of individuals, and to perform the general functions that have their origin in the motion of the total productive organism, as distinguished from the motion of its separate organs." The metaphor: "A single violin player is his own conductor: an orchestra requires a separate one." Thus "the work of directing, superintending and adjusting becomes one of the functions of capital, from the moment that the labour under capital's control becomes co-operative." And "as a specific function of capital, the directing function acquires its own special characteristics."

The Despotic Form and the Twofold Content: The Industrial Army and Its Officers

The "special characteristics" are now unpacked. The driving motive of capitalist production is "the self-valorization of capital to the greatest possible extent, i.e. the greatest possible production of surplus-value, hence the greatest possible exploitation of labour-power by the capitalist." As the number of co-operating workers increases, "so too does their resistance to the domination of capital, and, necessarily, the pressure put on by capital to overcome this resistance." Therefore the capitalist's control is twofold in content: "not only a special function arising from the nature of the social labour process, and peculiar to that process, but it is at the same time a function of the exploitation of a social labour process, and is consequently conditioned by the unavoidable antagonism between the exploiter and the raw material of his exploitation." A further ground for control is added: as the means of production extend, "the necessity increases for some effective control over the proper application of them, because they confront the wage-labourer as the property of another [fremdes Eigentum]." The footnote's Spectator citation — on the Manchester wirework company where partnership led to "a sudden decrease in waste," and on the Rochdale co-operatives that "did not leave a clear place for masters" ("Quelle horreur!") — is a sardonic illustration of how capitalist ideology recoils from co-operation without a master.

Marx then states the ultimate expropriation of agency: "the co-operation of wage-labourers is entirely brought about by the capital that employs them. Their unification into one single productive body, and the establishment of a connection between their individual functions, lies outside their competence. These things are not their own act, but the act of the capital that brings them together and maintains them in that situation." The social interconnection therefore confronts the workers "in the realm of ideas, as a plan drawn up by the capitalist, and, in practice, as his authority, as the powerful will of a being outside them, who subjects their activity to his purpose."

Hence the celebrated formulation: content is twofold; form is purely despotic. As co-operation extends, "this despotism develops the forms that are peculiar to it." Just as the capitalist was earlier relieved of actual labour once his capital reached a minimum, "so now he hands over the work of direct and constant supervision of the individual workers and groups of workers to a special kind of wage-labourer." The metaphor expands into a full political anatomy: "An industrial army of workers under the command of a capitalist requires, like a real army, officers (managers) and N.C.O.s (foremen, overseers), who command during the labour process in the name of capital. The work of supervision becomes their established and exclusive function."

Marx then exposes a bourgeois double standard. Political economists, comparing "the mode of production of isolated peasants or independent artisans with the plantation economy which rests on slavery," count supervisory labour as part of the faux frais de production (the Cairnes footnote notes that the peasant proprietor "appropriating the whole produce of his toil, needs no other stimulus to exertion. Superintendence is here completely dispensed with"). But when considering the capitalist mode of production, "they on the contrary identify the function of direction which arises out of the nature of the communal labour process with the function of direction which is made necessary by the capitalist and therefore antagonistic character of that process." Marx's counter-thesis: "It is not because he is a leader of industry that a man is a capitalist; on the contrary, he is a leader of industry because he is a capitalist. The leadership of industry is an attribute of capital, just as in feudal times the functions of general and judge were attributes of landed property." The Steuart footnote — "Why do large undertakings in the manufacturing way ruin private industry, but by coming nearer to the simplicity of slaves?" — and Marx's jab at Auguste Comte's school (which might "just as well have shown that feudal lords are an eternal necessity") complete the ideological critique: the "eternal necessity" of the capitalist director is a class apologetic dressed as a functional necessity.

The Free Gift: How Social Productivity Becomes a Power of Capital

The chapter's theoretical climax — the doctrine of the "free gift" — now unfolds through a careful legal-economic argument. "The worker is the owner of his labour-power until he has finished bargaining for its sale with the capitalist, and he can sell no more than what he has — i.e. his individual, isolated labour-power." This relation "is in no way altered by the fact that the capitalist, instead of buying the labour-power of one man, buys that of 100, and enters into separate contracts with 100 unconnected men instead of with one." The capitalist "can set the 100 men to work, without letting them co-operate. He pays them the value of 100 independent labour-powers, but he does not pay for the combined labour-power of the 100." This is decisive: co-operation's product is not purchased; it arises only from the workers' combination, and combination is imposed by capital. "Being independent of each other, the workers are isolated. They enter into relations with the capitalist, but not with each other. Their co-operation only begins with the labour process, but by then they have ceased to belong to themselves. On entering the labour process they are incorporated into capital."

The consequence is the dissociation of productive power from its producer. "As co-operators, as members of a working organism, they merely form a particular mode of existence of capital. Hence the productive power developed by the worker socially is the productive power of capital." Marx repeats and sharpens the point: "The socially productive power of labour develops as a free gift to capital whenever the workers are placed under certain conditions, and it is capital which places them under these conditions. Because this power costs capital nothing, while on the other hand it is not developed by the worker until his labour itself belongs to capital, it appears as a power which capital possesses by its nature — a productive power inherent in capital." This is the ideological inversion the chapter exists to expose: a socially produced, historically specific capacity is misrecognized as a natural, intrinsic property of capital. It is the same structure of fetishism that Marx anatomized in the commodity and in the wage-form, now operating on the productivity of the collective worker.

Historical Excursus: Ancient Cooperation, Asiatic Despotism, and Pre-Capitalist Forms

Marx widens the frame historically. "The colossal effects of simple co-operation are to be seen in the gigantic structures erected by the ancient Asiatics, Egyptians, Etruscans, etc." He quotes R. Jones at length: Oriental States, after supplying their civil and military establishments, retained a surplus applied to "works of magnificence or utility," and their "command over the hands and arms of almost the entire non-agricultural population has produced stupendous monuments." The Nile's teeming valley fed "a swarming non-agricultural population," and this food, belonging to monarch and priesthood, "afforded the means of erecting the mighty monuments." In moving colossal statues, "human labour almost alone was prodigally used... The number of the labourers and the concentration of their efforts sufficed." Jones's coral-reef metaphor is quoted: although each depositor is puny, "their number is their strength," and "the power of directing these masses gave rise to the palaces and temples, the pyramids, and the armies of gigantic statues." The key line: "It is that confinement of the revenues which feed them, to one or a few hands, which makes such undertakings possible." Marx then draws the modern parallel: this power of Asiatic and Egyptian kings, of Etruscan theocrats, "has in modern society been transferred to the capitalist, whether he appears as an isolated individual or, as in the case of joint-stock companies, in combination with others." The form of concentrated command persists across epochs; only its class bearer changes.

This leads to a contrast of historical forms. Co-operation at the beginning of civilization (hunting peoples) and as a predominant feature of the agriculture of Indian communities rests on two foundations: "the common ownership of the conditions of production," and the fact that "the individual has as little torn himself free from the umbilical cord of his tribe or community as a bee has from his hive." Both characteristics distinguish it from capitalist co-operation. By contrast, the "sporadic application of co-operation on a large scale in ancient times, in the Middle Ages, and in modern colonies, rests on direct relations of domination and servitude, in most cases on slavery." The capitalist form, uniquely, "presupposes from the outset the free wage-labourer who sells his labour-power to capital." Historically it develops "in opposition to peasant agriculture and independent handicrafts, whether in guilds or not." A substantial footnote situates peasant and artisan production as the economic foundation both of the feudal mode (on the one hand) and of classical antiquity's best period (on the other) — a lapidary materialist genealogy of the two great pre-capitalist formations.

The Historical Specificity and Pervasiveness of Simple Cooperation

Marx concludes with a reflection on historical appearance. "From the standpoint of the peasant and the artisan, capitalist co-operation does not appear as a particular historical form of co-operation; instead, co-operation itself appears as a historical form peculiar to, and specifically distinguishing, the capitalist process of production." That is, the pre-capitalist producer, lacking a comparative perspective, mistakes a historically specific social arrangement for co-operation as such — a form of epochal myopia. Symmetrically, just as the social productive power of labour "appears to be the productive power of capital," so "co-operation itself... appears to be a specific form of the capitalist process of production." It is "the first change experienced by the actual labour process when subjected to capital," and it "takes place spontaneously and naturally." The simultaneous employment of many wage-labourers, the necessary condition for this change, "also forms the starting-point of capitalist production. This starting-point coincides with the birth of capital itself."

The dialectical summary is balanced: "If then, on the one hand, the capitalist mode of production is a historically necessary condition for the transformation of the labour process into a social process, so, on the other hand, this social form of the labour process is a method employed by capital for the more profitable exploitation of labour, by increasing its productive power." Marx here refuses one-sidedness: capitalism is both a historically progressive socializer of labour and an exploitative appropriator of that socialization.

Finally, Marx delimits the epochal status of simple co-operation. "In its simple shape, as investigated so far, co-operation is a necessary concomitant of all production on a large scale, but it does not in itself represent a fixed form characteristic of a particular epoch in the development of the capitalist mode of production." At most it appears as such — "and then only approximately" — in the handicraft-like beginnings of manufacture, and in large-scale agriculture corresponding to the manufacture period, distinguished from peasant agriculture "mainly by the number of workers simultaneously employed and the mass of means of production concentrated for their use." Simple co-operation "has always been, and continues to be, the predominant form in those branches of production in which capital operates on a large scale, but the division of labour and machinery play only an insignificant part." The chapter closes with a lapidary statement of persistence: "Co-operation remains the fundamental form of the capitalist mode of production, although in its simple shape it continues to appear as one particular form alongside the more developed ones." The elementary sociality of combined labour is thus the substratum that manufacture and machinofacture will reorganize but never abolish.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter yields several fundamental principles of lasting theoretical weight. The first is the dialectic of quantity and quality under capital. Marx accepts that multiplying labour-powers leaves the rate of surplus-value and the value produced per unit of abstract labour unchanged — yet he demonstrates that a sufficient quantity of combined workers generates a qualitatively new social productive power. This is not mysticism but the logic of emergence: whole systems possess properties their elements lack. The "new productive power" of co-operation is the paradigm case, and it becomes the template for Marx's later analyses of manufacture's "collective worker" (Gesamtarbeiter) and of machinery's vast powers.

The second principle is the statistical-social logic of the average. The collective working day renders each participant's labour socially average by the mathematics of compensation of errors. This insight — that capital, by internalizing many workers, manufactures the conditions under which the law of value is reliably realized for the individual enterprise — dissolves the classical political-economy puzzle of how competition enforces general rates while individual capitals deviate. Co-operation is thus not merely productive but normalizing: it disciplines the workforce into social average labour-power.

The third principle is the economization of constant capital through joint consumption, which lowers the value of commodities, cheapens labour-power, and thereby raises relative surplus-value. This establishes a material feedback loop: the scale of co-operation feeds the rate of surplus-value, which feeds accumulation, which feeds further scale.

The fourth, and perhaps most consequential, principle is the "free gift" thesis and its ideological counterpart, the fetishism of capital's productivity. Social co-operation, because it is organized by and for capital and arises only within the capital-relation, is expropriated as though it were capital's intrinsic power. This is the precise mechanism by which a social and historical relation is naturalized and eternalized. The corollary is the twofold content / despotic form doctrine: capital's direction is simultaneously a technical coordination of social labour and a political apparatus of exploitation, and it is because of the latter that it takes the despotic, hierarchical form of managers, foremen, and overseers. The industrial army metaphor is not decorative; it theorizes the firm as a command structure whose necessity is grounded in antagonism, not technique.

The fifth principle is the historical-materialist genealogy of co-operation. By contrasting tribal/communal co-operation (grounded in common ownership), slave-based co-operation (grounded in domination), and capitalist co-operation (grounded in free wage-labour), Marx shows that co-operation as such is trans-historical while its forms are epoch-specific. The capitalist form is neither natural nor eternal; it is the historical product of the dissolution of feudal and petty production.

The broader implications reach across political economy, social theory, and political practice. For economic theory, the chapter demolishes the bourgeois identification of management with technical necessity, exposing it as an apology for class domination — and demonstrates that co-operation's productivity, far from vindicating capital's "contribution," is a social property capital merely commands. For social theory, it offers a rigorous account of how structure and agency interpenetrate: workers create a collective power greater than their individual selves, yet that very power is organized as an alien will outside them — an early and profound statement of what later theory would call reification and alienation. The workers' co-operation is real, but its "soul" is capital's plan.

For politics, the analysis yields a decisive evaluation of co-operatives and worker self-management. The Spectator's horror that the Rochdale pioneers "did not leave a clear place for masters" reveals the system's dependency on the master-function. Yet Marx's own argument implies that because co-operation's productive power is socially produced, it need not be capitalistically commanded — a possibility latent in the chapter and later developed in his remarks on associated labour. The chapter thus plants a seed: if the "free gift" is socially generated, it can in principle be socially reappropriated. And for historical perspective, the persistence of simple co-operation as "the fundamental form of the capitalist mode of production" that survives beneath manufacture and machinery means that the elementary reality of people working together, in a planned way, remains the irreducible human substratum of all production — a substratum whose liberation from the despotic form of capital's command stands as the chapter's enduring challenge.
