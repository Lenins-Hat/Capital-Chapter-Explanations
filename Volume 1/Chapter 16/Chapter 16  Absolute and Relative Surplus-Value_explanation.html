Volume 1 • Chapter 16: Absolute and Relative Surplus-Value

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 16 of Capital, Volume I, bears the same title as Part Five which it opens: The Production of Absolute and Relative Surplus-Value. That doubling is not redundant but programmatic. Having spent Part III dissecting absolute surplus-value (Chapters 7–11, culminating in the titanic historical sweep on the working day) and Part IV dissecting relative surplus-value (Chapters 12–15, on co-operation, manufacture, machinery and large-scale industry), Marx must now demonstrate that his readers have not been presented with two species of surplus-value, but two moments of a single dynamic. The chapter is therefore a hinge—a transitional synthesis that welds the two halves of the analytic framework into one dialectical unity and prepares the ground for the discussion of wages (Part VI) and accumulation (Part VII).

The chapter's core thesis is fourfold. First, the very category "productive labour" is not a technical description of work but a historically determined social relation: the worker is productive only because he produces surplus-value for capital, a relation which "stamps the worker as capital's direct means of valorization" and makes productivity "not a piece of luck, but a misfortune." Second, absolute surplus-value is the general foundation of the capitalist system and the presupposition of relative surplus-value; the latter does not replace the former but is built upon it, presupposing a working day already divided into necessary and surplus labour. Third, the apparent logical identity of the two forms—each contains the other—dissolves once we track the movement of surplus-value: once the capitalist mode of production has become universal, the difference becomes practical and forceful whenever the rate of surplus-value is to be raised, and the two paths (prolonging the day, or shortening the necessary component) are sharply distinct. Fourth, and most polemically, the origin of surplus-value lies not in nature, not in productivity as such, but in a specific historical social relation; nature supplies only the possibility of surplus labour, never its reality. This fourth thesis is what allows Marx to expose Ricardo's evasion, the Mercantilists' error, and Mill's "wretched evasions."

The intellectual objective of the chapter is thus threefold: to consolidate the theoretical result of the previous two Parts; to establish the natural and historical presuppositions of the surplus-labour relation so as to deny it any "occult" or trans-historical status; and, with these weapons in hand, to open the long subterranean polemic against bourgeois political economy that culminates in Theories of Surplus-Value. In the broader architecture of Capital, the chapter is both an overture to Part Five's remaining chapters (on changes in the magnitude of the price of labour-power and on the various formulae for the rate of surplus-value) and a decisive step toward the theory of the wage and the theory of accumulation. The brief invocation of the concepts of formal and real subsumption—with the footnote pointing forward to Marx's separate treatment of that topic—quietly announces the historical typology that will structure the whole account of capitalist development.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Return to Productive Labour: The Abstract Labour Process versus the Capitalist Process

Marx opens by reaching back to his earlier, deliberately abstract handling of the labour process, "independently of its historical forms, as a process between man and nature." The reader is reminded of a formula already offered: viewed from the standpoint of its result, the product, "both the instruments of labour and the object of labour are means of production, and that the labour itself is productive labour." This is the pivot on which the chapter turns. In the earlier analysis—the exposition of the simple labour process—Marx had already salted the argument with a caveat, relegated to a note: "This method of determining what is productive labour, from the standpoint of the simple labour process, is by no means sufficient to cover the capitalist process of production." Chapter 16 openly declares that "we must now develop this point further."

The methodological point is subtle and deserves emphasis. The original definition of productive labour was an abstraction—an analytical convenience, not a historical truth. Marx treats labour "in the abstract" not because labour ever existed in such form, but because the simple labour process is a necessary mental isolation of one aspect of reality: the relation between man and nature. The chapter's opening move is to break down that abstraction. The repetition of the abstraction was necessary; now its inadequacy must be exposed.

2.2 The Collective Labourer: Extension of the Concept of Productive Labour

The first move in that breakdown is the concept of the collective labourer. In "purely individual" labour, the worker "unites in himself all the functions that later on become separated." The solitary producer supervises his own activity; under developed social production, "he is supervised by others." Marx draws an anatomical analogy: "Just as head and hand belong together in the system of nature, so in the labour process mental and physical labour are united. Later on they become separate; and this separation develops into a hostile antagonism." Note that phrase "hostile antagonism"—it registers a claim that will not be cashed in fully here but that anticipates the whole argument on manufacture and machinery. The separation of mental from physical labour is not a neutral division of tasks; it is a relation of opposition, which, under capitalism, is realized in the subordination of the detail worker to the direction of capital.

The consequence is a broadening of the concept. The product is no longer the direct product of the individual producer but "a social product, the joint product of a collective labourer, i.e. a combination of workers, each of whom stands at a different distance from the actual manipulation of the object of labour." With the cooperative character of the labour process thus accentuated, "it is no longer necessary for the individual himself to put his hand to the object; it is sufficient for him to be an organ of the collective labourer, and to perform any one of its subordinate functions." Marx is explicit about the principled status of this extension: the original definition "remains correct for the collective labourer, considered as a whole," but "it no longer holds good for each member taken individually."

2.3 The Narrowing: Productive Labour as Surplus-Value Production

At precisely the point where the concept has been broadened, Marx narrows it again—this time on a wholly different axis. "Yet the concept of productive labour also becomes narrower. Capitalist production is not merely the production of commodities, it is, by its very essence, the production of surplus-value." The worker "produces not for himself, but for capital." Therefore "it is no longer sufficient ... for him simply to produce. He must produce surplus-value. The only worker who is productive is one who produces surplus-value for the capitalist, or in other words contributes towards the self-valorization of capital." This is the operative definition of the chapter.

To ram home its universality, Marx deploys the celebrated schoolmaster analogy: "a schoolmaster is a productive worker when, in addition to belabouring the heads of his pupils, he works himself into the ground to enrich the owner of the school. That the latter has laid out his capital in a teaching factory, instead of a sausage factory, makes no difference to the relation." The sphere of material production is thus shown to be a red herring. The distinguishing mark of productive labour is not the useful effect of the work, not the content of the product, but a "specifically social relation of production, a relation with a historical origin which stamps the worker as capital's direct means of valorization." This is a definition that is simultaneously an indictment: "To be a productive worker is therefore not a piece of luck, but a misfortune." The irony is bitter but precise—the term "productive" is a reward the worker earns only by producing his own subordination.

2.4 Classical Political Economy and the Historical Determination of the Concept

Marx concludes the concept's genealogy with a historical point. In the appendix volume on the history of theory—Volume 4, which he announces he will write (and which his manuscripts of 1862–63 in fact constitute; the editorial note tells us the work was published posthumously by Kautsky between 1905 and 1910, and first translated into English in three parts between 1963 and 1972 as Theories of Surplus-Value)—he will show that "the classical political economists always made the production of surplus-value the distinguishing characteristic of the productive worker." Their definition is thus internally variable: "Hence their definition of a productive worker varies with their conception of the nature of surplus-value." Marx illustrates with the Physiocrats, who "insist that only agricultural labour is productive, since that alone, they say, yields a surplus-value. For the Physiocrats, indeed, surplus-value exists exclusively in the form of ground rent." The insight is that the concept of productive labour is not merely social-historically determined; even the theory of it shifts with the theory of surplus-value.

2.5 The Two Forms Defined

The definitions proper now appear, and Marx's phrasing is exact. Absolute surplus-value is "the prolongation of the working day beyond the point at which the worker would have produced an exact equivalent for the value of his labour-power, and the appropriation of that surplus labour by capital." It "forms the general foundation of the capitalist system, and the starting-point for the production of relative surplus-value." Relative surplus-value, by contrast, "presupposes that the working day is already divided into two parts, necessary labour and surplus labour. In order to prolong the surplus labour, the necessary labour is shortened by methods for producing the equivalent of the wage of labour in a shorter time." The distinguishing feature of the two processes is captured in a single, lapidary contrast: "The production of absolute surplus-value turns exclusively on the length of the working day, whereas the production of relative surplus-value completely revolutionizes the technical processes of labour and the groupings into which society is divided." The first is extensive, requiring no transformation of the labour process; the second is intensive and revolutionary, transforming the mode of production itself.

2.6 Formal and Real Subsumption

From this contrast Marx derives a historical typology. Relative surplus-value "requires a specifically capitalist mode of production, a mode of production which, along with its methods, means and conditions, arises and develops spontaneously on the basis of the formal subsumption [Subsumtion] of labour under capital. This formal subsumption is then replaced by a real subsumption." The footnote directs the reader "see below, pp. 1019–38, for Marx's own exposition of the concepts of formal and real subsumption"—a future appendix (Results of the Immediate Process of Production), in which the full argument was later elaborated. Formal subsumption is the bare fact of wage-labour under the direct control of capital, without any transformation of the labour process itself. Real subsumption involves the thoroughgoing reorganization of the labour process on a specifically capitalist basis. The distinction is decisive: absolute surplus-value requires only formal subsumption; relative surplus-value requires real subsumption.

Marx then qualifies this with a discussion of hybrid forms—"certain hybrid forms, in which although surplus labour is not extorted by direct compulsion from the producer, the producer has not yet become formally subordinate to capital. In these forms, capital has not yet acquired a direct control over the labour process." These are the cases in which independent handicraftsmen or peasants continue to labour "in the inherited, traditional way," while alongside them "there steps the usurer or merchant with his usurer's capital or merchant's capital, which feeds on them like a parasite." Such parasitism is pre-capitalist: "The predominance of this form of exploitation in a society excludes the capitalist mode of production, although it may form the transition to capitalism, as in the later Middle Ages." Marx also notes the reappearance of such hybrid forms under modern conditions—"as in the case of modern 'domestic industry'"—but with a crucial qualification: "though their physiognomy is totally changed." Their outward appearance has been transformed even where a formal similarity persists. And crucially: "A merely formal subsumption of labour under capital suffices for the production of absolute surplus-value" (e.g., independent handicraftsmen becoming wage-labourers under a capitalist).

2.7 Interpenetration: Methods of Relative Surplus-Value as Methods of Absolute Surplus-Value

Now the chapter's central dialectical claim begins to unfold. "But we have seen how methods of producing relative surplus-value are, at the same time, methods of producing absolute surplus-value." The clearest instance: "the unrestricted prolongation of the working day turned out to be a very characteristic product of large-scale industry." Machinery, formally a means of raising productivity and thus of relative surplus-value, becomes in practice a device for lengthening the working day. The two are not separable processes, but fused.

Marx then tracks the historical trajectory: "The specifically capitalist mode of production ceases in general to be a mere means of producing relative surplus-value as soon as it has conquered an entire branch of production; this tendency is still more powerful when it has conquered all the important branches of production." Once it becomes "the universal, socially predominant form of the production process," it takes on a double character: it "only continues to act as a special method of producing relative surplus-value in two respects: first, in so far as it seizes upon industries previously only formally subordinate to capital, that is, in so far as it continues to proselytize, and second, in so far as the industries already taken over continue to be revolutionized by changes in the methods of production." The metaphor "proselytize" captures the missionary expansion of the capitalist mode of production from branch to branch; the second clause captures its immanent dynamism, its need for continual technical revolution.

2.8 The Apparent Illusoriness of the Distinction

Marx now poses the crucial dialectical problem. "From one standpoint the distinction between absolute and relative surplus-value appears to be illusory." Why? Because each contains the other: "Relative surplus-value is absolute, because it requires the absolute prolongation of the working day beyond the labour-time necessary to the existence of the worker himself. Absolute surplus-value is relative, because it requires a development of the productivity of labour which will allow the necessary labour-time to be restricted to a portion of the working day." The paradox is exact: each is the other's condition of possibility. There is no absolute surplus-value that does not presuppose a certain level of productivity, and no relative surplus-value that does not presuppose an extension of the working day beyond necessary labour.

2.9 Raising the Rate of Surplus-Value: The Real Distinction

But the "semblance of identity" is only a semblance. "If we keep in mind the movement of surplus-value, this semblance of identity vanishes." Once the capitalist mode of production is universal, the difference asserts itself "whenever there is a question of raising the rate of surplus-value." Assuming labour-power is paid at its value: if productivity and intensity of labour are given, the rate of surplus-value can be raised only by prolonging the working day in absolute terms; if the length of the working day is given, the rate can be raised only by changing the relative magnitudes of the components of the working day, i.e., necessary labour and surplus labour—and "if wages are not to fall below the value of labour-power, this change presupposes a change in either the productivity or the intensity of the labour." This is the analytic cores the chapter delivers: two distinct levers, an extensive one (length of the day) and an intensive one (productivity/intensity, given the day). The clause "if wages are not to fall below the value of labour-power" quietly excludes a third, illegitimate path—the reduction of wages below value—which will become central to the theory of wages in Part VI.

2.10 The Natural Basis of Surplus-Value

Marx now pivots to the natural and historical presuppositions of surplus labour. "If the worker needs to use all his time to produce the necessary means of subsistence for himself and his family, he has no time left in which to perform unpaid labour for other people." Surplus labour presupposes free time, which presupposes a certain level of labour productivity: "Unless labour has attained a certain level of productivity, the worker will have no such free time at his disposal, and without superfluous time there can be no surplus labour, hence no capitalists, as also no slave-owners, no feudal barons, in a word no class of large-scale landed proprietors." This is a remarkable bracketing—capitalists, slave-owners and feudal lords are grouped together as appropriators of surplus labour, materially distinct but formally identical in this respect.

But Marx immediately restricts the scope of the "natural" claim. Surplus-value rests on a natural basis "but only in the very general sense that there is no natural obstacle absolutely preventing one man from lifting from himself the burden of the labour necessary to maintain his own existence, and imposing it on another, just as there is no unconquerable natural obstacle to the consumption of the flesh of one man by another." The grisly cannibalism comparison (note: "According to a recent calculation there are still at least 4,000,000 cannibals in those parts of the earth which have so far been explored") is not rhetorical excess; it makes a rigorous point: both cannibalism and surplus appropriation have a natural possibility and no natural necessity. Marx explicitly warns against attaching "mystical notions" to spontaneously developed productivity—a barb aimed at those who would naturalize the social relation. It is "only when men have worked their way out of their initial animal condition, when therefore their labour has been to some extent socialized," that surplus labour of one person becomes a condition of existence for another.

At the dawn of civilization, productive powers are small, "but so too are the needs which develop with and upon the means of their satisfaction." The share of society living on the labour of others is "infinitely small compared with the mass of direct producers" but grows "both absolutely and relatively" with the advance of social productivity (note 3 cites: "Among the wild Indians in America, almost everything is the labourer's, 99 parts of a hundred are to be put upon the account of labour. In England, perhaps, the labourer has not two thirds"). The decisive conclusion: "The existing productivity of labour, from which it proceeds as its basis, is a gift, not of nature, but of a history embracing thousands of centuries."

2.11 Natural Conditions of the Productivity of Labour

Even setting aside the level of social development, the productivity of labour "remains fettered by natural conditions. These conditions can all be traced back to the nature of man himself (his race, etc.) and to the natural objects which surround him." External natural conditions divide into two great classes: (1) natural wealth in the means of subsistence—"a fruitful soil, waters teeming with fish, etc."; (2) natural wealth in the instruments of labour—"waterfalls, navigable rivers, wood, metal, coal, etc." At the dawn of civilization it is the first class that dominates; at a higher stage, the second. Marx's examples: England versus India; Athens and Corinth versus the shores of the Black Sea. The smaller the number of natural requirements and the greater the natural fertility of the soil and kindness of climate, "the smaller the amount of labour-time necessary for the maintenance and reproduction of the producer," and hence the greater the quantity of excess labour he can perform for others. This is the principle the ancient Egyptians illustrate; Marx cites Diodorus Siculus: Egyptian children cost their parents no more than twenty drachmas to raise, hence the enormous population and hence "why so many great works can be undertaken." But Marx immediately qualifies: "Nevertheless, the gigantic building projects of ancient Egypt owed less to the size of the population than to the large proportion of it that was freely disposable." The analogy between the individual worker and the working population is exact: less necessary labour-time means more surplus labour; a smaller portion of the population devoted to producing subsistence means a greater portion available for other work.

2.12 The Temperate Zone, Soil Differentiation, and the Domination of Nature

This section delivers one of the chapter's most brilliant counter-intuitive claims: fertility does not make a region fit for capitalism. The most fertile soil, precisely because it is fertile, may "keep him in hand, like a child in leading-strings"—man's own development "is not in that case a nature-imposed necessity." The note cites Thomas Mun's England's Treasure by Forraign Trade (1669): natural wealth "doth make the people careless, proud, and given to all excesses; whereas the second [wealth acquired through labour] enforceth vigilancy, literature, arts, and policy." And N. Forster (1767): "A soil incapable of produce by labour is quite as bad as a soil that produces plentifully without any labour." Marx concludes: "The mother country of capital is not the tropical region, with its luxuriant vegetation, but the temperate zone." The reason is that "it is not the absolute fertility of the soil but its degree of differentiation, the variety of its natural products, which forms the natural basis for the social division of labour"—the variety of nature, not its abundance, "spurs man on to the multiplication of his needs, his capacities, and the instruments and modes of his labour."

The argument now turns explicitly to the domination of nature: "It is the necessity of bringing a natural force under the control of society, of economizing on its energy, of appropriating or subduing it on a large scale by the work of the human hand, that plays the most decisive role in the history of industry." Marx's examples are the regulation of water in Egypt, Lombardy and Holland; irrigation in India and Persia, where artificial canals also "carry down mineral fertilizers from the hills, in the shape of sediment." The footnote on Egypt is notable for its materialist claim about ideology: "The necessity for predicting the rise and fall of the Nile created Egyptian astronomy, and with it the domination of the priests as the directors of agriculture" (citing Cuvier's Discours sur les révolutions du globe). Another footnote makes the same point about India: "One of the material foundations of the power of the state over the small and unconnected producing organisms of India was the regulation of the water supply. Its Mohammedan rulers understood this better than their English successors"—with the Orissa famine of 1866 as the devastating evidence ("more than a million Hindus in the district of Orissa, in the Bengal Presidency"). The Spanish and Sicilian industry under Arab rule is likewise located in irrigation works.

2.13 Possibility versus Reality: The Sago Gatherer

The chapter's most decisive natural-history passage now consolidates the argument: "Favourable natural conditions can provide in themselves only the possibility, never the reality of surplus labour, nor, accordingly, the reality of surplus-value and a surplus product." Natural conditions affect surplus labour only as limits—"by determining the point at which labour for others can begin"—and "in proportion as industry advances these natural limits recede." Marx then offers the sago-gatherer example: an East Indian who bores a hole into a sago tree, cuts down and divides the trunk, extracts and filters the pith—"one tree commonly yields 300 lb., and occasionally 500 to 600 lb."—goes into the forest and "cuts bread for himself, just as with us they cut firewood." Suppose this bread-cutter requires 12 working hours a week for all his needs. "Nature's direct gift to him is plenty of leisure time." But "before he can apply this leisure time productively for himself, a whole series of historical circumstances is required; before he spends it in surplus labour for others, compulsion is necessary." Under capitalist production "the good fellow would perhaps have to work six days a week, in order to appropriate to himself the product of one working day. In that case, the bounty of nature would not explain why he now has to work six days a week, or why he must provide five days of surplus labour. It explains only why his necessary labour-time would be limited to one day a week. But in no case would his surplus product arise from some innate, occult quality of human labour." The word "occult" is the clue: Marx is polemicizing against any naturalization of surplus-value. His target is Proudhon, quoted in note 9: "All labour must leave a surplus"—which Marx sarcastically caps with the aside "apparently this is also part of the 'rights and duties of the citizen'."

2.14 Productive Forces of Labour as Productive Forces of Capital

The natural-historical section of the chapter closes with a single, compressed thesis: "Thus both the historically developed productive forces of labour in society, and its naturally conditioned productive forces, appear as productive forces of the capital into which that labour is incorporated." This is the fetishism of capital in its most abstract form: the productive powers of labour, whatever their origin in nature or history, present themselves in the capitalist mode of production as attributes of capital itself. It also sets up the theoretical critique that follows.

2.15 Ricardo's Evasion: Origin versus Magnitude

The final movement of the chapter is a polemic in the history of economic thought. Ricardo "never concerns himself with the origin of surplus-value. He treats it as an entity inherent in the capitalist mode of production, and in his eyes the latter is the natural form of social production." In his discussion of the productivity of labour, "he seeks in it not the cause of the existence of surplus-value, but the cause that determines the magnitude of that value"—a crucial and precise distinction. Ricardo's school, by contrast, "has loudly proclaimed that the productive power of labour is the originating cause of profit (read: surplus-value)." Marx grants this is at least an advance over the Mercantilists, "who derive the excess of the price of a product over its cost of production from the act of exchange, from the sale of the product above its value." But Ricardo's school ultimately "merely evaded the problem rather than solving it." And Marx adds one of his most cutting observations: "In fact, these bourgeois economists instinctively and rightly saw that it was very dangerous to penetrate too deeply into the burning question of the origin of surplus-value."

2.16 The Critique of John Stuart Mill

The chapter's polemical climax takes down John Stuart Mill, who "half a century after Ricardo, solemnly claims superiority over the Mercantilists by clumsily repeating the wretched evasions of Ricardo's earliest vulgarizers." Marx subjects Mill's argument to a step-by-step demolition.

(a) The cause of profit. Mill: "The cause of profit is that labour produces more than is required for its support." Marx: "So far, nothing but the old story."

(b) The duration confusion. Mill continues: "the reason why capital yields a profit, is because food, clothing, materials and tools, last longer than the time which was required to produce them." Marx identifies the error immediately: "Here he confuses the duration of labour-time with the duration of its products." The reductio: "a baker, whose products last only a day, could never extract the same profit from his workers as a machine manufacturer, whose products last for twenty years or more." And the beautifully absurd bird's-nest aside: "it is very true that if a bird's nest did not last longer than the time it takes to build, the birds would have to do without nests."

(c) The exchange error. Mill: "profit arises, not from the incident of exchange, but from the productive power of labour ... If there were no division of employments, there would be no buying or selling, but there would still be profit." Marx's response is withering: "For Mill then, exchange, buying and selling, i.e. the general conditions of capitalist production, are a mere incident, and there would always be profits even without the purchase and sale of labour-power!"

(d) The 20 per cent fallacy. Mill: "If the labourers of the country collectively produce 20 per cent more than their wages, profits will be 20 per cent, whatever prices may or may not be." Marx concedes this is "in one respect, a rare piece of tautology"—since the rate of surplus-value, s/v, is definitionally the ratio of surplus-value to wages (20:100)—but then exposes the confusion between the rate of surplus-value and the rate of profit. "It is absolutely false to say that 'profits will be 20 per cent'. They will always be less, because they are calculated upon the sum total of the capital advanced." Marx's numerical illustration is exact: if the capitalist has advanced £500, of which £400 is laid out in means of production and £100 in wages, and the rate of surplus-value is 20 per cent, then s = £20, and "the rate of profit will be 20:500, i.e. not 20 per cent but 4 per cent." This anticipates the machinery of Volume III, where the rate of profit is s/(c + v) as distinct from the rate of surplus-value, s/v.

(e) The "optical illusion." Mill: "I assume, throughout, the state of things which ... prevails, with few exceptions, universally; namely, that the capitalist advances the whole expenses, including the entire remuneration of the labourer." Marx: "Strange optical illusion, to see everywhere a situation which as yet exists only exceptionally on our earth!" The editorial note is important here: Marx would certainly have altered this passage; the addition in square brackets—"where the labourers and capitalists are separate classes"—was omitted inadvertently by Marx and "clears Mill, at least formally, from the charge of being guilty of an 'optical illusion'." In his letter of 28 November 1878 to N. F. Danielson, Marx proposed replacing the passage beginning "Strange optical illusion" with: "Mr Mill is willing to concede that it is not absolutely necessary for it to be so, even under an economic system where workers and capitalists confront each other as separate classes."

(f) The worker as capitalist. Mill concedes that the capitalist's advancing of the whole remuneration "is not a matter of inherent necessity," and that "the labourer might wait, until the production is complete, for all that part of his wages which exceeds mere necessaries; and even for the whole, if he has funds in hand sufficient for his temporary support. But in the latter case, the labourer is to that extent really a capitalist in the concern, by supplying a portion of the funds necessary for carrying it on." Marx's reductio: "Mill might just as well have said that the worker who advances to himself not only the means of subsistence but also the means of production is in reality his own wage-labourer, or, indeed, that the American peasant is his own slave, because he does forced labour for himself instead of doing it for someone who is his master."

(g) Capitalist production "would still continue to exist even if it did not exist." Marx's summary of Mill's procedure: "After thus proving clearly that capitalist production would still continue to exist even if it did not exist, Mill now proceeds, quite consistently, to show that it would not exist even if it did exist." Mill's final absurdity: even where the worker is a wage-labourer to whom the capitalist advances the whole of his subsistence, "he ... may be looked upon in the same light [i.e., as a capitalist] since, contributing his labour at less than the market price (!), he may be regarded as lending the difference (?) to his employer and receiving it back with interest, etc." Marx's response: "In reality, the worker advances his labour gratuitously to the capitalist during, say, one week, in order to receive its market price at the end of the week, etc.: according to Mill this makes him into a capitalist!"

(h) The closing image. Marx ends the chapter: "On a level plain, simple mounds look like hills; and the insipid flatness of our present bourgeoisie is to be measured by the altitude of its 'great intellects'." Mill, in other words, is a "great intellect" only because the surrounding landscape is so flat.

2.17 The Editorial Note and Marx's Revised Passage

The chapter's penultimate note (Footnote * on p. 653) informs us that Marx—had he had the opportunity—would have altered the "Strange optical illusion" passage to acknowledge Mill's formal anticipation. The letter to Danielson is a fascinating glimpse into Marx's willingness to revise even the most polemical passages of Capital in the interest of accuracy. The editorial apparatus also points us to MEW 23, p. 540 for the textual source. The passage is included here as one of the few places where the reader is shown Marx's own awareness of the limits of his polemic.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The fundamental lessons of Chapter 16 may be summarized as follows. First, categories of political economy are historical, not natural. The concept of "productive labour" is shown to have no stable trans-historical content: under capitalism, to be productive is to produce surplus-value, a definition that both extends (to the collective labourer) and narrows (to the servant of capital). Marx's method here is exemplary: he takes a term that appears technical and neutral—"productive labour"—and demonstrates that it is a garment worn by a social relation. The schoolmaster who enriches the owner of the school is "productive"; the worker who produces only his own subsistence is not. The lesson is that the grammar of economic "productiveness" is a class grammar.

Second, the two forms of surplus-value are dialectically unified but practically distinct. Neither absolute nor relative surplus-value can exist without the other—each presupposes the other's conditions—yet once the capitalist mode of production is universal, the distinction is operative whenever the rate of surplus-value is to be raised. The path of prolonging the working day and the path of increasing productivity/intensity, given a fixed working day, are not interchangeable; they correspond to different historical moments, different technical regimes, and different political conflicts. The dialectic of identity-in-difference is therefore not a scholastic nicety but a tool of concrete analysis.

Third, surplus-labour rests on a historical, not a natural, foundation. Nature provides only possibility—fertile soil, abundant waters, "leisure time"—never the reality of surplus-value. The reality is produced by compulsion, by the social relation that separates the producer from the means of production, and by an "economic soil that is the product of a long process of development." The existing productivity of labour, from which capitalism proceeds, "is a gift, not of nature, but of a history embracing thousands of centuries." This is the theoretical ground on which Marx rejects both naturalism (the physiocrats' land, Proudhon's "all labour must leave a surplus") and the reduction of surplus-value to exchange (the Mercantilists).

Fourth, the critique of political economy is immanent. Ricardo never asks the origin question; he asks only the magnitude question, and he treats the capitalist mode of production as the natural form of social production. His school asserts the productivity of labour as the "originating cause" of profit but thereby only evades the problem. Mill, in turn, converts evasion into positive blunder: he confuses duration of labour with duration of product; he confuses the rate of surplus-value with the rate of profit (the £500/£400/£100/20% example yields 4 per cent, not 20 per cent); he imagines that "in the latter case, the labourer is to that extent really a capitalist in the concern"; and he manages the double miracle of showing that capitalist production "would still continue to exist even if it did not exist" and "would not exist even if it did exist." The polemic is not merely ad hominem: the errors of Mill are systematic, arising from the prior refusal to pose the origin question, and Marx's formal demolitions (the baker, the bird's nest, the American peasant as his own slave, the mounds that look like hills) are weapons in a war over categories, not merely targets of ridicule.

Broader implications. For the theory of Capital as a whole, the chapter secures the dual mechanism of surplus-value extraction that will inform the theory of accumulation (Part VII) and the theory of the wage (Part VI). It also clarifies that the development of capitalism is not a single-track process: the formal subsumption of labour under capital (merely wage-labour under a capitalist) suffices for absolute surplus-value, whereas the real subsumption (a specifically capitalist mode of production) is the condition of relative surplus-value—a distinction that will be of the first importance for the historical account of the transition to capitalism and, in the twentieth century, for debates on the "labour process." Finally, the chapter quietly demonstrates Marx's method: to move from abstract to concrete, from the simple labour process to the capitalist process of production, from natural possibility to historical reality, and from the appearance of identity to the reality of difference. Each of these movements is a model of the dialectical presentation that Capital as a whole enacts, and each carries within it a sharp political-indictment: the "productive worker" is a "misfortune," the "great intellects" of the bourgeoisie are mounds mistaken for hills, and the "felicity" of nature's bounty is a veil over the compulsion of surplus labour.
