Volume 1 • Chapter 10: The Working Day

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem

Chapter 10 of Capital Volume I — "The Working Day" — is the longest single chapter in the entire work, and it pivots on a deceptively simple question that the previous nine chapters had deliberately bracketed: once we know that labour-power is bought and sold at its value, and once we know the mechanics by which surplus-value is extracted, how long is the worker actually made to work? Where Chapters 7–9 established the qualitative logic of surplus-value production (the distinction between necessary and surplus labour-time, the rate of surplus-value), Chapter 10 turns to the quantitative frontier: the length of the working day becomes the terrain on which the whole system of exploitation is contested.

Marx's core thesis is that the working day is not a fixed, naturally given magnitude but a variable, contested one whose determination is the outcome of class struggle, not of economic law. The nature of commodity exchange itself imposes no limits on the length of the working day; the capitalist, in buying labour-power for a day, has the same right to extract the maximum use-value from it as any buyer of any commodity. Yet the peculiar nature of this commodity — that its consumption produces value — means that the worker also has a legitimate claim to insist that it not be consumed beyond its normal duration. Between equal rights, force decides.

The Intellectual Objective

The chapter has four major objectives:

  1. To demonstrate the antinomy of the working day: two parties with reciprocal rights, both derived from the law of exchange, come into collision; the resolution is historically contingent and political, not economic.

  2. To show that surplus labour is not an invention of capitalism — it predates capital, and the comparison with corvée and slavery reveals capitalism's distinctive form of the appetite for surplus labour (unlimited extension of the working day rather than direct physical coercion).

  3. To document — with overwhelming empirical evidence from factory inspectors' reports, parliamentary inquiries, and medical testimony — the actual state of the working day in nineteenth-century England, focusing especially on unregulated branches (potteries, matches, baking, lace, wallpaper) and on the barbarism of the unregulated labour process.

  4. To trace the historical struggle over the working day from the fourteenth-century Statutes of Labourers (which forcibly extended the working day) through the Factory Acts of 1833–64 (which forcibly limited it), demonstrating that "force" — state power — was necessary on both sides, and that the legally limited working day is a hard-won collective achievement of the working class.

Fit Within the Broader Framework

Chapter 10 belongs to Part III of Volume I ("The Production of Absolute Surplus-Value"). Where Chapters 7–9 established that surplus-value can be increased by extending the working day beyond necessary labour-time (absolute surplus-value), Chapter 10 explores the strategy, limits, and struggle surrounding that extension. It is simultaneously:

  • The bridge to Part IV (relative surplus-value), by showing that over-work and industrial decimation force capital to seek productivity increases as an alternative;
  • A historical materialist case study of the transition from pre-capitalist to capitalist exploitation;
  • A foundational statement on the working-class movement: the normal working day is "the product of a protracted and more or less concealed civil war between the capitalist class and the working class," and the English factory workers are "champions, not only of the English working class, but of the modern working class in general."

The chapter is also where Marx most explicitly shows that capital is not merely an economic category but a social relation of force, and that capitalism's vaunted "freedom of labour" resolves, upon inspection, into the worker's legal freedom to sell his entire active life — "his very capacity for labour" — for a subsistence wage.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Fundamental Antinomy: The Working Day as Variable and Contested

Marx opens with a return to first principles. The starting assumption is that labour-power is bought and sold at its value, which is determined, like that of all commodities, by the labour-time necessary to reproduce it. If it takes 6 hours to produce the worker's average daily means of subsistence, then 6 hours is the "necessary" component of the working day. But — and this is the crux — the extent of the working day itself is not thereby given. The necessary labour-time AB is a fixed quantity; the day itself, AC, is not.

Marx formalizes this with a simple but decisive schema:

Working day I: A ——— B–C (7 hours, surplus = 1 hour) Working day II: A ——— B——C (9 hours, surplus = 3 hours) Working day III: A ——— B————C (12 hours, surplus = 6 hours)

BC represents surplus labour-time, and the ratio $BC / AB$ determines the rate of surplus-value. Since AB = 6 hours:

  • Working day I: $BC/AB = 1/6$, rate = 10%;
  • Working day II: $BC/AB = 3/6$, rate = 50%;
  • Working day III: $BC/AB = 6/6$, rate = 100%.

This is why the rate of surplus-value alone cannot tell us the length of the working day. If the rate is 100%, the day might be 8, 10, 12, or more hours — the rate indicates that the two parts are equal in extent but says nothing about their absolute magnitude. The working day is therefore "capable of being determined, but in and for itself indeterminate."

The limits of variation. Although variable, the working day has bounds. The minimum limit cannot be determined in any strict sense: if BC = 0, we have the theoretical minimum — pure necessary labour — but under the capitalist mode of production, this is impossible. The working day can never be reduced to bare necessary labour, because that would abolish surplus-value and with it capitalism itself.

The maximum limit is conditioned by two factors:

  1. Physical limits: within 24 hours, a person can expend only a certain quantum of vital force. Just as a horse can work regularly for only 8 hours a day, so the human organism requires rest, sleep, feeding, washing, clothing.
  2. Moral (social) limits: the worker needs time for intellectual and social requirements, whose extent is "conditioned by the general level of civilization."

These limits are, however, "of a very elastic nature" — and so we find "working days of many different lengths, of 8, 10, 12, 14, 16 and 18 hours."

The rights-based antinomy. Marx now develops the dialectic that gives the chapter its dramatic force. The capitalist has bought labour-power at its daily value; the use-value belongs to him throughout one working day. As "capital personified," his soul is "the soul of capital," and capital's sole driving force is the drive to valorize itself — to absorb the greatest possible quantity of surplus labour. Capital is "dead labour which, vampire-like, lives only by sucking living labour, and lives the more, the more labour it sucks." If the worker consumes his disposable time for himself, "he robs the capitalist."

The capitalist thus takes his stand on the law of commodity-exchange: like all buyers, he seeks maximum benefit from the use-value of his purchase. "The time during which the worker works is the time during which the capitalist consumes the labour-power he has bought."

But then arises the voice of the worker, previously "stifled in the sound and fury of the production process." Marx gives this voice an extended, rhetorically magnificent hearing. The worker's argument runs:

  • The commodity you bought is peculiar: its use creates value, a greater value than it costs. That is why you bought it.
  • What appears to you as self-valorization of capital is, on my side, excess expenditure of labour-power.
  • On the market we know only one law: exchange of commodities. The consumption of the commodity belongs to the buyer, yes — but by the price you pay daily, I must be able to reproduce my labour-power daily, so as to sell it again.
  • Apart from natural deterioration through age, I must be able to work tomorrow with the same normal strength, health and freshness as today.
  • You preach "saving" and "abstinence" — very well, I will husband my sole wealth, my labour-power. I will expend only as much as is compatible with its normal duration and healthy development.
  • The arithmetic of the argument: if the average worker can live (with reasonable work) 30 years, then the daily value of my labour-power is $1/(365 \times 30)$ of its total value — i.e., $1/10{,}950$. If you consume it in 10 years, you pay only $1/3{,}650$ instead of $3/3{,}650$ — i.e., only one-third of its daily value — and you therefore rob me every day of two-thirds of the value of my commodity.
  • You pay me for one day's labour-power while you use three days of it. "That is against our contract and the law of commodity exchange."

The worker demands a normal working day, not on grounds of sentiment — "in money matters sentiment is out of place" — but because, like every other seller, he demands the value of his commodity. He may concede that the capitalist is a model citizen, a member of the R.S.P.C.A., in the odour of sanctity — but "the thing you represent when you come face to face with me has no heart in its breast. What seems to throb there is my own heartbeat."

The resolution: force. Both parties are right by the standards of commodity exchange. There is "an antinomy, of right against right, both equally bearing the seal of the law of exchange. Between equal rights, force decides. Hence, in the history of capitalist production, the establishment of a norm for the working day presents itself as a struggle over the limits of that day, a struggle between collective capital, i.e., the class of capitalists, and collective labour, i.e., the working class."

This is the theoretical keystone of the chapter: the working day is the locus where the purely economic analysis of surplus-value is revealed as inadequate, and where class struggle — a political, historical, and ultimately coercive force — enters as the determinant.

2.2 Surplus Labour Across Modes of Production: The Boyar and the Manufacturer

Having established the antinomy, Marx pivots to a comparative and historical analysis. Capital did not invent surplus labour. Wherever a part of society monopolizes the means of production, the worker — "free or unfree" — must add to the labour-time necessary for his own maintenance an extra quantity in order to produce the means of subsistence for the owner. Marx lists the historical forms of this appropriation: "an Athenian aristocrat, an Etruscan theocrat, a civis romanus, a Norman baron, an American slave-owner, a Wallachian boyar, a modern landlord or a capitalist."

The principle of variation. In economic formations where use-value rather than exchange-value predominates, surplus labour is restricted by a "more or less confined set of needs" — no boundless thirst for surplus labour arises from the character of production itself. Hence in antiquity over-work becomes "frightful" only when the aim is to obtain exchange-value in its independent monetary form — i.e., in the production of gold and silver. There, the recognized form of over-work is "forced labour until death" (Marx cites Diodorus Siculus on the Egyptian gold mines, where "no indulgence, no forbearance for the sick, the feeble, the aged... all, forced by blows, must work on until death").

The world-market catalyst. As soon as peoples whose production still moves within the lower forms of slave-labour, corvée, etc., are drawn into a world market dominated by the capitalist mode of production — whereby the sale of their products for export becomes their principal interest — "the civilized horrors of over-work are grafted onto the barbaric horrors of slavery, serfdom, etc."

Marx's key example is Negro labour in the southern United States. As long as production was chiefly directed to local needs, it preserved a "moderately patriarchal character." But as the export of cotton became vital, the over-working of the Negro — "and sometimes the consumption of his life in seven years of labour" — became "a factor in a calculated and calculating system." It was no longer a question of obtaining a certain quantity of useful products, but "the production of surplus-value itself." The logic of the corvée in the Danubian Principalities is the same.

The corvée as analytically transparent surplus labour. Marx turns to a sustained comparison between the Danubian Principalities and English factories, because the corvée presents surplus labour in "an independent and immediately perceptible form." If the working day consists of 6 hours necessary + 6 hours surplus, the free worker gives the capitalist $6 \times 6 = 36$ hours of surplus labour per week — equivalent to working 3 days for himself and 3 days gratis. But this fact is not directly visible: surplus and necessary labour are mingled. One can express the relation by saying that in every minute the worker works 30 seconds for himself and 30 seconds for the capitalist — but the two moments are inseparable in time and space.

With the corvée, by contrast, the necessary labour the Wallachian peasant performs for his own maintenance is distinctly marked off from his surplus labour for the boyar. One is performed on his own field, the other on the seignorial estate. Both parts of labour-time exist independently, side by side. In the corvée, surplus labour is "accurately marked off" from necessary labour. This changes nothing in the quantitative ratio — three days' surplus labour in the week remain three days yielding no equivalent — but it makes it visible.

The different form of the appetite. In the capitalist, the appetite for surplus labour appears as the drive for an unlimited extension of the working day. In the boyar, it appears more simply as "a direct hunt for days of corvée." This is a crucial analytical distinction: the quantitative relation of surplus to necessary labour can be identical while the form of exploitation differs. The specific difference of capitalism is that it does not need direct coercion to secure surplus labour — the wage relation, embedded in a mature market society, does the work — but it requires constant state intervention to limit the working day.

The Romanian case. Marx traces the historical development of the corvée in the Romanian provinces. Their original mode of production was based on communal property — but not in the Slav or Indian form. Part of the land was cultivated independently as free private property by commune members; another part — the ager publicus — was cultivated in common. The products of common labour served partly as a reserve fund against bad harvests, partly as a "state treasury" for war, religion, and communal expenses. In time, military and clerical dignitaries usurped the communal land and, along with it, the obligations owed to it. The labour of free peasants on communal land was transformed into corvée performed for the thieves who had taken that land.

This corvée soon developed into a servile relationship in point of fact though not legally, until Russia — "the liberator of the world" — "raised it to the level of a law on the pretext of abolishing serfdom." The Reglement organique of 1831, proclaimed by the Russian General Kiselev (who was the Russian viceroy during the occupation of 1828–1834, and himself a boyar-chosen instrument), codified the corvée. Marx's ironic note: "Thus, at one stroke, Russia both conquered the magnates of the Danubian Principalities and earned the applause of cretinous liberals throughout Europe."

The arithmetic of the corvée. According to the Reglement organique, every Wallachian peasant owes the "so-called landlord," besides payments in kind:

  • (1) 12 days of labour in general;
  • (2) 1 day of field labour;
  • (3) 1 day of wood-carrying.

This appears to be 14 days per year. But "with deep insight into political economy, the working day is not taken in its ordinary sense, but as the working day necessary to the production of an average daily product" — and that average daily product is determined "in such a sly manner that even a Cyclops would be unable to finish the job within 24 hours."

The Reglement dryly declares that by 12 working days one must understand the product of 36 days of manual labour; by 1 day of field labour, 3 days; by 1 day of wood-carrying, likewise 3 times as much. The sum total is now 42 days of corvée.

To this is added the jobbagio — service due to the lord for emergency requirements. Every village must furnish annually a definite contingent proportional to population. This additional corvée is estimated at 14 days per Wallachian peasant.

Thus the prescribed corvée amounts to 56 working days per year. But because of the severe climate, the agricultural year in Wallachia numbers only 210 days, of which:

  • 40 for Sundays and holidays,
  • 30 on average for bad weather,
  • total 70 days not counted,
  • 140 working days remain.

The ratio of the corvée to necessary labour is $56/84$ — that is, $66\tfrac{2}{3}$ percent. Marx notes this is a much smaller rate of surplus-value than that regulating the English agricultural labourer or factory worker. But this is only the legally prescribed corvée.

The liberal spirit of evasion. The Reglement organique was "able to facilitate its own evasion" in a spirit "yet more liberal than the English Factory Acts." After making 56 days out of 12, the nominal day's work of each of the 56 corvée days is so arranged that a portion must fall on the next day. For instance, an amount of land must be weeded in one day which would require twice as much time. The legal day's work for some kinds of agricultural labour can be interpreted so that "the day begins in the month of May and ends in the month of October." For Moldavia the regulations are even stricter: "'The 12 corvée days of the Reglement organique,' cried a boyar, drunk with victory, 'amount to 365 days in the year.'"

The English Factory Acts as negative expression. If the Reglement organique is the positive expression of the appetite for surplus labour — every paragraph legalizing it — the English Factory Acts are the negative expression of the same appetite. These laws curb capital's drive toward limitless draining of labour-power by forcibly limiting the working day "on the authority of the state, but a state ruled by capitalist and landlord."

Why did the state intervene? Marx identifies three forces:

  1. The daily more threatening advance of the working-class movement;
  2. The same necessity that forced the manuring of English fields with guano — the exhaustion of the soil in one case, the seizure of the vital force of the nation at its roots in the other;
  3. Periodical epidemics and the diminishing military standard of height in France and Germany — evidence cited from Liebig and others that conscript fitness was declining. Marx gives the data: in France the minimum height for infantry fell from 165 cm (pre-1789) to 157 cm (1818) to 156 cm (1832); in Saxony from 178 cm (1780) to 155; in Prussia 157. In Prussia, over nine years, 716 of every 1,000 conscripts were unfit — 317 for deficiency of height, 399 for bodily defects.

The specific form of the English Factory Act of 1850 (in force when Marx wrote, 1867). The Act allows 10 hours for the average working day: the first five days, 12 hours from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., including half an hour for breakfast and an hour for dinner, leaving $10\tfrac{1}{2}$ working hours; and 8 hours for Saturday, 6 a.m. to 2 p.m., minus half an hour for breakfast. Total: 60 working hours — $10\tfrac{1}{2}$ for each of the first five days, $7\tfrac{1}{2}$ for the last.

Factory inspectors as statistical witnesses. Marx notes that the guardians of these laws — factory inspectors directly under the Home Secretary, whose reports are published every six months by order of Parliament — provide "regular and official statistics of the voracious appetite of the capitalists for surplus labour." He quotes Inspector L. Horner's famous computation of "petty pilferings of minutes":

  • Before 6 a.m.: 15 minutes
  • After 6 p.m.: 15 minutes
  • At breakfast time: 10 minutes
  • At dinner time: 20 minutes
  • Total for five days: 60 minutes per day → 300 minutes
  • On Saturday before 6 a.m.: 15 minutes
  • At breakfast time: 10 minutes
  • After 2 p.m.: 15 minutes
  • Saturday total: 40 minutes
  • Weekly total: 340 minutes = 5 hours 40 minutes

Multiplied by 50 working weeks per year (allowing two for holidays and stoppages), this equals 27 working days of stolen time. Or, as another inspector notes, "Five minutes a day's increased work, multiplied by weeks, are equal to two and a half days of produce in the year"; and "an additional hour a day gained by small instalments... is nearly equivalent to working 13 months in the year."

The counter-cyclical intensification. Marx observes a counterintuitive phenomenon: crises, during which production is interrupted and factories work "short time," do not affect the tendency to extend the working day. On the contrary — "the less business there is, the more profit has to be made on the business done. The less time spent in work, the more of that time has to be turned into surplus labour-time." During the crisis of 1857–58, Horner reported: "It may seem inconsistent that there should be any over-working at a time when trade is so bad; but that very badness leads to the transgression by unscrupulous men, they get the extra profit of it." In Horner's district, 122 mills were given up, 143 were found standing, "yet over-work is continued beyond the legal hours."

The same occurred during the cotton crisis of 1861–65. The excuse offered was that workers would not leave the mill at the appointed hour, and compulsion was necessary — especially on Saturday afternoons, when machinery had to be cleaned. Marx's rebuttal: "if the hands remain in a factory after the machinery has ceased to revolve... they would not have been so employed if sufficient time had been set apart specially for cleaning, etc., either before 6 a.m. or before 2 p.m. on Saturday afternoons."

The legal arithmetic of evasion. Marx quotes the inspectors' summary: the small amounts of penalty and costs mean that manufacturers calculate "upon the chance of not being found out; and when they see the small amount of penalty and costs, which those who have been convicted have had to pay, they find that if they should be detected there will still be a considerable balance of gain." Where the additional time is gained "by a multiplication of small thefts in the course of the day, there are insuperable difficulties to the inspectors making out a case."

These small thefts — described by inspectors as "petty pilferings of minutes," "snatching a few minutes," and in workers' technical language as "nibbling and cribbling at meal-times" — are the micro-physics of surplus-value extraction. The famous maxim: "Moments are the elements of profit."

The classification of workers as persons. Marx notes the characteristic designation of workers as "full-timers" and children under 13 as "half-timers" — the worker "is here nothing more than personified labour-time. All individual distinctions are obliterated in that between 'full-timers' and 'half-timers.'"

2.3 Branches of English Industry Without Legal Limits to Exploitation

Marx now turns from the regulated textile districts to the "free" branches — those where exploitation was still (or had recently been) unfettered. These sections provide the most horrifying empirical evidence of the chapter.

Lace trade (Nottingham, 1860). Reading from the Daily Telegraph, Marx quotes the statement of Mr Broughton Charlton, county magistrate, chairman of a meeting at the Assembly Rooms, Nottingham, 14 January 1860: "there was an amount of privation and suffering among that portion of the population connected with the lace trade, unknown in other parts of the kingdom, indeed, in the civilized world... Children of nine or ten years are dragged from their squalid beds at two, three, or four o'clock in the morning and compelled to work for a bare subsistence until ten, eleven, or twelve at night, their limbs wearing away, their frames dwindling, their faces whitening, and their humanity absolutely sinking into a stone-like torpor."

The Rev. Montagu Valpy called the system "one of unmitigated slavery, socially, physically, morally, and spiritually." The meeting petitioned "that the period of labour for men shall be diminished to eighteen hours a day" — a demand Marx contrasts bitterly with the British horror at Virginian and Carolinian cotton planters: "Is their black-market, their lash, and their barter of human flesh more detestable than this slow sacrifice of humanity which takes place in order that veils and collars may be fabricated for the benefit of capitalists?"

Potteries of Staffordshire. Three parliamentary inquiries over 22 years (Scriven 1841, Greenhow 1860, Longe 1862/63) documented the conditions. Marx quotes the depositions of child workers:

  • William Wood, 9 years old (began at 7 years 10 months), ran moulds from 6 a.m. to about 9 p.m., six days a week — "I work till 9 o'clock at night six days in the week. I have done so for the last seven or eight weeks." Fifteen hours of labour for a child of 7½.
  • J. Murray, 12: "I turn jigger and run moulds. I come at 6. Sometimes I come at 4. I worked all night last night, till 6 o'clock this morning. I have not been in bed since the night before last. There were eight or nine other boys working last night. All but one have come this morning. I get 3 shillings and sixpence. I do not get any more for working at night. I worked two nights last week."
  • Fernyhough, 10: "I have not always an hour [for dinner]. I have only half an hour sometimes: on Thursday, Friday, and Saturday."

The medical verdict. Dr Greenhow states that the average life-expectancy in the pottery districts of Stoke-on-Trent and Wolstanton is "extraordinarily low." Although only 36.6% of the male population over 20 are employed in the potteries in Stoke, and 30.4% in Wolstanton, more than half the deaths among men of that age in the first district, and nearly two-fifths in the second, are the result of pulmonary diseases among the potters.

Dr Boothroyd (Hanley): "Each successive generation of potters is more dwarfed and less robust than the preceding one." Mr McBean: "Since I began to practise among the potters 25 years ago, I have observed a marked degeneration, especially shown in diminution of stature and breadth."

Dr J. T. Arledge (senior physician, North Staffordshire Infirmary): "The potters as a class, both men and women, represent a degenerated population, both physically and morally. They are, as a rule, stunted in growth, ill-shaped, and frequently ill-formed in the chest; they become prematurely old, and are certainly short-lived; they are phlegmatic and bloodless, and exhibit their debility of constitution by obstinate attacks of dyspepsia, and disorders of the liver and kidneys, and by rheumatism. But of all diseases they are especially prone to chest-disease, to pneumonia, phthisis, bronchitis, and asthma. One form would appear peculiar to them, and is known as potter's asthma, or potter's consumption. Scrofula attacking the glands, or bones, or other parts of the body, is a disease of two-thirds or more of the potters... That the 'degenerescence' of the population of this district is not even greater than it is, is due to the constant recruiting from the adjacent country, and intermarriages with more healthy races."

Mr Charles Parsons, House Surgeon: "my indignation has been aroused again and again at the sight of poor children whose health has been sacrificed to gratify the avarice of either parents or employers." He sums up the causes of disease in the phrase "long hours."

Match manufacture. Dates from 1833 (discovery of applying phosphorus to the match itself). Since 1845 it developed rapidly in England. It brought with it tetanus — discovered in 1845 by a Vienna doctor to be peculiar to match makers. Half the workers are children under 13 and young persons under 18. The trade has "such a bad reputation that only the most miserable part of the working class, half-starved widows and so forth, deliver up their children to it" — "ragged, half-starved, untaught children." Of the witnesses examined by Commissioner White (1863), 270 were under 18, fifty under 10, ten only 8, and five only 6 years old. Working day: 12 to 14 or 15 hours, night-labour, irregular meal-times, meals mostly taken in the workrooms themselves, "pestilent with phosphorus." Marx: "Dante would have found the worst horrors in his Inferno surpassed in this industry."

Wallpaper manufacture. Coarser sorts printed by machine, finer by hand (block printing). Most active months: October to April. Work often lasts, almost uninterruptedly, from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. or further into the night. Depositions:

  • J. Leach: "Last winter six out of nineteen girls were away from ill-health at one time from over-work. I have to bawl at them to keep them awake."
  • W. Duffy: "I have seen when the children could none of them keep their eyes open for the work; indeed, none of us could."
  • J. Lightbourne, 13: "We worked last winter till 9 [evening], and the winter before till 10. I used to cry with sore feet every night last winter."
  • G. Apsden: "That boy of mine... when he was 7 years old I used to carry him on my back to and fro through the snow, and he used to have 16 hours a day... I have often knelt down to feed him as he stood by the machine, for he could not leave it or stop."

The managing partner's own words. Mr Smith, of a Manchester factory: "We work on, with no stoppage for meals, so that the day's work of 10½ hours is finished by 4.30 p.m., and all after that is overtime." Marx's sarcastic aside: "(Does this Mr Smith take no meals himself during 10½ hours?)" Smith adds: "we seldom leave off working before 6 p.m.... we are really working overtime the whole year round... For all these, children and adults alike (152 children and young persons and 140 adults), the average work for the last 18 months has been at the very least 7 days, 5 hours, or 78½ hours a week. For the six weeks ending 2 May this year (1862), the average was higher — 8 days or 84 hours a week." And yet Smith smirks: "Machine-work is not great." Similarly, employers in block printing: "Hand labour is more healthy than machine-work." And Mr Otley, manager of a wallpaper factory: a clause allowing work between 6 a.m. and 9 p.m. "would suit us(!) very well, but the factory hours, 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., are not suitable. Our machine is always stopped for dinner." Marx: "(What generosity!)"

The logic of the meal-time. Marx exposes the manufacturers' argument that children may eat "as coal and water are supplied to the steam-engine, soap to wool, oil to the wheel — namely during the process of production itself, as merely auxiliary material for the instruments of labour." The Commission's report counters that the fear of "losing time" — that is, the time for appropriating the labour of others — is not a sufficient reason.

Baking. Marx calls baking "the most archaic, pre-Christian" of English trades — a method of production unchanged since the Roman Empire (the practice of making bread by machinery had only recently begun to make its way). Capital, in its early stages, "at first indifferent towards the technical character of the labour process it seizes control of. At the outset, it takes it as it finds it."

The adulteration of bread, especially in London, was revealed by the Committee on the adulteration of articles of food (1855–56) and Dr Hassall's Adulterations Detected. The consequence was the Act of 6 August 1860, "an inoperative law." Marx notes that "alum, either finely powdered or mixed with salt, is a normal article of commerce bearing the significant name of 'baker's stuff.'" And in a footnote on the general prevalence of "sophistication," he cites Chevallier's catalogue: 6 kinds of adulteration of sugar, 9 of olive oil, 10 of butter, 12 of salt, 19 of milk, 20 of bread, 23 of brandy, 24 of meal, 28 of chocolate, 30 of wine, 32 of coffee. "Even God Almighty does not escape this fate."

The journeymen bakers' conditions. From Tremenheere's report (1862) and the Second Report (1863): The London journeyman baker begins work at about 11 p.m. He "makes the dough" — a laborious process lasting from half an hour to three-quarters of an hour. He then lies down on the kneading-board and sleeps for about two hours. He then engages in rapid, continuous labour for about five hours — throwing out the dough, "scaling it off," moulding, putting into the oven, taking out the batch, etc. The bakehouse temperature ranges from 75° to 90°+ F. After the bread is made, distribution begins — many hours on foot carrying baskets or wheeling hand-carts. During "the London season," full-priced West End bakers generally begin at 11 p.m. and work through to 8 a.m.; the men then carry bread until 4, 5, 6, or even 7 p.m. "They may have, after they have done their work, sometimes 5 or 6, sometimes only four or five hours' sleep before they begin again." On Fridays they begin at 10 p.m., continuing to 8 p.m. Saturday, or more generally to 4 or 5 a.m. Sunday. On Sundays they must attend twice or three times to make preparations.

The "underselling masters" (three-fourths of London bakers) have it worse: the men begin Thursday night at 10 p.m. and continue with only slight intermission until late Saturday evening.

The classical political economy of the baking trade. Marx quotes George Read's History of Baking (1848): "The unpaid labour of the men was made the source whereby the competition was carried on." And a full-priced baker denounces his underselling competitors: "They only exist now by first defrauding the public, and next getting 18 hours' work out of their men for 12 hours' wages."

The adulteration of bread and the formation of a class of underselling bakers developed in England since the beginning of the eighteenth century — as soon as the corporate character of the trade was lost and "the capitalist stepped behind the nominal master baker in the shape of a miller or a flour factor." This laid the foundation for capitalist production, the unlimited extension of the working day, and night work.

The verdict. The Commission's report classes journeymen bakers among "the short-lived workers, who, having by good luck escaped the normal decimation of the children of the working class, rarely reach the age of 42." Yet the trade is always "overwhelmed with applicants" — from Scotland, the agricultural districts of the West of England, and Germany.

The bakeries of Ireland. Marx turns to the Irish bakers' agitation (1858–60). Their movement — supported by the public with "typically Irish warmth" — successfully established a rule of exclusive day-labour in Wexford, Kilkenny, Clonmel, Waterford. In Limerick, however, the master bakers (especially the miller bakers) defeated the movement. The Limerick example led to retrogression in Ennis and Tipperary. In Cork, the masters exercised their power of turning the men out of employment. In Dublin, the master bakers "have succeeded in leading the men into acquiescence in Sunday work and night work, contrary to the convictions of the men."

The English government's Committee — a government "armed to the teeth" in Ireland — merely "remonstrates, in funereal tones." Its argument is worth quoting at length, because it summarizes the bourgeois rationale for a somewhat limited working day: "The Committee believe that the hours of labour are limited by natural laws, which cannot be violated with impunity. That for master bakers to induce their workmen, by the fear of losing employment, to violate their religious convictions and their better feelings, to disobey the laws of the land, and to disregard public opinion... is calculated to provoke ill-feeling between workmen and masters... and affords an example dangerous to religion, morality, and social order... any constant work beyond 12 hours a day encroaches on the domestic and private life of the working man, and so leads to disastrous moral results... That work beyond 12 hours has a tendency to undermine the health of the working man, and so leads to premature old age and death."

Scotland and the railways. Marx moves north: the Scottish agricultural labourer protests against his 13–14 hours' work in a very severe climate, with 4 hours' additional work on Sunday — "in that land of Sabbatarians!" In London, simultaneously, three railwaymen — a guard, an engine-driver, a signalman — are up before a coroner's jury after a tremendous railway accident that dispatched hundreds of passengers to the next world. They testified that ten or twelve years before, their labour lasted only 8 hours a day. In the last five or six years it had been "screwed up to 14, 18 and 20 hours," and during holiday pressure often "40 or 50 hours without a break." Marx: "They are ordinary men, not Cyclops. At a certain point their labour-power ran out. Torpor seized them. Their brains stopped thinking, their eyes stopped seeing."

The "respectable British juryman" sent them to the Assizes on a charge of manslaughter, while piously hoping the capitalist railway magnates would in future be more "extravagant" in purchasing labour-power. Marx cites the reply of a North Staffordshire worker: a fireman on duty 14 hours 50 minutes, then recalled before he could drink his tea, then on duty 14 hours 25 minutes — total 29 hours 15 minutes without intermission; the week's total: 88 hours 40 minutes. He was paid for 6½ days and refused extra compensation beyond 10d.

The milliner and the blacksmith. Two final figures "whose striking contrast proves that all men are alike in the face of capital."

  • Mary Anne Walkley, 20 years old, employed in a "highly respectable" dressmaking establishment kept by "a lady with the pleasant name of Elise." In the last week of June 1863, the London papers published the story: "Death from simple over-work." The girls work on average 16½ hours without a break, during the season often 30. The height of the season required magnificent dresses for the ball in honor of the newly imported Princess of Wales. Mary Anne Walkley had worked uninterruptedly for 26½ hours, with sixty other girls, thirty in each room. The rooms provided only ⅓ of the necessary quantity of air. At night the girls slept in pairs in "stifling holes" divided by wooden partitions. She fell ill Friday, died Sunday — without, to the astonishment of Madame Elise, having finished the bit of finery. The doctor, Mr Keys, deposed: "Mary Anne Walkley died from long hours of work in an overcrowded work-room, and a too small and badly ventilated bedroom." The coroner's jury brought in a verdict that she died of apoplexy, "but there was reason to fear that her death had been accelerated by over-work." Even the Morning Star — organ of Cobden and Bright — exclaimed: "Our white slaves, who are toiled into the grave, for most part silently pine and die."

  • The blacksmith. "If the poets were true, there is no man so hearty, so merry as the blacksmith" — rising early, striking sparks before the sun, eating, drinking, sleeping. Working in moderation he is in one of the best of human positions, physically. But in the city, "the stress of work on that strong man" — in Marylebone, blacksmiths die at 31 per thousand per annum, or 11 above the mean for male adults of the country. "He can strike so many blows per day, walk so many steps, breathe so many breaths, produce so much work, and live an average, say, of fifty years; he is made to strike so many more blows, to walk so many more steps, to breathe so many more breaths per day, and to increase altogether a fourth of his life. He meets the effort; the result is, that producing for a limited time a fourth more work, he dies at 37 for 50."

2.4 Day-Work, Night-Work, and the Shift System

Marx now turns to the question of the natural day and the drive to appropriate labour throughout the entire 24 hours.

The economic logic. Constant capital — the means of production — exists, from the standpoint of valorization, only in order to absorb labour and, with every drop of labour, a proportional quantity of surplus labour. If the means of production fail to do this, "their mere existence forms a loss for the capitalist" — they represent a useless advance of capital while they lie fallow. This loss becomes positive as soon as interruption necessitates additional outlay when work begins again.

The impossibility of individual 24-hour labour. Capitalist production therefore drives, "by its inherent nature, towards the appropriation of labour throughout the whole of the 24 hours in the day." But since it is physically impossible to exploit the same individual labour-power constantly, night and day, capital must overcome this obstacle through alternation between labour-powers used by day and by night. This is the shift-system — "this alternation of two sets of workers."

Marx notes that the shift-system "predominated in the full-blooded springtime of the English cotton industry" and still flourished in the cotton-spinning factories of the Moscow gubernia. The 24-hour process exists as a system in many "as yet 'free'" branches of industry in Britain — in blast-furnaces, forges, rolling mills, and other metallurgical establishments. Here the labour process includes a great part of the 24 hours of Sunday, in addition to the 24 hours of the six working days. Workers include men and women, adults and children of both sexes; ages run from 8 (in some cases 6) to 18. In some branches, girls and women work through the night with the male personnel.

The medical evidence on night-work. Marx quotes a steel manufacturer: "It seems but natural that boys who work at night cannot sleep and get proper rest by day, but will be running about." Dr W. Strange on sunlight: "Light also acts upon the tissues of the body directly in hardening them and supporting their elasticity. The muscles of animals, when they are deprived of a proper amount of light, become soft and inelastic, the nervous power loses its tone from defective stimulation, and the elaboration of all growth seems to be perverted... In the case of children, constant access to plenty of light during the day, and to the direct rays of the sun for a part of it, is most essential to health." And in a letter to Commissioner White: "I have had opportunities formerly, when in Lancashire, of observing the effects of night-work upon children, and I have no hesitation in saying, contrary to what some employers were fond of asserting, those who were subjected to it soon suffered in their health."

The over-work beyond the already-excessive norm. Marx quotes the official English report on boys of 9 to 12: "It is impossible for any mind to realize the amount of work described in the following passages as being performed by boys of from 9 to 12 years of age... without coming irresistibly to the conclusion that such abuses of the power of parents and of employers can no longer be allowed to exist." Examples:

  • A boy at a rolling-mill where the proper hours were 6 a.m. to 5.30 p.m. worked four nights every week till 8.30 p.m. at least — for six months.
  • Another, at 9 years old, sometimes made three 12-hour shifts running; at 10, two days and two nights running.
  • A boy of 10 worked from 6 a.m. to 12 p.m. three nights, and till 9 p.m. the other nights.
  • Another, 13, worked from 6 p.m. till 12 noon next day, for a week at a time, sometimes three shifts together, e.g., Monday morning to Tuesday night.
  • Another, 12, worked in an iron foundry at Staveley from 6 a.m. to 12 p.m. for a fortnight on end; "could not do it any more."
  • George Allinsworth, age 9, came as cellar-boy Friday; next morning began at 3, "so I stopped here all night. Live five miles off. Slept on the floor of the furnace, over head, with an apron under me, and a bit of a jacket over me. The two other days I have been here at 6 a.m."

The illiteracy and degraded mind of the "labour-powers." Marx quotes the disturbing dialogues with one of the Commissioners. Jeremiah Haynes, 12: "Four times four is eight; four fours are sixteen. A king is him that has all the money and gold. We have a King (told it is a Queen), they call her the Princess Alexandra... A Princess is a man." William Turner, 12: "Don't live in England. Think it is a country, but didn't know before." John Morris, 14: "Have heard say that God made the world, and that all the people was drowned but one; heard say that one was a little bird." William Smith, 15: "God made man, man made woman." Edward Taylor, 15: "Do not know of London." Henry Matthewman, 17: "Had been to chapel, but missed a good many times lately. One name that they preached about was Jesus Christ, but I cannot say any others, and I cannot tell anything about him. He was not killed, but died like other people." "The devil is a good person. I don't know where he lives." "Christ was a wicked man." One girl spelt God as dog and did not know the name of the queen.

In the paper factories, night-work is the rule for all processes except rag-sorting. In some cases night-work is carried on incessantly through the whole week, by means of shifts, from Sunday night until midnight of the following Saturday. Various relay patterns exist — the 12-hour system, the 24-hour system, the "intermediate" system where all workers work 15 or 16 hours every day. Children under 13, young persons under 18, and women work under this night system. In glazing, girls of 12 work 14 hours a day for the whole month, "without any regular relief or cessation beyond two, or, at most, three breaks of half an hour each for meals."

The self-representation of capital. Marx now gives what he calls capital's own "regard" for the 24-hour system — in its "normal" form. Messrs Naylor and Vickers, steel manufacturers (600–700 workers): "The boys do not suffer from the heat. The temperature is probably from 86 degrees to 90 degrees... About twenty of the boys under the age of 18 work in the night sets... We could not well do without lads under 18 working by night. The objection would be the increase in the cost of production... of lads we could get any number... But from the small proportion of boys that we employ, the subject is of little importance or interest to us."

Messrs John Brown & Co. (Mr J. Ellis): "I do not think it would be very objectionable to require that no person under the age of 18 should work more than 12 hours in the 24. But we do not think that any line could be drawn over the age of 12, at which boys could be dispensed with for night-work. But we would sooner be prevented from employing boys under the age of 13, or even so high as 14, at all, than not be allowed to employ boys that we do have at night." Marx's gloss: "the increase of expense... would be more than the trade, with due regard to its being successfully carried out, could fairly bear." Marx translates: "Labour is scarce here, and might fall short if there were such a regulation" — "(In other words, Ellis, or Brown & Co., might be subjected to the fatal embarrassment of having to pay labour-power at its full value.)"

Cammell & Co.'s "Cyclopean Steel and Iron Works" — the managing director's written evidence, later suppressed on revision, recalled by Mr White: prohibition of night-labour of children and young persons "would be impossible, it would be tantamount to stopping their works," and yet their business employs little more than 6% of boys under 18, and less than 1% under 13.

Sanderson's confession of the real reason. E. F. Sanderson: "Great difficulty would be caused by preventing boys under 18 from working at night. The chief would be the increase of cost from employing men instead of boys... probably it would not be enough to enable the manufacturers to raise the price of steel, and consequently it would fall on them, as of course the men (how wrong-headed these people are!) would refuse to pay it." Boys get 4s. to 5s. a week. "The boys' work is of a kind for which the strength of boys is generally... quite sufficient, and consequently there would be no gain in the greater strength of the men to counterbalance the loss... The men would not like so well not to have boys under them, as men would be less obedient. Besides, boys must begin young to learn the trade. Leaving day-work alone open to boys would not answer the purpose."

Why could the boys not learn by day? "Owing to the men working days and nights in alternate weeks, the men would be separated half the time from their boys, and would lose half the profit which they make from them. The training which they give to an apprentice is considered as part of the return for the boys' labour, and thus enables the men to get it at a cheaper rate. Each man would want half of this profit." In other words, Messrs Sanderson would have to pay part of the adult men's wages out of their own pockets instead of by the night-work of the boys — their profit would fall.

Sanderson's final argument. Night-work on men alone would be intolerable; the difficulties would lead to total suppression of night-work. "As far as the work itself is concerned, this would suit as well, but —" Marx comments: "But Messrs Sanderson have something else to make besides steel. Steel-making is simply a pretext for profit-making. The steel furnaces, rolling-mills, etc., the buildings, machinery, iron, coal, etc., have something more to do than transform themselves into steel. They are there to absorb surplus labour, and they naturally absorb more in 24 hours than in 12... as soon as there is an interruption in their function of absorbing labour, they lose their character as capital, and are therefore a pure loss for the Sandersons."

Sanderson: "there would be the loss from so much expensive machinery, lying idle half the time, and to get through the amount of work which we are able to do on the present system, we should have to double our premises and plant, which would double the outlay." Marx replies: "But why should these Sandersons pretend to a privilege not enjoyed by the other capitalists who only work during the day, and whose buildings, machinery, raw material, therefore lie 'idle' during the night?" Sanderson's answer: "It is true that there is this loss from machinery lying idle in those manufactories in which work only goes on by day. But the use of furnaces would involve a further loss in our case. If they were kept up these would be a waste of fuel (instead of the present waste of the living substance of the workers) and if they were not, there would be loss of time in laying the fires and getting the heat up (whereas a loss of sleeping time, even that of 8-year-olds, is a gain of working time for the Sanderson clan), and the furnaces themselves would suffer from the changes of temperature (whereas those same furnaces suffer nothing from the alternation of day-work and night-work)."

The glass manufacturers' tenderness. Marx quotes Commissioner White's rebuttal of the glass manufacturers, who maintained that "regular meal-times" for children were impossible because they would cause a "pure loss" or a "waste" of heat radiated by the furnaces: "A certain amount of heat beyond what is usual at present might also be going to waste, if meal-times were secured in these cases, but it seems likely not equal in money-value to the waste of animal power now going on in glass-houses throughout the kingdom from growing boys not having enough quiet time to eat their meals at ease, with a little rest afterwards for digestion." Marx: "And this in 1865, 'the year of progress'!" Children in the glass-sheds walk 15 to 20 miles in every 6 hours, performing their work continuously; often the work lasts for 14 or 15 hours. In the 6-hour shift system, the unbroken period is at most 6 hours, from which must come time spent in coming and going, washing, dressing, and meals — "leaving a very short period indeed for rest, and none for fresh air and play, unless at the expense of the sleep necessary for young boys."

The vignette closes with a brilliant satirical image: "Meanwhile, late at night perhaps, Mr Glass-Capital, stuffed full with abstinence, and primed with port wine, reels home from his club, droning out idiotically 'Britons never, never shall be slaves!'"

2.5 The Historic Battle: From Statutory Extension to Statutory Limitation (14th–17th c.)

Marx now turns to the historical struggle, and identifies a central paradox:

Section 5 establishes the opposite tendency of early legislation. From the middle of the fourteenth to well into the middle of the eighteenth century, English Labour Statutes compulsorily lengthened the working day. Modern Factory Acts compulsorily shorten it. "While the modern Factory Acts compulsorily shorten the working day, the earlier statutes tried forcibly to lengthen it."

Why the difference? In its embryonic state, when capital "cannot yet use the sheer force of economic relations to secure its right to absorb a sufficient quantity of surplus labour," it must be aided by the power of the state. In its adult condition, the state becomes an obstacle. "Centuries are required before the 'free' worker, owing to the greater development of the capitalist mode of production, makes a voluntary agreement, i.e., is compelled by social conditions to sell the whole of his active life, his very capacity for labour, in return for the price of his customary means of subsistence, to sell his birthright for a mess of pottage." Hence the longer working day imposed by state power from the mid-fourteenth to the late seventeenth centuries is approximately the same length as the shorter working day which, in the second half of the nineteenth century, the state has here and there interposed as a barrier.

The Massachusetts comparison. "What has now been proclaimed, for instance in the State of Massachusetts, until recently the freest state of the North American republic, as the statutory limit of the labour of children under 12, was in England, even in the middle of the seventeenth century, the normal working day of able-bodied artisans, robust ploughmen and gigantic blacksmiths."

The Statutes of Labourers. - 23 Edward III, 1349 — the first "Statute of Labourers." Its immediate pretext was the Black Plague, but its cause is deeper. A Tory writer: "The difficulty of getting men to work on reasonable terms" (i.e., at a price yielding the employer a reasonable quantity of surplus labour) "grew to such a height as to be quite intolerable." Reasonable wages were fixed by law, as were the limits of the working day. - Statute of 1496 (Henry VII): the working day for all craftsmen and field labourers from March to September was supposed to last from 5 a.m. to between 7 and 8 p.m. Meal-times: 1 hour for breakfast, 1½ hours for dinner, half an hour for "noon-meate" — exactly twice as much as under the Factory Acts now in force. In winter, work from 5 a.m. until dark, with the same intervals. - Statute of Elizabeth, 1562: leaves the length of the working day untouched but limits intervals to 2½ hours in summer and 2 in winter. Dinner only 1 hour; the "afternoon-sleep of half an hour" only between mid-May and mid-August. Every hour of absence: 1d. subtracted.

The gap between statute and practice. Marx notes that in practice conditions were much more favourable to labourers than in the statute-book. William Petty, in the last third of the seventeenth century, says labouring men work ten hours per diem and make twenty meals per week — three on working days, two on Sundays — "whereby it is plain, that if they could fast on Friday nights, and dine in one hour and an half, whereas they take two, from eleven to one; thereby thus working 2½ more, and spending 2½ less, the above-mentioned tax might be raised."

Marx notes drily: "Was Dr Andrew Ure not right when he deplored the Twelve Hours' Bill of 1833 as a retrogression to the age of darkness?"

Child labour at the end of the seventeenth century. The complaint: "Our youth, here in England, do absolutely nothing before they come to be apprentices, and then they naturally require a long time — seven years — to be formed into complete craftsmen." Germany, on the other hand, is praised because children are educated from their cradle "at least to something of employment."

The eighteenth-century controversy over the working week. Still, until the epoch of large-scale industry, capital in England had not gained control of the worker's whole week. The fact that workers could live for a week on four days' wages did not seem to them a sufficient reason to work the other two days for the capitalist.

Postlethwayt's defense of the workers. Marx recounts the polemic. Postlethwayt (in Great Britain's Commercial Interest Explained and Improved): the workers' "relaxation of their own way" is what preserved their ingenuity and dexterity. "Many of them will execute as much work in four days as a Frenchman will in five or six. But if Englishmen are to be eternal drudges, 'tis to be feared they will degenerate below the Frenchmen."

The anonymous author of the Essay on Trade and Commerce (1770) replies: "That mankind in general, are naturally inclined to ease and indolence, we fatally experience to be true." If a bushel of wheat costs 5 shillings and the worker earns a shilling a day, he would work five days only; but as wages are higher relative to necessaries, the manufacturer who labours four days has a surplus to live idle the rest of the week. "I hope I have said enough to make it appear that the moderate labour of six days in a week is no slavery." And: "The labouring people should never think themselves independent of their superiors... The cure will not be perfect, till our manufacturing poor are contented to labour six days for the same sum which they now earn in four days."

The 'Ideal Workhouse' proposal. To this end, and for "extirpating idleness, debauchery and excess," promoting a spirit of industry, "lowering the price of labour in our manufactories, and easing the lands of the heavy burden of poor's rates," the author proposes to lock up paupers in an "Ideal Workhouse" — a "House of Terror." "The poor shall work 14 hours in a day, allowing proper time for meals, in such a manner that there shall remain 12 hours of neat labour."

Marx's punchline: Twelve working hours a day in the "Ideal Workhouse" of 1770! Sixty-three years later, in 1833, when Parliament reduced the working day for children of 13 to 18 to 12 full hours, in four branches of industry, "the Day of Judgement seemed to have dawned for English industry!" In 1852, when Louis Bonaparte sought to secure his position by tampering with the legal working day, the people of France cried: "the law that limits the working day to 12 hours is the one good that has remained to us of the legislation of the Republic." At Zurich, work of children over 10 is limited to 12 hours; in Aargau in 1862, children between 13 and 16 had their hours reduced from 12½ to 12; in Austria in 1860, the same reduction for children between 14 and 16.

"'What progress since 1770,' Macaulay might shout 'with exultation'."

The "House of Terror" realized. "The 'House of Terror' for paupers, only dreamed of by the capitalist mind in 1770, was brought into being a few years later in the shape of a gigantic 'workhouse' for the industrial worker himself. It was called the factory. And this time the ideal was a pale shadow compared with the reality."

2.6 The Factory Legislation of 1833–64: Struggle, Relay System, Capital's Revolt

Marx now narrates the century-long civil war over the working day in England.

The period of unregulated orgies. After capital had taken centuries to extend the working day to its normal maximum (12 hours), the birth of large-scale industry in the last third of the eighteenth century produced "an avalanche of violent and unmeasured encroachments. Every boundary set by morality and nature, age and sex, day and night, was broken down." Even the ideas of day and night became confused: as late as 1860, an English judge needed "the penetration of an interpreter of the Talmud" to explain judicially what was day and what was night. "Capital was celebrating its orgies."

The first resistance. As soon as the working class had recovered from the initial shock, it began to resist — first in England, the native land of large-scale industry. But for three decades (1802–1833) the concessions wrung from industry remained "purely nominal." Parliament passed five Labour Laws, "but was shrewd enough not to vote a penny for their compulsory implementation." They remained a dead letter. "The fact is, that prior to the Act of 1833, young persons and children were worked all night, all day, or both ad libitum."

The Factory Act of 1833. This is the first factory act "of any real substance." It covers cotton, wool, flax, and silk factories. Its provisions:

  • Ordinary factory working day: 5.30 a.m. to 8.30 p.m. (15 hours);
  • Within these limits, it is lawful to employ young persons (13–18) at any time of the day, provided no individual young person works more than 12 hours in any one day;
  • Sixth chapter: "There shall be allowed in the course of every day not less than one and a half hours for meals";
  • Employment of children under 9 forbidden;
  • Work of children between 9 and 13 limited to 8 hours a day;
  • Night-work (between 8.30 p.m. and 5.30 a.m.) forbidden for all persons between 9 and 18.

The "system of relays" introduced. The law-makers did not wish to interfere with adult male labour — "or, as they called it, 'the freedom of labour'" — so they created a special system to prevent the Factory Acts from having "such a frightful consequence" (the limitation of adult labour). The Central Board of the Commission (28 June 1833): "The great evil of the factory system as at present conducted has appeared to us to be that it entails the necessity of continuing the labour of children to the utmost length of that of the adults. The only remedy for this evil, short of the limitation of the labour of adults, which would, in our opinion, create an evil greater than that which is sought to be remedied, appears to be the plan of working double sets of children." This "plan of relays" — one set of children 5.30 a.m. to 1.30 p.m., another 1.30 p.m. to 8.30 p.m. — was intended to permit the adult working day to remain 15 hours.

The gilded pill. To reward the manufacturers for having ignored all previous child-labour acts, Parliament decreed that after 1 March 1834 no child under 11, after 1 March 1835 no child under 12, and after 1 March 1836 no child under 13 was to work more than 8 hours — i.e., delayed the effective age limit. "This 'liberalism,' so full of consideration for 'capital,'" Marx writes, "was the more noteworthy in that Dr Farre, Sir A. Carlisle, Sir B. Brodie, Sir C. Bell, Mr Guthrie, etc., in a word, the most distinguished physicians and surgeons in London, had declared in their evidence before the House of Commons that there was danger in delay." Dr Farre was blunter: "Legislation is necessary for the prevention of death, in any form in which it can be prematurely inflicted, and certainly this (the factory method) must be viewed as a most cruel mode of inflicting it."

The hypocrisy of the Emancipation Act. The same "reformed" Parliament, which in its delicate consideration for manufacturers condemned children under 13 to 72 hours of factory labour every week, in the Emancipation Act "forbade the planters, from the very beginning, to work any Negro slave for more than 45 hours a week."

Capital's agitation on the age limit. Capital began a noisy, long-lasting agitation against the age-limit of "children" (restricted to 8 hours and subject to compulsory education). "According to the anthropology of the capitalists, the age of childhood ended at 10, or, at the outside, 11." By 1835 they had intimidated the government into proposing to lower the age of childhood from 13 to 12. But "the 'pressure from without' became more threatening" — the House of Commons refused, and the Act of 1833 came into full operation, remaining unaltered until June 1844.

The new "relay system" of evasion. Under the 1833 Act, the timing of each "young person's" or "child's" work within the 15-hour day was left to the free decision of the lords of capital. This permitted a new "system of relays" — "the work-horses were not changed at fixed stations, but were always re-harnessed at different stations." Marx: "It annulled the whole Factory Act, not only in the spirit, but in the letter." The factory inspectors, with complex bookkeeping for each individual child, could not enforce the legal hours nor compel the legal meal-times. In 1844, the inspectors demonstrated to the Home Secretary the impossibility of any control under the newly invented relay system.

Changing circumstances and the 1844 Act. Meanwhile, the factory workers had made the Ten Hours' Bill their economic, as they had made the Charter their political, election cry. Some manufacturers who had complied with the 1833 Act complained of the "immoral competition" of "false brethren." The spokesmen of the manufacturing class began to change their language and attitude towards the workers — they had started their campaign to repeal the Corn Laws and needed workers' help. They promised not only that the loaf of bread would be twice its size but that the Ten Hours' Bill would be enacted in the free trade millennium. The Tories, threatened in their interest in the rent of land, "thundered with philanthropic indignation against the 'nefarious practices' of their foes."

The Factory Act of 7 June 1844 (effective 10 September 1844):

  • Placed under protection a new category — women over 18 — on the same footing as young persons: working hours limited to 12, night-work forbidden;
  • For the first time, the labour of adults was directly and officially controlled by legislation. The Factory Report of 1844–45 states ironically: "No instances have come to my knowledge of adult women having expressed any regret at their rights being thus far interfered with";
  • Working hours of children under 13 reduced to 6½, in certain circumstances to 7;
  • To abolish the abuses of the "relay system," the law established: working hours to be reckoned from the time when any child or young person begins work in the morning (so that if A begins at 8 and B at 10, B's day must end with A's); time regulated by a public clock; a printed notice stating hours for beginning/ending work and pauses; children beginning before 12 noon may not again be employed after 1 p.m. (afternoon shift must consist of different children); of the 1½ hours for meals, "one hour thereof at the least shall be given before three of the clock in the afternoon... and at the same period of the day"; no child or young person shall be employed more than 5 hours before 1 p.m. without an interval of at least 30 minutes; no child or young person or female shall be employed or remain in any room where manufacturing is being carried on during meal-times.

Marx's central methodological point: these highly detailed specifications, regulating times, limits, and pauses by the stroke of the clock, "were by no means a product of the fantasy of Members of Parliament. They developed gradually out of circumstances as natural laws of the modern mode of production. Their formulation, official recognition and proclamation by the state were the result of a long class struggle." One of their first consequences: the working day of adult males in factories became subject to the same limitations, since in most processes the cooperation of children, young persons, and women is indispensable. Thus, from 1844 to 1847, the 12-hour working day became universal in all branches under the Factory Act.

Capital's compensating "retrogression". At the manufacturers' instigation, the House of Commons reduced the minimum age for exploitable children from 9 to 8, "in order to ensure that 'additional supply of factory children' which is owed to the capitalists."

The Ten Hours' Bill enacted (1847). The years 1846–47 are "epoch-making." The Corn Laws were repealed; duties on cotton and other raw materials removed; free trade proclaimed. In the same years, the Chartist movement and the ten hours' agitation reached their peak. "Despite the fanatical opposition of the army of perjured Free traders, headed by Bright and Cobden, the Ten Hours' Bill, so long struggled for, made its way through Parliament."

The Factory Act of 8 June 1847 provided: - 1 July 1847: preliminary reduction of the working day for "young persons" (13–18) and all females to 11 hours; - 1 May 1848: definite limitation to 10 hours.

Capital's campaign to prevent implementation. Capital undertook a preliminary campaign to prevent the Act coming into full force on 1 May 1848. The moment was cleverly chosen: after two years of great suffering (the crisis of 1846–47), factory operatives were in narrow circumstances, many in debt. Manufacturers aggravated the situation by a general 10% reduction in wages as a "celebration" of the new free-trade era, then a further reduction of 8⅓% when hours were shortened to 11, then a reduction of twice that amount when shortened to 10. Wherever circumstances permitted, a reduction in wages of at least 25% took place.

The failure of the wage-reduction strategy. Under these prepared conditions, the agitation for repeal of the 1847 Act began. But no method of deceit, seduction, or intimidation could bring the workers to demand their own over-working. In relation to the half-dozen petitions complaining of "oppression by the Act," the petitioners declared under oral examination that their signatures had been extorted. "They felt themselves oppressed, but by something different from the Factory Act."

The inspectors' inquiries. Leonard Horner conducted many examinations. Of the workers examined (10,270 adult male labourers in 181 factories in Horner's district), about 70% declared in favour of 10 hours, a smaller percentage for 11, and an insignificant minority for the old 12. On the "friendly" trick of making adult males work 12–15 hours to fake a demonstration of workers' preferences: the majority of "overtimers" declared: "They would much prefer working 10 hours for less wages, but they had no choice; so many were out of employment... that if they refused to work the longer time, others would immediately get their places, so that it was a question with them of agreeing to work the longer time, or of being thrown out of employment altogether."

The Ten Hours' Act in force (1 May 1848) and capital's revolt. The Chartist fiasco (leaders imprisoned, organization dismembered) shattered the self-confidence of the English working class. The June insurrection in Paris and its bloody suppression united all fractions of the ruling classes in Britain and on the Continent under the common slogan of "the salvation of property, religion, the family and society." Everywhere the working class was outlawed, anathematized, placed under a loi des suspects.

Capital's revolt. The manufacturers broke out in open revolt — not only against the Ten Hours' Act but against all legislation since 1833. Marx calls this "a pro-slavery rebellion in miniature, carried on for over two years with a cynical recklessness and a terroristic energy which were so much the easier to achieve in that the rebel capitalist risked nothing but the skin of his workers."

The steps of the revolt. 1. Dismissal of young persons and women — in many cases half of them. 2. Restoration of night-work for adult males, which had almost disappeared. The Ten Hours' Act "leaves us no other alternative." 3. On meal-times: manufacturers claimed that they could fulfil the statutes by allowing an hour before 9 a.m. and half an hour after 7 p.m. — i.e., workers should eat their dinner before 9 in the morning or at home! The Crown lawyers decided that prescribed meal-times "must be in the interval during the working-hours, and that it will not be lawful to work for 10 hours continuously, from 9 a.m. to 7 p.m., without any interval." 4. On the letter of the law: the Act of 1844 prohibited employment after 1 p.m. of children aged 8–13 who had been employed before noon — but it did not regulate the 6½ hours' work of children whose day began at 12 midday or later. Children of 8 might, if they began at noon, be employed from 12–1 (1 hour), 2–4 (2 hours), and 5–8.30 (3½ hours) — a legal 6½ hours! Or, better, by being kept entirely from work till 2 p.m., they could then be kept until 8.30 p.m. without intermission. Marx quotes Shylock: "My deeds upon my head! I crave the law, / The penalty and forfeit of my bond." And later: "Ay, his breast, / So says the bond." Statistics presented to the House of Commons on 26 July 1850: 3,742 children still subjected to this "practice" in 257 factories. 5. The open revolt against the regulation of "young persons and women": the abolition of the "false relay system" was the main aim of the 1844 Act. Manufacturers declared that the sections prohibiting the unrestricted use of young persons and women in fractions of the 15-hour day had been "comparatively harmless" with 12 hours but were now a "grievous hardship." They informed the inspectors they would set themselves above the letter of the law and introduce the old system on their own account. Pretexts: this would allow them to pay higher wages; it was the only way to maintain Britain's industrial supremacy; what did the inspectors' trouble matter compared to "the great manufacturing interest of this country"?

The Home Secretary's capitulation and the courts. Factory Inspector J. Stuart, following Sir George Grey's circular of 5 August 1848, allowed the relay system for the 15-hour period to be restored throughout Scotland. English inspectors declared the Home Secretary had no dictatorial power to suspend the laws and continued their legal proceedings. But the courts — county magistrates — acquitted the manufacturers.

The Eskrigge case. A cotton-spinner named Eskrigge submitted a relay plan to the factory inspector, was refused, kept quiet. Months later, a spinner named Robinson (Eskrigge's relative) appeared before the borough magistrates of Stockport on a charge of introducing the very plan. The bench consisted of four Justices, three of them cotton-spinners, headed by Eskrigge himself. Eskrigge acquitted Robinson, then decided that what was right for Robinson was fair for Eskrigge, and introduced the plan into his own factory. Marx notes that the composition of this tribunal was itself a blatant violation of the law (by Section 10 of 1 & 2 William IV, c. 24 — Sir John Hobhouse's Factory Act — no owner of a cotton mill, nor the father, son, or brother of such an owner, could act as justice in factory inquiries).

Horner's despair. Horner reports: 10 prosecutions in 7 magisterial divisions, supported by magistrates in only one case. He considered further prosecution useless. The part of the 1848 Act framed for securing uniformity of hours is "no longer in force" in his district. The number of mill-owners working by shifts amounts to 114 and is rapidly increasing. Working time is extended to 13½ hours (6 a.m. to 7½ p.m.), in some instances to 15 hours (5½ a.m. to 8½ p.m.). Horner already possessed a list of 65 manufacturers and 29 overseers who unanimously declared that no system of supervision could prevent the most extensive over-work under this relay system.

The absurdity of the relay system. Sometimes the same children were shifted from the spinning-room to the weaving-room; sometimes shifted from one factory to another in the course of 15 hours. "How was it possible to control a system which 'under the guise of relays, is some one of the many plans for shuffling "the hands" about in endless variety, and shifting the hours of work and of rest for different individuals throughout the day, so that you may never have one complete set of hands working together in the same room at the same time'?"

Fourier comparison. Marx compares the relay system to Fourier's humorous sketches of the courts é sêances — "except that the 'attraction of labour' is here transformed into the attraction of capital." The working personnel was divided into 12–15 categories, constantly changing composition. During the 15 hours of the factory day, capital dragged the worker in for 30 minutes, then an hour, then pushed him out, then in afresh, "hounding him hither and thither, in scattered shreds of time, without ever letting go until the full 10 hours of work was done." Like actors committed to the theatre for the whole play, workers were committed to the factory for the whole 15 hours. Thus "the hours of rest were turned into hours of enforced idleness, which drove the young men to the taverns and the young girls to the brothels." Every new trick for keeping machinery going for 12 or 15 hours without increasing personnel meant the worker had to gulp his meals in a different fragment of time.

The wage trick. "During the 10 hours' agitation, the manufacturers cried out that the mob of workers were petitioning in the hope of obtaining 12 hours' wages for 10 hours' work. Now they reversed the medal. They paid 10 hours' wages for 12 or 15 hours' disposition over the workers' labour-power. This was the heart of the matter, this was the manufacturers' edition of the ten hours' law!"

The Court of Exchequer's verdict (8 February 1850). This "revolt of capital" was crowned with victory by a decision of the Court of Exchequer: the manufacturers were "certainly acting against the sense of the Act of 1844, but that this Act itself contained certain words that rendered it meaningless." Marx: "This verdict was tantamount to an abrogation of the Ten Hours' Bill." Many manufacturers who had been afraid to use the shift system now seized on it enthusiastically. Marx adds a footnote comparing this to the same Court's decision in the Alexandra case (1863), which reversed the meaning of the Foreign Enlistment Act against the arming of pirate ships.

The workers' counter-stroke. The victory of capital was immediately followed by a counter-stroke. The workers protested in Lancashire and Yorkshire in threatening meetings. "The so-called Ten Hours' Act, they said, was thus mere humbug, a parliamentary fraud. It had never existed!" Factory inspectors warned the government that class antagonisms had reached an unheard-of degree of tension. Manufacturers grumbled about contradictory decisions producing "anarchic" conditions: one law in Yorkshire, another in Lancashire. "The manufacturer in large towns could evade the law, the manufacturer in country districts could not find the people necessary for the relay system." Marx's analytical point: "the most fundamental right under the law of capital is the equal exploitation of labour-power by all capitalists."

The compromise: Factory Act of 5 August 1850. The working day for "young persons and women" was lengthened from 10 to 10½ hours for the first five days, and shortened to 7½ hours on Saturdays. Work had to take place between 6 a.m. and 6 p.m. (7 a.m. to 7 p.m. in winter), with pauses of not less than 1½ hours for meal-times, at exactly the same time for all. The relay system was ended once and for all. For child labour, the Act of 1844 remained in force.

The silk manufacturers' privilege. Silk manufacturers secured special "seigniorial rights over the children of the proletariat." In 1833 they had howled that if the liberty of working children of any age for 10 hours a day were taken away, it would stop their works. They extorted the privilege. Subsequent investigation showed the pretext was a deliberate lie. Throughout the following decade they spun silk for 10 hours a day "out of the blood of little children who had to be put on stools to perform their work." The 1844 Act "robbed" the silk manufacturers of the liberty of employing children under 11 for longer than 6½ hours, but secured them the privilege of working children 11–13 for 10 hours, and annulled compulsory education. Their pretext: "the delicate texture of the fabric in which they were employed, requiring a lightness of touch, only to be acquired by their early introduction to these factories." Marx: "The children were quite simply slaughtered for the sake of their delicate fingers, just as horned cattle are slaughtered in southern Russia for their hides and their fat." In 1850 the privilege was limited to silk-twisting and silk-winding, but to compensate capital the hours for children 11–13 were increased from 10 to 10½. Official medical inquiries proved that "the average death-rate is exceedingly high in the silk districts, and amongst the female part of the population is higher even than it is in the cotton districts of Lancashire."

The Greenhow table. Marx reproduces (in a compressed statistical table) Dr Greenhow's 1861 data comparing mortality in manufacturing districts to "eight healthy agricultural districts." Manufacturing districts show 100% higher mortality than the agricultural baseline of 340 per thousand for male adults. Even after the Factory Acts had improved conditions, the manufacturing districts lagged badly in health. Marx adds: "In general, the working population has greatly improved physically under the regime of the Factory Act. All medical testimony agrees on this point, and my own personal observation on various occasions has convinced me this is true."

The 1850 Act's loopholes and their closure. The Act of 1850 replaced the 15-hour period (6 a.m. to 8.30 p.m.) with a 12-hour period (6 a.m. to 6 p.m.), but only for young persons and women. It did not affect children, who could still be employed for half an hour before this period and 2½ hours after it, provided the total did not exceed 6½ hours. During the bill's discussion, the factory inspectors laid before Parliament statistics on the abuses arising from this anomaly. In vain. "In the background lurked the intention of using the children to force the working day of adult males up to 15 hours, in years of prosperity. The experience of the three years which followed demonstrated that such an attempt was bound to fail in face of the resistance of the adult male workers." The Act was finally completed in 1853 by the prohibition of "the employment of children in the morning before and in the evening after young persons and women." By then "half a century had elapsed since the passing of the first Factory Act."

The Printworks Act of 1845. The first extension of factory legislation beyond its original sphere. Marx calls it a "parliamentary abortion": it limits the working day for children 8–13 and for women to 16 hours between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m., without any legal pause for meal-times. It allows males over 13 to be worked at will day and night. He quotes inspectors: "The Printworks Act is admitted to be a failure." Children of 8 and upwards were still employed from 6 a.m. to 9 p.m. within the inspector's district.

The 1860s expansion and the fruits of regulation. The very manufacturers from whom the legal limitation of the working day had been wrung "now pointed boastfully to the contrast with the areas of exploitation which were still 'free'." The Pharisees of political economy "now proclaimed that their newly won insight into the necessity for a legally regulated working day was a characteristic achievement of their 'science'." Marx refers to W. Newmarch (editor of Tooke's History of Prices). He asks: "Is it a scientific advance to make cowardly concessions to public opinion?"

The extension of legislation. Once the factory magnates had resigned themselves, capital's power of resistance gradually weakened, while the working class's power of attack grew with the number of its allies in social layers not directly interested. Hence the comparatively rapid progress since 1860:

  • 1860: Dye-works and bleach-works brought under the Act of 1850 (12 hours provisional 1 Aug 1861, definitively 10 hours on 1 Aug 1862);
  • 1861: Lace and stocking factories;
  • 1863 (following the first report of the Commission on the Employment of Children, 1863): manufacturers of all earthenware products (not just potteries), matches, percussion-caps, cartridges, carpets and fustian cuttings, and employers of people engaged in the many processes included under "finishing";
  • 1863: bleaching in the open air and baking placed under special Acts. In the first case, the labour of young persons and women at night was forbidden (from 8 p.m. to 6 a.m.); in the latter, the employment of journeymen bakers under 18 between 9 p.m. and 5 a.m. was forbidden. Marx notes the great inadequacy even of these Acts — the bleachers' evasions, the loopholes, the "failure" of even the 1863 Act.

The manufacturers again tried the trick of demanding 12 hours "for the sake of the workers" during the cotton famine — but "it was mainly due to the action of the operative bleachers in Scotland that the bill was abandoned."

Marx's close on this section: "We shall return to the later proposals of the same Commission, which threaten to deprive all the important branches of English industry of their 'freedom,' with the exception of agriculture, mining and transport." (In a footnote he notes: "Since 1866, when I wrote the above passages, a reaction has set in once again.")

2.7 International Impact: France, America, the First International

The final section draws out the general conclusions of the historical sketch.

Preliminary clarifications. Marx reminds the reader that "the production of surplus-value, or the extraction of surplus labour, forms the specific content and purpose of capitalist production" — independent of any reconstruction of the mode of production that may arise from the subordination of labour to capital. The independent worker, legally qualified to act for himself, enters into a contract with the capitalist as seller of a commodity. If the historical sketch has shown the prominent part played by modern industry on the one hand and the labour of the physically and legally minor on the other, "the former is still for us only a particular department of the exploitation of labour, and the latter only a particularly striking example of it."

Two general conclusions can be derived by connecting the historical facts together:

First. Capital's drive towards boundless extension of the working day begins in the industries first revolutionized by water-power, steam, and machinery — the earliest creations of the modern mode of production (spinning and weaving of cotton, wool, flax, silk). "The changed material mode of production, and the correspondingly changed social relations of the producers, first gave rise to outrages without measure, and then called forth, in opposition to this, social control, which legally limits, regulates and makes uniform the working day and its pauses." Thus, for the first half of the nineteenth century, this control appears simply as legislation for exceptions. As soon as the Factory Acts had conquered the original domain, it was found that many other branches had entered the factory system — manufactures with more or less obsolete methods (potteries, glass-making), old-fashioned handicrafts (baking), and scattered domestic industries (nail-making) — all as completely under capitalist exploitation as the factories themselves. "Factory legislation was therefore compelled gradually to strip itself of its exceptional character, or to declare that any house in which work was done was a factory, as in England, where the law proceeds in the manner of the Roman Casuists."

Second. The isolated worker, the worker as 'free' seller of his labour-power, succumbs without resistance once capitalist production has reached a certain stage of maturity. Therefore the establishment of a normal working day is the product of "a protracted and more or less concealed civil war between the capitalist class and the working class." Since the contest takes place in the arena of modern industry, it is fought out first in England, the homeland of that industry. "The English factory workers were the champions, not only of the English working class, but of the modern working class in general, just as their theorists were the first to throw down the gauntlet to the theory of the capitalists."

Ure's outrage. Marx recalls that "the philosopher of the factory, Ure, considers it a mark of inextinguishable disgrace on the part of the English working class that they wrote 'the slavery of the Factory Acts' on their banners, as opposed to capital, which was striving manfully for the 'perfect freedom of labour'."

France. "France limps slowly behind England. The French twelve hours' law needed the February revolution to bring it into the world, and it has far more loopholes than its English model. Nevertheless, the French revolutionary method has its own peculiar advantages. At one stroke it dictates the same limits to the working day in all shops and factories without distinction, whereas the English legislation yields reluctantly to the pressure of circumstances, now on this point, now on that, and is well on the way to creating an inextricable tangle of contradictory enactments." Moreover, "the French law proclaims as a principle what in England was only won in the name of children, minors and women, and has only recently been claimed, for the first time, as a universal right."

Marx cites the Compte Rendu of the International Statistical Congress (Paris, 1855): the French law limiting daily labour to 12 hours does not confine work to definite fixed hours; for children's labour only, the working time is prescribed as between 5 a.m. and 9 p.m. "Therefore, some of the manufacturers use the right which this fatal silence gives them to keep their works going without intermission, day in, day out, with the possible exception of Sunday. For this purpose they use two different sets of workers... the work of the establishment lasts day and night. The law is satisfied, but is humanity?" The report also stresses "the destructive influence of night-labour on the human organism" and "the fatal influence of the association of the two sexes by night in the same badly lighted workshops."

The Belgian model. Belgium "shows no trace of this movement." The workers, "in perfect 'freedom'," are consumed at any period and through any length of time. Out of every 1,000 persons employed in coal and metal mines: 733 men, 88 women, 135 boys, 44 girls under 16. In blast-furnaces: 668 men, 149 women, 98 boys, 85 girls under 16. Average daily pay: 2s. 8d. for a man, 1s. 8d. for a woman, 1s. 2½d. for a boy. As a result, Belgium nearly doubled the amount and value of its coal, iron, etc. exports between 1850 and 1863.

Owen's example. Robert Owen, soon after 1810, not only maintained the necessity of limiting the working day in theory, but introduced the 10-hour day into his factory at New Lanark. "This was laughed at as a communist utopia; so was his 'combination of children's education with productive labour,' as well as the workers' co-operative societies he was the first to set up. Today, the first utopia is a Factory Act, the second figures as an official phrase in all Factory Acts, and the third is already being used as a cloak for reactionary swindles."

The United States. "In the United States of America, every independent workers' movement was paralysed as long as slavery disfigured a part of the republic. Labour in a white skin cannot emancipate itself where it is branded in a black skin. However, a new life immediately arose from the death of slavery. The first fruit of the American Civil War was the eight hours' agitation, which ran from the Atlantic to the Pacific, from New England to California, with the seven-league boots of the locomotive." The General Congress of Labour held at Baltimore in August 1866 declared: "The first and great necessity of the present, to free the labour of this country from capitalistic slavery, is the passing of a law by which eight hours shall be the normal working day in all States of the American Union. We are resolved to put forth all our strength until this glorious result is attained."

The International. At the same time (beginning of September 1866), the Congress of the International Working Men's Association, held at Geneva, passed the following resolution, proposed by the London General Council (and drafted by Marx himself): "We declare that the limitation of the working day is a preliminary condition without which all further attempts at improvement and emancipation must prove abortive... the Congress proposes eight hours as the legal limit of the working day."

The final synthesis. Marx closes with a powerful restatement of the chapter's central theme:

"It must be acknowledged that our worker emerges from the process of production looking different from when he entered it. In the market, as owner of the commodity 'labour-power,' he stood face to face with other owners of commodities, one owner against another owner. The contract by which he sold his labour-power to the capitalist proved in black and white, so to speak, that he was free to dispose of himself. But when the transaction was concluded, it was discovered that he was no 'free agent,' that the period of time for which he is free to sell his labour-power is the period of time for which he is forced to sell it, that in fact the vampire will not let go 'while there remains a single muscle, sinew or drop of blood to be exploited.'"

For "protection" against the serpent of their agonies, workers must put their heads together and, as a class, compel the passing of a law — "an all-powerful social barrier" — by which they can be prevented from selling themselves and their families into slavery and death by voluntary contract with capital. "In the place of the pompous catalogue of the 'inalienable rights of man' there steps the modest Magna Carta of the legally limited working day, which at last makes clear 'when the time which the worker sells is ended, and when his own begins.'"

The final Latin phrase: Quantum mutatus ab illo! — "What a great change from that time" (Virgil, Aeneid, Bk 2, line 274). The "illo" is both the worker as he entered the process and the historical era before the working-class movement had won the legal limitation of the working day.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Fundamental Principles Revealed

1. The working day is a social and political determination, not an economic one. Marx's central analytic achievement in this chapter is to show that the economic categories established in earlier chapters — value, surplus-value, the rate of surplus-value — are insufficient to determine the length of the working day. Economic law fixes the value of labour-power and the necessary labour-time, but it leaves the length of the working day indeterminate. That indeterminacy is resolved by force, by class struggle, and ultimately by state legislation. This is a profound methodological point: the capitalist economy cannot be understood purely in economic terms.

2. The antinomy of equal rights. The dialectical structure of the chapter rests on the collision between two equally valid claims derived from the law of commodity-exchange: the buyer's right to maximize the use-value of his purchase, and the seller's right to preserve the value of his commodity from premature consumption. "Between equal rights, force decides." This formula has enormous implications: it means that the "freedom of contract" which appears as the legal foundation of capitalism is, in fact, the legal form of an underlying relation of force.

3. Surplus labour is transhistorical; its specific form under capitalism is the drive to extend the working day. The comparative analysis of corvée and wage-labour shows that the quantitative ratio of surplus to necessary labour is not unique to capitalism, but that its specific form differs: the corvée makes surplus labour visible (distinct fields, distinct days), while wage-labour conceals it under the unity of a single working day. The capitalist's distinctive appetite appears as the drive for an unlimited extension of the working day, because, unlike the boyar, the capitalist cannot simply command more corvée days — he can only extend the day.

4. Capital is indifferent to the health and life of the worker. "By extending the working day, therefore, capitalist production... not only produces a deterioration of human labour-power... but also produces the premature exhaustion and death of this labour-power itself." "Capital asks no questions about the length of life of labour-power. What interests it is purely and simply the maximum of labour-power that can be set in motion in a working day. It attains this objective by shortening the life of labour-power, in the same way as a greedy farmer snatches more produce from the soil by robbing it of its fertility." The watchword is "Après moi le déluge!" — a formula still cited today in ecological and labor-degradation critiques.

5. The "reserve army" mechanism replaces the immediate self-interest of capital in preserving labour-power. Marx makes the crucial observation: even though capital's own long-term interest would seem to require the preservation of labour-power, the availability of a constant surplus population from the countryside, from Ireland, from Germany, and from the natural reproduction of the working class means that capital does not need to care for any individual worker — "when his place can at once be supplied from foreign preserves, the duration of his life becomes a matter of less moment than its productiveness while it lasts." This is the essence of the later concept of the industrial reserve army, deployed here to explain the self-destructive dynamic of capital.

6. The state is not neutral; it acts as an instrument of whichever class can bring more force to bear. The history of the working day demonstrates two opposite uses of state power: in the fourteenth through seventeenth centuries, the state forcibly extended the working day on behalf of nascent capital; in the nineteenth century, it forcibly limited the working day under working-class pressure. The state's class character is not fixed by its form (Parliament, courts, inspectors) but by the balance of forces operating on it. Note especially the complicity of the courts — county magistrates (the "Great Unpaid," "patrimonial jurisdiction of the ruling classes") acquitting manufacturers, the Court of Exchequer twisting the law's plain meaning to favor capital, the Home Secretary instructing inspectors not to prosecute.

7. The legally limited working day is the "modest Magna Carta" of the working class. The "inalienable rights of man" are dismissed as a "pompous catalogue," while the legally limited working day is "the modest Magna Carta" that "at last makes clear 'when the time which the worker sells is ended, and when his own begins.'" This is a profoundly important statement about the strategic orientation of the working-class movement: the working day is not just one demand among many, but a preliminary condition for all further improvement. "Further steps towards a reformation of society can never be carried out with any hope of success, unless the hours of labour be limited, and the prescribed limit strictly enforced."

Broader Practical, Theoretical, and Philosophical Implications

Practical implications for labor movements. The chapter is a "how-to" of class struggle in many ways. It shows that: - Workers' resistance to over-work is not a moral preference but a defense of their own commodity; - Isolated workers cannot defend themselves; only collective action ("collective labor" against "collective capital") can secure limits; - The struggle is not won once and for all — capital constantly finds new "dodges" (the relay system, the letter of the law, meal-times, wage reductions); - Workers' organizations must be ready to make alliances with other social forces when capital's own internal divisions appear; - The example of English factory workers is offered to workers internationally as a model of struggle.

Theoretical implications. The chapter does not merely illustrate an economic theory — it transforms it. It establishes that: - The category of surplus-value cannot be fully analyzed without a theory of the state, of class struggle, and of legislation; - Historical materialism must integrate the relative autonomy of legal and political institutions (Parliament, inspectorates, courts) with the underlying economic relation; - The distinction between "formal" and "real" subsumption is implicit in the argument that capital "takes the labour process as it finds it" but transforms working conditions through over-work; - The analysis of the working day is the essential precondition for understanding relative surplus-value (Part IV) — because relative surplus-value emerges in response to limits on the working day.

Philosophical implications. The chapter is a moral indictment of political economy's "freedom of labour," but the indictment is not moralistic. Marx is careful to argue that the capitalist is not personally responsible: "this does not depend on the will, either good or bad, of the individual capitalist. Under free competition, the immanent laws of capitalist production confront the individual capitalist as a coercive force external to him." The individual capitalist may be a model citizen, "in the odour of sanctity" — but "the thing you represent when you come face to face with me has no heart in its breast. What seems to throb there is my own heartbeat." The critique is structural, not moral. This is a foundational statement of Marx's method: the critique of capital is a critique of a system, and the system's "laws" — competition, valorization, the drive for surplus-value — are the effective causal forces, not the character of individuals.

The final image. Quantum mutatus ab illo — the worker who enters the market as a free agent, the "owner of the commodity 'labour-power'," emerges from the production process "looking different from when he entered it." He discovers that the period for which he is "free" to sell his labour-power is, in fact, the period for which he is forced to sell it. The "freedom of contract" is revealed as the form of his subjection. The legally limited working day is thus not a reformist compromise but a genuine conquest: it establishes, for the first time, a boundary between "the worker's" time and "the master's" time. The Magna Carta of the working class is not the Magna Carta of the barons, but the historical form of a broader claim — that human life should not be entirely absorbed into the process of capital's self-valorization. "The vampire will not let go 'while there remains a single muscle, sinew or drop of blood to be exploited.'" The workers must put their heads together and, as a class, compel the passing of a law — "an all-powerful social barrier."

The chapter thus ends with the theoretical articulation of a political program: the legally limited working day, the eight-hour day, as the precondition of any further human emancipation. That program, articulated by Marx in the Geneva resolution of the First International and echoed in the Baltimore declaration of the American workers in 1866, is not merely a moral aspiration but the necessary outcome of the historical analysis that Marx has conducted in the preceding 76 pages.

This concludes the exhaustive analysis of Chapter 10 of Marx's Capital, Volume I.
