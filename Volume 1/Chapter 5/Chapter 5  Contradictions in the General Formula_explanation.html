Volume 1 • Chapter 5: Contradictions in the General Formula

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

This chapter — Chapter 5 of Capital, Volume I, titled "Contradictions in the General Formula" — is the analytic pivot of Part II, "The Transformation of Money into Capital." Its task is purely negative and devastating: to prove that the value-form which defines capital, the circuit M–C–M′ (money buying commodities in order to sell them for more money), cannot be explained by anything that happens within circulation. The chapter subjects the general formula to a merciless thought-experiment that closes off every escape route the "vulgar economists" might take, and in doing so it converts a puzzle into an antinomy — a genuine contradiction that will drive the entire remaining argument of the book.

The central problem is stated in the opening sentence: the circulation-form in which money becomes capital "contradicts all the previously developed laws bearing on the nature of commodities, value, money and even circulation itself." The distinguishing feature of M–C–M′ is the inverted order of succession of the two antithetical acts, sale and purchase (buy first, sell second — the reverse of simple circulation C–M–C). Marx asks the foundational question of the whole chapter: "How can this purely formal distinction change the nature of these processes, as if by magic?" The thesis he will establish is that it cannot — no mere reordering can conjure surplus-value out of a realm whose immanent law is the exchange of equivalents.

The chapter proceeds by exhaustion. Marx demonstrates, one by one, that surplus-value cannot arise from:

  1. The formal inversion of sale and purchase (which has no existence for the other parties, A and B, and collapses back into ordinary circulation);
  2. The exchange of equivalents in its "pure form";
  3. Selling commodities above their value (the gain is lost again as a buyer);
  4. Buying commodities below their value (mirror-image failure);
  5. A special class of buyers-who-do-not-sell (whose money must have been given for nothing in the first place);
  6. Individual cunning or cleverness (a mere redistribution, like theft);
  7. Merchants' capital and usurers' capital (the "antediluvian," derivative forms);
  8. Production outside circulation (labour creates value but cannot create self-valorizing value).

The double result — the chapter's two-fold conclusion — is the celebrated antinomy: capital cannot arise from circulation, and it is equally impossible for it to arise apart from circulation. "It must have its origin both in circulation and not in circulation." The money-owner "must buy his commodities at their value, sell them at their value, and yet at the end of the process withdraw more value from circulation than he threw into it at the beginning." He must, and yet must not, "emerge as a butterfly" in the sphere of circulation. Marx closes with the challenge Hic Rhodus, hic salta! — "Rhodes is here; leap here and now."

Intellectual objective and fit: This chapter is the dialectical hammer-blow that shatters the illusionistic view of capital (circulation as a value-generating realm) in order to relocate the source of surplus-value to the sphere of production — specifically, to the "background which is not visible in the circulation itself." It is the indispensable bridge between Part I (commodity and money) and the solution to follow: the discovery of a commodity whose use-value is itself the source of value, namely labour-power. Without Chapter 5, Chapter 6 would be arbitrary; with it, the purchase of labour-power becomes a logical necessity rather than a device.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Formal Inversion of Sale and Purchase — and Its Instant Disappearance

Marx opens by isolating what appears to be the unique signature of capital: the inverted order of succession of sale and purchase. In simple commodity circulation (C–M–C) the owner sells first and buys afterward; in the capital circuit (M–C–M′) the money-owner buys first and sells afterward. The "magic" thesis of capital's apologists is that this purely formal reversal can transform the nature of the acts themselves — that beginning with a purchase and ending with a sale somehow generates value.

Marx dismantles this immediately with a social-relational argument. The inversion "has no existence for two of the three persons who transact business together." Consider three parties: the capitalist, A (from whom he buys), and B (to whom he sells). For A and B the distinction is invisible. A only knows that he sold to the capitalist; B only knows that he bought from the capitalist. The capitalist confronts A purely as a buyer (money) and B purely as a seller (commodities). Neither A nor B confronts him as "capital or a capitalist" — only as money or commodities. The "series" — the connection between the two acts — exists "for me alone."

Worse for the thesis: A and B, if the capitalist tried to explain his "meritorious" inversion, would protest that he had the order exactly backward. From A's standpoint the capitalist began with a sale (A's sale of commodities to him); from B's standpoint the capitalist concluded with a purchase (B's purchase from him). And beyond that, A and B would declare the whole circuit "superfluous and nothing but hocus-pocus": henceforth A would buy direct from B and B sell direct to A. The entire three-party operation would "shrink down to a single, one-sided phase of the ordinary circulation of commodities." The crucial conclusion: the inversion of the order of succession does not take us outside the sphere of simple circulation. Therefore we must investigate whether simple circulation, "by its nature," could permit valorization.

The Test Case: Simple Exchange of Equivalents

Marx reduces the analysis to its purest form: barter between two commodity-owners whose mutual debts balance on settlement day. In this model money serves only as money of account (a measuring device expressing values in prices) and never confronts commodities "in a material shape."

Here a sharp distinction between use-value and exchange-value does the work. With respect to use-value, both parties may genuinely gain. Marx cites Destutt de Tracy: "Exchange is an admirable transaction by which both sides gain — always (!)." The division-of-labor example makes this concrete: A, a wine-grower, may produce more wine in a given labour-time than B could, and B produces more corn than A could. So A gets more corn for the same exchange-value, and B more wine, than either would obtain by producing both goods himself. With respect to use-value, "exchange is a transaction by which both sides gain."

But "It is otherwise with exchange-value." Marx deploys Mercier de la Rivière: exchanging corn worth 50 for wine worth 50 "produces no increase of exchange-value either for the one or the other; for each of them already possessed, before the exchange, a value equal to that which he acquired." This is the first statement of the equivalence principle: pure exchange merely transfers equal values.

Critically, introducing money as a medium of circulation — splitting the transaction into two physically distinct acts (sale, then purchase) — "does not alter this situation." Why? Because the value of a commodity is expressed in its price before it enters circulation; it is therefore a precondition of circulation, not a result. The price is fixed prior to the act; circulation merely moves an already-determined magnitude.

The Change of Form and the Immanence of Equivalence

Marx now generalizes. Considering exchange "in the abstract," i.e., disregarding everything that does not follow from the immanent laws of simple commodity circulation, all that happens (setting aside the replacement of one use-value by another) is a metamorphosis — a mere change in the form of the commodity. The same value, "the same quantity of objectified social labour," remains in the owner's hands: first as his commodity, then as money, then again as commodity. "This change of form does not imply any change in the magnitude of the value." Marx's decisive analogy: the transformation is no more value-changing than "the changing of a £5 note into sovereigns, half-sovereigns and shillings." (A £5 note equals 5 sovereigns equals 10 half-sovereigns equals 100 shillings — same value, different form.)

The conclusion is stated with the force of a theorem: "in so far as the circulation of commodities involves a change only in the form of their values, it necessarily involves the exchange of equivalents, provided the phenomenon occurs in its purity." This is the lemma on which everything rests. Marx pauses to flay the vulgar economists, who "have practically no inkling of the nature of value" and who, "whenever they wish to consider the phenomenon in its purity, after their fashion, assume that supply and demand are equal, i.e. that they cease to have any effect at all." That is — the very abstraction Marx is performing rigorously, they perform accidentally and then forget.

From use-value, both may gain; from exchange-value, "Where equality exists there is no gain" (Galiani). Prices may diverge from values, but such divergence "appears as an infringement of the laws governing the exchange of commodities." In its pure form, the exchange of commodities is an exchange of equivalents, and thus it is not a method of increasing value.

The Condillac Fallacy: Substituting Use-Value for Exchange-Value

Marx now names the specific intellectual error that generates the illusion of circulation-born profit: "an inadvertent substitution, a confusion of use-value and exchange-value." The exemplar is Condillac, whom Marx quotes at length. Condillac's claim: "It is not true that in an exchange of commodities we give value for value. On the contrary, each of the two contracting parties in every case gives a less for a greater value… If we really exchanged equal values, neither party could make a profit… The value of a thing consists solely in its relation to our needs." The trick here is sliding from "value-in-use" (where both parties can gain) to "value-in-exchange" (where they cannot).

Marx's critique has two layers. First, Condillac "confuses use-value with exchange-value." Second, and more devastatingly, Condillac "in a really childish manner assumes that, in a society in which the production of commodities is well developed, each producer produces his own means of subsistence, and throws into circulation only what is superfluous." Le Trosne's answer is decisive: "In a developed society absolutely nothing is superfluous." And Le Trosne teases Condillac's tautology: if both parties receive more for an equal amount and part with less, "they both get the same."

Marx notes the political stake: Condillac's error is "frequently repeated by modern economists, especially when the point is to show that the exchange of commodities in its developed form, commerce, is productive of surplus-value." He cites S. P. Newman: "Commerce… adds value to products, for the same products in the hands of consumers are worth more than in the hands of producers." Marx's rebuttal is elegant and cutting: "commodities are not paid for twice over, once on account of their use-value, and a second time on account of their value." It is true that use-value is more serviceable to the buyer than the seller — but "its money-form is more so to the seller. Would he sell it otherwise?" The buyer "converting stockings into money" is no more an "act of production" than the seller is. Marx also fires a footnote broadside at Wilhelm Roscher, who had enlisted Condillac as guarantor of his own "childish notions."

Consequence: "If commodities… of equal exchange-value, and consequently equivalents, are exchanged, it is plain that no one abstracts more value from circulation than he throws into it." Surplus-value formation does not occur. But since real processes do not occur in their pure form, Marx grants a test: let us assume an exchange of non-equivalents.

The First Deception Hypothesis: Selling Above Value

Marx sets up the sphere rigorously. The market is frequented only by commodity-owners; the power they exercise over each other "is no other than the power of their commodities." The material variety of commodities is the driving force of exchange and makes buyers and sellers mutually dependent. Apart from this material (use-value) variety, the only distinction between commodities is the distinction between their natural form and their converted form — between commodities and money. Consequently, owners "can be differentiated only into sellers, those who own commodities, and buyers, those who own money." The dramatis personae are thus exhausted by two roles.

Now the first deception hypothesis: suppose an "inexplicable privilege" lets the seller sell above value — sell a commodity worth 100 for 110, a 10% nominal increase. The seller pockets a surplus-value of 10. But — and this is the whole argument — "after he has sold he becomes a buyer." A third owner now confronts him as seller, and enjoys the same privilege. "Our friend gained 10 as a seller only to lose it again as a buyer." Net result: all owners sell to each other at 10% above value, "which is exactly the same as if they sold them at their true value." Marx's analogy: "A universal and nominal price increase of this kind has the same effect as if the values of commodities had been expressed for example in silver instead of in gold." All money-names rise; the relations between values remain unchanged. He cites John Gray: sellers "are not enriched… since what they gain as sellers, they precisely expend in the quality of buyers."

The Mirror Hypothesis: Buying Below Value

Marx now runs the mirror-image test: suppose the buyer has the privilege of purchasing below value. Does this differ? No — because the buyer "was a seller before he became a buyer; he had already lost 10 per cent as a seller before he gained 10 per cent as a buyer." Every buyer was, before his purchase, someone who had to sell to obtain the money. Quoting Le Trosne: whoever must sell a product worth 24 livres for 18 livres will, when spending those 18 livres, receive only what 18 livres would have bought — the loss simply reasserts itself on the buying side. "Everything remains as it was before."

Therefore: "The formation of surplus-value, and therefore the transformation of money into capital, can consequently be explained neither by assuming that commodities are sold above their value, nor by assuming that they are bought at less than their value." Marx footnotes Mercier de la Rivière to the same effect: a seller can raise his prices only if he accepts higher prices for what he buys, and a consumer pays less only if he accepts less for what he sells.

Torrens, "Effectual Demand," and the Parasite Consumer Class

Marx anticipates a sophisticated evasion — Colonel Torrens's "effectual demand," defined as the power and inclination of consumers "to give for commodities… some greater portion of… capital than their production costs." Marx reduces this to its kernel: it merely disguises "the following simple phrase: the owner of commodities possesses, as a seller, the privilege of selling too dear." In circulation "producers and consumers confront each other only as buyers and sellers." One producer confronts another; the only difference is that one buys and one sells. Selling dear as producer and overpaying as consumer "does not carry us a single step further" (citing Ramsay: "Who are the consumers?").

The consistent upholders of the nominal-price theory are therefore forced into an absurd implication: they must assume "a class of buyers who do not sell, i.e. a class of consumers who do not produce." Marx leaps ahead to anticipate this and demolish it: such a class's purchase-money "must constantly flow into its coffers without any exchange, gratis, whether by might or by right, from the pockets of the commodity-owners themselves." To sell commodities above value to such a class "is only to get back again, by swindling, a part of the money previously handed over for nothing."

His historical illustration is the tribute economy of antiquity: the towns of Asia Minor paid a yearly money tribute to Rome; Rome then bought commodities from them, and bought them too dear; the provincials "cheated the Romans, and in this way swindled back from their conquerors a portion of the tribute." Yet — and this is the punchline — "the provincials remained the ones who had been cheated. Their goods were still paid for with their own money." No wealth was created; the same money simply circled back. "That is not the way to get rich or to create surplus-value."

The Clever Individual: The Robinsonade of the Sharp Trader

Marx now considers whether his perplexity stems from treating people "merely as personified categories, instead of as individuals." Perhaps individual cleverness — not an institutional privilege — is the source. Suppose A is "clever enough to get the advantage of B and C without their being able to take their revenge." A sells wine worth £40 and obtains corn worth £50 — "has converted his £40 into £50… and has transformed his commodities into capital."

Marx examines this with arithmetical precision. Before the exchange: £40 of wine (A) + £50 of corn (B) = a total value of £90. After the exchange: still £90. "The value in circulation has not increased by one iota; all that has changed is its distribution between A and B." What appears on one side as loss appears on the other as surplus — "what appears on one side as a minus appears on the other side as a plus." Marx's conclusion is blunt: "The same change would have taken place if A, without the disguise provided by the exchange, had directly stolen the £10 from B."

The general theorem follows: "The sum of the values in circulation can clearly not be augmented by any change in their distribution." Marx's illustration here employs a currency-dealer trope ("no more than a Jew can increase the quantity of the precious metals in a country by selling a farthing from the time of Queen Anne for a guinea") — a rhetorical flourish reflecting the period's idiom rather than any part of the logical argument, but it graphically makes his point: an advantageous trade is a redistribution of fixed value, not an augmentation of it. Therefore: "The capitalist class of a given country, taken as a whole, cannot defraud itself." Marx cites Destutt de Tracy, who (ironically, as a member of the Institut) held the opposite view — that industrialists profit because "they all sell for more than it has cost to produce. And to whom do they sell? In the first instance to one another." Marx has just shown this to be circular.

The Two Theorems: Equivalence and Non-Equivalence

Marx now states the double conclusion in its sharpest form, supported by J. B. Say (who borrowed the point, "almost word for word," from the Physiocrats, just as his celebrated "Products can only be bought with products" reproduces Le Trosne's "Products can only be paid for with products"):

"If equivalents are exchanged, no surplus-value results, and if non-equivalents are exchanged, we still have no surplus-value. Circulation, or the exchange of commodities, creates no value."

He cites Wayland: "Exchange confers no value at all upon products." This is the exhaustion of the possibilities within circulation — the two logical branches (equal or unequal exchange) are both dead ends for surplus-value.

Merchants' Capital and Usurers' Capital: The "Antediluvian" Forms

This result explains why Marx has so far excluded "the well-known and so to speak antediluvian forms" of capital: merchants' capital and usurers' (interest-bearing) capital. His treatment here is a compressed but crucial digression.

Merchants' capital is the form M–C–M′ "at its purest" — buying in order to sell dearer. But the entire movement takes place "within the sphere of circulation." Since it is impossible, by circulation alone, to explain the transformation of money into capital, merchants' capital "appears to be an impossibility, as long as equivalents are exchanged." Its only apparent escape is to be "derived from the twofold advantage gained, over both the selling and the buying producers, by the merchant who parasitically inserts himself between them." Marx cites Franklin's aphorism: "war is robbery, commerce is cheating." Should valorization of merchants' capital not rest on frauds practised on producers, "a long series of intermediate steps would be necessary, which are as yet entirely absent," given the present assumption of simple circulation. (Opdyke's dictum — "Under the rule of invariable equivalents commerce would be impossible" — and Engels's note that trade's "equivalent" is not a true equivalent are marshalled to show that merchants' capital presupposes precisely the divergence Marx has bracketed.)

Usurers' capital is worse. In merchants' capital, the two extremes (money thrown out and augmented money withdrawn) are at least mediated through a purchase and a sale. But in usurers' capital, M–C–M′ "is reduced to the unmediated extremes M–M′, money which is exchanged for more money, a form incompatible with the nature of money and therefore inexplicable from the standpoint of the exchange of commodities." Marx invokes Aristotle's Politics (c. 10): chrematistics is a double science — commerce and household economics; the latter is "necessary and praiseworthy," the former "based on circulation and with justice disapproved (for it is not based on Nature, but on mutual cheating)." The usurer "is most rightly hated, because money itself is the source of his gain, and is not used for the purposes for which it was invented." Money "originated for the exchange of commodities, but interest makes out of money, more money. Hence its name" — the Greek τόκος signifying both interest and offspring — "for the offspring resembles the parent." Aristotle: of all modes of making a living, usury "is the most contrary to Nature."

Marx closes the digression with a forward-looking promise: in the course of the investigation he will show that merchants' capital and interest-bearing capital are derivative forms, and it will become clear "why, historically, these two forms appear before the modern primary form of capital." This is a dense historical materialist claim — that the "antediluvian" forms are parasitic on and presuppose a productive base that they did not themselves create.

Outside Circulation: The Impotence of Production Alone

The penultimate movement turns the proposition upside down. Having shown surplus-value cannot arise from circulation — so that "for it to be formed, something must take place in the background which is not visible in the circulation itself" (citing Ramsay: "Profit… is not made by exchanging. Had it not existed before, neither could it after") — Marx asks: can surplus-value originate anywhere else than in circulation? He examines the only remaining sphere: the commodity-owner standing in relation to his own commodity, outside any contact with other owners.

His relation to his own commodity "is limited to this, that the commodity contains a quantity of his own labour which is measured according to definite social laws." That quantity is expressed by the magnitude of value and, reckoned in money of account, by the price — say £10. But his labour does not receive a double representation: it is not represented both in the value of the commodity and in an excess over and above that value — "it is not represented in a price of 10 which is simultaneously a price of 11." The essential formula: labour creates value, but not self-valorizing value. "The commodity-owner can create value by his labour, but he cannot create values which can valorize themselves."

Marx's concrete illustration is the metamorphosis of leather into boots. The owner adds fresh labour to the leather, and the boots have more value than the leather did, "because it contains a greater quantity of labour." But — crucially — "the value of the leather remains what it was. It has not valorized itself, it has not annexed surplus-value during the making of the boots." The increased value of the boots equals exactly the labour newly added; the original value was merely transferred, not expanded. Hence: "It is therefore impossible that, outside the sphere of circulation, a producer of commodities can, without coming into contact with other commodity-owners, valorize value, and consequently transform money or commodities into capital."

The Double Result and the Statement of the Problem

All strands now converge on the chapter's governing antinomy:

"Capital cannot therefore arise from circulation, and it is equally impossible for it to arise apart from circulation. It must have its origin both in circulation and not in circulation."

Marx announces "a double result." First, the transformation of money into capital "has to be developed on the basis of the immanent laws of the exchange of commodities, in such a way that the starting-point is the exchange of equivalents." Second — the conditions of the problem in their full, contradictory formulation:

"The money-owner, who is as yet only a capitalist in larval form, must buy his commodities at their value, sell them at their value, and yet at the end of the process withdraw more value from circulation than he threw into it at the beginning. His emergence as a butterfly must, and yet must not, take place in the sphere of circulation. These are the conditions of the problem. Hic Rhodus, hie salta!"

The larva/butterfly metaphor captures the developmental logic of Part II: the money-owner begins as potential capital, and his transformation into actual capital (the butterfly) is precisely what remains to be explained. The Latin tag — from Aesop's fable of the boaster who claimed to have made an immense leap at Rhodes, challenged to "leap here and now" — functions simultaneously as a defiance to rival theorists (produce the explanation here and now, on these exact conditions) and, as Marx's footnote notes, an allusion to the Preface of Hegel's Philosophy of Right, where the phrase marks the philosopher's task of comprehending what is rather than what ought to be.

Footnote 24 — a substantial methodological note — clarifies the precise meaning of the equivalence starting-point. It states that the formation of capital must be possible "even though the price and the value of a commodity be the same, for it cannot be explained by referring to any divergence between price and value." If prices do differ from values, we must first reduce the former to the latter, disregarding the divergence as "accidental," so as to observe capital-formation against the pure exchange of commodities without "disturbing incidental circumstances." Marx then makes the realist case: "The continual oscillations in prices, their rise and fall, compensate each other, cancel each other out, and carry out their own reduction to an average price which is their internal regulator." This average price is "the guiding light of the merchant or the manufacturer in every undertaking of a lengthy nature." A rational manufacturer formulates the problem thus: How can we account for the origin of capital on the assumption that prices are regulated by the average price, i.e. ultimately by the value of the commodities? And here Marx adds a further precision — the word "ultimately" is necessary because "average prices do not directly coincide with the values of commodities, as Adam Smith, Ricardo, and others believe." A sub-footnote reinforces this: Ricardo "accepts Smith's confusion or identification of exchange-value with cost-price or natural price," based on the notion that exchange-value is formed by adding together wages, profit, and rent.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The fundamental principle — the sterility of circulation — is here established with the rigor of a mathematical theorem. The chapter's most durable lesson is the equivalence theorem: any act of exchange, considered in its pure form, transfers value without creating it, and any deviation from equivalence (over-selling or under-buying) merely redistributes an unchanged total. This is the "no-free-lunch" theorem of value theory, and it stands at the logical foundation of the entire critique of political economy.

The systemic law it reveals is that capital is a contradictory category, not a simple or self-evident one. The antinomy "in and not in circulation" cannot be resolved by refinement of the circulation sphere; it can only be resolved by discovering a hidden hinge that belongs simultaneously to circulation (it is a contract, a purchase) and to production (its consumption generates value). That hinge — the commodity labour-power, whose use-value is its capacity to create more value than it costs — is the solution Marx spends the next chapter unveiling. Thus Chapter 5's negativity is generative: it forces the very discovery that constitutes the foundation of surplus-value theory.

The chapter also functions as a polemical clearinghouse. It systematically invalidates the pre-Marxian and contemporary explanations of profit: the mercantilist concern with selling dear (the "balance of trade" logic), the Physiocratic and Condillacian conflation of use-value and exchange-value, Torrens's "effectual demand," Malthus's and Chalmers's glorification of non-producing consumers, and the naive apologia of commerce as an "act of production." Marx exposes a common structural flaw: each illegitimately transfers to exchange-value a property (mutual gain) that belongs only to use-value.

Broader theoretical implications. First, the chapter's insistence on starting from the exchange of equivalents (rather than from price-value divergences) establishes the methodological priority of value over price and of production over exchange — the germ of the distinction between surplus-value and its phenomenal forms (profit, rent, interest) treated in Volume III. Second, the dialectic of the larva and the butterfly, and the placement of an apparently paradoxical thesis ("must, and yet must not, take place in circulation"), demonstrate the systematic rather than eclectic character of the analysis: contradictions are not to be evaded but carried forward until the categories that resolve them are discovered. Third, the digression on merchants' and usurers' capital embeds a historical-materialist chronology: the "antediluvian" parasitic forms precede the modern primary form precisely because they presuppose forces they do not generate — a claim Marx will vindicate in the historical sections of Capital and in the Grundrisse. Finally, the chapter's footnote 24, with its insistence that average prices do not directly coincide with values (against Smith and Ricardo), signals that Marx's value theory is not the classical "cost-of-production" theory but a theory of the social labour concealed beneath price — the theoretical seed of the transformation problem and of Marx's distinctive critique of classical political economy.

The essential takeaway: Circulation is the sphere of appearance, not of origin. Whatever surplus-value may be, it is not made by the act of exchange; the question of its origin drives us, with mathematical compulsion, "into the hidden abode of production." That is the lasting bequest of Chapter 5 — and its Hic Rhodus, hic salta! remains one of the most consequential challenges ever posed in economic thought.
