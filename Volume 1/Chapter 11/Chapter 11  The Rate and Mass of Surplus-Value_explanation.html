Volume 1 • Chapter 11: The Rate and Mass of Surplus-Value

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 11, "The Rate and Mass of Surplus-Value," is the culminating theoretical chapter of Part IV of Capital Volume I, the section devoted to the production of absolute surplus-value. It occupies a strategic hinge-point in the architecture of the work. Having spent Chapters 7–10 establishing the labour-process, the distinction between constant and variable capital, the rate of surplus-value (s/v, or surplus labour/necessary labour), and the historical struggle over the working day, Marx now poses a fresh and deceptively simple question: how is the rate of exploitation related to the mass of value extracted? The rate is an intensive magnitude—a ratio, a "degree." The mass is an extensive magnitude—a quantity of value, a sum of money. The chapter's central intellectual objective is to demonstrate rigorously that the second is governed by the first through a determinate algebraic relationship, and to draw out that relationship's three corollary laws.

The chapter's core thesis is that, once three magnitudes are fixed—(1) the value of labour-power, (2) the degree of exploitation, and (3) the number of workers employed—the mass of surplus-value is wholly determined by the product of the variable capital advanced and the rate of surplus-value. In the notation Marx himself sets out, S = v × (a'/a) × n, equivalently S = P × (a'/a) × n, equivalently S = (s/v) × V. From this single equation, Marx derives a set of consequences that carry the whole weight of the volume's subsequent argument: the substitutability of the number of workers for their intensity of exploitation; the absolute barrier imposed by the length of the working day on any such substitution; the direct proportionality between variable capital and surplus-value mass (together with the corollary that constant capital contributes nothing to newly created value); the social significance of the total working day as a national quantity bounded by population and by the biologically limited day; and the threshold concept that not every sum of money can function as capital at all.

Methodologically, the chapter is a masterclass in what we might call the dialectic of appearance and law. Its third law—that surplus-value mass varies directly with variable capital—"clearly contradicts all experience based on immediate appearances," because the cotton-spinner with a vast constant capital and a tiny variable capital pockets no less profit than the baker who employs almost entirely variable capital. Marx does not resolve this contradiction here; he explicitly defers it, insisting that "many intermediate terms are still needed," anticipating the transformation of surplus-value into profit (Book III) and the disintegration of the Ricardian school (Book IV). This deferral is not evasion but architectural precision: the chapter marks where the analysis must later go.

Finally, the chapter pivots from pure quantitative algebra to a historical and philosophical register. The emergence of a capitalist from a mere money-owner is shown to require a minimum capital, and that minimum crosses over—by what Marx, invoking Hegel, calls a dialectical inversion—from a merely quantitative accumulation into a qualitative social transformation: the birth of a class wholly released from manual labour. In its closing pages the chapter unveils the formal subsumption of labour under capital, describes capital as a coercive relation surpassing all prior systems of compulsory labour in ruthless energy, and then stages a remarkable inversion: under the valorization process, it is not the worker who employs the means of production but "the means of production which employ the worker." The chapter ends not with an equation but with a case study in bourgeois ideology—the letter of a Scottish mill-owner who genuinely believes his spindles mechanically contain a "power of self-valorization"—as an illustration of how deeply the fetish-form of capital penetrates capitalist consciousness. Thus Chapter 11 completes Part IV and opens the door both to relative surplus-value (Part V) and to the concept of capital's self-expansion as a social totality.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Presupposition: Fixed Labour-Power Value and the Direct Translation of Rate into Mass

The chapter opens by stating its controlling presupposition, one inherited from all preceding analysis: "the value of labour-power, and therefore the part of the working day necessary for the reproduction or maintenance of that labour-power, is assumed to be a given, constant magnitude." This is the necessary labour-time —the fraction of the day the worker spends reproducing the value of his own subsistence. By holding it constant, Marx isolates one variable at a time, allowing a clean algebra to emerge. He immediately illustrates with a numerical example: if necessary labour is 6 hours, expressed as 3 shillings of gold, then 3 shillings is the daily value of one labour-power and hence the value of the capital advanced to purchase it. If the rate of surplus-value is 100 per cent, this 3-shilling variable capital yields 3 shillings of surplus-value—six hours of unpaid surplus labour. The rate is thus demonstrably equivalent to a specific, physical quantity of surplus-labour time.

From this starting point Marx generalizes along two axes. Aggregation across workers: variable capital is the "monetary expression for the total value of all the labour-powers the capitalist employs simultaneously." Given a fixed value per labour-power, the magnitude of variable capital varies directly with the number of workers. If one labour-power costs 3 shillings daily, then 100 labour-powers require 300 shillings, and n labour-powers require n × 3 shillings. Scaling of surplus-value: if 3 shillings produces 3 shillings of surplus-value, then 300 shillings produces 300 shillings, and n × 3 shillings produce n × 3 shillings of surplus-value. The mass of surplus-value is therefore the surplus-value produced by one worker's working day multiplied by the number of workers employed.

This yields the first law: the mass of surplus-value equals the variable capital advanced multiplied by the rate of surplus-value—i.e., it is determined by the product of (a) the number of labour-powers simultaneously exploited by the same capitalist and (b) the degree of exploitation of each individual labour-power.

The Algebra of Exploitation: Formalizing the Rate–Mass Relationship

Marx then renders the argument symbolically—a rare and deliberate move that marks how much weight the equation is intended to bear. He defines:

  • S — the mass of surplus-value
  • s — the surplus-value supplied by an individual worker in the average day
  • v — the variable capital advanced daily for the purchase of one individual labour-power (equivalently, the daily value of one labour-power)
  • V — the sum total of variable capital
  • P — the value of an average labour-power
  • a'/a — the degree of exploitation, i.e. surplus labour divided by necessary labour (the rate of surplus-value)
  • n — the number of workers employed

The famous formula then appears as three equivalent expressions bracketed together:

S = v × (a'/a) × n = P × (a'/a) × n = (s/v) × V

Each expression repays close reading, because the equivalence is not trivial—it encodes the entire argument of the chapter in miniature.

The first form, S = v × (a'/a) × n, says: take the daily value of a single labour-power (v), multiply by its degree of exploitation (a'/a), and multiply by the number of workers (n). It is the arithmetic of the individual capitalist's payroll.

The second form, S = P × (a'/a) × n, is formally identical but reframes v as P, "the value of an average labour-power." This substitution is critical: it introduces an explicit averaging assumption, allowing Marx to abstract from workers who are faster or slower than the norm.

The third form, S = (s/v) × V, is the most theoretically significant, because (s/v) is precisely the rate of surplus-value and V is total variable capital. This form announces the general result: mass of surplus-value = total variable capital × rate of surplus-value, or S = V × (a'/a). The mass of surplus-value is a product of one extensive quantity (V, the total wage-bill or value of labour-power purchased) and one intensive ratio (the degree of exploitation).

Immediately after setting out the equation, Marx adds a crucial caveat: "We assume throughout, not only that the value of an average labour-power is constant, but that the workers employed by a capitalist are reduced to average workers." He concedes that "there do exist exceptional cases in which the surplus-value produced does not increase in proportion to the number of workers being exploited"—but then notes that in such cases "the value of the labour-power does not remain constant." The law is thus stated conditionally: it holds ceteris paribus, and the exception proves the assumption by violating it. This is a characteristic Marxian move—laying bare the counterfactuals on which a law's validity depends.

The Compensation Theorem: Substitution Between Number of Workers and Degree of Exploitation

The derivation of the first law immediately generates a striking secondary result, which we may call the compensation theorem. If the mass of surplus-value is the product of two factors—variable capital and rate of exploitation—then a decrease in one factor can be compensated for by a proportionate increase in the other, leaving the mass unchanged. Marx illustrates arithmetically. Suppose the capitalist must advance 300 shillings to exploit 100 workers daily, and the rate of surplus-value is 50 per cent. This produces 150 shillings of surplus-value, or "100 × 3 working hours." Now suppose the rate of surplus-value doubles—which, given a necessary labour of 6 hours, means the working day expands from 6 to 12 hours (6 necessary + 6 surplus)—while variable capital is halved to 150 shillings, i.e. 50 workers. This too yields 150 shillings, or "50 × 6 working hours." The mass is identical.

The inverse also holds: "a fall in the rate of surplus-value leaves the mass of surplus-value which has been produced unaltered, if the amount of the variable capital, i.e. the number of workers employed, increases in the same proportion."

The theorem's strategic significance crystallizes in a single sentence: "Within certain limits, therefore, the supply of labour exploitable by capital is independent of the supply of workers." This is a claim of enormous political-economic weight. It means that capital's demand for labour—for labour-time extracted—is not simply a demand for labourers. Capital can, up to a point, substitute intensity for breadth. This is precisely the lever by which Marx anticipates and refutes the vulgar economist who imagines that the market price of labour is determined by supply and demand. In a delicious footnote Marx writes that this elementary law "appears to be unknown to the vulgar economist, who imagines, like an inverted Archimedes, that in the determination of the market price of labour by supply and demand he has found the fulcrum by means of which he cannot so much move the world, as bring it to a standstill." The image—an Archimedes who claims a fulcrum but who only stands still—mocks the pretension of supply-and-demand theory to explain wages when it has failed to notice that the mass of exploitable labour is itself manipulable through the length of the working day.

The Second Law: The Absolute Barrier Set by the Working Day

Compensation, however, is not unlimited. Marx immediately insists that "there are limits, which cannot be overcome, to the compensation." He states the reason with mathematical clarity: whatever the value of labour-power, the total value a worker can produce in a day is always less than the value in which 24 hours of labour are objectified. If 12 shillings expresses 24 hours of objectified labour, no worker can produce 12 shillings of new value in a day, because no worker can work 24 hours. So the lengthening of the working day has a hard ceiling.

He illustrates with numbers. Take the necessary labour = 6 hours, so the 12-hour day yields 6 surplus hours. A capital of 1,500 shillings employing 500 workers at a 100 per cent rate produces 1,500 shillings of surplus-value, or 6 × 500 = 3,000 working hours. Now take a capital of 300 shillings employing 100 workers at 200 per cent (an 18-hour day: 6 necessary + 12 surplus). This produces 600 shillings of surplus-value, or 12 × 100 = 1,200 working hours. Crucially, "its total value-product, the equivalent of the variable capital advanced plus the surplus-value, can, day in, day out, never reach the sum of 1,200 shillings or 24 × 100 working hours." That is, even pushing the day to its 18-hour point, the total value-product (v + s = 300 + 600 = 900 shillings) falls short of the absolute maximum conceivable (24 hours × 100 workers × 0.5 shillings/hour = 1,200 shillings). The absolute limit of the average working day—"by nature always less than 24 hours"—fixes an absolute limit to any compensation scheme.

Marx calls this the "self-evident second law," but insists it is "of importance for the explanation of many phenomena." Specifically, it illuminates a contradiction within capital itself: capital has "a tendency to reduce as much as possible the number of workers employed, i.e. the amount of its variable component"—a tendency Marx says will be developed later (see Chapter 25, Sections 2–3, on the general law of capitalist accumulation)—while it simultaneously has "the other tendency to produce the greatest possible mass of surplus-value." These two drives collide precisely because of the second law: cutting the workforce while raising the rate of exploitation can compensate only within the bounds of the physically possible day. Beyond that bound, shrinking variable capital does shrink surplus-value. Marx adds the converse: if the mass of labour-power or the variable capital increases but not in proportion to the fall in the rate of surplus-value, the surplus-value mass diminishes.

The Third Law: Variable Capital as the Exclusive Determinant of Surplus-Value Mass

The third law follows from the same equation. Given the rate of surplus-value (degree of exploitation), the value of labour-power, and a fixed limit to the working day and its necessary component, "the mass of value and surplus-value produced by the individual capitalist is clearly exclusively dependent on the mass of labour that he sets in motion." And since—under the assumptions—the mass of labour set in motion depends on the mass of labour-power, i.e., the number of workers he exploits, and that number is determined by "the amount of the variable capital advanced," it follows that with a given rate of surplus-value and a given value of labour-power, the masses of surplus-value produced vary directly as the amounts of variable capital advanced.

Here Marx pauses for a critical clarification that will govern his later treatment of capital composition. A capitalist divides his capital into a constant part (outlay on means of production) and a variable part (outlay on living labour-power). Marx notes that the proportion between these parts—the composition of capital—differs between branches of production and, within a branch, changes with the "technical foundations" and the "ways of linking together the processes of production in society." Yet "whatever the proportion between the constant and the variable part of a given capital, whether it is 1:2, or 1:10 or 1:x, the law just laid down is not affected by this." Why? Because, by the prior analysis of Chapter 8, the value of constant capital re-appears in the value of the product, but does not enter into the newly produced value—the newly created value-product. More spinners certainly require more raw material, spindles, and machinery, but the value of these additional means of production, whether it "may rise, fall, remain unaltered, be large or small; it has no influence on the valorization process performed by the labour-powers which set the means of production in motion."

This sharpens the third law into its mature statement: "the masses of value and of surplus-value produced by different capitals—the value of labour-power being given and its degree of exploitation being equal—vary directly as the amounts of the variable components of these capitals, i.e. the parts which have been turned into living labour-power."

Labour Process versus Valorization Process: The Methodological Pivot

The interplay just described between labour process and valorization process is the conceptual hinge of the chapter. Marx distinguishes two viewpoints on the same production process. From the standpoint of the labour process, the worker relates to the means of production per his own purposeful activity—tanning skins, he deals with "the skins as his simple object of labour." In that view, means of production are the raw material and instruments of his work. But from the standpoint of the valorization process, the very same means of production "are at once changed into means for the absorption of the labour of others." It is this dual character that later allows Marx to describe the conversion of money into means of production as the conversion of dead labour into a "title, both by right and by might, to the labour and surplus labour of others." The distinction is the theoretical engine by which the chapter moves from the algebraic laws of Parts IV's opening chapters toward the qualitative-historical conclusion of its closing ones.

The Contradiction with Appearances and the Method of Intermediate Terms

Marx now confronts the fact that his third law—the direct proportionality of surplus-value to variable capital—"clearly contradicts all experience based on immediate appearances." The example is vivid and durable: "a cotton spinner, who... employs much constant capital and little variable capital, does not, on account of this, pocket less profit or surplus-value than a baker, who sets in motion relatively much variable capital and little constant capital." Immediate experience already knows that profits do not track the wage bill—they track the total capital advanced.

Marx treats this contradiction with unusual methodological candour, refusing a cheap resolution. He writes that "for the solution of this apparent contradiction, many intermediate terms are still needed, just as, from the standpoint of elementary algebra, many intermediate terms are needed before we can understand that 0/0 may represent an actual magnitude." The reference to 0/0 is technical and important: it is the mathematical figure for an indeterminate form that can contain a determinate result once the proper mediating operations are performed. The implication is that the apparent discord between law and appearance is not a refutation of the law but a signal that further mediating categories—price of production, general rate of profit, transformation—must be developed.

Marx then situates classical economics in relation to this problem. Classical political economy "holds instinctively to this law, although it has never actually formulated it, because it is a necessary consequence of the law of value." Its response to the discordance with experience is "by making a violent abstraction"—an ad hoc retreat. And "we shall see later how the school of Ricardo came to grief on this stumbling-block." A footnote reinforces this, quoting the direct statement of the contradiction: capitals of equal magnitude but unequal organic composition "set in motion unequal quantities of unpaid labour; consequently they cannot appropriate equal quantities of surplus-value... But capitals of equal magnitude, no matter what their organic composition, yield equal profits." Marx points to Book IV, Chapter 20, "Disintegration of the Ricardian School," as the place where this intellectual impasse is traced.

By contrast, vulgar economics—"which like the Bourbons 'has really learnt nothing'"—simply refuses to look behind the appearance, clinging to "the mere semblance as opposed to the law which regulates and determines the phenomena." Marx's philosophical counterpoint is sharp and playful: "In antithesis to Spinoza, it believes that 'ignorance is a sufficient reason.'" Spinoza had argued that ignorance of true causes is not a sufficient reason for believing in final causes; the vulgar economist, in Marx's inverted paraphrase, elevates ignorance itself to an explanation. The essayistic sneer is more than ornament—it names a methodological failure: the refusal to reason beyond appearances.

The Social Working Day and Population as the Mathematical Limit

The chapter then scales the analysis from the individual capitalist to society as a whole. "The labour which is set in motion by the total capital of a society, day in, day out, may be regarded as a single working day." If, say, a million workers each work a ten-hour day, the social working day consists of ten million hours. Given the length of that day—whether its limits are "fixed physically or socially"—the mass of surplus-value can be increased only in one of two ways: (1) by increasing the number of workers, i.e. the size of the working population, or (2) by lengthening the working day itself. "The growth of population here forms the mathematical limit to the production of surplus-value by the total social capital. And, inversely, with a given population this limit is formed by the possible lengthening of the working day."

Marx ties this to a striking historical source, citing in footnote 3 an 1821 tract, An Essay on the Political Economy of Nations, which already spoke of the "Labour, that is the economic time, of society" as "a given portion, say ten hours a day of a million people, or ten million hours," noting that "capital has its boundary of increase" attained "in the actual extent of economic time employed." This citation serves a double function: it demonstrates that even bourgeois writers had glimpsed the natural limits to valorization, and it grounds Marx's abstraction of the "social working day" in real economic literature. Marx concludes the passage by foreshadowing the next chapter: "It will however be seen in the next chapter that this law holds only for the form of surplus-value dealt with up to the present"—that is, only for absolute surplus-value, produced by extending the working day. Once relative surplus-value is introduced—through productivity gains and the shortening of necessary labour—the population limit can be partly circumvented.

The Qualitative Leap: Minimum Capital and the Genetic Origins of the Capitalist

The chapter's argument now pivots from extensive magnitude to social threshold. A crucial consequence, which Marx here announces as the chapter's final thematic development, is this: "not every sum of money, or value, can be transformed into capital at will." This is a direct attack on the idea that capital is merely "money that begets money"—a purely quantitative property of any sufficiently patient hoarder of cash. Rather, the transformation presupposes "that a certain minimum of money or of exchange-value is in the hands of the individual possessor." The minimum equals at least the cost price of one labour-power employed year-round for surplus-value production.

Marx then reason through an example that inverts the standard picture of capitalist affluence. If a self-employed worker owned his own means of production and were satisfied to live as a worker, he would need only the labour-time necessary to reproduce his subsistence—say 8 hours a day—plus means of production sufficient for 8 working hours. The would-be capitalist, however, who makes him work these 8 hours plus, say, 4 surplus hours, must additionally furnish the means of production for those extra 4 hours. And crucially: if the capitalist is to live on the appropriated surplus-value as well as—and "no better than"—an ordinary worker, he must employ not one but two workers to simply subsist. If he wishes to live twice as well and turn half the surplus back into capital, he must multiply both the number of workers and the minimum capital advanced by eight.

At the low end, any capitalist who continues to work alongside his employees is a "hybrid, a man between capitalist and worker, a 'small master'." A more developed stage of capitalist production requires the capitalist to devote his full time to functioning "as capital personified"—to the "appropriation and therefore the control of the labour of others [fremde Arbeit]" and to the sale of products. The historical warrant for this threshold claim is supplied by the guild system of the Middle Ages, which "tried forcibly to prevent the transformation of the master of a craft into a capitalist, by limiting the number of workers a single master could employ to a very low maximum." Hence the possessor of money or commodities "actually turns into a capitalist only where the minimum sum advanced for production greatly exceeds the known medieval maximum."

The theoretical pay-off is one of the most philosophically charged passages in the volume. Marx declares that "here, as in natural science, is shown the correctness of the law discovered by Hegel, in his Logic, that at a certain point merely quantitative differences pass over by a dialectical inversion into qualitative distinctions." Accumulating more shillings is a quantitative process; but at a threshold, it becomes a different kind of thing—the capacity to command and supervise others' labour full-time, to be a capitalist rather than a worker. Engels' footnote amplifies this with a scientific illustration from the molecular theory of chemistry, in particular the homologous series of carbon compounds named by C. Gerhardt in 1843: paraffins (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂), normal alcohols (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂O), and normal fatty acids (CₙH₂ₙO₂). In these series, adding a single CH₂ unit to a molecule's formula—"a simply quantitative addition"—produces a qualitatively different body. The Hegelian law of the transformation of quantity into quality thereby receives an empirical, chemical analogue, and Marx plants a marker for the later concept of capital's "thresholds of production."

Historical Mediation: Guild Limits, State Subsidies, Joint-Stock Companies

Marx then deepens the historical dimension of the minimum-capital argument. The threshold "changes with the different stages of development of capitalist production, and is at given stages different in different spheres of production, according to their special technical conditions." Certain spheres, even at the dawn of capitalist production, required "a minimum of capital which is not yet to be found in the hands of single individuals." This gave rise to two historical responses. First, state subsidies to private persons—as in France in the time of Colbert and in some German states down to Marx's own epoch. Second, the formation of companies with a legally secured monopoly over particular branches—"the forerunners of the modern joint-stock companies." Marx footnotes Martin Luther, who in Von Kaufshandlung und Wucher (1524) called these institutions "the Company Monopolia" and thundered: "Who is so stupid that he cannot see that the trading companies are nothing but pure monopolies?" The passage thus quietly inaugurates the historical problematic of the joint-stock company as a response to capital thresholds—a theme Marx will resume in Volume III.

Capital as Command and Coercion: Formal Subsumption and the Surpassing of Earlier Systems

The chapter's last movement is its most historically sweeping and theoretically general. Marx announces he will not here examine "in detail the changes which take place in the relation between the capitalist and the wage-labourer in the course of the process of production, nor... the characteristics of capital itself," but will emphasize "certain main points."

First, capital as command: "Capital developed within the production process until it acquired command over labour, i.e. over self-activating labour-power, in other words the worker himself." The capitalist, "who is capital personified," now ensures that the worker performs his labour "regularly and with the proper degree of intensity."

Second, capital as coercive relation: capital "compels the working class to do more work than would be required by the narrow circle of its own needs." As "an extractor of surplus labour and an exploiter of labour-power," it "surpasses all earlier systems of production, which were based on directly compulsory labour, in its energy and its quality of unbounded and ruthless activity." This is a critical historical claim. Earlier systems—slavery, serfdom, corvée—extracted surplus labour directly, by force of law or personal domination. Capitalist exploitation, ironically, appears more "free," yet surpasses them in intensity and inexorability, because it operates through the apparently voluntary wage contract and the legal compulsion of the market.

Third, formal subsumption: "At first capital subordinates labour on the basis of the technical conditions within which labour has been carried on up to that point in history. It does not therefore directly change the mode of production." The production of absolute surplus-value by the simple extension of the working day appeared "independently of any change in the mode of production itself"—and "was no less effective in the old-fashioned bakeries than in the modern cotton factories." This is the theoretical formulation of what Marx elsewhere calls the formal subsumption of labour under capital: capital takes over an existing labour process, changes only its social form (who commands whom, over what duration), not its technical content. The passage is crucial for Part V, which will examine the real subsumption—when capital transforms the technical process itself through cooperation, manufacture, and machinery.

The Grand Inversion: Dead Labour Commanding Living Labour

The chapter's grandest theoretical passage is its articulation of the inversion of subject and object in capitalist production. Reusing the labour process/valorization process distinction, Marx writes: "It is no longer the worker who employs the means of production, but the means of production which employ the worker. Instead of being consumed by him as material elements of his productive activity, they consume him as the ferment necessary to their own life-process, and the life-process of capital consists solely in its own motion as self-valorizing value." This is the very core of Marx's account of capital as self-expanding value—value which, having become autonomous ("capital personified"), treats the worker as an input rather than a subject.

The passage then extends to the physical plant. Idle furnaces and workshops "that stand idle by night, and absorb no living labour, are 'a mere loss' to the capitalist. Hence furnaces and workshops constitute 'lawful claims upon the night-labour' of the labour-powers." Dead labour—machinery, buildings—makes legal-moral demands on living labour to be fed with activity. As soon as "a certain sum of money is transformed into means of production... the means of production themselves are transformed into a title, both by right and by might, to the labour and surplus labour of others." The title is de jure (ownership) and de facto (control); "right" and "might" operate in tandem.

The Carlile & Co. Episode: Bourgeois Consciousness as Fetishised Form

Marx closes the chapter with a concrete historical episode that makes the inversion visible in the mind of an actual capitalist. The setting is the English manufacturers' revolt of 1848–50—the agitation against the Ten Hours Bill. The documentary source is a letter printed in the Glasgow Daily Mail of 25 April 1849, under the heading "The Relay System," written by the head of "one of the oldest and most respectable houses in the West of Scotland"—Messrs Carlile Sons & Co. of the linen and cotton thread factory at Paisley, a company existing "for about a century," in operation since 1752, conducted by four generations of the same family.

The letter, as Marx quotes it, bemoans the limiting of the working day to 10 hours: "They amount to the most serious damage to the mill-owner's prospects and property. If he"—i.e. his "hands"—"worked 12 hours before, and is limited to 10, then every 12 machines or spindles in his establishment shrink to 10, and should the works be disposed of, they will be valued only as 10, so that a sixth part would thus be deducted from the value of every factory in the country." Marx comments with surgical sarcasm. In this "West of Scotland bourgeois brain, which has inherited the capitalist qualities of 'four generations'," the value of the means of production (spindles, etc.) is "so inextricably confused with the quality they possess, as capital, of valorizing themselves"—i.e. of "swallowing up every day a definite quantity of the unpaid labour of others"—that Carlile & Co.'s head "actually imagines that if he sells his factory, not only will the value of the spindles be paid to him, but, in addition, their power of self-valorization, not only the labour contained in them... but also the surplus labour which they help to pump out daily from the brave Scots of Paisley."

The fetish is total: the industrialist believes the valorizing power is a physical property of the machines, capitalised into the sales-price and diminished proportionately by any shortening of the working day. Marx's analysis had already shown why the belief is incoherent: constant capital contributes to the product's value only the value already congealed in it (its own reproduction cost), never any new value; the surplus-value is produced by living labour, not by the machinery. Yet in the mind of the capitalist, the social relation appears as a natural property of things. This is the fundamental form of the capital fetish—and Marx uses the episode to close the chapter not with an equation but with a diagnosis of consciousness.

Marx then quotes the reaction of Factory Inspector Stuart—himself "a Scotsman and, unlike the English factory inspectors, a complete prisoner of the capitalist mode of thought"—who praises Carlile's letter in his own report as "the most useful of the communications which any of the factory-owners working with relays have given to those engaged in the same trade, and which is the most calculated to remove the prejudices of such of them as have scruples respecting any change of the arrangement of the hours of work." The inspector's endorsement is the final ironic touch: even the state's supervisory apparatus fails to see through the fetish. The official enforcer of the factory acts shares the misrecognition of the very capitalists he regulates.

The chapter thus terminates not with a mathematical conclusion but with a demonstration of the ideological consequences of the value-form: that capital's self-valorizing character appears to those who own it as an inherent, quasi-physical property of the machinery they possess, and is therefore misrecognised as an object rather than understood as a social relation.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

Chapter 11 yields a set of fundamental principles that structure the rest of Marx's project.

The first principle is the analytic decomposition of capital's expansion into one extensive and one intensive magnitude. Mass of surplus-value is total variable capital × rate of surplus-value. This forbids the naive view that capital's growth is simply a matter of accumulating more money; the intensity of exploitation—how hard a given wage-bill is worked—is equally constitutive. The corollary, that the supply of exploitable labour is partly independent of the supply of workers, dismantles the vulgar supply-and-demand theory of wages. Wage-fund arguments—which pretend that a fixed pool of capital determines a fixed wage level—miss the precise mechanism by which capital can raise its surplus without increasing its wage bill.

The second principle is the absolute barrier. Because the working day is physically less than 24 hours, compensation between breadth and intensity exhausts itself at a hard limit. From this follows the structural contradiction within capital itself: its drive to economise on variable capital (throw workers out) collides with its drive to maximise surplus-value mass. Crises, chronic unemployment, and the pressure to intensify labour are all downstream of this tension.

The third principle is the composition-neutrality of newly created value. Constant capital, however vast, transfers only its own value to the product and creates no new value. This yields the third law—surplus-value mass varies directly as variable capital, given equal rates of exploitation—and immediately sets up the great apparent contradiction with the empirical equality of profit rates across industries of differing composition. Marx's refusal to resolve this here, and his insistence on "many intermediate terms," serves as a template for the relation between Volume I and Volume III: Volume I analyses value and surplus-value; Volume III will analyse price of production and profit, in which the law of value remains operative but only through mediations. The chapter is thus a bridge between the abstract analysis of capitalist production and the concrete analysis of capitalist competition and distribution.

The fourth principle is the threshold concept. Not every quantum of money is capital. There is a minimum below which money cannot become capital, and this minimum is historically variable, technically conditioned, and socially enforced (via guild limits, state subsidies, monopoly charters, and eventually joint-stock companies). The dialectical overtones—Hegel's transformation of quantity into quality, illustrated by organic chemistry's homologous series—signal that the emergence of a capitalist class is not a smooth accumulation but a qualitative leap that reconstitutes social relations.

The fifth principle is the concept of formal subsumption. At its origins, capital does not need to transform the labour process itself; it merely takes over existing techniques and extends and intensifies them. Absolute surplus-value is thus historically the "first" form of capitalist exploitation, "no less effective in the old-fashioned bakeries than in the modern cotton factories." This concept will be the baseline against which the "real subsumption" of Part V is measured.

The sixth and culminating principle is the inversion of subject and object: it is the means of production that employ the worker, not the worker who employs the means of production. Dead labour commands living labour. Idle machinery exerts a "lawful claim" upon the night-labour of the workers. The means of production become a title—by right and by might—to the labour of others. From this inversion follows the fetish-form of capital, beautifully exhibited in the Carlile & Co. episode: the capitalist cannot distinguish the machinery's value from its capacity to pump out surplus labour, and therefore thinks any diminution of the working day physically shrinks the value of his plant. This failure of recognition is not a simple error of an ignorant businessman; it is the necessary form in which the capitalist social relation appears to those enmeshed in it. Even the state inspector shares the illusion.

The broader implications are threefold. Theoretically, the chapter roots the later transformation problem in a single rigorous accounting: mass of surplus-value, total value, value-product, price of production, and profit are all internally linked, and the apparent contradictions between the value-theoretic law and competitive appearances are resolvable only through a chain of intermediate categories (Book III) and a critical history of political economy (Book IV). Historically, the chapter locates the emergence of the capitalist within a specific institutional matrix—the breakdown of guild limits, the granting of charters and subsidies, and the eventual emergence of joint-stock capital—showing that the "free" wage-labour relation was constructed, not discovered. Ideologically, the chapter demonstrates that the surface form of capital—machinery, plant, money "begetting" money—systematically conceals the underlying social relation, generating the fetishised consciousness in which even the machinery appears to labour on its own behalf and the shortening of the working day appears as a destruction of value rather than the liberation of life-time from capital's grip. The chapter thus closes Part IV not merely with a set of economic laws but with a theory of how those laws are misrecognised—setting the stage for the analysis of real subsumption, the production of relative surplus-value, and, eventually, the critique of political economy as a whole.
