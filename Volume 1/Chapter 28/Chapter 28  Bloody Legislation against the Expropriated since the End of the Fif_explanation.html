Volume 1 • Chapter 28: Bloody Legislation against the Expropriated since the End of the Fif

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

This chapter — the twenty-eighth in Capital, Volume I — is the second act of Marx's drama of "so-called primitive accumulation." Having shown in the preceding chapter that the agricultural population was forcibly expropriated from the soil — dispossessed of the commons, ejected by enclosures, and rendered homeless — Marx here confronts a puzzle that any naive theory of economic development cannot explain. The expropriated masses were, formally, "free." They had been liberated from feudal bonds and from feudal retainers. Yet this freedom was catastrophic, because they possessed no means of production, no legal protection, and no means of subsistence. The nascent manufactures of the sixteenth century could not possibly absorb them "as fast as it was thrown upon the world." The result was a mass descent into mendicancy, robbery, and vagabondage — not chiefly from moral defect ("partly from inclination") but overwhelmingly from structural compulsion ("in most cases under the force of circumstances").

The chapter's core thesis is thus stated at the outset and laboriously documented thereafter: the very men who had been dispossessed by force were then legislated against as though they had chosen their fate. They were treated as "voluntary criminals," flogged, branded, enslaved, exiled to the galleys, and executed — in a hellish sequence of statutes spanning Western Europe from Henry VII to Louis XVI. This "bloody legislation against the expropriated" served one overriding function: to terrorize and discipline these ruined producers into accepting the discipline of wage-labour. The chapter then turns, in its second movement, to the companion instrument: the forcing down of wages by Act of Parliament. Here Marx reconstructs the long history of state wage-regulation — the Statute of Labourers (1349) and its offspring — showing that the state dictated a maximum wage but never a minimum, and that only a change in the balance of class forces (the threatening attitude of the proletariat, the proposal of a minimum wage by Whitbread in 1796, the eventual repeal of wage legislation in 1813) could reverse this centuries-long direction.

The intellectual objective is polemical and demystifying. Marx aims to shatter the illusion that wage-labour is a natural, voluntary, and self-regulating relationship. His central theoretical pivot — introduced near the middle of the chapter and elaborated nowhere more concisely than here — is the distinction between the ordinary operation of capitalist domination, carried by the "silent compulsion of economic relations," and the historical genesis of that domination, which required direct, bloody, extra-economic state force. Only in retrospect, once the system is fully mature, does dependence on capital appear as a "self-evident natural law." Historically, it was manufactured at the tip of a whip and the edge of a branding iron. This chapter therefore sits as the indispensable bridge in the "So-Called Primitive Accumulation" section: between the expropriation from the soil and the genesis of the capitalist farmer and industrial capitalist that follow.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Opening Problem: A Free but Rightless Proletariat That the Economy Would Not Absorb

Marx opens by finishing a sentence begun conceptually in the previous chapter. The proletariat "created by the breaking-up of the bands of feudal retainers and by the forcible expropriation of the people from the soil" was a double creation: expelled from one mode of life and not yet admitted to another. The chapter insists on a critical timing problem. Nascent manufactures were growing, but not fast enough to swallow the human flood expelled from the countryside. Simultaneously, these "men, suddenly dragged from their accustomed mode of life, could not immediately adapt themselves to the discipline of their new condition." Here the word discipline is planted — it will become the chapter's refrain. The newly landless were "turned in massive quantities into beggars, robbers and vagabonds, partly from inclination, in most cases under the force of circumstances."

The footnote here is indispensable to Marx's argument, because it exposes a term that the translation renders as "rightless." The star note explains that Marx uses the word vogelfrei — literally "as free as a bird" — meaning "free but outside the human community and therefore entirely unprotected and without legal rights." This is the conceptual heart of the opening: the expropriated worker possessed freedom of a peculiarly lethal kind. He was free of feudal obligation and equally free of any legal personhood or protection. He had nothing to sell but his labour-power, and — crucially — nobody obliged to buy it. This "free and rightless" condition is Marx's answer to the classic bourgeois narrative of free labour.

The response of the European state was not relief but repression. "At the end of the fifteenth and during the whole of the sixteenth centuries, a bloody legislation against vagabondage was enforced throughout Western Europe." The critical theoretical claim arrives immediately: "The fathers of the present working class were chastised for their enforced transformation into vagabonds and paupers. Legislation treated them as 'voluntary' criminals, and assumed that it was entirely within their powers to go on working under the old conditions which in fact no longer existed." This is the ideological inversion that Marx mercilessly dissects — the legal system transposes a structural catastrophe (dispossession) into a moral crime (idleness), and punishes the victim for the crime of having been made destitute.

Henry VIII and the Escalating Terror of the Vagabond Statutes

The English chronology begins under Henry VII and then unfolds with a bureaucratic, almost clinical cataloguing of cruelty. Marx's method here is to let the statutes speak for themselves, listing their escalating penalties as evidence rather than commentary. Henry VIII, 1530: aged and incapable beggars receive a beggar's licence — a faint gesture of mercy that only sharpens the harshness of the rest — while "sturdy vagabonds" are whipped and imprisoned. They are "to be tied to the cart-tail and whipped until the blood streams from their bodies," then made to swear an oath to return to their birthplace or to where they lived for the last three years and to "put themselves to labour." Marx's interjection — "What grim irony!" — is one of the rare moments when the scholar's mask slips into open moral fury. The oath compels the pauper to perform an impossibility: to find wage-work in a locality from which agricultural wage-work had been systematically destroyed.

This statute is then repeated and strengthened by 27 Henry VIII, c. 25, with a graduated ladder of savagery. First arrest: whipping. Second arrest: whipping "and half the ear sliced off." Third relapse: "the offender is to be executed as a hardened criminal and enemy of the common weal." The phrase "enemy of the common weal" is doing important analytical work: it registers how completely the expropriated are presumed to be enemies of society — an ideological designation that pre-justifies the harshest punishments and aligns the vagabond with the traitor and the felon.

Edward VI, 1547: The Statute of Enslavement

The reign of Edward VI marks the apex of the terror and contains the most infamous provision in Marx's entire catalogue — a statute that literally redeems the ancient institution of slavery under the guise of punitive labour law. Marx transcribes the essentials with a cold precision that mirrors and mocks the statute's own language. Any person who "refuses to work" is condemned as a slave "to the person who has denounced him as an idler." The denunciation alone constitutes the offense; there is no trial of fact, no proof of a refusal. The master feeds the slave "on bread and water, weak broth and such refuse meat as he thinks fit." He has "the right to force him to do any work, no matter how disgusting, with whip and chains."

The statute then elaborates a whole penal architecture around this resurrected slavery. Absence for a fortnight produces slavery for life plus branding with the letter S. Running away three times brings execution as a felon. The master enjoys full proprietary rights: he may "sell him, bequeath him, let him out on hire as a slave, just as he can any other personal chattel or cattle." Any slave attempt "against the masters" is capital offence. Justices of the peace are obliged, upon information, to hunt the offenders down. A vagabond idling for three days is taken to his birthplace, branded with a red-hot iron with the letter V on the breast, and set to work "in chains, on the roads or at some other labour." If he lies about his birthplace, he becomes the slave for life of that place, its inhabitants, or "its corporation," branded with an S. Finally, "all persons have the right to take away the children of the vagabonds and keep them as apprentices" — young men until 24, girls until 20 — and runaway children become their masters' slaves for those terms, subject to irons and whipping. The clause about "an iron ring round the neck, arms or legs of his slave, by which to know him more easily and to be more certain of him" is the chapter's most physically concrete image of the reduction of the wage-labourer's ancestor to livestock.

Marx then shows how this brutal order decayed into a quieter, half-hidden survival. The statute's closing provision allowed certain poor people to be employed "by a place or by persons who were willing to give them food and drink and to find them work," creating a category of "slaves of the parish." Marx notes with dry understatement that such parish slaves "were still to be found in England in the mid nineteenth century under the name of 'roundsmen'." The terror of 1547 had not vanished; it had merely been domesticated into a rural institution still visible in Marx's own century.

A further stab at euphemism appears in the footnote, where Marx cites the anonymous Essay on Trade (1770), a pamphlet that could whitewash the 1547 statute as evidence that "the English seem to have set, in good earnest, about encouraging manufactures and employing the poor" — quoting the statute's branding clause as if it were a poor-employment scheme. Marx's inclusion of this citation is a miniature lesson in how the "encouragement of manufacture" was marketed while its coercive core was hidden.

Elizabeth, James I, and the Narrowing Choice between Service and Death

The Elizabethan statutes sharpen the statute's coercive logic into a binary: service or death. The 1572 Act decreed that unlicensed beggars above fourteen be severely flogged and branded on the left ear "unless someone will take them into service for two years." A repetition of the offence, if the offender is over eighteen, is punished by execution "unless someone will take them into service for two years." A third offence brings death "without mercy as felons." Parallel statutes — 18 Elizabeth, c. 13, and another of 1597 — follow. The mechanism could not be starker: the outlawed vagabond is offered the mercy of employment, and employment alone redeems him. This is wage-labour legislated into existence as the sole alternative to annihilation.

Under James I the machinery of terror expands again. Any wandering beggar is declared a "rogue and vagabond." Justices of the peace in Petty Sessions may publicly whip and imprison them — six months for the first offence, two years for the second — and "while in prison they are to be whipped as much and as often as the justices of the peace think fit." The vagueness of "as often as the justices think fit" hands the entire measure of cruelty to local magistrates — men drawn, in practice, from the propertied classes whose interests lay in compelling the poor into dependent labour. "Incorrigible and dangerous rogues" are branded with an R on the left shoulder and set to hard labour; a fresh conviction for begging brings execution "without mercy." These statutes remained legally binding until the early eighteenth century, only repealed by 12 Anne, c. 23 — a juridical terror with a century-long half-life.

Thomas More, the Statistics of Hanging, and the Necessary Criminality of the Dispossessed

Perhaps the most rhetorically devastating evidence in the chapter comes in a long footnote quoting Thomas More's Utopia. Marx enlists a sixteenth-century Lord Chancellor against the sixteenth-century state. More describes enclosure as the work of one "insatiable glutton and accursed plague of his native land" who "may join field to field and surround many thousand acres with one fence." Tenants are evicted — "some of them, either circumvented by fraud or overwhelmed by violence, are stripped even of their own property, or else, wearied by unjust acts, are driven to sell." The human procession is enumerated with grave tenderness: "men and women, husbands and wives, orphans and widows, parents with little children and a household not rich but numerous, since farm work requires many hands." Away they must go from their only familiar homes and "find no shelter to go to." Their household goods, sold in a forced rush, fetch a trifle, which is soon spent "in wandering from place to place." Then More delivers the conclusion from which the chapter's whole argument flows: "what remains for them but to steal and be hanged — justly, you may say! — or to wander and beg. And yet even in the latter case they are cast into prison as vagrants for going about idle when, though they most eagerly offer their labour, there is no one to hire them."

This passage performs multiple tasks at once. It corroborates the mass expropriation, it demonstrates that the criminality of the poor was structurally compelled rather than chosen, and it exposes the sham of "voluntary" criminality by showing them eagerly offering labour to no purchaser. More's qualification "justly, you may say!" is a bitter sarcasm Marx clearly relishes.

Marx then adds the statistical ledger of this repression. Citing Holinshed (the Description of England, though the edition is really Harrison's), he notes that "72,000 great and petty thieves were put to death" in the reign of Henry VIII alone. In Elizabeth's time, "rogues were trussed up apace, and there was not one year commonly wherein three or four hundred were not devoured and eaten up by the gallowes" (Strype). In Somersetshire in a single year, 40 persons were executed, 35 robbers burnt in the hand, 37 whipped, and 183 discharged as "incorrigible vagabonds." Strype further opined that even this enormous total comprised "not even a fifth of the actual criminals, thanks to the negligence of the justices and the foolish compassion of the people," and that other counties were no better off, "while some were even worse off." The phrase "foolish compassion" is another ideological jewel: mercy itself was condemned as a failure of discipline.

The Continental Replication: France, the Netherlands, Holland

Marx refuses to let England stand as an idiosyncratic case. The same legislation is shown sweeping Western Europe. In France, by the middle of the seventeenth century a "kingdom of vagabonds" (royaume des truands) had been established in Paris. Even under Louis XVI, the Ordinance of 13 July 1777 decreed that "every man in good health from 16 to 60 years of age, if without means of subsistence and not practising a trade, should be sent to the galleys." This is again the criminalization of structural unemployment — to be capable of work and not to be employed is itself the crime. The Netherlands follows with the Statute of Charles V (October 1537), Holland with the first Edict of the States and Towns (10 March 1614), and the United Provinces with the Plakaat of 26 June 1649. The geographic dispersal proves the thesis: this is not a peculiarity of English law but the general European response to the same underlying economic fact — the forced creation of a mass of dispossessed producers whose absorption by capital was incomplete.

The Function of the Terror: Discipline for Wage-Labour

The chapter now delivers its summarizing judgment. "Thus were the agricultural folk first forcibly expropriated from the soil, driven from their homes, turned into vagabonds, and then whipped, branded and tortured by grotesquely terroristic laws into accepting the discipline necessary for the system of wage-labour." The phrase "grotesquely terroristic" captures Marx's horror — and also his analytical point. The violence was grotesque because it was disproportionate to any reasonable social purpose; it was terroristic because its true function was not punishment but subjection. The word "accepting" is carefully chosen: the expropriated did not freely exchange their labour-power, nor did they even prefer it to their previous condition — they were "disciplined" into it.

The Great Theoretical Pivot: Silent Compulsion versus Historical Genesis

With the historical narrative established, Marx pauses to state, with extraordinary clarity, the theoretical architecture of the whole section. "It is not enough," he writes, "that the conditions of labour are concentrated at one pole of society in the shape of capital, while at the other pole are grouped masses of men who have nothing to sell but their labour-power. Nor is it enough that they are compelled to sell themselves voluntarily." Two conditions of capitalism must be distinguished and both are inadequate on their own: (1) the concentration of the means of production as capital at one pole, and (2) the existence of a propertyless mass that sells labour-power. But Marx adds a third, cultural condition: a working class that "by education, tradition and habit looks upon the requirements of that mode of production as self-evident natural laws."

This is the inner mechanism of mature capitalist rule. "The organization of the capitalist process of production, once it is fully developed, breaks down all resistance." The continuous generation of a "relative surplus population" — the reserve army of labour — "keeps the law of the supply and demand of labour, and therefore wages, within narrow limits which correspond to capital's valorization requirements." This "silent compulsion of economic relations sets the seal on the domination of the capitalist over the worker." Here is Marx's most important theoretical distinction in the chapter: once capitalism is mature, direct extra-economic force is used "only in exceptional cases." In the ordinary run of things, the worker can be left to the "natural laws of production" — it is sufficient to rely on "his dependence on capital, which springs from the conditions of production themselves, and is guaranteed in perpetuity by them."

But this serene "natural" order conceals a violent birth. "It is otherwise during the historical genesis of capitalist production." At the historical moment of transition, the rising bourgeoisie — lacking the mature disciplines of factory and labour market — "needs the power of the state, and uses it to 'regulate' wages, i.e. to force them into the limits suitable for making a profit, to lengthen the working day, and to keep the worker himself at his normal level of dependence." And Marx concludes, with lapidary brevity: "This is an essential aspect of so-called primitive accumulation." Because the "natural laws of production" were not yet sufficient to compel obedience, the state stepped in as the direct instrument of class domination. The "silent" compulsion of the mature economy is therefore revealed as the inheritor of a loud, bloody, and overt compulsion.

The Statute of Labourers, 1349: Wage Regulation from the Beginning

Marx now turns to the second instrument: direct wage regulation. He first establishes the historical baseline of the fourteenth century, when the class of wage-labourers "arose in the latter half of the fourteenth century" — but was only "a very small part of the population," well protected "by the independent peasant proprietors in the countryside and by the organization of guilds in the towns." At that stage, "masters and artisans were not separated by any great social distance either on the land or in the towns." The subordination of labour to capital was "only formal" — the mode of production had not yet acquired specifically capitalist character. Technically, "the variable element in capital preponderated greatly over the constant element" — that is, capital was mostly laid out in the wage-bill rather than in machinery and fixed capital. Because capital accumulated rapidly and the supply of labour-power grew only slowly, "the demand for wage-labour therefore grew rapidly with every accumulation of capital, while the supply only followed slowly behind." And critically, "a large part of the national product which was later transformed into a fund for the accumulation of capital still entered at that time into the consumption-fund of the workers." In other words: at the dawn of wage-labour, workers enjoyed a relatively favourable bargaining position and could consume a larger share of the product.

Precisely because of this, the ruling classes intervened. "Legislation on wage-labour, which aimed from the first at the exploitation of the worker and, as it progressed, remained equally hostile to him, begins in England with the Statute of Labourers issued by Edward III in 1349." The Ordinance of 1350 of King John in France corresponds exactly, "identical in content." Marx notes that the statutes' clauses compelling an extension of the working day have already been treated in Chapter 10, Section 5, and he does not repeat them. The passage of the Statute was "at the urgent insistence of the House of Commons" — the propertied assembly of the kingdom, not the Crown acting alone. A Tory writer is quoted in one of the chapter's most revealing sarcasms: "Formerly the poor demanded such high wages as to threaten industry and wealth. Next, their wages are so low as to threaten industry and wealth equally and perhaps more, but in another way." The point of the quotation is to puncture the perennial complaint about wages: first "too high" was the crisis; later "too low" would be the crisis; the ruling class was, conveniently, threatened by both, so that it could insist on intervention in either direction — but only ever downward. Two epigraphs sharpen the class content: Adam Smith, "Whenever the legislature attempts to regulate the differences between masters and their workmen, its counsellors are always the masters"; and Linguet, "The spirit of the laws is property."

The Mechanics of Wage Regulation: Asymmetry of Punishment, the Maximum and Never the Minimum

Marx then details how the wage statutes actually operated. A tariff of wages was fixed by law "for town and country, for piece-work and day-work." Agricultural labourers were to hire by the year; urban workers "on the open market." It was forbidden, on pain of imprisonment, "to pay higher wages than those fixed by the statute, but the taking of higher wages was more severely punished than the giving of them." Marx adds a parallel example from Elizabeth's Statute of Apprentices (Sections 18 and 19): ten days' imprisonment for the payer, twenty-one for the receiver. This asymmetry is not accidental: the worker, being the weaker party, must be terrorized more thoroughly; the employer, being the stronger, is treated with comparative indulgence. A statute of 1360 increased the penalties and "authorized the masters to extort labour at the legal rate of wages by using corporal punishment." All combinations by masons and carpenters — "contracts, oaths, etc." — were declared null and void. And from the fourteenth century until 1825, "workers' combinations are treated as heinous crimes."

Then comes the decisive sentence: "The spirit of the Statute of Labourers of 1349 and its offshoots shines out clearly in the fact that while the state certainly dictates a maximum of wages, it on no account fixes a minimum." This is the chapter's most condensed statement of the class nature of wage regulation. The state is a class instrument used to cap wages, never to floor them. When the state did propose a floor — Whitbread's minimum-wage bill of 1796 — it was defeated.

The Sixteenth-Century Deterioration and the Persistence of Coercive Regulation

Marx next notes the brutal consequence. In the sixteenth century "the condition of the workers became much worse." The money wage rose, but not in proportion to the depreciation of money and the corresponding rise in commodity prices; "Real wages therefore fell." Yet the laws keeping them down remained in force "together with the ear-clipping and branding of those 'whom no one was willing to take into service'." The savagery of the vagabond statutes and the wage statutes were two prongs of the same policy — the first compelling the expropriated into employment, the second ensuring that employment paid as little as possible.

The Elizabethan Statute of Apprentices (5 Elizabeth, c. 3) gave justices of the peace the power to fix certain wages and modify them according to season and commodity prices. James I extended labour regulation to weavers, spinners, and indeed "all other possible categories of worker." 5 George II extended the anti-combination laws to all manufactures. The footnote on James I adds a blazing example of conflict of interest: a clause from 2 James I, c. 6 shows that "certain clothiers took it upon themselves, in their capacity of justices of the peace, to dictate the official tariff of wages in their own workshops." The employers were also the judges of their employees' wages — a fusion of economic and juridical power that reappears in the chapter's later comments about the 1871 Act.

The German case, drawn from G. Freytag, extends the argument. After the Thirty Years' War, statutes for keeping down wages multiply. Landed proprietors, troubled by labour shortage in depopulated districts, forbade villagers to let rooms to single men and women; these men and women were to be reported to the authorities and thrown into prison "if they were unwilling to become servants, even if they were employed at any other work, such as sowing seeds for the peasants at a daily wage, or even buying and selling corn." For a whole century the decrees of the German princelings "contain bitter and repeated complaints about the wicked and impertinent rabble" unwilling to accept its legally prescribed wage. Individual landowners were forbidden to pay more than the state tariff. Freytag's own observation, which Marx quotes with approval, is a devastating irony: the Silesian farm servants of 1652 had meat twice a week, whereas in the current century there were districts where they had it only three times a year; mid-seventeenth-century wages were higher than in succeeding centuries. So the period of the most explicit and coercive wage regulation was also the period producing the longest-run deterioration of the workers' condition.

The Eighteenth Century: Retreat and Reversal on Wages

Marx tracks the transformation of wage regulation into a dead letter during the age of manufacture properly so called. The capitalist mode of production had become "sufficiently strong to render legal regulation of wages as impracticable as it was unnecessary" — direct regulation was no longer needed because the "silent compulsion" of dependence on capital could do the work. But the ruling classes would not disarm: "they were unwilling to be without the weapons of the old arsenal in case some emergency should arise." So even in the eighteenth century, 7 George I, c. 13 forbade a daily wage higher than 2s. 7½d. for London journeymen tailors (except in cases of general mourning); 13 George III, c. 68 handed the regulation of silk-weavers' wages to the justices of the peace; a 1796 dispute required two judgments of the higher courts to decide whether justice-of-the-peace wage orders held for non-agricultural workers; and in 1799 Parliament confirmed that Scottish mining workers' wages should continue to be regulated by a statute of Elizabeth and by Scottish Acts of 1661 and 1671.

The turning point is dramatized as "a hitherto unheard-of occurrence in the House of Commons." For over 400 years, laws had set a maximum beyond which wages "absolutely must not rise." Then, in 1796, Whitbread proposed a legal minimum wage for agricultural labourers. This is the reversal that reveals the class content of all previous legislation: only now, under pressure, did anyone propose using the state to protect the worker. Pitt opposed the bill — but conceded that "the condition of the poor was cruel." The ruling class admitted the suffering, and still refused the remedy. Finally, in 1813, the wage-regulation laws were repealed. Marx explains why: they had become "an absurd anomaly as soon as the capitalist began to regulate his factory by his own private legislation, and was able to make up the wage of the agricultural labourer to the indispensable minimum by means of the poor-rate." The "poor-rate" — the compulsory parish levy for relief — now served, ironically, as a subsidy that allowed employers to pay below-subsistence wages: the state no longer had to set a maximum because it supplemented the worker's income out of the rates. The transition is from legal wage-setting to the modern apparatus of factory discipline and social subsidy.

Marx then gives one of the sharpest pieces of evidence for the continued class character of contract law: the statutes of labourers' provisions on contracts between master and workman, notice, and the like still allowed "only a civil action against the master who breaks his contract, but permit, on the contrary, a criminal action against the worker who breaks his contract." A footnote records that such inequality remained "in full force" until the Employers and Workmen Act of 1875. So the differential criminalisation of the worker lasted five centuries.

The Combination Laws, 1871, and the Permanent Trade Union of Capital

The chapter reaches the modern era. The "barbarous laws against combinations of workers collapsed in 1825 in the face of the threatening attitude of the proletariat." But they "disappeared only in part"; certain "pretty survivals" lasted until 1859. Then, in 1871, the Trade Union Act (29 June) "purported to remove the last traces of this class legislation by giving legal recognition to trade unions." Marx immediately exposes the trick: on the same date, a companion Act — the Criminal Law Amendment Act, "an act to amend the criminal law relating to violence, threats and molestation" — "in fact re-established the previous situation in a new form." Marx names it with precision: "This Parliamentary conjuring-trick withdrew the means the workers could use in a strike or lock-out from the common law and placed them under exceptional penal legislation, the interpretation of which fell to the manufacturers themselves in their capacity of justices of the peace." Again the fusion of economic and juridical power: employers sat as the very magistrates who would interpret the Act against strikers.

Marx then broadens the indictment from the Act to the party. Two years earlier, "the same House of Commons, and the same Mr Gladstone ... had brought in a bill for the removal of all exceptional penal legislation against the working class." But it never went beyond the second reading; the matter was "drawn out" until the "great Liberal party," in alliance with the Tories, "found the courage to turn decisively against the very proletariat that had carried it into power." Worse, the Liberal party allowed the English judges — "ever ready to wag their tails for the ruling classes" — to "exhume the earlier laws against 'conspiracy' and apply them to combinations of workers." Marx draws the conclusion: "It is evident that only against its will, and under the pressure of the masses, did the English Parliament give up the laws against strikes and trade unions, after it had itself, with shameless egoism, held the position of a permanent trade union of the capitalists against the workers throughout five centuries." The phrase "permanent trade union of the capitalists" is one of Marx's most piercing characterizations of the capitalist state: the state itself is the disciplined, organized combination of the employing class against the employed.

France, Le Chapelier, and the Bourgeois Libertarian Illusion

The chapter closes with the French parallel and one of its finest examples of ideological self-contradiction. During the very first storms of the revolution — in 1791 — "the French bourgeoisie dared to take away from the workers the right of association they had just acquired." The decree of 14 June 1791 declared every combination of workers "an assault on liberty and the declaration of the rights of man," punishable by a fine of 500 livres and one year's deprivation of the rights of an active citizen. Marx emphasizes the law's longevity: "This law ... has outlived revolutions and changes of dynasties. Even the Terror left it untouched. It was only struck out of the Penal Code quite recently."

Then Marx quotes Le Chapelier, the rapporteur of the committee, at length — not to refute him, but to let his absurdity speak. Le Chapelier grants "that wages ought to be a little higher than they are ... that they ought to be high enough for him that receives them to be free from that state of absolute dependence which results from the lack of the necessaries of life, and which is almost a state of slavery." Granting all this, workers may nonetheless not be permitted "to inform themselves about their own interests, nor to act in common and thereby lessen their 'absolute dependence', 'which is almost a state of slavery', because by doing this they infringe 'the liberty of their former masters, who are the present entrepreneurs', and because a combination against the despotism of the former masters of the corporations is — guess what! — a restoration of the corporations abolished by the French constitution!" The crescendo of irony — "guess what!" — marks one of the chapter's most effective rhetorical blows. The revolutionary bourgeoisie abolished the old guilds in the name of liberty, then invoked the memory of those very guilds to forbid workers from organizing — while admitting that the workers' condition was "almost a state of slavery." Marx is showing the deep structure of bourgeois liberty: the freedom to combine belongs exclusively to capital.

The footnotes drive the point home. Article I of the Le Chapelier law states that "the abolition of any form of association between citizens of the same estate and profession is one of the foundations of the French constitution," so that all such associations are forbidden "under any pretext and in any form." Article IV declares that workers' joint deliberations to refuse work or insist on a particular price "shall be declared unconstitutional, derogatory to liberty and the declaration of the rights of man, etc." — and treats such conduct as a felony, "just as in the old statutes of labourers." The continuities are thus complete: the French revolutionary decree, ostensibly a repudiation of the ancien régime, reproduces the logic of the Statute of Labourers almost line for line. Reaction and revolution alike legislate against the workers' combination.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter reveals several fundaments. First and most important: capitalism was not born liberal. The mature wage relation, governed by the "silent compulsion of economic relations," is a historical achievement built on direct state terror. The "free" worker is, in the double sense Marx insists upon, "vogelfrei" — free but rightless, unprotected, disposable. Second, the state is disclosed not as a neutral arbiter but as the "permanent trade union of the capitalists," an instrument that set wage maxima but never minima, that criminalized the worker's combination while permitting the master's, that punished breaches of contract asymmetrically, and that, in the 1871 Acts and the Le Chapelier decree, recreated the old coercion in new forms. Third, the criminalization of structural outcomes — vagabondage, unemployment, poverty — as "voluntary" moral failure is a persistent ideological technique, running from the 1530 statute declaring the "sturdy vagabond" to be a "voluntary criminal," through the Elizabethan branding of unlicensed beggars, to the modern deployment of conspiracy law against strikers.

Fourth, and most subtle, is the chapter's theoretical method: the distinction between ordinary and genetic compulsion. Mature capitalism's equality between buyer and seller of labour-power is real in form and mystificatory in content; but its historical origin lies in the naked use of state violence to create a propertyless class with no choice but to sell. When the system's ideologists treat wage-labour as a "natural" phenomenon, they erase this genealogy.
