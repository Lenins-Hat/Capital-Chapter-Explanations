Volume 1 • Chapter 24: The Transformation of Surplus-Value into Capital

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 24 of Marx's Capital, Volume 1, constitutes one of the most philosophically explosive and dialectically intricate chapters in the entire work. Its title — "The Transformation of Surplus-Value into Capital" — announces a problem that is at once technical, historical, and juridical: not merely how a portion of the surplus product is reinvested, but how the very legal architecture of bourgeois society — the property rights allegedly grounded in commodity exchange — is, through its own inner logic, inverted into a system of class appropriation that is its structural opposite. The chapter's central thesis is that accumulation of capital is not an external addition to capitalist production but the very mechanism through which capitalist property relations reproduce and expand themselves while continuing to invoke the juridical forms of equality and equivalence. Where the preceding chapters established how surplus-value arises from capital, this chapter reverses the lens: how does capital arise from surplus-value?

The intellectual objective is threefold. First, to demonstrate, at the level of the individual capitalist and then of the class as a whole, the material and value mechanics of accumulation — how a £10,000 spinning enterprise generates £2,000 of surplus-value, which, when capitalized, produces £400 more, and so on in the "spiral" that Sismondi named. Second, to expose and demolish a set of apologetic doctrines — Adam Smith's reduction of accumulation to the consumption of surplus product by productive workers, Senior's theology of "abstinence," Bentham's dogma of a fixed "labour fund" — that obscure the exploitative content of accumulation behind the language of saving, self-denial, and natural limits. Third, and most crucially, to demonstrate what Marx calls a "dialectical inversion": the property laws of commodity production, which began as the right of the producer to own his own product, transform — through the immanent, inexorable working out of their own principles — into the laws of capitalist appropriation, which is the right of the non-producer to own the product of others' labour.

This is the beating theoretical heart of Volume 1's final section. The earlier parts of the book anatomized the origin of surplus-value (the purchase of a peculiar commodity, labour-power, whose use-value exceeds its exchange-value). Chapter 24 performs the complementary movement: it shows that this origin-relation, repeated and capitalized, is self-reproducing and self-expanding. Capital is not a thing but a social relation that generates the conditions of its own perpetuation on an ever-larger scale. The mysterious "original accumulation" that political economy assumed as a primal fact — the capitalist's putative "own labour and that of his forefathers" — is here exposed as a vanishing quantity, a magnitudo evanescens, smothered by the accumulated mass of unpaid labour that constitutes capital's true substance.

The chapter also functions as a hinge into the broader architecture of Capital. It anticipates the analysis of the circulation of social capital in Volume 2 (which Marx promises to provide in Part Three of that volume) and the analysis of the trinity formula in Volume 3. It sets the stage for the historical account of primitive accumulation (Chapter 26 onwards in the Penguin edition and Chapters 26–33 in older editions), by demonstrating that the "so-called primitive accumulation" is not — as political economy would have it — the pre-history of capitalist accumulation but rather the historical precondition that allowed the system of self-expanding appropriation to get underway. The chapter thus sits between the micro-logic of surplus-value production and the macro-logic of capitalist totality: it is the bridge from the exchange relation taken in isolation to the class relation taken as a whole.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The inaugural movement: capital arising from surplus-value and the spinning example

Marx opens with a compact statement of the problem's inversion: "Earlier we considered how surplus-value arises from capital; now we have to see how capital arises from surplus-value." The technical term for this — the "employment of surplus-value as capital, or its reconversion into capital" — is accumulation of capital. Marx immediately marks his intellectual debts and their limits by quoting Malthus ("Accumulation of capital; the employment of a portion of revenue as capital") and the second edition of Malthus's Principles of Political Economy ("Conversion of revenue into capital"). The turn to Malthus is no idle courtesy: Malthus, a political economist of the Ricardian generation, had hit upon the vocabulary that would become the apologetic shell of the concept, while missing its class content entirely.

To ground the analysis materially, Marx introduces his running example: a master-spinner with an advanced capital of £10,000, four-fifths (£8,000) in constant capital — cotton, machinery, etc. — and one-fifth (£2,000) in wages. Assume a rate of surplus-value of 100 per cent. The spinners produce 240,000 lbs. of yarn with a total value of £12,000. The surplus-value of £2,000 is "contained in the surplus, or net product, of 40,000 lbs. of yarn" — one-sixth of the gross product. Marx adds a philosophically pointed observation: "£2,000 is £2,000. Neither seeing nor smelling will tell us that this sum of money is surplus-value." Money is indifferent to its origin; value, once it takes the money form, erases the historical trace of exploitation. This is a motif running through the entire Marxian corpus: the scandal is not that theft is visible but that it is invisible, that the exchange form homogenizes unpaid labour with paid, surplus with the original capital.

Accumulation proceeds: the £2,000 is now laid out in the same proportions as the original capital — £1,600 for constant capital and £400 for additional spinning workers. The new capital of £2,000 "then functions in the spinning-mill and in its turn brings in a surplus value of £400." The process scales: "It is the old story: Abraham begat Isaac, Isaac begat Jacob and so on." Original capital produces surplus-value; that surplus-value becomes additional capital, which produces its own surplus-value; the second £400 becomes additional capital producing £80, and so on in geometric descent.

Marx deliberately brackets several complications: the portion of surplus-value consumed by the capitalist (analyzed later); whether the additional capital is attached to the original or separated from it; whether the same capitalist employs the additional capital or hands it to others. The point is structural: "by the side of the newly formed capital, the original capital continues to reproduce itself and to produce surplus-value, and this is true of all accumulated capital in relation to the additional capital engendered by it." The mass of capital shares a common quality — every increment traces back to unpaid labour, but each increment also continues, in its turn, to extort further unpaid labour.

The interrogation of "original capital" and the dialectic of appropriation

Now Marx inserts his famous juridical interrogation. The original capital of £10,000 — where did its owner get it from? "From his own labour and that of his forefathers," say "the spokesmen of political economy." Marx concedes that this assumption "appears to be the only one consonant with the laws of commodity production" — a crucial concession that sets up the dialectical reversal. "But it is quite otherwise with regard to the additional capital of £2,000." Its origin is transparent: "There is not one single atom of its value that does not owe its existence to unpaid labour." The means of production and the subsistence of workers, which the £2,000 purchases, are nothing but "parts of the tribute annually exacted from the working class by the capitalist class."

Marx then deploys one of the chapter's most memorable images: the conqueror who buys commodities from the conquered with money stolen from them. The exchange of equivalent for equivalent at the point of purchase of labour-power is perfectly consistent with — indeed is the medium of — a systematic appropriation without equivalent. Even if the additional capital employs the producer of that very capital, the worker "must buy back the fruits of his previous labour with more labour than they cost." Property relations have become a cycle of expropriation masquerading as exchange.

The "spiral" of reproduction and the dialectical inversion

Sismondi's phrase "the cycle of simple reproduction alters its form and ... changes into a spiral" is invoked to capture the qualitative difference between mere repetition and accumulation. Marx then spells out the cumulative logic:

  • The first additional £2,000 presupposes a value of £10,000 "advanced by the capitalist, and belonging to him by virtue of his 'original labour.'"
  • The second additional £400 presupposes only the prior accumulation of the £2,000 "of which the £400 is the capitalized surplus-value."
  • Therefore "the ownership of past unpaid labour is thenceforth the sole condition for the appropriation of living unpaid labour on a constantly increasing scale."
  • And: "The more the capitalist has accumulated, the more is he able to accumulate."

This is the positive feedback loop — the "dialectical inversion" — that Marx will now name explicitly. Every individual transaction conforms to the laws of commodity exchange: the capitalist buys labour-power, the worker sells it "at what we shall assume is its real value"; equivalents are exchanged. And yet "the laws of appropriation or of private property, laws based on the production and circulation of commodities, become changed into their direct opposite through their own internal and inexorable dialectic."

Marx analyzes the exchange relation between capitalist and worker in three moments of inversion:

  1. The form is exchange, the content is appropriation. The social relation appears as a succession of free exchanges, but what it accomplishes is "the constant appropriation by the capitalist, without equivalent, of a portion of the labour of others which has already been objectified, and his repeated exchange of this labour for a greater quantity of the living labour of others." The form mystifies the content; it is "a mere semblance."
  2. The laws of property invert. Originally, "the rights of property seemed to us to be grounded in a man's own labour." Now, "property turns out to be the right, on the part of the capitalist, to appropriate the unpaid labour of others or its product, and the impossibility, on the part of the worker, of appropriating his own product." The identity of property and labour gives way to their separation as "the necessary consequence of a law that apparently originated in their identity."
  3. The inversion arises from the application, not the violation, of commodity laws. This is the most important and most subtle point: "however much the capitalist mode of appropriation may seem to fly in the face of the original laws of commodity production, it nevertheless arises, not from a violation of these laws but, on the contrary, from their application." There is no fraud, no cheating of the worker, no paying below value. The dialectic is immanent. This is a direct refutation of any theory of exploitation grounded in unequal exchange, cheating, or monopoly pricing — a refutation that separates Marx from the "utopian socialism" of Proudhon and others.

Marx then walks through the "consecutive phases of motion" of this dialectic, in the passage added by Engels to the fourth German edition (based on the French edition of 1872). The original transformation of values into capital proceeded "in complete accordance with the laws of exchange": the worker sold his labour-power, the capitalist bought it; the worker received the value of his commodity and alienated its use-value. The resulting product contains (a) the value of the means of production consumed, (b) the equivalent of wages, and (c) a surplus-value. But this surplus-value arises not because the seller was defrauded but because the buyer used the commodity — a purchase of labour-power is the purchase of a use-value capable of producing value, and its consumption is legitimate. From this, Marx draws three juridical corollaries:

  • (1) The product belongs to the capitalist, not the worker.
  • (2) The value of the product includes a surplus-value "which costs the worker labour but the capitalist nothing, and which none the less becomes the legitimate property of the capitalist."
  • (3) The worker has retained his labour-power and may sell it again.

The laws are not broken; they are fulfilled to their logical conclusion — and that conclusion is the transformation of private property based on labour into private property based on appropriation of others' labour.

Simple reproduction and the perpetual legitimization of appropriation

Simple reproduction, Marx continues, is "only the periodic repetition of this first operation; each time, money is freshly transformed into capital." The law "gains the opportunity to operate continuously." Sismondi's formulation — "Several successive acts of exchange have only made the last represent the first" — captures the temporal disguise: each successive sale obscures the accumulated history behind it.

Sismondi's distinction between workers who "acquire each year a fresh right to their share by fresh labour" and capitalists who "have already acquired, by their original labour, a permanent right to their share" is quoted, and Marx adds his mocking observation: "It is indeed a well-known fact that the sphere of labour is not the only one in which primogeniture works miracles." The point — cutting to the bone — is that the capitalist's apparent "permanent right" to a share of the annual product is nothing but the cumulative sediment of past appropriations, now trumped up as inheritance.

Nor does the argument change under expanded reproduction. Whether the capitalist squanders all surplus-value in dissipation (simple reproduction) or consumes only part and converts the rest to money (accumulation), the surplus-value "is his property; it has never belonged to anyone else." This is the surface-level truth — under the rules of commodity exchange, the capitalist does own the surplus-value, just as he owns the capital advanced. If worker B is paid from the surplus-value produced by worker A, that is no concern of B's; what B has a right to claim is that the capitalist pay him the value of his labour-power. Marx acidifies this logic by re-writing a passage from Sismondi: "Both of them still benefited: the worker because he was advanced the fruits of his labour (should read: of the unpaid labour of other workers) before the work was done (should read: before his own labour had borne fruit); the employer, because the labour of this worker was worth more than his wages (should read: produced more value than the value of his wages)."

The isolation of transactions and the inadmissibility of class categories

A crucial methodological passage follows. If we shift perspective from individual transactions to "capitalist production in the uninterrupted flow of its renewal" — from individual capitalist and worker to capitalist class and working class — "the matter looks quite different." But "in so doing we should be applying standards entirely foreign to commodity production." Because commodity production knows only "mutually independent buyer and seller"; relations between them cease when the contract expires. Each transaction is discrete; a repeated transaction is "the result of a new agreement which has nothing to do with the previous one."

Marx's dialectical genius lies in his refusal to simply refute the bourgeois standpoint. He grants it completely on its own ground: "If, therefore, commodity production, or one of its associated processes, is to be judged according to its own economic laws, we must consider each act of exchange by itself, apart from any connection with the act of exchange preceding it and that following it." And, "since sales and purchases are negotiated solely between particular individuals, it is not admissible to look here for relations between whole social classes." In other words: taken in isolation, every transaction is just; the injustice is systemic, arising from the repetition and the totality that commodity exchange cannot see.

The dialectical punchline: "However long a series of periodic reproductions and preceding accumulations the capital functioning today may have passed through, it always preserves its original virginity." As long as the laws of exchange are observed in each isolated act, "the mode of appropriation can be completely revolutionized without in any way affecting the property rights which correspond to commodity production." This is why the same legal rights persist across epochs of wildly different appropriation — from the artisan who enriched himself only through his own labour to the modern capitalist who appropriates unpaid labour "over and over again."

The generalization follows: "This result becomes inevitable from the moment there is a free sale, by the worker himself, of labour-power as a commodity." And this generalization — wage-labour as the basis of society-wide commodity production — is the moment when commodity production "unfolds all its hidden potentialities." Marx's retort to those who say wage-labour "adulterates" commodity production: "To say that the intervention of wage-labour adulterates commodity production is to say that commodity production must not develop if it is to remain unadulterated." Thus, "to the extent that commodity production, in accordance with its own immanent laws, undergoes a further development into capitalist production, the property laws of commodity production must undergo a dialectical inversion so that they become laws of capitalist appropriation."

A footnote added by Engels (based on the French edition) targeting Proudhon is worth flagging: "We may well, therefore, feel astonished at the cleverness of Proudhon, who would abolish capitalist property — by enforcing the eternal laws of property which are themselves based on commodity production!" This is one of Marx's most devastating critiques of petty-bourgeois socialism: the project of restoring "property" against "capitalist property" is incoherent because property in the Marxian sense is already, immanently, capitalist property in embryo.

Vanishing original capital and the "accumulated wealth" of political economy

The section closes with the magnitudo evanescens argument. In simple reproduction, all capital — whatever its origin — becomes "accumulated capital, or capitalized surplus-value." In the "flood of production," the original capital advanced becomes "a vanishing quantity (magnitudo evanescens in the mathematical sense)" compared to the directly accumulated capital. Whether it functions in the hands of the original accumulator or of others is immaterial. Hence political economy's intuition (Malthus: "Capital, viz., accumulated wealth employed with a view to profit"; R. Jones: "Capital... consists of wealth saved from revenue, and used with a view to profit") is correct as description, but political economy fails to see what class relation is being described. The formula "all existing capital is accumulated or capitalized interest" (from an 1851 issue of The Economist) is technically correct, since interest is a fragment of surplus-value — but it conceals the origin in appropriation.

Transition: political economy's erroneous conception of reproduction on an increasing scale

Section 2 clears away a semantic confusion in classical political economy. The confusion concerns the phrase "capital is consumed by productive workers."

Marx grants that the consumption of surplus product by productive rather than unproductive workers is characteristic of accumulation — the classical economists are right about that much. But they draw the wrong conclusion. Adam Smith made it "the fashion to present accumulation as nothing more than the consumption of the surplus product by productive workers," which amounts to saying "the capitalization of surplus-value consists merely in turning surplus-value into labour-power." Ricardo follows Smith in this: "'When we say that revenue is saved, and added to capital, what we mean is, that the portion of revenue, so said to be added to capital, is consumed by productive instead of unproductive labourers. There can be no greater error than in supposing that capital is increased by non-consumption.'"

Marx objects: "There can be no greater error than the one repeated after Adam Smith by Ricardo and all subsequent political economists." In fact, surplus-value — like any value advanced — divides into constant and variable capital, into means of production and labour-power. To say that the whole surplus is consumed by productive workers is to identify accumulation with the hiring of more workers, ignoring the fact that a substantial part of the additional capital must be laid out as constant capital (machinery, raw materials). Adam Smith's error culminates in "the absurd conclusion" that while each individual capital divides into a constant and variable part, "the capital of society can be entirely resolved into variable capital." The absurdity: Smith argues that a cloth manufacturer's £2,000 flows to weavers, but also to wool and machinery sellers, who in turn pay their own workers, "and so on until the whole £2,000 is spent in the payment of wages." Marx's cutting rejoinder: "It is evident that the entire thrust of this argument lies in the words 'and so on,' which send us from pillar to post. In fact, Adam Smith breaks off the investigation just where the difficulties begin."

Marx then explains the material reality. "The annual process of reproduction is easily understood, as long as we look solely at the sum total of the year's production. But every single component of this annual product must be brought into the market as a commodity, and there the difficulties begin." The movements of individual capitals and personal revenues "cross and intermingle" in a general circulation that confuses the observer — and Marx promises a full analysis in Part Three of Volume 2 (the reproduction schemas). He credits the Physiocrats with the Tableau Économique, the first attempt "to depict the year's production in the shape in which it emerges from circulation."

Marx notes acidly that "political economy has not failed to exploit, in the interests of the capitalist class, Adam Smith's doctrine that the whole of that part of the net product which is transformed into capital is consumed by the working class." The apologetic payoff of Smith's error: if the entire surplus reinvested is consumed by workers, then accumulation is a boon to labour, and there is nothing extractive about it. The Smithian error is functional to class apologetics.

The footnote on John Stuart Mill — "One of the great merits..." [actually Marx's criticism] — targets Mill's inability to spot the error even when "it cries out for rectification from a purely technical standpoint, entirely within the bourgeois field of vision." Mill reproduces Smith: "The capital itself in the long run becomes entirely wages, and when replaced by the sale of produce becomes wages again." Marx notes Mill "registers the confusion of his masters with the dogmatism of a disciple."

A further footnote notes the related error — that the price of commodities resolves into wages, profit and rent — and quotes Storch's confession: "It is impossible to resolve the necessary price into its simplest elements." Marx finds this confession "a fine kind of economic science." The point: Smith's confusion between fixed/circulating capital and constant/variable capital (which Marx claims to be the first to have properly distinguished — see footnote 55) leads to the "dogma that the price of commodities is made up of wages, profit (interest) and ground rent."

Section 3: Division of surplus-value into capital and revenue — the abstinence theory

Having established that surplus-value is both a fund for consumption and a fund for accumulation, Marx turns to the division between them. "With a given mass of surplus-value, then, the larger the one part, the smaller the other." The ratio determines the magnitude of accumulation. And "it is the owner of the surplus-value, the capitalist, who makes this division. It is an act of his will." Marx notes the double meaning of the English/French term "revenue" — sometimes meaning surplus-value as a whole, sometimes only the portion consumed — and retains it for consistency.

He then gives the celebrated description of the capitalist as "capital personified":

"Except as capital personified, the capitalist has no historical value, and no right to that historical existence which, to use Lichnowsky's amusing expression, 'ain't got no date.' It is only to this extent that the necessity of the capitalist's own transitory existence is implied in the transitory necessity of the capitalist mode of production."

The German prince Lichnowsky's 1848 Frankfurt Assembly outburst against Polish independence — "keinen Datum nicht hat" — is repurposed as a witty jab at the historical groundlessness of capitalist existence. The capitalist, as a private person, has no raison d'être; only as capital incarnate does he have one.

Marx then articulates the historical mission of the capitalist:

"in so far as he is capital personified, his motivating force is not the acquisition and enjoyment of use-values, but the acquisition and augmentation of exchange-values. He is fanatically intent on the valorization of value; consequently he ruthlessly forces the human race to produce for production's sake. In this way he spurs on the development of society's productive forces, and the creation of those material conditions of production which alone can form the real basis of a higher form of society, a society in which the full and free development of every individual forms the ruling principle."

This is a critical passage for understanding Marx's dialectical evaluation of capitalism: the capitalist is not merely a villain; he is a historically necessary instrument for the development of the productive forces that will eventually enable a higher social form. "Only as a personification of capital is the capitalist respectable." Marx compares him to the miser: what in the miser is individual mania, in the capitalist is "the effect of a social mechanism in which he is merely a cog." The capitalist is compelled by competition — "external and coercive laws" — to accumulate: "It compels him to keep extending his capital, so as to preserve it, and he can only extend it by means of progressive accumulation."

The ascetic injunctions follow in Marx's characteristic satirical mode: "his own private consumption counts as a robbery committed against the accumulation of his capital, just as, in double-entry book-keeping, the private expenditure of the capitalist is placed on the debit side of his account against his capital." Accumulation is "the conquest of the world of social wealth... the extension of the area of exploited human material and, at the same time, the extension of the direct and indirect sway of the capitalist." A footnote quotes Luther's magnificent invective against usurers — "the heathen were able, by the light of reason, to conclude that a usurer is a double-dyed thief and murderer" — reminding us that moral condemnation of finance long predates Marx's political economy.

The Faustian conflict and Dr Aikin's Manchester

Now Marx introduces a historical dimension: "With the development of the capitalist mode of production, with the growth of accumulation and wealth, the capitalist ceases to be merely the incarnation of capital. He begins to feel a human warmth towards his own Adam." The "classical" capitalist brandished consumption as "abstinence" from accumulating; the "modernized" capitalist can entertain accumulation as "renunciation" of pleasure. Marx quotes Goethe's Faust — "Two souls, alas, do dwell within his breast; The one is ever parting from the other" — to capture the Faustian conflict between the passion for accumulation and the desire for enjoyment. (This is the chapter's central image for the capitalist psychology: internalized asceticism versus the reality of growing luxury.)

Dr Aikin's Description of the Country from Thirty to Forty Miles round Manchester (1795) is then invoked to trace four historical phases of the Manchester trade:

  1. First period: manufacturers worked hard for a livelihood; enrichment came chiefly through robbing parents who bound their children as apprentices to them — high premiums, starved apprentices — while the manufacturers themselves "lived like misers" and "were far from consuming even the interest on their capital."
  2. Second period: they acquired little fortunes but worked as hard and lived as plainly as before.
  3. Third period: "luxury began"; the trade was pushed by riders sent out for orders into every market town. Before 1690, "few or no capitals of £3,000 to £4,000 acquired by trade existed here"; but around then, "traders... began to build modem brick houses, instead of those of wood and plaster." Even in the early eighteenth century, a Manchester manufacturer who placed a pint of foreign wine before his guests exposed himself to neighbors' headshakings. It was not until 1758 that a person actually engaged in business was seen with his own carriage.
  4. Fourth period (last 30 years of the eighteenth century): "expense and luxury have made great progress, supported by a trade extended by means of riders and factors through every part of Europe."

Marx adds: "What would the good Dr Aikin say if he could rise from the grave and see the Manchester of today'?"

The "accumulate, accumulate!" imperative and Malthus's compromise

The celebrated slogan: "Accumulate, accumulate! That is Moses and the prophets!... Therefore save, save, i.e. reconvert the greatest possible portion of surplus-value or surplus product into capital! Accumulation for the sake of accumulation, production for the sake of production: this was the formula in which classical economics expressed the historical mission of the bourgeoisie in the period of its domination." Classical economics did not deceive itself over "the nature of wealth's birth-pangs" — but this honesty is cold comfort: "If, in the eyes of classical economics, the proletarian is merely a machine for the production of surplus-value, the capitalist too is merely a machine for the transformation of this surplus-value into surplus capital."

Marx quotes J.B. Say — "The savings of the rich are made at the expense of the poor" — and Sismondi's more radical variant: "The Roman proletarian lived almost entirely at the expense of society... One might almost say that modern society lives at the expense of the proletarians, on the portion of the wages of labour which it withdraws from their pockets." The Sismondi passage reveals that even within bourgeois political economy, the class-exploitation content was recognized; the question was what to do about it.

Malthus, "around the beginning of the 1820s," proposed a division of labor: the actual production-capitalist accumulates, while the landed aristocracy, place-men, and beneficed clergy do the spending. The rationale, in Malthus's own words: "It is of the highest importance... to keep separate the passion for expenditure and the passion for accumulation." The proposal triggered a protest from Ricardian spokesmen: "What, exclaimed one of their spokesmen, does Mr Malthus preach high rents, heavy taxation, etc. so that the industrious may constantly be kept up to the mark by the pressure of unproductive consumers?" But — as Marx notes — this same Ricardian, hostile to Malthus's high-rent proposal, was perfectly prepared to keep wages low. "'Increased demand on the part of the labourers means nothing more than their disposition to take less of their own product for themselves, and leave a greater part of it to their employers; and if it be said, that this begets glut, by lessening consumption (on the part of the workers) I can only reply that glut is synonymous with large profits.'"

The intra-class dispute — "how the booty pumped out of the workers may most advantageously be divided for the purposes of accumulation" — had to fall silent, Marx observes, before the class struggle: the July Revolution (1830), the Lyon uprising of the silk-workers, English rural arson (the Swing Riots), Owenism in England, Saint-Simonism and Fourierism in France. "The hour of vulgar economics had arrived."

Senior's abstinence theory and its demolition

Enter Nassau W. Senior — just a year before he discovered at Manchester that profit is "the product of the unpaid 'last hour of the twelve hours of labour'" (the target of Marx's "Senior's Last Hour" in Chapter 7 of this volume) — announced another discovery: "I substitute," he proudly says, "for the word capital, considered as an instrument of production, the word abstinence." Marx's verdict: "An unparalleled example of the 'discoveries' of vulgar economics! It replaces an economic category with a sycophantic phrase, and that is all."

Senior's argument: "When the savage makes bows, he exercises an industry, but he does not practise abstinence." Hence in earlier states of society the instruments of labour were constructed "without the abstinence of the capitalist." And: "The more society progresses, the more abstinence is demanded" — namely, "from those whose business it is to appropriate the industry and the products of others."

Marx enumerates the reductio ad absurdum: if the corn is not all eaten but in part sown, "abstinence of the capitalist"; if the wine gets time to mature, "abstinence of the capitalist"; the capitalist "robs himself whenever he 'lends (!) the instruments of production to the worker.'" This last move — the interpolation of "(!!)" — mocks the identification of lending to a worker with victimization of the lender. Even the conservation of a capital requires "a constant effort to resist the temptation of consuming it," Courcelle-Seneuil claims. The reductio: "The simple dictates of humanity therefore plainly enjoin the release of the capitalist from his martyrdom and his temptation, in the same way as the slave-owners of Georgia, U.S.A., have recently been delivered by the abolition of slavery from the painful dilemma over whether they should squander the surplus product extracted by means of the whip from their Negro slaves entirely in champagne, or whether they should reconvert a part of it into more Negroes and more land."

A footnote extends the mockery: "It has never occurred to the vulgar economist to make the simple reflection that every human action may be conceived as an 'abstinence' from its opposite. Eating is abstinence from fasting, walking is abstinence from standing still, working is abstinence from idling, idling is abstinence from working, etc." Spinoza's "Determinatio est negatio" is invoked — and the reduction absorbs every determination into an infinite regress of abstinences, rendering the concept worthless. Another footnote quotes Scrope on sow-wheat-abstinence and de Molinari's ludicrous characterization of the capitalist as lending "his instruments of production to the worker, instead of devoting their value to his own consumption."

The famous footnote on John Cazenove is also present: Cazenove objected that "It is not the capital, but the use of the capital productively, which is the cause of profits." Marx adds that "John Stuart Mill, on the contrary, both copies Ricardo's theory of profit, and annexes to it Senior's 'remuneration of abstinence.' He is as much at home with absurd and flat contradictions as he is at sea with the Hegelian 'contradiction,' which is the source of all dialectics."

Richard Jones and the universal reality of expanded reproduction without the abstaining capitalist

Marx now makes the historically crucial point that expanded reproduction — production on a scale that expands — is not unique to capitalism; it occurs "in economic formations of society of the most diverse kinds." What is unique to capitalism is that this expanded reproduction appears as the function of a capitalist. The distinction: in non-capitalist forms, the worker's means of production, product, and means of subsistence do not confront him as capital. Richard Jones, former occupant of the East India College Haileybury chair (successor of Malthus), provides the empirical study: the Indian peasantry cultivating their own land never see their product take "the shape of a fund saved from revenue, which fund has, therefore, gone through a previous process of accumulation." In provinces least disturbed by English rule, non-agricultural workers are directly employed by magnates to whom tribute or rent is rendered; some of this is consumed in kind, some converted into luxuries, and the remainder forms the wage of workers who own their own implements. "Here, production and reproduction on an increasing scale go on their way without any intervention from that peculiar saint, that knight of the woeful countenance, the 'abstaining' capitalist." The allusion is to Quixote — the "knight of the woeful countenance" — a fitting image for the sanctimonious investor celebrated by political economy. A footnote from Jones: "Profits... unimportant source of accumulation, compared with wages and rents, in the earlier stages of society... When a considerable advance in the powers of national industry has actually taken place, profits rise into comparative importance as a source of accumulation."

Section 4: Circumstances determining the extent of accumulation

Assuming the ratio of capitalization to consumption is given, the absolute magnitude of accumulation depends on the absolute magnitude of surplus-value. Marx enumerates several factors.

First, the degree of exploitation of labour-power. Political economy confuses the acceleration of accumulation due to increased productivity with that due to increased exploitation. Marx notes that the previous chapters assumed wages equal to the value of labour-power, but the "forcible reduction of the wage of labour beneath its value plays too important a role in the practical movement of affairs for us not to stay with this phenomenon for a moment. In fact, it transforms the worker's necessary fund for consumption, within certain limits, into a fund for the accumulation of capital."

Marx quotes John Stuart Mill: "Wages have no productive power; they are the price of a productive power... If labour could be had without purchase, wages might be dispensed with." Marx's reply: "But if the workers could live on air, it would not be possible to buy them at any price. This zero cost of labour is therefore a limit in a mathematical sense, always beyond reach, although we can always approximate more and more nearly to it. The constant tendency of capital is to force the cost of labour back towards this absolute zero."

The historical evidence: the anonymous author of the Essay on Trade and Commerce (1770) declares England's historical mission to be to force down English wages to the French and Dutch level, complaining: "But if our poor... will live luxuriously... then labour must, of course, be dear." The Northamptonshire manufacturer laments that "Labour is one-third cheaper in France than in England; for their poor work hard, and fare hard." Marx notes the "pious fraud" of the manufacturer, who compares English manufacturing workers to French agricultural labourers, as he himself later confesses "in his confused way." Engels adds a footnote from the third German edition: "Today, thanks to the competition on the world market which has grown up since then, we have advanced much further. 'If China,' says Mr Stapleton, M.P., to his constituents, 'should become a great manufacturing country, I do not see how the manufacturing population of Europe could sustain the contest without descending to the level of their competitors.'... The desired goal of English capital is no longer Continental wages, oh no, it is Chinese wages!"

The Benjamin Thompson (Count Rumford) episode follows: his Essays are "a cookery book with recipes of all kinds for replacing the ordinary, but expensive food of the worker with various surrogates." Marx quotes the "particularly successful recipe": "5 lb. of barley-meal, 7½d.; 5 lb. of Indian corn, 6½d.; 3d. worth of red herring, 1d. salt, 1d. vinegar, 2d. pepper and sweet herbs, in all 20¾d.; make a soup for 64 men, and at the medium price of barley and of Indian corn... this soup may be provided at ¼d. the portion of 20 ounces." The biographical footnote on Rumford (Massachusetts-born, fought for England, Bavarian service, created Count in 1790) invites the reader to recognize the obscene recycling of a Yankee in the service of industrial profit. Marx adds that "with the advance of capitalist production, the adulteration of food has rendered Thompson's ideal superfluous" — and the footnote documents thirty-one of thirty-four specimens of opium from London chemists adulterated with poppy heads, wheat-flour, gum, clay, sand, some containing no morphine at all.

The Speenhamland system of 1795 — parish relief to top up wages — is documented. Burke's quip: "The squires of Norfolk had dined... when they fixed the rate of wages; the squires of Berks evidently thought the labourers ought not to do so." The magistrate-farmer A. Bennett's testimony before the House of Lords inquiry of 1814 — that the weekly income of every family is made up to the gallon loaf (8 lb. 11 oz.) and 3d. per head, deducted if clothes are found — is quoted. Marx concludes with a bourgeois writer's lament: "For years... they (the farmers) have degraded a respectable class of their countrymen, by forcing them to have recourse to the workhouse... the farmer, while increasing his own gains, has prevented any accumulation on the part of his labouring dependants." And a note on "domestic industry" as a case of direct robbery of the worker's consumption fund.

Second, the growing difference between capital employed and capital consumed. Even if the number of workers is fixed, capital has an elasticity. To increase labour by 50%, one can either hire 50 more workers (requiring additional constant capital) or extend the working day from 8 to 12 hours, in which case existing instruments of labour suffice, being merely consumed more rapidly. In extractive industries (mines, etc.), raw materials form no part of the capital advanced; the object of labour is provided by nature free of charge. Constant capital consists "almost exclusively of instruments of labour which can very easily absorb an increased quantity of labour (day and night shifts, for example)." In agriculture, land once prepared produces "a marvellous effect" from additional labour. In manufacturing, raw material must be expanded, but the additional products of extractive industry and agriculture redound to manufacturing's advantage. The general result: "by incorporating with itself the two primary creators of wealth, labour-power and land, capital acquires a power of expansion that permits it to augment the elements of its accumulation beyond the limits apparently fixed by its own magnitude."

Third, the productivity of social labour. "The mass of the products in which a certain value, and therefore a surplus-value of a given magnitude is embodied, increases along with the productivity of labour." Even at a constant rate of surplus-value, the mass of surplus product grows. The relative magnitude of the accumulation fund can increase at the expense of the consumption fund while the capitalist's real enjoyment increases. The increasing productivity of labour is accompanied by a cheapening of the worker, and thus a higher rate of surplus-value even when real wages rise — and real wages "never rise in proportion to the productivity of labour." Hence "same value in variable capital therefore sets in motion more labour-power," and "same value in constant capital is embodied in more means of production... It therefore supplies both more product-creating agencies and more value-creating agencies, in other words absorbers of labour." Thus "the production of surplus-value increases more rapidly than the value of the additional capital."

Productivity also impacts the original capital. Fixed capital (machinery, etc.) is replaced periodically; if the productivity of labour in its construction has risen, the replacement machines are "more efficient and (considering their increased efficiency) cheaper." Chemistry multiplies useful materials, applications, and recycling opportunities: "it also teaches capital how to throw back the waste from the processes of production and consumption into the cycle of the process of reproduction, and thus, without any previous outlay of capital, it creates fresh materials for it." Science and technology give capital "a power of expansion which is independent of the given magnitude of the capital actually functioning." This development is accompanied by "a partial depreciation of the functioning capital; but in so far as this depreciation makes itself acutely felt in competition, the main burden falls on the worker, in whose increased exploitation the capitalist seeks compensation for his loss."

Marx then carefully distinguishes value from mass: "Labour transmits to the product the value of the means of production consumed by it." The same quantity of labour adds the same sum of new value; but the old value transmitted grows with productivity. The English spinner versus the Chinese spinner — both working the same hours at the same intensity, both creating equal values in a week — but the Englishman's automatic machine, allowing him to spin hundreds of pounds where the Chinese spins one, transmits "a sum of old values, many hundred times as great." Engels's Condition of the Working Class in England is quoted: "In 1782 the whole wool crop of the preceding three years lay unused for want of workers, and would have continued so to lie if the newly invented machinery had not come to its assistance and spun it." The natural property of living labour — to preserve old value while creating new — becomes a power of capital for "its own self-preservation." This is the crucial fetishism point: "All the powers of labour project themselves as powers of capital, just as all the value-forms of the commodity do as forms of money." The free service of past labour (the "free service" of means of production, akin to the free service of water, steam, air, electricity) is progressively accumulated.

Marx's footnote here takes a swipe at Ricardo's failure to grasp this: "a million men always produce in manufactures the same value" applies only to the new value added; the mass of transmitted old value varies widely with productivity. Say's attempt to resolve the "difficulty" by conflating use-value with exchange-value is quoted at length — "the difficulty is met by calling use-value exchange-value, if you please" — and an English critic's dismissal of Say's conjuring tricks is quoted.

Marx generalizes the fetishism: "Since past labour always disguises itself as capital, i.e. since the debts owed to the labour of A, B, C etc. are disguised as the assets of the non-worker X, bourgeois citizens and political economists are full of praise for the services performed by past labour." MacCulloch is criticized for wanting payment for these "services" in the form of interest and profit; his formula — "The profits of capital are only another name for the wages of accumulated labour" — is pithily rebutted: "MacCulloch took out a patent on the 'wages of past labour' long before Senior patented the 'wages of abstinence.'"

The most cutting passage on fetishism: "The practical agents of capitalist production and their ideological word-spinners are as incapable of thinking of the means of production separately from the antagonistic social mask they wear at present as a slave-owner is of thinking of the worker himself as distinct from his character as a slave."

Fourth, the magnitude of the capital advanced. With a given degree of exploitation, the mass of surplus-value depends on the number of workers simultaneously exploited, which corresponds, in varying proportions, with the magnitude of the capital. Hence "the more that capital increases by means of successive accumulations, the more does the sum of value increase that is divided into a fund for consumption and a fund for accumulation." The capitalist can both live better and "renounce" more.

Section 5: The so-called labour fund

The chapter's final section demolishes the dogma that capital is a fixed magnitude or that there is a "labour fund" fixed by Nature. Marx's summary of the preceding inquiry: "capital is not a fixed magnitude, but a part of social wealth which is elastic, and constantly fluctuates with the division of surplus-value into revenue and additional capital." Further, "even with a given magnitude of functioning capital, the labour-power, science and land... incorporated in it form elastic powers of capital, allowing it, within certain limits, a field of action independent of its own magnitude."

Marx reminds the reader he has ignored all circulation relations that could produce different efficiencies, and that he has presupposed limits set by capitalist production in its spontaneous development, disregarding "any more rational combination which could be effected directly and in a planned way with the means of production and the labour-power at present available." This last point is significant: the analysis is not an apology for unplanned production but an argument that even the existing potential is unrecognized by capitalist dogmas.

Classical political economy "has always liked to conceive social capital as a fixed magnitude of a fixed degree of efficiency. But this prejudice was first established as a dogma by the arch-philistine, Jeremy Bentham, that soberly pedantic and heavy-footed oracle of the 'common sense' of the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie. Bentham is among philosophers what Martin Tupper is among poets. Both could only have been manufactured in England."

A lengthy footnote savages Bentham: "The principle of utility was no discovery made by Bentham. He simply reproduced in his dull way what Helvetius and other Frenchmen had said with wit and ingenuity in the eighteenth century." Marx's criticism: "To know what is useful for a dog, one must investigate the nature of dogs... Applying this to man... [Bentham] assumes that the modern petty bourgeois, especially the English petty bourgeois, is the normal man. Whatever is useful to this peculiar kind of normal man, and to his world, is useful in and for itself. He applies this yardstick to the past, the present and the future. The Christian religion, for example, is 'useful,' 'because it forbids in the name of religion the same faults that the penal code condemns in the name of the law.' Art criticism is 'harmful' because it disturbs worthy people in their enjoyment of Martin Tupper, etc." The valuation: "If I had the courage of my friend Heinrich Heine, I should call Mr Jeremy a genius in the way of bourgeois stupidity."

Bentham's dogma "renders the commonest phenomena of the production process, for instance its sudden expansions and contractions, and even accumulation itself, absolutely incomprehensible." It was used "by Bentham himself, as well as by Malthus, James Mill, MacCulloch, etc., for apologetic purposes" — specifically to represent variable capital (or its material form, the labour fund) as a fixed size. Variable capital "was turned by this fable into a separate part of social wealth, confined by natural chains and unable to cross the boundary to the other parts."

Marx then quantifies the variability. To set a definite mass of constant capital in motion, a definite mass of living labour is required, given by technology. But the number of workers required to embody this mass of labour-power is not given, because it varies with the degree of exploitation of individual labour-power. Nor is the price of labour-power given — only a minimum limit, "which is moreover very elastic." The actual facts underlying the dogma are: (1) "the worker has no right to interfere in the division of social wealth into means of enjoyment for the non-worker and means of production"; (2) "it is only in favourable and exceptional cases that he can enlarge the so-called 'labour fund' at the expense of the 'revenue' of the rich." A footnote from John Stuart Mill concedes: "The produce of labour is apportioned at present in an inverse ratio to the labour — the largest portions to those who have never worked at all, the next largest to those whose work is almost nominal, and so in a descending scale..." — Marx notes this honestly, and adds that while Mill should be rebuked for the contradiction between his dogmas and his modern tendencies, "it would be very unjust to lump them together with the herd of vulgar economic apologists."

The reductio of the labour fund dogma comes from Fawcett: "The circulating capital of a country is its wage-fund. Hence, if we desire to calculate the average money wages received by each labourer, we have simply to divide the amount of this capital by the number of the labouring population." Marx's riposte: "That is to say, we first add together the individual wages actually paid, and then we assert that the sum thus obtained forms the total value of the 'labour fund' handed down to us by the grace of God and Nature. Lastly, we divide the sum thus obtained by the number of workers, in order to find out how much each is permitted to receive on the average. A very shrewd way of proceeding, this is." The circularity is devastating: the "labour fund" is simply the sum of wages already paid, dressed up as a natural datum, then divided to produce the average wage — tautology masquerading as law.

The final blow against the fixed-fund doctrine: Fawcett himself admits that much of the annual saving "is used as capital, not in England, but in foreign countries." Marx: "with the additional capital thus exported, a part of the 'labour fund' invented by God and Bentham naturally also flows out of the country." The labour fund can cross national borders; it is not chained to any natural magnitude. A footnote qualifies: "It might be said that not only capital but also workers, in the shape of emigrants, are annually exported from England." Marx responds that the emigrants are "in great part not workers" — "most of them are the sons of farmers" — and that the exported capital is a much greater proportion of annual accumulation than emigration is of annual population increase.

Marx closes the chapter (implicitly) by having cleared the ground: the argument that the labour fund is elastic, capable of expanding across the boundaries of "revenue" and "capital," cross-nationally mobile, and subject to change with the degree of exploitation — demolishes the last bastion of the apologetic claim that wages are limited by a natural, immovable fund.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter's central lesson is the immanent, self-unfolding dialectic of capitalist appropriation. Marx does not argue that capitalism violates its own laws of exchange — he argues that it fulfils them, and that this fulfilment inverts them. The property right grounded in a man's own labour becomes the right to appropriate the labour of others; the exchange of equivalents becomes the systematic exchange of the worker's living labour for a smaller quantity of objectified labour; the freedom of contract becomes the compulsion to sell one's labour-power on pain of starvation. This is not a critique from outside the bourgeois horizon but a demonstration that the bourgeois horizon, followed to its necessary conclusions, produces the very inversion it denies. Property and labour, initially identified, are now separated as structural opposites — and this separation is the necessary consequence of a law that originated in their identity.

From this follows a profound methodological lesson: the isolated transaction is always just, but the systemic repetition of just transactions produces systematic injustice. The bourgeois economist's insistence on judging each exchange on its own terms is not a mistake of analysis but a structural feature of the commodity form: the form abstracts from history, from accumulation, from class. Marx grants the abstraction in order to overcome it: only when the transactions are viewed in their temporal succession, in their material interconnection, in their reproduction of the class relation, does the injustice become visible. This methodological move — taking the isolated transaction and the connected totality as two opposite but equally necessary standpoints — is the dialectical core of the chapter.

The demonstration that the "original capital" becomes a magnitudo evanescens is a decisive blow to the ideology of the "frugal capitalist" who earned his initial stock by labour and abstinence. Even granting the possibility of such an origin (which Marx neither affirms nor denies here, since the historical question belongs to "primitive accumulation"), the accumulated mass of capital is overwhelmingly unpaid labour; whatever the first atom of capital was, its present existence is the capitalized subjugation of living labour. The ownership of past unpaid labour is "thenceforth the sole condition for the appropriation of living unpaid labour on a constantly increasing scale."

The critique of political economy provided in the chapter has three main axes:

  1. The Smithian confusion (accumulation as mere conversion of surplus into wages) whose apologetic payoff is that accumulation benefits workers directly.
  2. The abstinence theory (Senior, Mill, Cazenove, Scrope, de Molinari) whose function is to convert appropriation into sacrifice, exploitation into ascetic virtue. The mockery of "abstinence from fasting" and "abstinence from standing still" dissolves the concept into linguistic nonsense.
  3. The labour fund dogma (Bentham, Fawcett) whose function is to represent wages as bounded by a natural, fixed magnitude, thus denying workers any legitimate claim to alter the distribution.

The positive content of the chapter is equally important. Marx identifies the elasticity of capital: the power to expand beyond the limits of the value magnitude through (a) increased exploitation, (b) increased productivity, (c) the incorporation of nature (land, mines, air, water, chemistry, science) as a free productive force, (d) the difference between capital employed and consumed, (e) the magnitude of advance. Capital is not a thing but a power-laden social relation that generates, from within itself, forms of expansion not derivable from any static equality between inputs and outputs.

The historical-theoretical dimension is developed through the reading of Richard Jones and Dr Aikin. Jones supplies the empirical demonstration that expanded reproduction occurs without the "abstaining capitalist" in non-capitalist modes, thus undercutting the claim that capitalist property relations are naturally required for economic development. Aikin shows that asceticism and luxury are historical stages of the capitalist class's self-formation — the miser-capitalist gives way to the luxury-capitalist as accumulation deepens. This historical dialectic is essential to Marx's argument: capitalist psychology is not a natural feature of human nature but a function of the position within the accumulation process. The Faustian conflict between accumulation and enjoyment is produced, not pre-given.

The broader philosophical implications reach beyond political economy. The chapter's treatment of fetishism — "All the powers of labour project themselves as powers of capital, just as all the value-forms of the commodity do as forms of money" — extends Chapter 1's commodity fetishism to the accumulated capital relation. The slave-owner's inability to see the worker apart from his slave character is Marx's model for the capitalist's inability to see the means of production apart from their capitalist form. This recognition of the social mask as constitutive of the perceived object connects Marx to the Hegelian critique of immediacy: what appears as a simple thing (capital) contains a hidden social history (expropriation), and critique consists in detecting the smuggled history behind the naturalized present.

The final image — that part of the "labour fund invented by God and Bentham" flows out of the country with capital export — punctures the naturalistic apologetics with a concrete international dynamic. Capital is mobile, elastic, and internationalist; the wage-fund doctrine is provincial. The chapter thus lays the conceptual groundwork for the analysis of the world market and the colonial dimension that Marx will develop in the polemical material on Wakefield's colonization theory (Chapter 33).

The chapter's political implications are direct. If the property laws of commodity production invert into laws of capitalist appropriation, then appeals to the "sanctity of property" and appeals to "restoring the identity of property and labour" (Proudhon) are incoherent within the capitalist order. Only a transformation of the property relations themselves — a revolutionary supersession, not a "reform" or "enforcement of eternal laws" — can dissolve the inversion. At the same time, Marx's insistence that capitalism is historically necessary for the development of the productive forces heads off any romantic or moralistic rejection: the socialist project is not to return to a prior simplicity but to appropriate, on a new basis, the productive powers capitalism itself has developed. "The creation of those material conditions of production which alone can form the real basis of a higher form of society, a society in which the full and free development of every individual forms the ruling principle."

In sum, Chapter 24 is a masterpiece of dialectical exposition. It takes the apparently innocuous concepts of saving, reinvestment, and property and shows that, followed rigorously through their own logic, they reveal a systematic relation of appropriation that is invisible to the individual look but unavoidable to the total view. It demonstrates that the critique of political economy is not external moralizing but the unfolding of the very categories of political economy against themselves. And it establishes that the accumulation of capital is not a matter of the capitalist's virtue or vice — his frugality or his extravagance, his abstinence or his luxury — but the necessary self-reproduction of a social relation into which he is locked, "a cog" in a mechanism that compels him to accumulate on pain of loss, while the entire structure of law, ideology, and economic science conspires to represent this compulsion as natural, just, and beneficent. The capitalist's "original virginity" — as Marx puts it — is preserved only in the abstract standpoint of commodity exchange. In the actual movement of accumulation, every "atom" of the modern capital mass is the crystallized labour of the dispossessed.
