Volume 1 • Chapter 18: Different Formulae for the Rate of Surplu

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 18, "Different Formulae for the Rate of Surplus-Value," is the closing chapter of Part V of Capital, Volume I — the part devoted to "The Production of Absolute and Relative Surplus-Value." It is a short chapter (five pages, roughly 1,770 words) and it introduces no new empirical material, no new historical investigation, and no new factory report. What it does instead is something rarer and, structurally, something more consequential: it formalizes. It gathers up the conceptual labour of the preceding chapters — the working day, necessary and surplus labour, the value of labour-power, variable capital, the value-product — and asks a deceptively simple question: in what mathematical shape should the exploitation of labour be written down?

The central problem is that one and the same social relation admits of several distinct symbolic representations, and these representations are not interchangeable in their effects. Marx presents three sets of formulae. Formula I — surplus-value over variable capital; surplus-value over the value of labour-power; surplus labour over necessary labour — is, he says, "rigorously definite and correct," and it captures the real degree of exploitation. Formula III — surplus-value over the value of labour-power; surplus labour over necessary labour; unpaid labour over paid labour — is a restatement of the same truth in "popular" idiom, and it carries a conceptual trap that must be disarmed. Formula II — surplus labour over the working day; surplus-value over the value-product; surplus product over total product — is derivative, and although it expresses the identical proportion, it "falsely" expresses the rate of surplus-value when it is treated as a direct expression of capital's self-valorization.

From that diagnosis follows the chapter's polemical core. If Formula II is read as the measure of exploitation, an "erroneous law" results: surplus labour or surplus-value can never reach 100 per cent. And a measure that structurally forbids its own object from exceeding a ceiling of one hundred has three downstream effects, all of them intellectual and political at once: it fixes the working day as a constant magnitude (thereby naturalizing a historically contested, class-determined variable); it conceals the specific character of the capital-relation — namely that variable capital is exchanged for living labour-power and that the worker is therefore excluded from the product; and it substitutes for exploitation the "false semblance of a relation of association," in which worker and capitalist appear as partners dividing the proceeds in proportion to their respective contributions.

The chapter's intellectual objective is thus threefold. First, to fix the correct measure — the rate of surplus-value — as the index of the "degree of exploitation," and to demonstrate its unboundedness (Marx cites Lavergne's estimate of English agricultural wages to show a real-world rate of 300 per cent). Second, to expose the ideological mechanism by which the derivative formulae invert essence into appearance. Third, and most ambitiously, to convert the analysis of the rate into a definition of its object: "Capital ... is not only the command over labour, as Adam Smith thought. It is essentially the command over unpaid labour." All surplus-value — whether it later crystallizes as profit, interest, or rent — is "the materialization of unpaid labour-time," and the secret of capital's self-valorization is nothing more than its "disposal over a definite quantity of the unpaid labour of other people [fremder Arbeit]."

Positioned as it is, the chapter functions as a hinge. It closes the theory of surplus-value production and opens onto the theory of wages (Part VI) by insisting on the distinction between the value of labour-power and the value of labour; it prepares the theory of accumulation (Part VII) by fixing the rate whose magnitude accumulation will presuppose; and it pre-announces the later analysis of the forms of surplus-value — profit, interest, rent — as so many disguises of a single substance. Methodologically, it is also a compact demonstration of Marx's general procedure: the modes of appearance generated by capitalist production itself are not mere errors of economists, they are real representational forms — "this mode of presentation ... arises, by the way, out of the capitalist mode of production itself" — whose inversion of the essence must be scientifically undone.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

I. The Opening Move: One Relation, Three Symbolic Registers

Marx opens by simply laying out Formula I as an established result:

$$\frac{\text{Surplus-value }(s)}{\text{Variable capital}} \;=\; \frac{\text{Surplus-value}}{\text{Value of labour-power}} \;=\; \frac{\text{Surplus labour}}{\text{Necessary labour}}$$

The first two members are ratios of values; the third is a ratio of the times during which those values are produced. His claim is that these are "mutually replaceable" and "rigorously definite and correct." The replaceability is the crucial theoretical assertion: because value is congealed labour-time, and because the value of labour-power is itself determined by the labour-time socially necessary to reproduce the worker, a ratio of values and a ratio of times are two notations for one thing. The equality is not a convenience; it is the direct expression of the labour theory of value in the domain of exploitation. This is why the formula can be written indifferently as a money ratio (3s./3s.), a time ratio (6 hours/6 hours), or a percentage (100 per cent), without any loss.

The historical note attached here is important and characteristically generous in form, sharp in substance: classical political economy "worked out" these formulae "in substance, but not in a conscious form." Smith and Ricardo possessed the content — they knew that profit derived from the unpaid labour of the worker, that rent was a deduction — but they never isolated the rate as a formal category, and so they drifted into the derivative formulae of Set II. This is the Marxian theory of the relationship to predecessors in miniature: not invention but the elevation of an implicit content into conscious, explicit form. The French-translation footnote attached to the third member of Formula I reinforces this claim: Marx there placed parentheses around the "surplus labour/necessary labour" expression "because the concept of surplus labour is not found clearly expressed in bourgeois political economy." The parenthesis is a small, almost pedantic editorial gesture, and it is also a precise historical judgement: the third term is Marx's own contribution, and by bracketing it he indicates where the classics stopped.

II. Why Formula I Is "Rigorously Definite and Correct"

It is worth unpacking why Formula I earns the description "rigorously definite." Consider variable capital. It is capital advanced for the purchase of labour-power; its magnitude equals the value of that labour-power, i.e. the value of the means of subsistence required to reproduce the worker at a given historical and moral standard. But this value is realized only in the necessary labour portion of the working day — the portion in which the worker reproduces the equivalent of his own wage. Hence variable capital and necessary labour are the same magnitude in two units. The denominator of Formula I therefore measures the cost of the worker to capital, and the numerator measures the surplus value obtained beyond that cost. The ratio is thus precisely a rate of return on the one capital outlay whose consumption generates new value.

Two properties follow, and both will matter. First, the denominator is strictly positive — necessary labour cannot be zero, because a worker who receives nothing cannot reproduce himself and the system cannot continue. Second, the ratio itself has no upper bound. Nothing in the definition of $s/v$ restricts it to 1 or any other value; it can be 50 per cent, 100 per cent, 300 per cent, or higher, depending on the relation between the length of the working day and the value of labour-power. Formula I is "definite" in the sense that it names the thing it measures without distortion: it is the rate at which capital expands itself by consuming the labour-power it has purchased.

III. The Derivative Formulae (II) and the Handling of Constant Capital

Marx then presents the second set, which he calls derivative:

$$\frac{\text{Surplus labour}}{\text{Working day}} \;=\; \frac{\text{Surplus-value}}{\text{Value of the product}} \;=\; \frac{\text{Surplus product}}{\text{Total product}}$$

Here the same proportion is expressed "alternately in the form of labour-times, of the values in which those labour-times are embodied, and of the products in which those values exist." Three registers again — time, value, and physical product — but with a different denominator. Under I the denominator was the worker's share (necessary labour, variable capital); under II the denominator is the whole (working day, value-product, total product). The numerator is unchanged; only the base has been shifted from a part to the whole.

A clarification immediately follows, and it is easy to miss its significance: by "value of the product" the political economists mean "only the value newly created in a working day, the constant part of the value of the product being excluded." This is a technical but decisive restriction. The value of the product in the full sense would be $c + v + s$, including the value transferred from raw materials and machinery. What Formula II's middle term uses is the value-product, i.e. $v + s$ — the new value added by living labour in the day. This same exclusion is carried into the empirical footnote: Lavergne's estimate of the agricultural labourer's share "of course" leaves out "that part of the product which merely replaces the constant capital advanced." Without this exclusion the ratio would be meaningless as a measure of the division between wages and surplus, because constant capital is neither wage nor surplus. The restriction also matters because it shows that Formula II is not simply careless arithmetic — it is a correct description of a different thing, namely the division of the freshly created value between the two classes.

IV. The Arithmetical Demonstration: Twelve Hours, Six and Six, Three Shillings and Three Shillings

Marx then makes the argument numerically, using the same assumptions as the earlier chapters: a twelve-hour working day, a labour-power value of 3 shillings corresponding to six hours, and therefore six hours of surplus labour producing 3 shillings of surplus-value. Under Formula I:

$$\frac{6\text{ hours surplus labour}}{6\text{ hours necessary labour}} \;=\; \frac{\text{surplus-value of 3s.}}{\text{variable capital of 3s.}} \;=\; 100 \text{ per cent}$$

Under Formula II, on the same facts:

$$\frac{6\text{ hours surplus labour}}{12\text{ hours working day}} \;=\; \frac{\text{surplus-value of 3s.}}{\text{value-product of 6s.}} \;=\; 50 \text{ per cent}$$

The same day, the same exploitation, two answers: 100 per cent and 50 per cent. This is the pivot of the whole chapter. The formal relation between the two measures is worth stating explicitly, because Marx's arithmetic implies it even where he does not write the algebra. Let $e = s/v$ be the rate of surplus-value. Then the Formula II ratio is $s/(v+s) = e/(1+e)$. Conversely, $e = [s/(v+s)] / [1 - s/(v+s)]$. So a Formula I rate of 100 per cent ($e = 1$) corresponds to a Formula II ratio of $1/2 = 50$ per cent; a Formula I rate of 300 per cent ($e = 3$) corresponds to $3/4 = 75$ per cent; and as $e \to \infty$, the Formula II ratio approaches but never exceeds 1. The two measures are related by a monotone transformation — which is precisely why the confusion is so seductive and so dangerous. Nothing is arithmetically wrong. The meaning has been replaced.

V. The Erroneous Law: Why the Share Can Never Reach 100 Per Cent

From the misreading follows the "erroneous law": surplus labour or surplus-value can never reach 100 per cent. Marx's derivation of it is a small model of rigorous inference. The surplus labour is only "an aliquot part" of the working day — that is, an exact fractional portion of a whole — and surplus-value is likewise only an aliquot part of the value-product. A part must be less than its whole. Therefore surplus labour must always be less than the working day, and surplus-value always less than the value-product. For the ratio to reach $100:100$ — unity — the two terms would have to be equal. They cannot be, because one is contained in the other. Q.E.D. The "law" is thus not an empirical generalization but a definitional artefact of the wrong denominator. It is worth stressing that Marx is not accusing the political economists of an arithmetic mistake. He grants that Formula II expresses "in reality, only the proportion in which the working day, or the value produced by it, is divided between the capitalist and the worker." That proportion is real, it is measurable, and it does cap out at 100 per cent — as any proportional share must. The error is categorical: to mistake a share for a rate, to treat a distribution ratio as "a direct expression of the degree of capital's self-valorization."

VI. The Self-Dissolving Limit: Why the Whole-Day Asymptote Cannot Be Attained

Marx then pushes the argument one step further and produces a genuinely dialectical result. Having said that surplus labour must be less than the working day, he asks what would be required for the gap to close: "In order for the surplus labour to absorb the whole day (i.e. an average day of any week or year) the necessary labour would have to sink to zero. But if necessary labour vanishes, surplus labour does as well, since it is only a function of the necessary [labour]."

The limit is not merely unattained; it is self-dissolving. Surplus labour is not an independent magnitude that could in principle swallow the day; it is defined in relation to necessary labour, and it exists only as the excess over that necessary labour. Annihilate the base and the excess vanishes with it. Hence: "The ratio $\frac{\text{surplus labour}}{\text{working day}}$, or $\frac{\text{surplus-value}}{\text{value-product}}$, can therefore never reach the limit of $100/100$, still less rise above it." The Formula II measure is asymptotically bounded above by unity and structurally prevented from reaching it. Note the parenthetical "(i.e. an average day of any week or year)" — a small but characteristic precaution: the comparison must be made over an average period, since daily hours fluctuate, and the point holds for the average as much as for any single day.

Contrast this with the rate of surplus-value, which, "however, the real degree of exploitation of labour, is able to do this" — is able to reach and exceed 100 per cent. The two measures thus differ not merely in magnitude but in their structural ceilings: one is bounded by 1 and cannot attain it; the other is unbounded. That is the formal statement of the chapter's central contrast.

VII. The Empirical Refutation: Lavergne and the 300 Per Cent Rate

Marx does not leave the unboundedness of the rate as a purely formal possibility. He supplies an empirical instance: the estimate of Léonce de Lavergne, according to which the English agricultural labourer receives only one-quarter of the product (or of its value), while the capitalist farmer takes three-quarters, "whatever the subsequent division of the booty between the capitalist, the landowner and others." On that calculation, the labourer's surplus labour stands to his necessary labour as 3:1, "which gives a rate of exploitation of 300 per cent." This figure is doing several jobs at once. First, it demonstrates concretely that rates above 100 per cent occur in the real world, so the "erroneous law" is not merely internally arbitrary but empirically false. Second, it illustrates the relationship between the two measures on real data: a 300 per cent rate of exploitation corresponds under Formula II to a share of three-quarters — a number comfortably under 100 per cent, and therefore perfectly consistent with the false law. Third, it introduces a distinction Marx will build on later: the decomposition of the surplus itself among capitalist, landowner, and others. The "booty" is one quantity, differently divided; the fact of appropriation is prior to the distribution of appropriated value. In a footnote Marx adds his own critical caveat about the source: Lavergne, "as a blind admirer of England, is inclined to estimate the share of the capitalist too low, rather than too high." Even the empirical illustration is handled with methodological scepticism.

VIII. The Political Effect: Fixing the Working Day as a Constant

Having exposed the erroneous law, Marx draws out its first consequence: "Through the use of the formulae given under II, the political economists' favourite method of treating the working day as constant in magnitude became a fixed usage, because in those formulae surplus labour is always compared with a working day of a given length."

This is a superb observation about how the shape of a formula governs thought. Under Formula II, the denominator is the working day, taken as a fixed quantum; the measure can only register movements in the numerator. The formula therefore cannot, in and of itself, express a change in the length of the working day — precisely the variable that was the central object of class struggle in the nineteenth century and the central object of Marx's own Chapters 8 and 10. By writing the relation this way, the political economists embedded a presupposition — a given working day — into their analytic apparatus, so that the historical and political determination of the working day's length simply dropped out of view. It is worth underlining that this is precisely the criticism Marx developed at length in the earlier chapters on the struggle over the working day: the length of the working day is not a natural constant but a contested, historically variable magnitude won through legislation and struggle. Formula II, whatever its author's intentions, renders the outcome of that struggle invisible — it appears as an unargued premise, not as a battleground.

IX. The Ideological Effect: From Exploitation to "Association"

The second consequence is deeper and is stated with Marx's characteristic move from logic to social form. When political economy treats surplus-value and the value of labour-power as fractions of the value-product, it "conceal[s] the specific character of the capital-relation, namely the fact that variable capital is exchanged for living labour-power, and that the worker is accordingly excluded from the product." Three distinct concealments are being named. First, the object of exchange is obscured: capital buys labour-power, not labour, and the formula's tidy partitioning of "the value-product" whispers that the worker is a participant in the output. Second, the exclusion of the worker from the product is obscured: the product belongs, legally and economically, to the capitalist; the worker never owned it, and receives a wage, not a share. Third, and most insidiously, "instead of revealing the capital-relation they show us the false semblance of a relation of association, in which worker and capitalist divide the product in proportion to the different elements which they respectively contribute towards its formation."

The phrase "false semblance" (Schein in the Hegelian register) is precisely chosen. It is not a lie told by dishonest economists; it is the appearance generated by the surface of the capitalist process, in which two parties appear to meet, exchange, and part with their respective returns. Marx flags this explicitly: this mode of presentation "arises, by the way, out of the capitalist mode of production itself, and whose significance we shall unearth later on." He does not discharge the promissory note in this chapter; he records it, so that the apologetic capacity of the surface-form can be traced back to its source in the real relations rather than to mere error. The footnote to this passage is a small masterpiece of sarcasm. Since "all the developed forms of the capitalist process of production are forms of co-operation, nothing is easier ... than to make abstraction from their specifically antagonistic character, and, merely by verbal alterations, make them sound like forms of free association." He names Count A. de Laborde's De l'esprit d'association dans tous les intérêts de la communauté (Paris, 1818), and then H. Carey "the Yankee," who "occasionally performs this conjuring trick, with similar success, even with the relations prevailing under slavery." The point is aimed at a whole tradition — the harmonious-association school that reaches from the Restoration through Carey's American protectionism and beyond — and it makes explicit that the ideological payoff of the derivative formulae is not incidental but essential.

X. The Rodbertus Footnote: Marx's Judgement of Predecessors, and Engels's Correction

The first footnote of the chapter opens a different front. Marx cites "for example" Rodbertus's Sociale Briefe an von Kirchmann (Third Letter, Berlin, 1851) as a locus of the erroneous law, and adds a characteristically generous appraisal: in spite of its erroneous theory of rent, the pamphlet "sees through the nature of capitalist production," and Marx promises to return to it later — a promise discharged, as the editorial note points out, in Theories of Surplus-Value, Part 2, Chapters 8 and 9.

Engels, preparing the third German edition, appended a correction that is historically and theoretically revealing. He observes that "it may be seen from this how favourably Marx judged his predecessors, whenever he found in them a real advance, or new and sound ideas," and then reports that the publication of Rodbertus's letters to Rudolf Meyer showed "that the above acknowledgement needs to be restricted to some extent." In those letters Rodbertus wrote that "capital must be rescued not only from labour, but from itself," best achieved "by treating the acts of the industrial capitalist as economic and political functions that have been delegated to him with his capital, and by treating his profit as a form of salary"; he added that "the irruption of Marx into society ... must be warded off," accusing Marx's book of being "not so much an investigation of capital as a polemic against the present form of capital, a form which he confuses with the concept of capital itself." Engels's verdict on this collapse is dry: "The bold onslaught mounted by Rodbertus in his 'social letters' finally dwindled down to ideological commonplaces of this kind."

The footnote thus accomplishes three things simultaneously. It documents the prevalence of the "erroneous law" (Rodbertus held it). It exhibits Marx's method of scholarly acknowledgement — credit for the genuine insight, criticism for the error, no retrospective settling of scores. And through Engels's addition, it administers a sharp lesson about the difference between a critique of capital and a reformist proposal to pay off capital with a salary — thereby insulating the chapter's own argument (that capital is command over unpaid labour) against precisely the misreading that Rodbertus exemplifies.

XI. Reconversion: How Formula II Collapses Back into Formula I

After the critique, Marx shows the escape route. "But in any case, the formulae given under III can always be reconverted into the original formulae." (The text's "III" here appears to be a typographical artefact for "II" — the formulae under discussion are exhausted by I and II; this is the reading consistent with what follows.) The demonstration is arithmetic:

$$\frac{\text{Surplus labour of 6 hours}}{\text{Working day of 12 hours}} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{necessary labour} = 12 - 6 = 6 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{\text{Surplus labour of 6 hours}}{\text{Necessary labour of 6 hours}} = 100 \text{ per cent}$$

The reversal is exactly the algebraic inverse noted above: since necessary labour is the working day minus surplus labour, the whole is decomposable into its two parts, and the ratio can be rewritten on the correct (part-to-part) base. Two things follow. First, the error of Formula II is remediable — it is not a mathematical dead end but a representational choice, and the choice can be unmade. Second, and importantly for the political argument: the reconversion shows that nothing is actually concealed from the analyst who knows how to read the whole. The concealment operates only for those who take the share for the rate.

XII. Formula III: Unpaid Labour and the Trap of Popular Language

Marx then returns to a formulation he says he has "occasionally anticipated":

$$\frac{\text{Surplus-value}}{\text{Value of labour-power}} \;=\; \frac{\text{Surplus labour}}{\text{Necessary labour}} \;=\; \frac{\text{Unpaid labour}}{\text{Paid labour}}$$

The first two members are Formula I restated. The third is a popular gloss, and Marx immediately warns against its dangers: "After the analysis given above, it is no longer possible to be misled by the formula unpaid labour / paid labour into concluding that the capitalist pays for labour and not for labour-power." The trap is elementary but consequential. If capital paid for labour, then all labour would be paid for, and surplus-value would be inexplicable. The formula unpaid labour / paid labour is safe only when the reader already understands that the wage is the price of labour-power, and that "unpaid labour" is not a refused wage but a description of that portion of the working day in which the worker produces value for which no equivalent is returned. Hence: "The capitalist pays the value of the labour-power (or, if the price diverges from this, he pays the price) and receives in exchange the right to dispose of the living labour-power itself." The distinction between value and price is admitted without fuss in the parenthesis, but the essential point is the object of the exchange: not the labour, but the capacity to labour. The popular phrase thus becomes, in the hands of a careless reader, exactly the formulation that destroys the theory it was meant to express.

XIII. The Two Periods of the Working Day: The Equivalent and the Surplus

Marx then spells out in plain narrative why "unpaid labour" is the right popular phrase, and in doing so recapitulates the whole mechanism of surplus-value. The time during which the capitalist utilizes the labour-power "is divided into two separate periods." In the first, "the worker produces a value that is only equal to the value of his labour-power, i.e. he produces its equivalent. Thus the capitalist receives, in return for advancing the price of the labour-power, a product of the same price. It is the same as if he had bought the product ready-made in the market." This is the necessary-labour phase — pure exchange, no surplus, a transaction that would satisfy the most rigorous standard of equivalent exchange. In the second period, "the period of surplus labour, the utilization of the labour-power creates a value for the capitalist without costing him any value in return. He is thus able to set labour-power in motion without paying for it. It is in this sense that surplus labour can be called unpaid labour."

The phrasing "It is the same as if he had bought the product ready-made in the market" is doing defensive work. It shows that the first phase contains no injustice in the terms of commodity exchange; the appropriation occurs entirely in the second phase, where a purchased capacity is made to yield more than it cost. The inference "It is in this sense that surplus labour can be called unpaid labour" is therefore carefully hedged: "in this sense" — not in the sense that a wage has been withheld, but in the sense that the value it produced has no counterpart in what was paid for it.

XIV. The Two Complementary Footnotes: The French Parentheses and the Physiocrats

Two footnotes round out the chapter's scholarly apparatus and both bear on the historical argument. One, attached to the "surplus labour/necessary labour" member of Formula I, records that Marx placed parentheses round that expression in the French translation "because the concept of surplus labour is not found clearly expressed in bourgeois political economy." As noted earlier, this marks the third member of Formula I as Marx's own theoretical addition, distinguishing his formula from what the classics actually wrote.

The other footnote, appended to the analysis of unpaid labour, concedes a partial foresight to the Physiocrats: "Although the Physiocrats could not penetrate the mystery of surplus-value, this much was clear to them, that it was 'independent and disposable wealth, which he' (the possessor of the surplus value) 'has not bought, and nevertheless sells' (Turgot, Réflexions sur la formation et la distribution des richesses, p. 11)." Turgot's phrase captures the whole logic in a single image: a wealth that was never purchased yet is freely disposed of and sold. That is the essence of surplus-value, seen through the eyes of an eighteenth-century Frenchman who nonetheless believed agriculture alone produced it. The footnote thus works the same way as the reference to the classics at the chapter's opening: it credits real perception where it existed, while noting the limit of the form in which it was held.

XV. The Climax: Capital as Command over Unpaid Labour

The chapter ends not with arithmetic but with a redefinition. "Capital, therefore, is not only the command over labour, as Adam Smith thought. It is essentially the command over unpaid labour." The reference to Smith is pointed. In Smith's account, capital dominates labour because the accumulation of stock and the division of labour place the wage-earner under the direction of the employer; "command over labour" is a structural feature of advanced exchange. Marx accepts the description and then sharpens it: the defining power of capital is not command over labour as such but command over unpaid labour — its power consists in setting labour in motion and appropriating the surplus product without equivalent. "All surplus-value, whatever particular form (profit, interest or rent) it may subsequently crystallize into, is in substance the materialization of unpaid labour-time."

This sentence is the seed of the entire later analysis of the forms of surplus-value. Profit, interest, and rent, which appear in the economic consciousness as three independent sources of income and as three distinct revenues of three distinct classes (enterprise, finance, landownership), are here declared to be one substance in three crystallized shapes: materialized unpaid labour-time. The dissolution of that appearance into its essence is the project of the volumes to come. And the chapter closes by naming the resolution of its central problem: "The secret of the self-valorization of capital resolves itself into the fact that it has at its disposal a definite quantity of the unpaid labour of other people [fremder Arbeit]." Every term is loaded. "A definite quantity" — quantification matters, because the amount of unpaid labour is what the rate of surplus-value measures and what the working day limits. "Others'" — the German fremder registers at once "of others" and "alien," so that the alienated labour of the worker is the very material of capital's autonomy. And "self-valorization" — the appearance that value magically expands of its own accord is dispelled: the expansion is nothing but the absorption of alien labour-time. The rate of surplus-value, then, was never a technical curiosity. It was, from the beginning, the arithmetic of a social relation.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The first and most portable principle this chapter establishes is that the measure is not neutral. Two ratios drawn from the same facts — 100 per cent and 50 per cent — are the same proportion of the same day, and yet they license opposite conclusions about the nature and the limits of exploitation. A formula is a theory in shorthand: its denominator encodes what the relation is being measured against, and with that choice come commitments about what the phenomenon is. To divide by the worker's share is to ask how much surplus is extracted per unit of the worker's cost, and to get an unbounded rate. To divide by the whole day is to ask what fraction of the product is taken, and to get a bounded share. Both questions are legitimate; only the first answers the question "what is the degree of exploitation?" Marx's lesson is that the choice of denominator is not a matter of taste but a theoretical decision with ideological consequences.

The second principle is the structural distinction between rate and share. The share is bounded by 1; the rate is not. Any habit of thought that treats the worker's proportional share of the value-product as the measure of exploitation will be blind to rising rates whenever the working day lengthens or the value of labour-power falls — because a higher rate simply moves the share asymptotically toward, but never beyond, 100 per cent. This is a general analytic trap, not a nineteenth-century curiosity: any bounded distributional indicator is a systematically misleading proxy for an unbounded relational magnitude.

The third principle is dialectical, and it emerges from the as-yet-unreachable limit: surplus labour is "only a function of the necessary [labour]." Exploitation is not an independent quantum that might in principle consume the day; it exists only as the excess over reproduction. Necessary labour and surplus labour are therefore relational, not additive, magnitudes — destroy one and the other collapses with it. This is why the concept of a "limit" to exploitation cannot be modelled as a mere upper bound on a share; the limit dissolves itself when approached. Exploitation is structurally dependent on the very element it appears to threaten.

The fourth principle is definitional: capital is command over unpaid labour. The chapter corrects Adam Smith, but not by rejection — it accepts Smith's description of capital as command over labour and identifies what that command is for. The command is not the essence; the appropriation without equivalent is. This is the conceptual foundation for the treatment of profit, interest, and rent as forms of one substance, and it is the answer to the puzzle of self-valorization: value expands because it acquires a claim on the labour-time of others.

From these principles follow the broader implications. Politically, the naturalization critique matters: the working day can appear as a constant magnitude only because the formula presupposes it as fixed. Once the presupposition is exposed, the length of the working day reappears for what it was — a variable struggled over, legislated, and enforced. Analytically, the distinction between the value of labour-power and the value of labour, adumbrated in the warning against the phrase "unpaid labour," is the hinge on which the subsequent critique of the wage-form turns; without it the wage appears to pay for the entire working day, and surplus-value becomes unthinkable. Ideologically, the "false semblance of a relation of association" is a durable trope: from Laborde's esprit d'association and Carey's harmonious treatment of slavery, through the twentieth-century language of partnership and stakeholder value, the presentation of the wage relation as co-operation in production recurs because the surface of the capitalist process continues to generate it. Marx's contribution is to show that this appearance is not a lie but an effect, arising out of the mode of production itself — and therefore traceable, analysable, and refutable. Finally, methodologically, the chapter models the relation of critical theory to its predecessors: to find in the classics "in substance, but not in a conscious form" what they had not isolated as such, and to mark with a parenthesis (as Marx did in the French translation) exactly where the addition begins. The chapter thus closes Part V by doing three things at once: it fixes the rate, it exposes the form that inverts it, and it names the substance that all the forms — profit, interest, rent — will turn out to conceal.
