Volume 1 • Appendix: Results of the Immediate Process of Production

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The "Results of the Immediate Process of Production" (hereafter Resultate) constitutes one of the most theoretically significant yet historically neglected texts in the Marxian corpus. Written between June 1863 and December 1866, this manuscript was originally conceived as Part Seven of Volume 1 of Capital, intended to serve a dual didactic function: first, as a comprehensive summary of the entire first volume's analysis of capitalist production, and second, as a bridge or transition to Volume 2's examination of the circulation process of capital. The text was first published in 1933 by Adoratsky in Volume II of Arkhiv Marksa i Engelsa, simultaneously in Russian and German, but only became an object of intense scholarly study when reprinted in Western European languages in the late 1960s.

The chapter's fundamental thesis is that capitalist production must be understood not merely as the production of commodities or even as the production of surplus-value, but as the production and reproduction of the specifically capitalist relations of production themselves. This tripartite structure—commodities as products of capital, capitalist production as production of surplus-value, and capitalist production as reproduction of capitalist relations—constitutes Marx's most sophisticated attempt to synthesize the objective and subjective dimensions of the capitalist mode of production into a unified theoretical framework.

The intellectual objective of the Resultate is nothing less than to demonstrate how the capitalist process of production, when properly understood in its totality, produces not only material wealth in the form of commodities but also reproduces and expands the very social relationship upon which it depends: the relationship between capital and wage-labour. This involves showing how what appears on the surface as a free and equal exchange between commodity owners (the capitalist and the worker meeting in the marketplace) is in fact the constant reproduction of a relationship of domination and exploitation, achieved through the mediation of apparently voluntary contractual arrangements.

The chapter's central theoretical innovation lies in Marx's introduction of the distinction between the "formal subsumption" and the "real subsumption" of labour under capital. This distinction, which Mandel correctly identifies as "the key aspect of the Resultate," provides the analytical framework for understanding the historical development of capitalism from its earliest manifestations in simple manufacture to its mature form in large-scale industry. Formal subsumption characterizes the period when capital takes over pre-existing labour processes developed under earlier modes of production—handicraft, peasant agriculture, guild production—without fundamentally transforming their technical character. Real subsumption, by contrast, characterizes the modern factory system, where capital has revolutionized the production process itself through the application of machinery, science, and technology, creating a specifically capitalist mode of production that is qualitatively different from all preceding forms.

This distinction enables Marx to resolve several theoretical puzzles that had troubled both classical political economy and earlier socialist critics of capitalism. First, it explains how surplus-value can be produced under conditions of formal equality and free exchange: the worker sells his labour-power at its value, receives a wage equivalent to the value of his means of subsistence, yet capital nevertheless extracts surplus labour because the value created by labour-power in use exceeds the value of labour-power as a commodity. Second, it explains the historical dynamics of capitalist development: why capitalism first emerges in certain branches of production and certain geographical locations, how it spreads from one sphere to another, and why it tends toward constant revolutionization of the forces of production. Third, it provides the basis for understanding the distinctive forms of mystification and fetishism that characterize capitalist society, showing how the social relations of production come to appear as properties of things—how capital appears as a productive force in its own right, how the social productivity of labour appears as the productivity of capital, and how the domination of capital over labour appears as the natural order of things.

The Resultate also contains Marx's most extended discussion of the distinction between productive and unproductive labour under capitalism. Against the confusion of classical political economy, which oscillated between defining productive labour in terms of its material result (the production of tangible commodities) and in terms of its capacity to generate profit, Marx insists that productive labour under capitalism is precisely that labour which is directly exchanged for capital and which produces surplus-value. Labour that produces commodities but is exchanged for revenue rather than capital—the labour of the independent artisan who sells his products to consumers, or the labour of the servant who performs personal services—is unproductive in the specifically capitalist sense, regardless of its material usefulness. This distinction is crucial for understanding the dynamics of capital accumulation, since only the exchange of capital for productive labour can satisfy the conditions for the reconversion of surplus-value into capital.

The chapter's treatment of the "collective worker" (Gesamtarbeiter) represents another significant theoretical advance. With the development of the specifically capitalist mode of production, Marx argues, the real lever of the overall labour process is increasingly not the individual worker but the "socially combined labour-power" of all those who participate in production. This includes not only manual labourers but also engineers, technologists, managers, and even scientists whose intellectual labour is incorporated into the production process. The concept of the collective worker thus provides the theoretical basis for understanding how the social productivity of labour—the productive power that arises from co-operation, division of labour, and the application of science—comes to appear as the productive power of capital, systematically mystifying the real source of wealth under capitalism.

The Resultate also contains Marx's most explicit statement of the necessity for constant expansion of the capitalist market. Precisely because capitalist production is production through a growing mass of machinery, growing fixed capital, and a growing organic composition of capital, it is necessarily mass production of commodities on an constantly increasing scale, whose realization demands a constantly growing market. This insight, which Mandel notes has been "so often contested by his critics and by some of his followers too," provides the theoretical foundation for understanding the inherent expansionary dynamic of capitalism and the systemic pressures that drive it toward globalization.

Finally, the Resultate includes several "Isolated Fragments" that, while not properly part of the main text, contain significant insights into Marx's views on trade unions, emigration, the centralization of capital in different countries, and the historical process of expropriation. The fragment on trade unions is particularly important, as it presents Marx's most developed analysis of how combinations of workers can intervene in the labour market to prevent wages from falling below the value of labour-power, and how they can achieve increases in real wages by incorporating new needs into the "moral-historical element" of the value of labour-power.

In terms of its place within the broader Marxian theoretical framework, the Resultate occupies a unique position. It is simultaneously a summary of Volume 1's analysis and a preview of themes developed more fully in Volume 2 (the circulation of capital) and Volume 3 (the unity of production and circulation, the formation of prices of production, and the distribution of surplus-value among different fractions of capital). It represents Marx's most sustained attempt to think through the relationship between the immediate process of production and the broader social relations that constitute the capitalist mode of production, and it contains some of his most sophisticated reflections on the nature of social determination and historical materialism.

The Resultate thus stands as a crucial text for understanding Marx's mature theoretical project, providing insights that are not available, or not available in the same form, in the published version of Capital. It shows Marx thinking through the complexities of capitalist production at a level of abstraction that is both more concrete than the analysis of value in Volume 1 and more fundamental than the analysis of circulation in Volume 2. It is, in short, an indispensable text for anyone seeking to understand the full scope and depth of Marx's critique of political economy.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

The Architecture of the Resultate: Historical Context and Editorial Decisions

Before engaging with the substantive arguments of the Resultate, it is essential to understand the text's peculiar history and the editorial decisions that shaped its present form. As Mandel explains in his introduction, the Resultate was written after the completion of the massive 1861-3 manuscript (the twenty-three notebooks that would eventually become the Theories of Surplus-Value and portions of Capital) but before Marx's final revision of Volume 1 for publication. The original plan for Capital had included a Part Seven entitled "Result of the Production Process," which was to follow the analysis of accumulation. However, when Marx introduced a new Part Six on wages (a topic he had originally intended to treat in a separate volume on wage-labour), the original Part Seven was displaced and eventually discarded entirely from the published version.

Mandel notes that it is "impossible to give a definitive answer" as to why Marx abandoned the Resultate, but suggests that the reason may have lain in Marx's "wish to present Capital as a 'dialectically articulated artistic whole.'" The Resultate had a "double didactic function"—as a summary of Volume 1 and as a bridge between Volumes 1 and 2—that may have seemed out of place in the final structure of the work. Whatever the reason, the text remained unpublished during Marx's lifetime and was only recovered and published in the twentieth century.

This historical context is crucial for understanding the Resultate's unique character. Unlike the published version of Capital, which is organized around the logical development of categories (commodity, money, capital, surplus-value, accumulation), the Resultate is organized around a thematic concern: the way in which the capitalist process of production produces and reproduces the specifically capitalist relations of production. This thematic organization allows Marx to develop arguments and make connections that are less prominent, or even absent, in the published version.

The editorial decision to retain the original order of the three sections—with "Commodities as the Product of Capital" first, rather than last as Marx suggested in his later note—is also significant. The French edition, which Mandel follows, retains this order "partly because Marx never undertook the 'final revision' which would justify the change, and partly because the subject-matter as it stands unfolds more naturally and logically in this way." This decision shapes the reader's experience of the text, beginning with the most concrete and immediate result of capitalist production (the commodity) and then moving to the underlying relations that produce it.

Section I: Commodities as the Product of Capital

Marx begins the Resultate with an analysis of the commodity as the product of capital, a starting point that might seem to contradict the opening of Capital itself, where the commodity appears as the "elementary form of bourgeois wealth" and the "prerequisite for the emergence of capital." Marx immediately acknowledges this apparent circularity: "As the elementary form of bourgeois wealth, the commodity was our point of departure, the prerequisite for the emergence of capital. On the other hand, commodities appear now as the product of capital."

This circularity, Marx insists, is not a logical error but a reflection of the actual historical development of capital. "Capital is predicated on the exchange of commodities, trade in commodities, but it may be formed at various stages of production, common to all of which is the fact that capitalist production does not yet exist, or only exists sporadically." At the same time, "a highly developed commodity exchange and the form of the commodity as the universally necessary social form of the product can only emerge as the consequence of the capitalist mode of production."

The dialectical relationship between commodity and capital is thus both historical and logical. Historically, commodity exchange precedes the emergence of capital; capital can only arise on the foundation of a certain level of development of trade and commerce. Logically, however, once capitalist production has become established, the commodity becomes the universal form of the product, and all the conditions of production themselves appear as commodities that leave circulation and enter production.

Marx identifies three crucial points that emerge from this analysis:

First, "Capitalist production is the first to make the commodity into the general form of all produce." Before capitalism, a large part of what was produced did not take the form of commodities; it was produced for direct consumption by the producer or for tribute paid in kind to a lord or state. Only with capitalism does the commodity become the universal elementary form of wealth.

Second, "The production of commodities leads inexorably to capitalist production, once the worker has ceased to be a part of the conditions of production (as in slavery, serfdom), or once primitive common ownership has ceased to be the basis of society (India). In short, from the moment when labour-power in general becomes a commodity." This is a crucial claim about the historical logic of commodity production. Once labour-power itself becomes a commodity—once workers must sell their capacity to work in order to survive—the conditions are established for the emergence of capitalist production.

Third, "Capitalist production destroys the basis of commodity production in so far as the latter involves independent individual production and the exchange of commodities between owners or the exchange of equivalents. The formal exchange of capital and labour-power becomes general." This is a dialectical claim of considerable importance. Commodity production, in its "pure" form, involves the exchange of equivalents between independent producers who own their own means of production. But capitalist production, while universalizing the commodity form, simultaneously destroys the material basis of this independence: the worker no longer owns the means of production and therefore cannot engage in the exchange of equivalents with other commodity owners. The exchange of capital and labour-power, which appears as an exchange of equivalents (wages for labour), is in fact the mechanism through which the worker's independence is destroyed and the capitalist's control is established.

Having established these general points, Marx proceeds to a detailed analysis of how the commodity, as the product of capital, differs from the commodity regarded as an autonomous article. This analysis involves several distinct but interrelated arguments.

The first difference concerns the nature of the labour objectified in the commodity. In the commodity regarded by itself, it "remains quite undecided (and is in fact a matter of indifference) from whom this objectified labour is derived." In the commodity as the product of capital, however, we can distinguish between "paid and unpaid labour." Marx is careful to note that this is "not strictly true since the labour itself is not bought or sold directly." What is bought and sold is labour-power, the capacity to work. But "the commodity contains a specific overall quantity of objectified labour. A portion of this objectified labour (aside from constant capital for which an equivalent has been paid) is exchanged for the equivalent of the worker's wages; another portion is appropriated by the capitalist without any equivalent being paid." It is "a convenient abbreviation" to describe the one as paid and the other as unpaid labour.

The second difference concerns the relationship between the individual commodity and the total product of capital. The individual commodity "does not only appear materially as a part of the total produce of capital, but as an aliquot part of the total produced by it." We are "no longer concerned with the individual autonomous commodity, the single product. The result of the process is not individual goods, but a mass of commodities in which the value of the capital invested together with the surplus-value—i.e. the surplus labour appropriated—has reproduced itself, and each one of which is the incarnation of both the value of the capital and the surplus-value it has produced."

This has important implications for the determination of price. The labour expended on each commodity "can no longer be calculated—except as an average, i.e. an ideal estimate." The calculation of the value of the individual commodity begins with "that portion of the constant capital which only enters into the value of the total product in so far as it is used up," continues with "the conditions of production that are consumed communally," and ends with "the direct social contribution of many co-operating individuals whose labour is averaged out." When determining the price of an individual article, it appears "as a merely ideal fraction of the total product in which the capital reproduces itself."

Marx illustrates this with a detailed numerical example. Let us assume that the total product equals 1,200 ells of linen. The capital invested equals £100, of which £80 is constant capital and £20 variable. The rate of surplus-value is 100 per cent, so that the worker labours for half the working day for himself and the other half gratis for the capitalist. The surplus-value produced equals £20, and the total value of the 1,200 ells equals £120, of which £80 represents the constant capital invested and £40 comes from the additional living labour.

How are we to determine the value of the individual commodity, in this case the ell of linen? "Obviously, by dividing the total price of the aggregate product by the number of units as divided into aliquot parts in accordance with a given measure." The total price is divided by the number of measured units in which the use-value is expressed: £120 / 1,200 = 2 shillings per ell of linen.

Marx then explores how this price changes with variations in productivity. If the linen weaver's productivity is increased fourfold, the constant capital set in motion will increase proportionally (from £80 to £320 worth of flax, etc.), and the number of ells produced will also increase proportionally (from 1,200 to 4,800). The additional weavers' labour, however, would still be expressed as £40, since its quantity remains unaltered. Thus the total price of the 4,800 ells is now £360, and the price of each ell is 1 shilling 6 pence. The price of each ell has fallen by one-quarter because "the constant capital in each ell had absorbed one-quarter less additional living labour in the process of converting that labour into linen."

Marx then presents an alternative example where the total money invested remains the same while the productivity of labour produces very different amounts of the same use-value, for example wheat, merely as the result of varying natural conditions. He assumes that £7 represents the amount of work spent on an acre of land. Of this sum £4 is additional labour and £3 is labour already objectified in constant capital. Of this £4, let £2 be employed for wages and £2 remains as surplus labour, at the rate of 100 per cent. The crop, however, varies with the seasons:

However, this same labour results in very different quantities of wheat, and the individual quarter of wheat, therefore, has very different prices.

Marx draws an important conclusion from this analysis: "This variation in the prices of the individual commodities produced with the same capital has absolutely no effect on the rate of surplus-value, or the proportion of surplus-value to variable capital, or the way in which the working day is divided into paid and unpaid labour." The total value in which the additional labour is expressed remains unchanged because the same amount of living labour is added to the constant capital. The relation of surplus-value to wages, or of paid to unpaid labour, remains the same, irrespective of whether the ell costs 2s. or, with the growing productivity of labour, 1½s.

This leads Marx to formulate two general laws:

(1) "Where commodity prices vary the rate and quantity of surplus-value can remain constant."

(2) "Where commodity prices are constant the rate and quantity of surplus-value can vary."

These laws apply "generally to all commodities including those that do not enter directly or indirectly into the reproduction of labour-power and whose price therefore, whether high or low, is irrelevant to the determination of the value of labour-power itself."

Marx illustrates the second law with a detailed example. Let us assume that the working day remains the same, i.e. 10 hours, but that the necessary labour is reduced from 5 hours to 4 because of increased productivity not in the constant capital employed in weaving, or in the weaving itself, but in other branches of industry whose products form part of the working wage. This means that the workers now work 6 hours for the capitalist, instead of 5, and 4 for themselves instead of 5. The ratio of surplus labour to necessary had been 5:5, i.e. 100 per cent. It is now 6:4, i.e. 150 per cent.

Now as previously, twenty men are employed for 10 hours, which gives 200 hours. In both cases they set the same constant capital of £80 in motion. The value of the total product remains £120, the number of ells still stands at 1,200, and the price of each ell is still 2s. But whereas formerly it took 5 hours per day, i.e. £20 per week to buy his means of subsistence, it now takes only 4 hours, = £16 per week, to buy the same amount. The payment of the twenty who now perform only 16 hours of necessary labour is now £16 as against £20 previously. The variable capital has fallen from £20 to £16, but still sets the same amount of absolute labour in motion.

The Dialectic of Price and Value in the Product of Capital

Marx then turns to a crucial theoretical point: the distinction between the commodity as an autonomous article and the commodity as a depository of capital. He argues that "even if the commodity is sold at its price, the value of the capital invested in its production may not be realized, and the surplus-value created by that capital even less so." This is because the individual commodity, as the product of capital, is not just a thing with a certain value but "an aliquot part of the total product of that capital."

Marx illustrates this with a continuation of his previous example. A capital of £100 reproduced itself in the form of 1,200 ells of linen at a price of £120. The £80 constant capital is embodied in 800 ells, or ⅔ of the aggregate product; £20 variable capital or wages amount to 200 ells, or ⅙ of the total; and £20 surplus-value is likewise the equivalent of 200 ells or ⅙. If we now suppose that not one ell, but let us say 800 ells were sold at the right price, i.e. £80, and the other two portions were unsaleable, then only ⅔ of the original capital value of £100 would have been reproduced. As the depository of the total capital, the 800 ells would have been sold at less than their value, at ⅔ of their value, to be precise, since the value of the whole product = 120 and 80 is but ⅔ of that, the missing 40 being the remaining third.

Marx emphasizes that "what is at issue here is not just that the commodity's sale price should reflect its value, as in the case of the commodity conceived as an autonomous thing, but that, as a depository of the capital invested in it, its sale price should also reflect the fact that it is an aliquot part of the total product of that capital." If only 800 ells are sold out of a total product of 1,200 = £120, then these 800 ells do not represent ⅔ of the total value, but the total value itself, i.e. they represent the value of £120 and not £80, and the individual commodity does not equal £80/800 = 8/80 = 4/40 = 2/20 = 2s., but £120/800 = 12/80 = 3/20 = 3s. Thus as an individual product it would have been sold 50 per cent too dear, if it had been sold at 3s. instead of 2s.

This analysis has important implications for understanding the necessity of market expansion under capitalism. Marx notes: "It follows from this that, with the development of capitalist production and the resultant reduction in prices, there must be an increase in the quantity of goods, in the number of articles that must be sold. That is to say, a constant expansion of the market becomes a necessity for capitalist production." This point, which Mandel identifies as one of the most important in the Resultate, will be developed further in Volume 2 and Volume 3.

The Proudhonian Confusion and Its Resolution

Marx then engages in an extended polemic against Proudhon, using the analysis developed above to resolve what Proudhon had presented as an insoluble economic paradox. Proudhon had argued: "It is impossible that the interest on capital, adding itself in commerce to the worker's wages to make up the price of commodities, should enable the worker to buy back what he has himself produced. To live by working is a principle which, under the regimen of interest, entails a contradiction."

Marx acknowledges that "this is quite right" as far as appearances go. If the worker receives only his wages, and if the price of commodities includes both wages and surplus-value, then it seems impossible for the worker to buy back the full product of his labour. But Marx argues that Proudhon's error lies in considering the commodity in isolation rather than as the product of capital. "If, instead of considering the commodity in isolation, he were to view it as the product of capital, he would discover that the week's product breaks down into one part whose price = the weekly wage, = the variable capital laid out during the week and containing no surplus-value, etc., and another part whose price consists entirely of surplus-value. And even though the price of the commodity includes all these elements, it is in fact only the first part that the worker buys back."

Marx's solution to Proudhon's paradox is thus that the working class, taken as a whole, buys back only that portion of the total product whose value is equivalent to the wages it has received. The rest of the product—the portion that represents surplus-value—is appropriated by the capitalist class and consumed by them or reinvested. The apparent contradiction arises only when we consider the individual commodity in isolation rather than as an aliquot part of the total product of capital.

Marx notes that Proudhon's "apparently profound and insoluble economic paradoxes" consist in the fact that "he regards the confusion wrought by economic phenomena in his own mind as the laws governing those phenomena." The vulgar economists who criticized Proudhon—Marx mentions Forcade—fared no better, since they merely pointed out that the problem was even more intractable than Proudhon had implied (since the price of commodities includes not just wages + interest but also the cost of raw materials) without offering any real solution.

Marx also notes that Proudhon's formulation is misleading in another way: it assumes "that the true price of the commodity = the wages contained in it = the amount of paid labour contained in it, while the surplus-value, interest, etc. is no more than a surcharge, an arbitrary extra on top of the true price of the commodity." This is precisely the opposite of Marx's analysis, which shows that surplus-value is not a surcharge added to the value of the commodity but a portion of the value created by labour that is appropriated by capital without equivalent.

The Formal Subsumption of Labour under Capital

Having completed his analysis of the commodity as the product of capital, Marx turns to the second major theme of the Resultate: capitalist production as the production of surplus-value. He begins with the concept of formal subsumption, which he defines as follows: "The labour process becomes the instrument of the valorization process, the process of the self-valorization of capital—the manufacture of surplus-value. The labour process is subsumed under capital (it is its own process) and the capitalist intervenes in the process as its director, manager. For him it also represents the direct exploitation of the labour of others. It is this that I refer to as the formal subsumption of labour under capital."

Marx emphasizes that formal subsumption "is the general form of every capitalist process of production; at the same time, however, it can be found as a particular form alongside the specifically capitalist mode of production in its developed form, because although the latter entails the former, the converse does not necessarily obtain."

The essential characteristics of formal subsumption are:

First, "The pure money relationship between the man who appropriates the surplus labour and the man who yields it up: subordination in this case arises from the specific content of the sale—there is not a subordination underlying it in which the producer stands in a relation to the exploiter of his labour which is determined not just by money (the relationship of one commodity owner to another), but, let us say, by political constraints."

Second, "his objective conditions of labour (the means of production) and the subjective conditions of labour (the means of subsistence) confront him as capital, as the monopoly of the buyer of his labour-power. The more completely these conditions of labour are mobilized against him as alien property, the more effectively the formal relationship between capital and wage-labour is established, i.e. the more effectively the formal subsumption of labour under capital is accomplished, and this in turn is the premiss and precondition of its real subsumption."

Marx is careful to note that under formal subsumption, "There is no change as yet in the mode of production itself. Technologically speaking, the labour process goes on as before, with the proviso that it is now subordinated to capital." However, two developments emerge within the production process:

(1) "an economic relationship of supremacy and subordination, since the consumption of labour-power by the capitalist is naturally supervised and directed by him";

(2) "labour becomes far more continuous and intensive, and the conditions of labour are employed far more economically, since every effort is made to ensure that no more (or rather even less) socially necessary time is consumed in making the product—and this applies both to the living labour that is used to manufacture it and to the objectified labour which enters into it as an element in the means of production."

Marx also notes that under formal subsumption, "the compulsion to perform surplus labour, and to create the leisure time necessary for development independently of material production, differs only in form from what had obtained under the earlier mode of production." However, "this formal change is one which increases the continuity and intensity of labour; it is more favourable to the development of versatility among the workers, and hence to increasing diversity in modes of working and ways of earning a living. Lastly, it dissolves the relationship between the owners of the conditions of labour and the workers into a relationship of sale and purchase, a purely financial relationship. In consequence the process of exploitation is stripped of every patriarchal, political or even religious cloak."

Marx illustrates the historical transition to formal subsumption with examples: "When a peasant who has always produced enough for his needs becomes a day labourer working for a farmer; when the hierarchic order of guild production vanishes making way for the straightforward distinction between the capitalist and the wage-labourers he employs; when the former slave-owner engages his former slaves as paid workers, etc., then we find that what is happening is that production processes of varying social provenance have been transformed into capitalist production."

The mystification inherent in the capital-relation emerges at this point. "The value-sustaining power of labour appears as the self-supporting power of capital; the value-creating power of labour as the self-valorizing power of capital and, in general, in accordance with its concept, living labour appears to be put to work by objectified labour."

Marx also discusses the historical forms that precede formal subsumption: usurer's capital and merchant's capital. In India, for example, "the capital of the usurer advances raw materials or tools or even both to the immediate producer in the form of money. The exorbitant interest which it attracts... is just another name for surplus-value. It transforms its money into capital by extorting unpaid labour, surplus labour, from the immediate producer. But it does not intervene in the process of production itself, which proceeds in its traditional fashion, as it always had done." Similarly, merchant's capital "commissions a number of immediate producers, then collects their produce and sells it, perhaps making them advances in the form of raw materials, etc., or even money." In neither case do we find formal subsumption of labour under capital, since the immediate producer still performs the functions of selling his wares and making use of his own labour.

The Real Subsumption of Labour under Capital

The real subsumption of labour under capital represents a qualitative transformation of the production process itself. Marx defines it as follows: "The real subsumption of labour under capital is developed in all the forms evolved by relative, as opposed to absolute surplus-value. With the real subsumption of labour under capital a complete (and constantly repeated) revolution takes place in the mode of production, in the productivity of the workers and in the relations between workers and capitalists."

The real subsumption of labour under capital involves:

First, "The social productive forces of labour, or the productive forces of directly social, socialized (i.e. collective) labour come into being through co-operation, division of labour within the workshop, the use of machinery, and in general the transformation of production by the conscious use of the sciences, of mechanics, chemistry, etc. for specific ends, technology, etc. and similarly, through the enormous increase of scale corresponding to such developments."

Second, "This entire development of the productive forces of socialized labour (in contrast to the more or less isolated labour of individuals), and together with it the use of science (the general product of social development), in the immediate process of production, takes the form of the productive power of capital. It does not appear as the productive power of labour, or even of that part of it that is identical with capital. And least of all does it appear as the productive power either of the individual worker or of the workers joined together in the process of production."

Third, "With the real subsumption of labour under capital, all the changes in the labour process already discussed now become reality. The social forces of production of labour are now developed, and with large-scale production comes the direct application of science and technology. On the one hand, capitalist production now establishes itself as a mode of production sui generis and brings into being a new mode of material production. On the other hand, the latter itself forms the basis for the development of capitalist relations whose adequate form, therefore, presupposes a definite stage in the evolution of the productive forces of labour."

Marx emphasizes the historical significance of real subsumption: "It has already been noted that a definite and constantly growing minimum amount of capital is both the necessary precondition and the constant result of the specifically capitalist mode of production. The capitalist must be the owner or proprietor of the means of production on a social scale and in quantities that beggar comparison with the possible production of the individual and his family."

The real subsumption of labour under capital also has a negative side: "production in contradiction, and indifference, to the producer. The real producer as a mere means of production, material wealth as an end in itself. And so the growth of this material wealth is brought about in contradiction to and at the expense of the individual human being."

Productive and Unproductive Labour

Marx's discussion of productive and unproductive labour is one of the most important sections of the Resultate. He begins by defining productive labour from the standpoint of capitalist production: "Since the immediate purpose and the authentic product of capitalist production is surplus-value, labour is only productive, and an exponent of labour-power is only a productive worker, if it or he creates surplus-value directly, i.e. the only productive labour is that which is directly consumed in the course of production for the valorization of capital."

Marx distinguishes this from the definition of productive labour from the standpoint of the labour process: "Looked at from the simple standpoint of the labour process, labour seemed productive if it realized itself in a product, or rather a commodity. From the standpoint of capitalist production we may add the qualification that labour is productive if it directly valorizes capital, or creates surplus-value."

Marx emphasizes that "Only the labour which posits the variable capital and hence the total capital as C+ΔC = C+Δv is productive. It is therefore labour which directly serves capital as the agency of its self-valorization, as means for the production of surplus-value."

This leads Marx to a crucial distinction: "Every productive worker is a wage-labourer, but not every wage-labourer is a productive worker. Whenever labour is purchased to be consumed as a use-value, as a service and not to replace the value of variable capital with its own vitality and be incorporated into the capitalist process of production—whenever that happens, labour is not productive and the wage-labourer is no productive worker. His work is consumed for its use-value, not as creating exchange-value; it is consumed unproductively, not productively."

Marx illustrates this with examples: "Milton, who wrote Paradise Lost, was an unproductive worker. On the other hand, a writer who turns out work for his publisher in factory style is a productive worker... A singer who sings like a bird is an unproductive worker. If she sells her song for money, she is to that extent a wage-labourer or merchant. But if the same singer is engaged by an entrepreneur who makes her sing to make money, then she becomes a productive worker, since she produces capital directly. A schoolmaster who instructs others is not a productive worker. But a schoolmaster who works for wages in an institution along with others, using his own labour to increase the money of the entrepreneur who owns the knowledge-mongering institution, is a productive worker."

Marx also emphasizes the development of the "collective worker" with the development of the real subsumption of labour under capital: "with the development of the real subsumption of labour under capital, or the specifically capitalist mode of production, the real lever of the overall labour process is increasingly not the individual worker. Instead, labour-power socially combined and the various competing labour-powers which together form the entire production machine participate in very different ways in the immediate process of making commodities... Some work better with their hands, others with their heads, one as a manager, engineer, technologist, etc., the other as overseer, the third as manual labourer or even drudge."

Marx draws two important conclusions:

First, "An ever increasing number of types of labour are included in the immediate concept of productive labour, and those who perform it are classed as productive workers, workers directly exploited by capital and subordinated to its process of production and expansion."

Second, "the activity of this aggregate labour-power is its immediate productive consumption by capital, i.e. it is the self-valorization process of capital, and hence... the immediate production of surplus-value, the immediate conversion of this latter into capital."

Net and Gross Product

Marx's discussion of net and gross product is relatively brief but important. He argues: "Since the purpose of capitalist production (and hence of productive labour) is not the existence of the producer, but the production of surplus-value, it follows that all necessary labour which does not produce surplus labour is superfluous and worthless to capitalist production. The same holds good for a nation of capitalists. All produit brut [gross produce] which merely reproduces the worker, i.e. which creates no produit net [surplus produce] is just as superfluous as the worker himself."

Marx quotes Ricardo with approval: "'Provided its net real income, its rents and profits be the same, it is of no importance whether the nation consists of ten or of twelve millions of inhabitants... If five millions of men could produce as much food and clothing as was necessary for ten millions, food and clothing for five millions would be the net revenue.'"

Marx comments: "Even the philanthropists can have no objection to bring forward against this statement of Ricardo's. For it is always better that of ten million people only 50 per cent should vegetate as pure production-machines for five millions, than that of twelve millions, seven or 58⅓ per cent should vegetate as such."

Marx also notes that this view "conflicts with, for example, the antiquated view typical of earlier modes of production according to which the city authorities would, for instance, prohibit inventions so as not to deprive workers of their livelihood. In such a society the worker was an end in himself and appropriate employment was his privilege, a right which the entire order was concerned to maintain. It conflicts, moreover, with the idea of the protectionist system (as opposed to free trade)... Finally, it conflicts with the view of Adam Smith that, for example, the investment of capital in agriculture is 'more productive' because the same capital provides work for more hands."

The Mystification of Capital

Marx's discussion of the mystification of capital is one of the most philosophically rich sections of the Resultate. He argues that "Since—within the process of production—living labour has already been absorbed into capital, all the social productive forces of labour appear as the productive forces of capital, as intrinsic attributes of capital, just as in the case of money, the creative power of labour had seemed to possess the qualities of a thing."

Marx identifies three reasons why this mystification is even more pronounced for capital than for money:

(1) "although labour is an expression of labour-power, although it represents the effort of the individual worker, and so belongs to him (it is the substance with which he pays the capitalist for what he receives from him), it nevertheless objectifies itself in the product and so belongs to the capitalist. - Even worse, the social configuration in which the individual workers exist, and within which they function only as the particular organs of the total labour-power that makes up the workshop as a whole, does not belong to them either. On the contrary, it confronts them as a capitalist arrangement that is imposed on them";

(2) "these social productive forces of labour, or productive forces of social labour, came into being historically only with the advent of the specifically capitalist mode of production. That is to say, they appeared as something intrinsic to the relations of capitalism and inseparable from them";

(3) "with the development of the capitalist mode of production the objective conditions of labour take on a different form owing to the scale on which, and the economy with which, they are employed... As they develop they become increasingly concentrated; they represent social wealth and, to put the matter in a nutshell, their scope and their effect is that of the conditions of production of labour socially combined. And quite apart from the combination of labour, the social character of the conditions of labour—and this includes machinery and capital fixe of every kind—appears to be entirely autonomous and independent of the worker."

Marx also notes that profits can rise as a result of the economical use of collective conditions of labour: "The fact that the value of the prime mover does not increase at the same rate as its power: economies in the price of raw materials, recycling of waste, reduction in administrative costs, or in storage costs as the result of mass production, etc.—all these relative savings accruing to constant capital and coinciding with the absolute growth in its value are based on the fact that these means of production, i.e. both the means and the materials of labour, are used collectively."

And he adds: "science, which is in fact the general intellectual product of the social process, also appears to be the direct offshoot of capital (since its application to the material process of production takes place in isolation from the knowledge and abilities of the individual worker). And since society is marked by the exploitation of labour by capital, its development appears to be the productive force of capital as opposed to labour."

Marx concludes this section with a powerful summary: "The capitalist himself wields power only inasmuch as he is the personification of capital... The relationship becomes more complicated, however, and apparently more mysterious, with the emergence of the specifically capitalist mode of production. Here we find that it is not only such things—the products of labour, both use-values and exchange-values—that rise up on their hind legs and face the worker and confront him as 'Capital'. But even the social form of labour appears as a form of development of capital, and hence the productive forces of social labour so developed appear as the productive forces of capitalism."

Section III: Capitalist Production as the Production and Reproduction of the Specifically Capitalist Relations of Production

The third major section of the Resultate develops the argument that capitalist production is not merely the production of commodities and surplus-value but the production and reproduction of the specifically capitalist relations of production themselves. Marx begins: "The product of capitalist production is not only surplus-value; it is also capital."

Marx emphasizes the transformative character of the production process: "In the first instance, even after its conversion into the factors of the labour process... the value or money invested is only capital in itself, only potentially... Only when it finds itself within that process, only when living labour is really incorporated into the objectively existent forms of capital, only when additional labour is sucked into the process, only then do we find that this labour is converted into capital."

The result of this process is that "the worker sold the right to control his labour-power in exchange for the necessary means of subsistence... Looking at him, what is the result? Simplement et purement the reproduction of his labour-power... In contrast to this, capital does not emerge from the process as it entered it. It only becomes real capital, value valorizing itself, in the course of the process. It now exists as capital realized in the form of the aggregate product, and as such, as the property of the capitalist, it now confronts labour once more as an autonomous power even though it was created by that very labour."

Marx draws out the implications of this analysis for understanding accumulation: "The surplus-value is changed back into additional capital; it manifests itself as the formation of new capital or of enlarged capital. Hence capital has created capital; it has not just realized itself as capital. The process of accumulation is itself an intrinsic feature of the capitalist process of production. It entails the new creation of wage-labourers, of the means to realize and increase the available amount of capital."

This leads to a crucial insight: "capital not only produces capital, it produces a growing mass of men, the material through which alone it can function as additional capital. Therefore, it is not only true to say that labour produces on a constantly increasing scale the conditions of labour in opposition to itself in the form of capital, but equally, capital produces on a steadily increasing scale the productive wage-labourers it requires. Labour produces the conditions of its production in the form of capital, and capital produces labour, i.e. as wage-labour, as the means towards its own realization as capital."

Marx emphasizes that the reproduction of the capitalist relation is not merely quantitative but qualitative: "This relation is not merely reproduced, it is produced on a steadily more massive scale, so that it creates ever new supplies of workers and encroaches on branches of production previously independent. In addition, as we have seen in our account of the mode of production specific to capitalism, the relation is reproduced in a fashion increasingly favourable to the one side, the capitalists, and increasingly unfavourable to the other side, the wage-labourers."

Marx also emphasizes that the wage relation, which appears as a free exchange between commodity owners, is in fact the mechanism through which the worker's subordination is perpetuated: "If we consider the continuity of the process of production, the labourer's wage is only that part of the product constantly produced by the worker, who converts it into the means of subsistence and hence into the means for the preservation and increase of the labour-power which capital requires to valorize value for itself, i.e. for its own life-process. The maintenance and increase of labour-power appear therefore merely as the reproduction and extension of its own conditions of reproduction and accumulation."

Marx draws a sharp contrast between the appearance and the reality of the capital-labour relationship: "This destroys the last vestiges of the illusion, so typical of the relationship when considered superficially, that in the circulation process, in the market-place, two equally matched commodity owners confront each other, and that they, like all other commodity owners, are distinguishable only by the material content of their goods, by the specific use-value of the goods they desire to sell each other."

Marx identifies two distinct features of the reproduction of the capitalist relation:

First, "with regard to the initial process within the sphere of circulation, the sale and purchase of labour-power... The capitalist process of production is not just the conversion into capital of the value or of the commodity which the capitalist partly puts on the market and partly retains within the labour process. On the contrary, these products transformed into capital are not his products, but the products of the worker. He constantly sells him a portion of his product—the necessities of life for his labour—in order to maintain and increase the labour-power, i.e. the purchaser himself. And he borrows from him in return another portion of his product, the objective conditions of labour, as capital, as the means whereby capital can valorize itself. Thus, while the worker produces his produce as capital, the capitalist reproduces the worker as a wage-labourer and hence as the vendor of his labour."

Second, "For capitalist relations to establish themselves at all presupposes that a certain historical level of social production has been attained... But for the time being they need to be developed only to the point that permits the formal subsumption of labour under capital. On the basis of that change, however, specific changes in the mode of production are introduced which create new forces of production, and these in turn influence the mode of production so that new real conditions come into being. Thus a complete economic revolution is brought about. On the one hand, it creates the real conditions for the domination of labour by capital, perfecting the process and providing it with the appropriate framework. On the other hand, by evolving conditions of production and communication and productive forces of labour antagonistic to the workers involved in them, this revolution creates the real premisses of a new mode of production, one that abolishes the contradictory form of capitalism."

Marx concludes the main text of the Resultate with a powerful summary: "It is not just the objective conditions of the process of production that appear as its result. The same thing is true also of its specific social character. The social relations and therefore the social position of the agents of production in relation to each other, i.e. the relations of production, are themselves produced: they are also the constantly renewed result of the process."

The Isolated Fragments

The Resultate concludes with several "Isolated Fragments" that Marx evidently intended to revise and incorporate into the main text. These fragments contain significant insights on a variety of topics.

The first fragment, on "The Sale of Labour-Power and the Trade Unions," develops Marx's analysis of the peculiar nature of labour-power as a commodity. Marx notes that "the commodity sold only passes into the hands of the purchaser as a use-value after the conclusion of the contract between buyer and seller. Its exchange-value, like that of every other commodity, is determined before it goes into circulation... The exchange-value of this commodity existed, therefore, before its sale, while its use-value consists only in the subsequent expression of its power. That is to say, the alienation of the power and its actual expression, i.e. its existence as a use-value, do not coincide in time."

This temporal disjunction has important implications: "where the formal alienation of the commodity through sale does not coincide in time with the real transfer of its use-value to the purchaser, the buyer's money functions as we have seen in the first instance as a means of payment. The labour-power is sold for a day, a week, etc., but it is paid for only after it has been consumed for a day or a week, etc. In all countries where capitalist relations are in the process of development, labour-power is only paid for after it has been used up. As a rule, then, the worker advances the use of his commodity to the capitalist. He permits its consumption by the buyer, he allows him credit, before he receives its exchange-value in payment."

Marx then turns to the trade unions, arguing that "the value of labour-power constitutes the conscious and explicit foundation of the trade unions, whose importance for the English working class can scarcely be overestimated. The trade unions aim at nothing less than to prevent the reduction of wages below the level that is traditionally maintained in the various branches of industry. That is to say, they wish to prevent the price of labour-power from falling below its value."

Marx quotes Dunning with approval: "there is 'a great distinction between the level of wages as determined by supply and demand, i.e. by the level produced by the fair operation of exchange that exists when buyer and seller negotiate on equal terms, and the level of wages which the seller, the labourer, must put up with when the capitalist negotiates with each man singly, and dictates a reduction by exploiting the chance need of individual workers (which exists independently of the general relations of supply and demand). The workers combine in order to achieve equality of a sort with the capitalist in their contract concerning the sale of their labour. This is the rationale (the logical basis) of the trade unions.'"

Marx also discusses the "sweating" system, in which "A sweater is someone who undertakes to deliver a certain quantity of work at normal prices to an entrepreneur, but who then has it carried out for a lower price by others. The difference, which goes to make up his profit, is sweated out of the workers who actually perform the labour and represents nothing but the difference between the value of the labour-power that is paid by the first entrepreneur and the price which is equivalent to less than the value of that labour-power and which is paid by the sweater to the actual workers."

The second fragment, on "Different Modes of Centralization of the Means of Production in Different Countries," compares the development of capitalist production in England and France. Marx quotes a factory inspector's report showing that "the number of factories in France is three times as large as those in England, while the number of persons employed in them is only ½ greater; but the very different proportions of machinery and moving power will best be shown by the following comparison":

France England
Number of factories 12,986 4,330
Number of persons employed 706,450 596,082
Average number of persons in each factory 54 137
Average number of spindles to each person employed 7 43
Average number of persons to each loom 2 (power and handloom) 2 (powerloom only)

Marx draws the conclusion: "For each worker in an English factory, compared to the Frenchmen, there is far more machinery for working and also far more power-driving machinery (mechanic power), and hence, too, far more raw materials are processed by him in the same time. The productive power of his labour is, therefore, much greater, as is the capital that employs him."

Marx emphasizes that this difference is related to the degree of expropriation of the immediate producers: "It becomes quite plain to see here that because of historical and other circumstances which have had a varying effect upon the relative magnitude of the concentration of the means of production, there is a correspondingly greater or smaller expropriation of the mass of immediate producers... And this stands in inverse ratio to the 'saving' and 'hoarding' of the immediate producer himself, which in France is huge in comparison with England."

Marx concludes: "Capitalist accumulation and concentration are based upon, and correspond to, the facility of appropriating other people's surplus labour in great masses and the corresponding inability of these people themselves to lay any claim to their own surplus labour. It is, therefore, the most ludicrous delusion, fallacy or imposture to explain and account for this capitalist accumulation by confounding it with, and, as far as the phraseology is concerned, converting it into, a process quite its opposite, exclusive of it, and corresponding to a mode of production upon whose ruins alone capitalist production can be reared."

The third fragment, on "Ireland. Emigration," discusses the relationship between emigration and the labour market. Marx argues that "the emigration of capital, i.e. of that part of annual revenue which is invested abroad, particularly in the colonies and the United States of America, is far greater in proportion to the annual accumulation fund than is the number of emigrants in proportion to the annual growth in the population." He notes that "emigration from England consists if we consider its principal part, the agricultural sector, not of working men, but of tenant-farmers' sons, etc. Hitherto it was more than made good by immigration from Ireland."

Marx argues that "If emigration from England were really to swell to serious dimensions in relation to the annual growth in the population, it would spell the end of its dominance of the world market." He also discusses the Irish famine and its aftermath, noting that "Production has declined faster than the population, and so have the means of occupying the agricultural labourers, even though their wages are no higher now, if we take the differences in the price of necessaries into account, than they were in 1847. The population, however, has diminished from 8 million to around 4½ million in 15 years."

The fourth fragment, on "Expropriation and Depopulation in Eastern Germany during the Eighteenth Century," quotes Gustav Freytag on the process of "Bauernlegen" (peasant-clearing): "ever since the landlords had begun to concentrate their efforts on raising the yield of their properties, they found it to their advantage to drive out many of their subjects and to add their fields to their own estates. The people thus expropriated, having no home of their own, became destitute."

The fifth fragment, on "Property and Capital," develops Marx's critique of the confusion between capitalist property and petty-bourgeois property: "Although the formation of capital and the capitalist mode of production are essentially founded not merely on the abolition of feudal production but also on the expropriation of the peasantry, craftsmen and in general of the mode of production based on the private ownership by the immediate producer of his conditions of production; although, once capitalist production has been introduced, it continues to develop at the same rate as that private property and the mode of production based on it is destroyed, so that those immediate producers are expropriated in the name of the concentration of capital (centralization)... both the theory of capitalist production (political economy, philosophy of law, etc.) and the capitalist himself in his own mind is pleased to confuse his mode of property and appropriation, which is based on the expropriation of the immediate producer in its origins, and on the acquisition of the labour of others in its further progress, with its opposite: with a mode of production that presupposes that the immediate producer privately owns his own conditions of production."

The sixth fragment, on "The Colliers," describes the brutal conditions faced by coal miners during a strike in Durham in November 1863: "The people were evicted, wives and children included, in the harshest weather; and their furniture was put into the street... A large number slept in the open; some broke into their evacuated dwellings and occupied them during the night. The next day the mine-owners had the doors and windows barred and nailed up, to deprive the evicted people of the luxury of sleeping through ice-cold nights on the bare floors of the empty cottages."

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles Revealed

The Resultate reveals several fundamental principles that are essential for understanding Marx's mature analysis of capitalism:

First, the dialectical unity of production and social relations. Perhaps the most important lesson of the Resultate is that capitalist production cannot be understood merely as a technical process of transforming inputs into outputs, nor merely as an economic process of generating profit. Rather, it must be understood as a social process that simultaneously produces material wealth and reproduces the specific social relations—the relations of production—that define capitalism as a historical mode of production. "The social relations and therefore the social position of the agents of production in relation to each other, i.e. the relations of production, are themselves produced: they are also the constantly renewed result of the process." This insight has profound implications for understanding both the stability and the dynamics of capitalism: the system reproduces itself not through external coercion but through the normal functioning of its economic processes.

Second, the historical specificity of capitalist categories. The Resultate demonstrates that economic categories that appear to be natural and eternal—the commodity, money, capital, productive labour—are in fact historically specific forms that emerge only under particular conditions. The commodity becomes the universal form of the product only with the development of capitalist production; capital is not a thing but a social relation that appears in the form of things; productive labour is not merely labour that produces useful things but labour that is directly exchanged for capital and produces surplus-value. This historicization of economic categories is essential for understanding capitalism as a historically transient mode of production rather than a natural and permanent feature of human society.

Third, the centrality of subsumption to understanding capitalist development. The distinction between formal and real subsumption provides a powerful analytical framework for understanding the historical development of capitalism. Formal subsumption characterizes the initial stage of capitalist development, when capital takes over pre-existing labour processes without fundamentally transforming them; real subsumption characterizes the mature stage, when capital has revolutionized the production process through machinery, science, and technology. This distinction helps explain why capitalism first emerges in certain branches of production and certain geographical locations, how it spreads from one sphere to another, and why it tends toward constant revolutionization of the forces of production.

Fourth, the mystification inherent in capitalist relations. The Resultate provides one of Marx's most sustained analyses of the mystification and fetishism that characterize capitalist society. The social productive forces of labour—the productivity that arises from co-operation, division of labour, and the application of science—appear as the productive forces of capital. The value-creating power of labour appears as the self-valorizing power of capital. The domination of capital over labour appears as the natural order of things. This mystification is not merely an ideological illusion but is rooted in the actual functioning of the capitalist process of production: "the entire process is progressively intensified as their labour-power is itself modified by these forms to such an extent that it is rendered impotent even when it exists autonomously."

Fifth, the necessary expansion of the capitalist market. The Resultate contains Marx's most explicit statement of the necessity for constant expansion of the capitalist market. Precisely because capitalist production is production through a growing mass of machinery, growing fixed capital, and a growing organic composition of capital, it is necessarily mass production of commodities on an constantly increasing scale, whose realization demands a constantly growing market. This insight, which Mandel notes has been "so often contested by his critics and by some of his followers too," provides the theoretical foundation for understanding the inherent expansionary dynamic of capitalism and the systemic pressures that drive it toward globalization.

Sixth, the historical mission of capitalism. Despite its exploitative and contradictory character, capitalism has a historical mission: to develop the productive forces of social labour to a level that makes possible a new form of society. "by evolving conditions of production and communication and productive forces of labour antagonistic to the workers involved in them, this revolution creates the real premisses of a new mode of production, one that abolishes the contradictory form of capitalism. It thereby creates the material basis of a newly shaped social process and hence of a new social formation." This dialectical understanding of capitalism's historical role—as both an exploitative system and a necessary stage in human development—is essential for understanding Marx's critique of political economy as a whole.

Broader Practical and Theoretical Implications

The Resultate has profound implications for a wide range of theoretical and practical questions:

For the theory of value. The Resultate demonstrates that the labour theory of value is not merely a theory about the determination of prices but a theory about the social relations that underlie the production and distribution of wealth under capitalism. The value of a commodity is not merely a technical magnitude but a social relation—the relation between the labour time socially necessary for its production and the labour time embodied in other commodities. The Resultate shows how this social relation is mediated by the capitalist process of production, in which the commodity appears not as an autonomous article but as an aliquot part of the total product of capital.

For the theory of exploitation. The Resultate provides a rigorous demonstration of how exploitation is not an incidental feature of capitalism but is essential to its functioning. The extraction of surplus-value is not a matter of individual capitalists' greed or cruelty but is the necessary condition for the existence of capital as a social relation. "Without the exchange of variable capital for labour-power the total capital could not valorize itself and so the formation of capital and the transformation of the means of production and means of subsistence into capital could not take place." Exploitation is thus not a moral failing of capitalism but a structural necessity of the system.

For the theory of the state. Although the Resultate does not directly address the theory of the state, its analysis of the reproduction of capitalist relations has important implications for understanding the role of the state in maintaining and reproducing capitalism. If the capitalist relations of production are reproduced through the normal functioning of the economic process, then the state's role is not primarily to enforce these relations through coercion but to maintain the conditions under which they can reproduce themselves. This includes protecting property rights, enforcing contracts, maintaining the conditions for the accumulation of capital, and managing the contradictions and crises that arise from the capitalist process.

For the theory of revolution. The Resultate provides the theoretical basis for understanding both the necessity and the possibility of revolutionary transformation. The necessity arises from the contradictions of capitalism—the conflict between the development of the productive forces and the capitalist relations of production that fetter them. The possibility arises from the development of the collective worker, whose socialization and concentration create the material basis for a new form of social organization. "The social forms of their own labour—both subjectively and objectively—or, in other words, the forms of their own social labour, are utterly independent of the individual workers... And this entire process is progressively intensified as their labour-power is itself modified by these forms to such an extent that it is rendered impotent even when it exists autonomously."

For the theory of ideology. The Resultate provides important insights into the nature of ideology under capitalism. The mystification that characterizes the capitalist process of production—the appearance of social relations as properties of things—is not merely an illusion that can be dispelled by correct analysis but is rooted in the actual functioning of the capitalist economy. The ideology of the bourgeoisie, which presents capitalism as the natural and eternal form of production, is not merely a conscious deception but a reflection of the way things actually appear under capitalism. This understanding of ideology as rooted in material relations, rather than merely in ideas, is essential for understanding how capitalist societies maintain their legitimacy and reproduce their social relations.

Conclusion

The Resultate stands as one of Marx's most significant achievements, providing a comprehensive synthesis of his analysis of the capitalist process of production and its role in reproducing the specifically capitalist relations of production. Its theoretical innovations—the distinction between formal and real subsumption, the concept of the collective worker, the analysis of the reproduction of the capital-labour relationship—continue to inform contemporary Marxist scholarship and provide essential tools for understanding the dynamics of capitalism in the twenty-first century.

The text's emphasis on the production and reproduction of social relations, rather than merely on the production of material goods, anticipates many of the themes that would be developed in twentieth-century Marxist theory, from Gramsci's concept of hegemony to Althusser's analysis of ideological state apparatuses to contemporary work on the social reproduction of capitalism. Its demonstration of the necessary expansion of the capitalist market anticipates the globalization of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. Its analysis of the mystification inherent in capitalist relations anticipates contemporary work on fetishism, ideology, and the cultural politics of capitalism.

At the same time, the Resultate remains a text that is deeply rooted in its historical moment, written in the context of the mid-nineteenth century industrial capitalism of Western Europe and responding to the theoretical and political debates of that period. Its insights must be critically appropriated and developed in light of the transformations of capitalism since Marx's time—the development of monopoly capitalism, imperialism, welfare state capitalism, neoliberalism, and contemporary forms of globalized, financialized capitalism.

But the fundamental insight of the Resultate—that capitalist production is not merely the production of things but the production and reproduction of social relations—remains as relevant as ever. In an era when capitalist relations have penetrated every corner of the globe and every aspect of social life, when the production of surplus-value has become the organizing principle of human society, and when the contradictions of capitalism threaten the very conditions of human survival, Marx's analysis of how these relations are produced and reproduced remains an indispensable tool for understanding and transforming the world in which we live.
