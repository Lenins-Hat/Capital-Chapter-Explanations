Volume 1 • Chapter 12: The Concept of Relative Surplus-Value

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 12 opens Part Four of Capital, Volume One, and it is a hinge chapter in the strict architectural sense: it does not merely add a new category to the theory of surplus-value, it re-founds the theory on a new terrain. Everything preceding it — the analysis of the commodity and money, the transformation of money into capital, the labour process, the production of surplus-value, and the long treatment of the working day — had operated with one technique of exploitation: the prolongation of the working day beyond the point at which the worker reproduces the value of his own labour-power. Chapter 12 announces that this technique, now retrospectively christened absolute surplus-value, is only the first and cruder of capital's two fundamental modes of extracting surplus labour. The second, relative surplus-value, is the subject of Part Four, and this chapter supplies its concept — its formal definition, its necessary presuppositions, its scope conditions, and the precise mechanism by which it is generated.

The central problem is posed with quasi-geometric precision. Given a working day of fixed length (line AC, twelve hours) whose internal division is also fixed (AB = ten hours of necessary labour, BC = two hours of surplus labour), how can surplus labour be prolonged without any extension of AC? Marx's answer is that the surplus segment can grow only by pushing back its own starting point B toward A — that is, by curtailing necessary labour-time. But since the value of labour-power is determined by the labour-time required to reproduce it, a fall in that value is impossible without a rise in the productivity of labour, and a rise in productivity is impossible without a revolution in the mode of production itself (tools, methods of working, technical and social conditions of the labour process). Hence the chapter's thesis: relative surplus-value arises from the curtailment of necessary labour-time consequent upon a revolution in the productive forces, occurring within a working day of unchanged length. The chapter's definition is thus: "I call that surplus-value which arises from the curtailment of the necessary labour-time, and from the corresponding alteration in the respective lengths of the two components of the working day, relative surplus-value."

The intellectual objective of the chapter is threefold. First, to derive rather than assert the category, by showing that no other route to increased surplus labour is available under the assumption of equivalent exchange. Second, to specify the scope condition under which the mechanism operates at the level of the total social capital: the productivity revolution must "seize upon those branches of industry whose products determine the value of labour-power" — the wage-good industries and the industries supplying their means of production — and the resulting gain in the general rate of surplus-value is proportional to the weight of each commodity in the worker's necessary consumption. Third, and most subtly, to reconcile the individual capitalist's behaviour with the class interest of capital, by showing that an innovating capitalist captures a transient extra surplus-value (the difference between the individual value of his cheapened commodity and its social value) quite independently of whether his commodity enters the worker's subsistence — thereby providing a universal, structural motive for raising productivity, over and above the collective cheapening of labour-power.

Its place in the broader framework: Part Four (Chapters 12–15) moves from concept (12) to the concrete historical methods by which relative surplus-value is produced — co-operation (13), the division of labour and manufacture (14), and machinery and large-scale industry (15). Chapter 12 is therefore the conceptual gateway to capital's specifically modern form of domination, in which capital no longer merely "takes over the labour process in its given or historically transmitted shape" and lengthens it, but actively transforms the labour process. It also plants the seed of two later theoretical structures: the theory of competition and market value (the chapter's treatment of individual vs. social value and the coercive law of competition), and the law of the tendential fall in the value of commodities alongside rising surplus-value — the apparent paradox Marx resolves through Quesnay's riddle.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Formal Problem: Setting Up the Working-Day Diagram

Marx begins by retrospectively characterizing the terrain of Chapters 7–11. The portion of the working day that "merely produces an equivalent for the value paid by the capitalist for his labour-power" — necessary labour-time — has until now been treated "as a constant magnitude." Marx immediately qualifies this: it is constant "under given conditions of production and at a given stage in the economic development of society." The constancy is therefore relative to a historical stage, not absolute. This qualification is essential because the whole chapter will consist in showing what happens when the condition is lifted.

The recap of the prior results is precise: the worker could work 2, 3, 4, 6, or more hours beyond his necessary labour-time; both the rate of surplus-value (surplus labour ÷ necessary labour) and the length of the working day depended on how far this extra time was prolonged. So while necessary labour-time was constant, "the total working day was variable." The variable element in the earlier analysis was the length of the day; the constant element was its floor.

Marx then imposes a new set of givens: a working day whose length and whose division between necessary and surplus labour are both fixed. He renders this in a line diagram:

A----------B--C

where the whole line AC = a 12-hour working day, AB = 10 hours of necessary labour, BC = 2 hours of surplus labour. The question that drives the entire chapter: "How can the production of surplus-value be increased, i.e. how can surplus labour be prolonged, without any prolongation, or independently of any prolongation, of the line AC?"

The geometric solution is to move the internal partition point rather than the endpoints:

A---------B' -B--C

If B'B = half of BC = 1 hour, then moving B to B' gives B'C in place of BC: surplus labour rises by half, from 2 to 3 hours, while the working day remains 12 hours. Marx immediately draws the decisive structural consequence: this extension of surplus labour from BC to B'C is "evidently impossible without a simultaneous contraction of the necessary labour-time from AB to AB', from 10 hours to 9 hours." The prolongation of surplus labour corresponds to a shortening of necessary labour; a portion of the labour-time previously consumed "in reality, for the worker's own benefit would be converted into labour-time expended for the capitalist." The alteration, then, is "not in the length of the working day, but in its division."

This is the formal skeleton of relative surplus-value: a purely internal redistribution of a constant day, achieved by moving the boundary between the two parts.

2.2 The First Route and Its Exclusion: Cutting Wages Below the Value of Labour-Power

Marx now asks whether the surplus could be enlarged by another route — and here the chapter performs one of its most important methodological exclusions. The duration of surplus labour is given when the length of the working day and the value of labour-power are given; the value of labour-power "i.e. the labour-time necessary to produce labour-power, determines the labour-time necessary for the reproduction of the value of labour-power."

He works an example that also functions as an illustration of the classical subsistence-wage doctrine, backed by a chain of citations in footnote 1: 1 hour of work = 6d (sixpence); the daily value of labour-power = 5s (shillings). Hence the worker must work 10 hours a day to replace the value paid by capital for his labour-power, i.e. to produce an equivalent for the value of the means of subsistence he consumes daily. Given the value of those means of subsistence → the value of labour-power → the duration of necessary labour-time. Surplus labour is then the residual: 12 − 10 = 2 hours. "It is not easy to see how, under the given conditions, the surplus labour can possibly be prolonged beyond 2 hours."

But, Marx concedes, the capitalist could pay 4s. 6d. instead of 5s. — or even less. Nine hours' labour-time would suffice to reproduce 4s. 6d., so 3 hours of surplus labour would accrue and surplus-value would rise from 1s. to 1s. 6d. This, however, "could be attained only by pushing the wage of the worker down below the value of his labour-power." The consequences are spelled out materially: with the 4s. 6d. produced in 9 hours, the worker commands one-tenth less of the means of subsistence; "the reproduction of his labour-power can take place only in a stunted form." Surplus labour would be prolonged "only by transgressing its normal limits"; its domain extended "only by a usurpation of part of the domain of necessary labour-time."

The crucial exclusion: "Despite the important part which this method plays in practice, we are excluded from considering it here by our assumption that all commodities, including labour-power, are bought and sold at their full value." The assumption of equivalent exchange — the postulate that all commodities, labour-power included, are bought and sold at their full value — is the analytical price of admission for the theory of relative surplus-value in its pure form. Under that assumption, the labour-time necessary for the reproduction of labour-power "cannot be lessened by a fall in the worker's wages below the value of his labour-power, but only by a fall in this value itself." The argument is then closed with a directional, almost logical point: given the length of the working day, "the prolongation of the surplus labour must of necessity originate in the curtailment of the necessary labour-time; the latter cannot arise from the former." In the example chosen, the value of labour-power had to fall in fact by one-tenth — from 10 hours to 9 — for surplus labour to be prolonged from 2 to 3 hours.

This is a crucial nuance for readers: Marx is not denying that wage-cutting occurs "in practice"; he is bracketing it to isolate a mechanism whose logic is independent of cheating, fraud, or the deviation of price from value. The chapter's scientific claim is that relative surplus-value is possible under the strictest honesty of exchange. That is what makes it a systemic category rather than a moral one.

2.3 The Necessary Presupposition: Revolutionizing the Mode of Production

A fall in the value of labour-power implies that the same means of subsistence formerly produced in 10 hours can now be produced in 9. "But this is impossible without an increase in the productivity of labour." Marx's illustrative example is deliberately artisanal: a cobbler with a given set of tools makes one pair of boots in a working day of 12 hours; to make two pairs in the same time, "the productivity of his labour must be doubled; and this cannot be done except by an alteration in his tools or in his mode of working, or both." Hence "the conditions of production of his labour, i.e. his mode of production, and the labour process itself, must be revolutionized."

He then supplies his formal definition: "By an increase in the productivity of labour, we mean an alteration in the labour process of such a kind as to shorten the labour-time socially necessary for the production of a commodity, and to endow a given quantity of labour with the power of producing a greater quantity of use-value." Two elements should be parsed separately. First, productivity is defined socially, not individually — it is measured by a change in socially necessary labour-time, the average labour-time required under prevailing social conditions. Second, it is defined as a change in the use-value productivity of labour: the same quantity of labour yields more use-values. It is emphatically not defined as producing more value: value depends on the quantity of labour-time, not on the productivity with which that time is expended. This definitional duality — more goods, same value — is the pivot on which the whole chapter turns.

The contrast with the earlier analysis is then drawn explicitly. In the production of absolute surplus-value, the mode of production was assumed "given and invariable"; capital could take over the labour process "in its given or historically transmitted shape, and then simply to prolong its duration." But "when surplus-value has to be produced by the conversion of necessary labour into surplus labour, it by no means suffices for capital to take over the labour process in its given or historically transmitted shape." The technical and social conditions of the process, "and consequently the mode of production itself must be revolutionized before the productivity of labour can be increased." Only then does the value of labour-power fall and the portion of the day necessary for reproducing it shrink.

This passage is, in the architecture of Capital, the point at which capital ceases to be merely the formal appropriator of a pre-existing labour process and becomes its active transformer — the move from what Marx elsewhere calls formal to real subsumption of labour under capital.

2.4 The Formal Definition of Absolute and Relative Surplus-Value

With the mechanism established, Marx coins the terminology:

"I call that surplus-value which is produced by the lengthening of the working day, absolute surplus-value. In contrast to this, I call that surplus-value which arises from the curtailment of the necessary labour-time, and from the corresponding alteration in the respective lengths of the two components of the working day, relative surplus-value."

Three points of precision are worth extracting. (i) The distinction rests on which variable changes: under absolute surplus-value, the working day lengthens while the division between necessary and surplus labour remains (or need not) change; under relative surplus-value, the working day stays fixed and the boundary point moves. (ii) Both are forms of surplus-value, i.e. both are objectifications of unpaid surplus labour — the difference is the method, not the substance. (iii) The definition of relative surplus-value is relational: it specifies that the two components of the day change in opposite directions, with their sum constant. Analytically, relative surplus-value is a pure redistribution.

2.5 The Scope Condition: Which Branches Must Be Seized?

Marx now asks a question that a careless reader might overlook: if raising productivity cheapens commodities, does any increase in productivity anywhere cheapen labour-power? The answer is an emphatic no, and the reasoning yields a precise scope condition. "In order to make the value of labour-power go down, the rise in the productivity of labour must seize upon those branches of industry whose products determine the value of labour-power, and consequently either belong to the category of normal means of subsistence, or are capable of replacing them."

But the scope condition has a second, technically subtle layer. "The value of a commodity is determined not only by the quantity of labour which gives it its final form, but also by the quantity of labour contained in the instruments by which it has been produced." Thus a pair of boots embodies the cobbler's labour plus the labour in the leather, wax, thread, etc. Consequently, a fall in the value of labour-power is also brought about by productivity increases in "those industries which supply the instruments of labour and the material for labour, i.e. the physical elements of constant capital which are required for producing the means of subsistence." Hence the scope condition must be stated recursively: the wage-goods industries, plus the industries supplying their means of production. And the negative corollary: "an increase in the productivity of labour in those branches of industry which supply neither the necessary means of subsistence nor the means by which they are produced leaves the value of labour-power undisturbed." Luxury industries, for instance, can be revolutionized without touching the rate of surplus-value.

2.6 Proportionality, Aggregation, and the "Cheapening of Shirts": The Sum of Reductions

The chapter then addresses the magnitude of the effect, and here a second major nuance appears. "The cheapening of the commodity, of course, causes only a relative fall in the value of labour-power, a fall proportional to the extent to which that commodity enters into the reproduction of labour-power." Shirts, for example, are a necessary means of subsistence, "but are only one out of many." The total sum of necessary means of subsistence consists of various commodities, each the product of a distinct industry, and the value of each enters as a component part into the value of labour-power. Crucially, "the total decrease of necessary labour-time is equal to the sum of all the different reductions in labour-time which have occurred in those various distinct branches of production." This is an aggregative law: no single cheapening has more than a fractional effect, but the effects are additive across the relevant industrial system.

Marx then makes a methodological move of considerable importance, which he flags as deliberate: "Here we treat this general result as if it were the direct result and the direct purpose in each individual case." That is, he is aware that no individual capitalist who cheapens shirts "necessarily aims to reduce the value of labour-power and shorten necessary labour-time in proportion to this." The individual capitalist contributes to the general rate of surplus-value "only in so far as he ultimately contributes to this result." Then the principle: "The general and necessary tendencies of capital must be distinguished from their forms of appearance." This is the first appearance of the essence/appearance distinction that governs the chapter's methodology, and it recurs twice more.

2.7 The Methodological Excursus: Essence, Competition, and the Real vs. Apparent Motion of the Heavenly Bodies

Marx then explicitly declines to undertake a full analysis of competition — "it is not our intention here to consider the way in which the immanent laws of capitalist production manifest themselves in the external movement of the individual capitals, assert themselves as the coercive laws of competition, and therefore enter into the consciousness of the individual capitalist as the motives which drive him forward." Nevertheless he states a principle: "a scientific analysis of competition is possible only if we can grasp the inner nature of capital, just as the apparent motions of the heavenly bodies are intelligible only to someone who is acquainted with their real motions, which are not perceptible to the senses." This is a striking epistemological analogy — invoking the distinction between geocentric apparent motion and heliocentric real motion — and it does double duty: it justifies the abstraction from competition performed in this chapter (which analyzes what competition must express even when the agents are unconscious of it), and it pre-announces that competition will later be analyzed as the form of appearance of the immanent laws.

On the strength of the results already achieved, but "merely on the basis of the results already achieved," Marx then adds a set of remarks — which are in fact the analytical heart of the chapter.

2.8 The Numerical Core: Twelve Articles, Individual Value, Social Value, and the Genesis of Extra Surplus-Value

The argument now proceeds through a sustained arithmetical construction. Stipulations: 1 hour of labour = 6d; a 12-hour working day produces a value of 6s. Under currently prevailing productivity, 12 articles are produced in those 12 hours. The value of the means of production used up in each article is 6d. Under these circumstances each article costs 1s. — 6d. for the value of the means of production and 6d. for the value newly added. (Check: 12 articles × 6d. means of production = 6s. constant capital; 12 articles × 6d. new value = 6s. new value; total 12s. = 12 articles × 1s. ✓)

Now let one capitalist double the productivity of labour, producing 24 articles instead of 12 in a 12-hour day. The value of the means of production per article is unchanged (6d). Hence the value of each article falls to 9d: 6d. for means of production + 3d. for the value newly added by labour. Note the crucial invariance: the day's labour "creates, as before, a new value of 6s. and no more," which is now spread over twice as many articles. Each article embodies 3d. instead of 6d.; equivalently, "only half an hour of labour-time, instead of a whole hour, is now added to the means of production while they are being transformed into each article."

Then the decisive theoretical step: "The individual value of these articles is now below their social value." Definitionally, the individual value of a commodity is the labour-time it actually costs this producer; the social value is "the labour-time socially required for its production" — the average under prevailing social conditions. Each article "costs, on an average, 1s., and represents 2 hours of social labour; but under the altered mode of production it costs only 9d., or contains only 1½ hours' labour." Marx states the principle baldly: "The real value of a commodity, however, is not its individual, but its social value."

The payoff follows immediately. If the innovating capitalist sells at the social value of 1s., he sells 3d. above the individual value of each article, "and thus he realizes an extra surplus-value of 3d." Here is the birth of the concept later elaborated as super-profit (and, in Volume III, as differential rent-like phenomena): the innovator pockets the gap between individual and social value.

2.9 The Market Constraint and the Price Cut: Why Extra Surplus-Value Survives at 10d.

Marx does not let the argument rest at the social-value price, because a quantitative obstacle appears. The working day of 12 hours is now represented "for him" by 24 articles instead of 12. Therefore "in order to get rid of the product of one working day, the demand must be double what it was, i.e. the market must become twice as extensive." Since, other things equal, a capitalist can command a more extensive market only if his prices fall, "he will therefore sell them above their individual but below their social value, say at 10d. each. By this means he still squeezes an extra surplus-value of one penny out of each."

This is a genuinely important piece of analysis, and it produces a general motive: "This augmentation of surplus-value is pocketed by the capitalist himself, whether or not his commodities belong to the class of necessary means of subsistence, and therefore participate in determining the general value of labour-power." Therefore — and Marx italicizes the inference logically rather than typographically — "quite independently of this, there is a motive for each individual capitalist to cheapen his commodities by increasing the productivity of labour." In other words: the incentive to revolutionize production does not depend on the commodity being a wage-good. Relative surplus-value in the strict sense (a rise in the general rate) requires wage-goods; but the drive to cheapen is universal across all branches because of extra surplus-value. This is how Marx links the micro-rationality of the individual capitalist to the macro-tendency of capital as a whole — without collapsing the two.

2.10 Recomputing the Partition: 5:1 Becomes 5:3, and the "Intensified Labour" Question

Marx now insists — against any reading that would treat the innovator's gain as a mere market accident or a species of unequal exchange — that "even in this case, the increased production of surplus-value arises from the curtailment of the necessary labour-time, and the corresponding prolongation of the surplus labour." He re-derives the partition of the day under the innovated conditions.

Stipulations: necessary labour-time = 10 hours; value of a day's labour-power = 5s.; surplus labour-time = 2 hours; daily surplus-value = 1s. Now the capitalist produces 24 articles and sells them at 10d. each, making 20s. in all. Since the value of the means of production is 12s., "14⅖ of these articles merely replace the constant capital advanced." (Check: 12s. ÷ 10d. per article = 12s. ÷ (10/12)s. = 14.4 = 14⅖ articles ✓.) "The labour of the 12-hour working day is represented by the remaining 9⅗ articles." Since the price of labour-power is 5s., 6 articles represent the necessary labour-time (5s. ÷ 10d. = 6 ✓) and 3⅗ articles the surplus labour. "The ratio of necessary labour to surplus labour, which under average social conditions was 5:1, is now only 5:3."

Marx then offers an alternative derivation. The value of the product of the 12-hour day is 20s.; of this, 12s. represent the value of the means of production, "a value that merely re-appears in the finished product." There remain 8s., "which are the expression in money of the value newly created during the working day." This sum is greater than that in which average social labour of the same kind is expressed: 12 hours of the latter are expressed by only 6s. Hence: "The exceptionally productive labour acts as intensified labour; it creates in equal periods of time greater values than average social labour of the same kind." (Footnote 4 flags that intensified labour was discussed in Chapter 1, Section 2, pp. 135–6.)

The worker, still paid 5s. as the daily value of labour-power, now needs to work only 7½ hours to reproduce this value (not 10). His surplus labour is therefore increased by 2½ hours (from 2 to 4½), and the surplus-value he produces grows from 1s. into 3s. (Check: 8s. new value − 5s. wages = 3s. surplus; necessary share = 5/8 × 12 = 7.5 hours; surplus share = 3/8 × 12 = 4.5 hours; ratio 7.5:4.5 = 5:3 ✓; surplus-value rate rises from 20% to 60%.)

A nuance the reader must register: there is a subtle tension in the text between the earlier statement that "the day's labour creates, as before, a new value of 6s. and no more" and the later statement that the value newly created during the working day is 8s. Marx reconciles these by the "intensified labour" formula: the innovator sells above the individual value of the commodity (9d.) but below its social value (12d.), at 10d., and the excess over individual value is realized as value that the exceptionally productive labour has, in effect, added. Analytically the extra 2s. is a realized surplus attributable to the innovation, and Marx's framework treats exceptionally productive labour as equivalent to intensified labour — labour of a higher degree of intensity, which by the Chapter 1 analysis creates more value per unit of time. The mechanism by which the deviation between individual and social value is converted into value remains one of the most debated passages in Volume I; but within the chapter, the conclusion is unambiguous: the result is still a curtailment of necessary labour-time, not an extension of the working day.

The methodological punchline follows: "Hence the capitalist who applies the improved method of production appropriates and devotes to surplus labour a greater portion of the working day than the other capitalists in the same business. He does as an individual what capital itself taken as a whole does when engaged in producing relative surplus-value."

2.11 The Self-Dissolution of Extra Surplus-Value and Competition as a Coercive Law

The innovation, however, is self-undermining at the level of the individual. "This extra surplus-value vanishes as soon as the new method of production is generalized, for then the difference between the individual value of the cheapened commodity and its social value vanishes." When all producers use the new method, the socially necessary labour-time falls to the new level, social value falls to individual value, and the extra 3d. (or 2s.) disappears.

But the law of value does not disappear. It operates with two faces: "The law of the determination of value by labour-time makes itself felt to the individual capitalist who applies the new method of production by compelling him to sell his goods under their social value; this same law, acting as a coercive law of competition, forces his competitors to adopt the new method." Footnote 5 is a remarkable pre-Marxian formulation of this compulsion from a 1720 pamphlet: if a neighbour "by doing much with little labour, can sell cheap, I must contrive to sell as cheap as he… every art, trade, or engine, doing work with labour of fewer hands, and consequently cheaper, begets in others a kind of necessity and emulation, either of using the same art, trade, or engine, or of inventing something like it."

The class-level conclusion is then drawn with care: "The general rate of surplus-value is therefore ultimately affected by the whole process only when the increase in the productivity of labour has seized upon those branches of production and cheapened those commodities that contribute towards the necessary means of subsistence, and are therefore elements of the value of labour-power." Thus the analysis separates three levels: (a) the individual capitalist, who transitorily gains extra surplus-value wherever he innovates; (b) the branch, from which the extra surplus-value vanishes upon generalization; and (c) social capital as a whole, whose rate of surplus-value rises only if the cheapened commodities enter the value of labour-power. This three-level architecture is the analytical spine of the chapter.

2.12 The Proportionality Laws and Capital's Immanent Drive

Marx now summarizes in the form of proportionalities. "The value of commodities stands in inverse ratio to the productivity of labour." Since the value of labour-power depends on the values of commodities, so too does it stand in inverse ratio. But "relative surplus-value, however, is directly proportional to the productivity of labour. It rises and falls together with productivity." This is the chapter's central proportional statement: productivity up → commodity values down → value of labour-power down → relative surplus-value up.

He then restates the arithmetic of value conservation within the day: "The value of money being assumed to be constant, an average social working day of 12 hours always produces the same new value, 6s., no matter how this sum may be apportioned between surplus-value and wages." But if productivity raises the value of labour-power's determinants, and the daily value of labour-power falls from 5s. to 3s., then surplus-value rises from 1s. to 3s.; "10 hours were necessary for the reproduction of the value of the labour-power; now only 6 are required. 4 hours have been set free, and can be annexed to the domain of surplus labour." Hence the chapter's most quoted formulation of capital's dynamic: "Capital therefore has an immanent drive, and a constant tendency, towards increasing the productivity of labour, in order to cheapen commodities and, by cheapening commodities, to cheapen the worker himself." Footnote 6 assembles eighteenth- and nineteenth-century sources asserting precisely this: that whatever lowers the price of provisions lowers the price of labour, that "wages are decreased in the same proportion as the powers of production increase," and that "machinery, it is true, cheapens the necessaries of life, but it also cheapens the labourer."

2.13 The Quesnay Riddle and Its Solution

The chapter then poses its paradox in the form of a historical riddle, attributing it to Quesnay, "one of the founders of political economy," who "used to torment his opponents with this question, and they could find no answer to it." The substance: the capitalist's "sole concern is to produce exchange-value," yet he "continually strives to bring down the exchange-value of commodities." Quesnay's own formulation, quoted in French in footnote 7, notes that reducing the costs of labour in manufacture lowers the price of the finished article, yet the physiocrats believed the production of wealth through artisans' labour consisted in augmenting the exchange-value of their products — an apparent contradiction.

Marx's solution is the simultaneity theorem established over the preceding sections: since the same process both cheapens commodities and augments the surplus-value contained in them, there is no paradox. The capitalist does not care about the absolute value of the commodity. "All that interests him is the surplus-value present in it, which can be realized by sale. Realization [Realisierung] of the surplus-value necessarily carries with it the replacement of the value advanced." The falling absolute value of the product is not merely compatible with, but the means to, a rising surplus-value. What looks like the capitalist working against his own interest (producing exchange-value, yet depreciating it) is in fact the very expression of his interest — because he is interested not in value as such but in unpaid value, in the surplus-value residue.

Note that Marx does not deny the real-world phenomenon that cheapening may also arise from competition among capitalists selling at a loss; he locates the systemic logic in the identity of the cheapening and surplus-augmenting process. The solution is also a correction of the physiocrats and of the classical school: they saw cheapening and could not square it with wealth-as-exchange-value because they lacked the distinction between the value of a commodity and the surplus-value it contains.

2.14 The Real Objective: Shortening Necessary Labour-Time, Not the Working Day

The chapter closes with a sharp polemic that also serves as a conceptual boundary-marking. "The shortening of the working day, therefore, is by no means what is aimed at in capitalist production, when labour is economized by increasing its productivity." What is aimed at is only "the shortening of the labour-time necessary for the production of a definite quantity of commodities." The distinction is drawn with a concrete image: if the worker's productivity rises tenfold and he produces ten times as many commodities, spending one-tenth as much labour-time on each, he may still work 12 hours and produce 1,200 articles instead of 120; "indeed, his working day may simultaneously be prolonged, so as to make him produce say 1,400 articles in 14 hours."

This yields the polemical target: "in the treatises of economists of the stamp of MacCulloch, Ure, Senior and the like, we may read on one page that the worker owes a debt of gratitude to capital for developing his productivity, because the necessary labour-time is thereby shortened, and on the next page that he must prove his gratitude by working in future for 15 hours instead of 10." The two claims — the boon of productivity and the demand for longer hours — are not contradictions in the ideology because both serve the same master.

The chapter's final sentence states the objective precisely: "The objective of the development of the productivity of labour within the context of capitalist production is the shortening of that part of the working day in which the worker must work for himself, and the lengthening, thereby, of the other part of the day, in which he is free to work for nothing for the capitalist." And the final line defers the concrete study: "How far this result can also be attained without cheapening commodities will appear from the following chapters, where we examine the particular methods of producing relative surplus-value." That forward pointer to Chapters 13–15 (co-operation, manufacture, machinery) sets the agenda of the rest of Part Four: the channels of relative surplus-value that operate through organization and technology rather than through cheapening of wage-goods per se.

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

First principle — the internalization of class conflict into the working day. The chapter establishes that capital's exploitation does not require the arbitrary lengthening of the day (though it pursues that too). It can install itself inside a day of constant legal and physiological length by continuously shifting the internal boundary between necessary and surplus labour. This yields a systemic law of capital: surplus labour can grow without limit in principle within a fixed working day, because its ceiling is set not by clock-time but by the productivity of the wage-good sectors. The working day is thus at once a fixed container and a moving frontier.

Second principle — the primacy of the mode of production over the act of exchange. By excluding wage-cutting below the value of labour-power and assuming equivalent exchange throughout, the chapter demonstrates that relative surplus-value is a phenomenon of production, not of circulation. No fraudulent exchange is required; the innovating capitalist buys labour-power at its value and sells the product below its former social value, yet realizes extra surplus-value. The source of all surplus-value is unpaid labour time; the method of relative surplus-value is the technological reorganization of the labour process so that the time needed to reproduce labour-power shrinks.

Third principle — the recursive scope of exploitation. Relative surplus-value at the level of social capital requires productivity gains in wage-good industries and in the industries producing their means of production — a recursively expanding industrial system. Thus even industries that produce nothing the worker consumes contribute to the cheapening of labour-power if they supply constant capital to wage-good industries. This is the chapter's most technically dense contribution to the theory: it integrates the analysis of constant capital into the theory of surplus-value, showing that the value of the instruments and materials of production is as determinant of the value of labour-power as the labour spent finishing the goods.

Fourth principle — the concrete universal of the productivity drive. Extra surplus-value supplies a universal motive for cheapening across all branches — including luxury goods and capital goods — because any innovator can sell above individual and below social value. The chapter thereby explains, without recourse to the intentions of capitalists, why capital is structurally, continuously technological and competitive: the law of value, acting as the coercive law of competition, forces generalization; generalization erases the extra surplus-value; the race begins again. Capital is here revealed as an internally driven growth machine whose competitive dynamics are the phenomenal form of the law of value.

Fifth principle — the essence/appearance distinction as scientific method. The chapter's repeated insistence — general and necessary tendencies versus forms of appearance; the real versus the apparent motions of the heavenly bodies; treating the aggregate result as if it were the direct purpose — provides the methodology for the rest of Capital. Competition is not the origin of the laws but the mode in which they assert themselves as coercive necessities. This explains why Marx can analyze relative surplus-value without a full theory of market prices, competition, or credit: those belong to a later level of analysis.

Broader practical and theoretical implications. The chapter retroactively repositions the earlier analysis of absolute surplus-value as the historically earlier, more primitive form, and it positions relative surplus-value as capital's mature and specifically "modern" mode — one that will be developed concretely through co-operation, manufacture, and machinery. It thereby supplies the analytical bridge from the theory of value to the theory of industrial organization. Its immediate political-economic implication is the refutation of the "gratitude" ideology of MacCulloch, Ure, and Senior: the shortening of necessary labour-time is not a benefit conferred on the worker but the objective of capital, whose counterpart is either the intensification of labour, the lengthening of the day, or the lowering of the value of labour-power through the recursive cheapening of the worker's own subsistence. And its deepest philosophical implication is that the capitalist's seeming irrationality — producing exchange-values while systematically destroying them — is no irrationality at all, once one understands that his object is not value in its absolute magnitude but surplus-value, the immanent social relation of exploitation embedded in the commodity. The "Quesnay riddle" is thus resolved not by a tweak to doctrine but by the completed distinction between value, use-value, and surplus-value — the distinction that makes the whole of Capital possible.
