Volume 1 • Chapter 9: The Rate of Surplus-Value

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

Chapter 9 of Capital, Volume One, occupies a pivotal position in Marx's architecture. It sits within Part Three, "The Production of Absolute Surplus-Value," immediately after the chapters that established the labour process, the valorization process, and the crucial distinction between constant and variable capital, and immediately before the long historical chapter on the working day. Its function is to convert a qualitative insight — that surplus-value arises exclusively from the variable portion of capital — into a precise, quantitative measuring instrument. The chapter's core thesis is announced bluntly on its opening pages and defended relentlessly thereafter: the rate of surplus-value (s/v) is "an exact expression for the degree of exploitation of labour-power by capital, or of the worker by the capitalist." Everything else in the chapter — the algebraic manipulation, the farm and factory examples, the demolition of Nassau Senior's "last hour," the definition of the surplus product — exists to establish, illustrate, and defend that single proposition.

The central problem the chapter confronts is twofold. First, there is a formal/analytical problem: surplus-value presents itself initially as the mere excess of a product's value over the value of its constituent elements, an excess that seems to belong to the total capital advanced (C = c + v). To isolate its true source, Marx must show that the constant capital c contributes nothing to the creation of new value but merely re-appears in the product, and must therefore be "posited as zero." This is not sleight of hand but a legitimate analytical abstraction, defended by analogy with the chemist ignoring his retorts. Second, and more consequentially, there is an ideological problem: because the surplus is habitually compared to the total capital rather than its variable part, the measured degree of exploitation is systematically understated. In Marx's example, the true rate of 100 percent appears as a mere 18 percent when mis-computed as s/C — a "pleasant surprise to Mr Carey and other harmonizers." The chapter thus doubles as an exposé of how a superficially technical comparison conceals a social relation.

The chapter's intellectual objectives are fourfold: (i) to derive the rate of surplus-value from the transformations C → C′, (v + s) = v + Δv; (ii) to demonstrate the equivalence of two languages for the same ratio — objectified labour (s/v) and living labour (surplus labour/necessary labour); (iii) to work through two detailed empirical illustrations (a Manchester spinning mill and Jacob's 1815 agricultural data) to train the reader in the "novel principles"; and (iv) to expose the "last hour" fallacy as the canonical ideological abuse of the category — the claim that profits depend entirely on the final hour of the working day.

Within the broader framework of Marx's project, this chapter supplies the ratio whose multiplication by other determinants will later generate the theory of the rate of profit (explicitly deferred to Volume Three). It also, crucially, links the abstract categories of value to the physical working day: the working day is defined as the sum of necessary labour and surplus labour, and the surplus product as the material embodiment of surplus-value. The chapter thus bridges the pure theory of value with the concrete, historical struggles over the length of the working day — setting the stage for everything that follows.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

2.1 The Algebraic Frame: From C = c + v to C′ = (c + v) + s

Marx opens by stating how surplus-value "presents itself to us first": as the amount by which the value of the product exceeds the value of its constituent elements. He then decomposes the capital advanced, C, into its two components: c, the sum of money laid out on means of production (raw material, auxiliary material, instruments of labour) — the portion "turned into constant capital" — and v, the sum expended on labour-power — the portion "turned into variable capital." The initial identity is therefore C = c + v. His worked example: £500 = £410 constant + £90 variable.

When the production process concludes, a commodity emerges whose value is (c + v) + s, where s is surplus-value. With the same figures: value = (£410 + £90) + £90 = £590. The original capital has changed from C to C′, from £500 to £590, and the difference, £90, is the surplus-value. Since the value of the constituent elements of the product equals the value of the capital advanced, "it is a mere tautology to say that the excess of the value of the product over the value of its constituent elements is equal to the valorization of the value of the capital advanced." Marx immediately announces that this tautology nonetheless requires closer inspection — and the entire first section is that inspection.

2.2 The Tautology, Fixed Capital, and the Malthus Footnote

The first complication is fixed capital. Not all of the value of the instruments of labour passes into the product in any given cycle; only the fraction worn away transfers. Marx distinguishes the value of the means of production actually consumed from the total value of the machinery employed. In his illustrative breakdown, c = £410 consists of £312 raw material, £44 auxiliary material, and £54 for machinery wear; but the machinery's total value is £1,054. Only the £54 is "advanced for the purpose of turning out the product."

Should one also count the remaining £1,000 that continues to exist in the machinery in its old form, it would have to appear on both sides of the calculation — as advance and as product — yielding £1,500 on one side and £1,590 on the other. The difference, and therefore the surplus-value, would still be £90. This is why Marx stipulates that "when we refer to constant capital advanced for the production of value, we always mean the value of the means of production actually consumed in the course of production." He footnotes Malthus's Principles of Political Economy (2nd edn, 1836, p. 269) to the same effect: "If we reckon the value of the fixed capital employed as a part of the advances, we must reckon the remaining value of such capital at the end of the year as a part of the annual return." The principle is one of consistency — a value that persists must never be counted only on one side.

2.3 The Value-Product and the Legitimacy of Setting c = 0

Returning to C′ = (c + v) + s, Marx draws the decisive distinction between the value of the product and the value-product ("the new value actually created in the process"). The value of the product is £590; the value-product is only v + s = £180 (£90 variable + £90 surplus). The £410 constant capital is not created; it "merely re-appears."

To isolate the creation of value, Marx posits c = 0. He defends this through a limiting thought-experiment: imagine branches of industry where the capitalist could dispense with all previously produced means of production — no raw material, no auxiliary material, no instruments — employing only labour-power and materials supplied by nature. Then C = (0 + v) = v, C′ = v + s, and C′ − C = s, "as before." Conversely, if s = 0 — if labour-power produced only its equivalent — then C′ = (c + v) + 0, so C′ = C; the capital would not have valorized itself.

The justification for the abstraction is explicitly mathematical: "This is merely an application of a mathematical rule, employed whenever we operate with constant and variable magnitudes, related to each other only by the symbols of addition and subtraction." And it is an everyday practice: to calculate England's profit from the cotton industry, one first deducts what is paid for cotton to the United States, India, Egypt — positing the merely-reappearing capital as a zero magnitude.

He reinforces the point with a methodological analogy of remarkable staying power: the fact that retorts and other vessels are necessary to a chemical process does not prevent the chemist from ignoring them when analysing the results. The means of production provide only "the material in which fluid, value-creating labour-power has to be incorporated"; neither their nature nor their value matters to the creation of value. All that is needed is "a sufficient supply of material to absorb the labour expended." That material may rise or fall in value, or be valueless in itself, "like the land and the sea" — with no influence on the variation in the quantity of value. This is underscored by footnote 2, citing Lucretius — nil posse creari de nihilo ("out of nothing, nothing can be created") — and stating the materialist premise: "'Creation of value' is the transposition of labour-power into labour. Labour-power itself is, above all else, the material of nature transposed into a human organism."

2.4 The Metamorphosis of v: Dead Labour into Living Labour

A subtler difficulty emerges. In the formula C′ = £410c + £90v + £90s, the £90 figure is "a given and therefore a constant quantity," so treating it as variable seems absurd. Marx's resolution is vital: the £90 is "merely a symbol for the process undergone by this value." What is purchased is a definite quantity of objectified labour — a constant value. But within production, "the place of the £90 is taken by labour-power which sets itself in motion, dead labour is replaced by living labour, something stagnant by something flowing, a constant by a variable." The result is the reproduction of v plus an increment of v.

Hence the crucial expression v + s = v + Δv. From the standpoint of capitalist production, "the whole process appears as the independent motion of what was originally constant value, but has now been transformed into labour-power." Marx's dense closing remark — that expressions like "£90 variable capital" or "self-valorizing value" seem contradictory — dissolves into the observation that they "express a contradiction immanent in capitalist production." This is the germ of the fetishism critique: the capital relation inverts living labour into a predicate of value.

2.5 From Absolute to Relative Magnitude: The Rate of Surplus-Value

With c set to zero, the capital advanced reduces from c + v to v, and the value of the product (c + v) + s becomes the value produced v + s. Of the £180 new value, subtracting the £90 variable capital leaves £90 surplus. This £90 is the absolute quantity of surplus-value. The relative quantity — "the ratio in which the variable capital has valorized its value" — is given by s/v, here 90/90, or 100 percent. This relative magnitude is what Marx christens the rate of surplus-value (with a footnote noting the English terms "rate of profit" and "rate of interest," and forecasting Volume Three's demonstration that "the rate of profit is no mystery, when one knows the laws of surplus-value").

2.6 Necessary Labour and Surplus Labour: Two Languages for One Ratio

Marx now re-derives the ratio from the side of labour-time. The worker does not directly produce his own means of subsistence; within a system of social division of labour he produces a particular commodity — yarn, say — whose value equals the value of his means of subsistence (or the money for it). If the value of his daily means of subsistence represents on average 6 hours of objectified labour, he must work 6 hours to produce that value. During that time he produces only an equivalent for the value of his labour-power already advanced — which is why the production of new value looks like mere reproduction.

Marx defines:

  • Necessary labour-time: the portion of the working day during which reproduction of the equivalent takes place.
  • Necessary labour: the labour expended in that time — necessary "for the worker, because independent of the particular social form of his labour; necessary for capital and the capitalist world, because the continued existence of the worker is the basis of that world."

The second period, in which labour is no longer necessary, is surplus labour-time, and its labour is surplus labour. It creates surplus-value, which "for the capitalist, has all the charms of something created out of nothing." Marx insists that conceiving surplus-value as "merely a congealed quantity of surplus labour-time, as nothing but objectified surplus labour," is as important as conceiving value generally as congealed labour-time.

He then delivers the chapter's second foundational equivalence:

s/v = surplus labour / necessary labour

This states "the same thing in different ways; in the one case in the form of objectified labour, in the other in the form of living, fluid labour." From this it follows immediately that "the rate of surplus-value is therefore an exact expression for the degree of exploitation of labour-power by capital, or of the worker by the capitalist."

Footnote 5 acknowledges a terminological doubling: the term "necessary labour-time," previously used for the time required to produce any commodity, is now also used for the time needed to produce the specific commodity labour-power. Marx concedes the inconvenience "cannot be entirely avoided in any science," comparing the higher and lower branches of mathematics. Footnote 4 (added by Engels) reminds the reader that "in reality it is the worker who does the 'advancing' to the capitalist, not the capitalist to the worker." And footnote 6 attacks Wilhelm Roscher — "Thucydides" of political economy by his own claim — who held that in the lowest stages of civilization "it is the strong who compel the weak to be thrifty." Marx retorts: thrifty with what — with labour? with surplus products "which are not even available"? Such apologies arise, he says, from "their real ignorance" and "an apologetic dread of a scientific analysis of value and surplus-value which might produce a result unpalatable to the powers that be."

2.7 The Degree versus the Amount of Exploitation, and the Confusion with the Rate of Profit

Two critical caveats now appear. Footnote 7 draws a distinction that must not be glossed: the rate of surplus-value measures the degree of exploitation, but "it is in no sense an expression for the absolute magnitude of the exploitation." If necessary labour = 5 hours and surplus labour = 5 hours, the rate is 100 percent and the amount is 5 hours. If necessary = 6 and surplus = 6, the rate remains 100 percent while the actual amount has increased by 20 percent. The point is that the rate and the absolute magnitude vary independently.

The second caveat is the chapter's most politically charged analytic move. With s = £90 and C = £500, "the usual way of reckoning" would yield 18 percent — "a rate so low it might well cause a pleasant surprise to Mr Carey and other harmonizers." (Carey is identified in the footnote as a proponent of the view that bourgeois relations of production are inherently harmonious; Marx devoted a section of the Grundrisse to a critique of such "harmonizers.") But the true rate is not s/C = s/(c + v) = 90/500, but s/v = 90/90 = 100 percent, "more than five times the apparent degree of exploitation."

Marx then notes that even without knowing the length of the working day, the number of workers, or the duration of the labour process, the rate s/v still discloses — through its equivalent expression, surplus labour/necessary labour — the relation between the two parts of the working day: here an equality, 100 percent, meaning the worker works half the day for himself and half for the capitalist.

He concludes the methodological recipe: take the total value of the product, posit the constant capital re-appearing in it as zero; what remains is the value actually created. If s is given, deduct it to find v; if v is given, find s; if both are given, compute s/v.

2.8 The Two Numerical Illustrations

Example One: The Manchester Spinning Mill (April 1871 data). Marx reports figures supplied by a Manchester manufacturer, chosen (per footnote 8) to replace erroneous data in the first edition. The mill runs 10,000 mule spindles spinning No. 32 yarn from American cotton, producing 1 lb. of yarn per spindle per week — 10,000 lb. weekly. With waste at 6 percent, 10,600 lb. of cotton are consumed (600 lb. wasted). The price of cotton in April 1871 was 7¾d. per lb., so the raw material costs approximately £342.

  • 10,000 spindles, including preparation machinery and motive power, at £1 per spindle = £10,000.
  • Depreciation at 10 percent = £1,000 a year = £20 a week.
  • Rent of the building £300 a year = £6 a week.
  • Coal: 100 h.p. indicated, at 4 lb. of coal per horse-power per hour over 60 hours, plus heating coal = 11 tons a week at 8s. 6d. per ton = about £4¾ a week; gas £1 a week; oil etc. £4¾ a week. Total auxiliary materials = £10 a week.

The constant part of the weekly product's value is therefore £378. Wages are £52 a week. Yarn at 12¼d. per lb. gives £510 for the 10,000 lb. Surplus-value = £510 − £430 = £80 (where £430 = £378 constant + £52 wages). Setting the constant part to zero leaves £132 as the weekly value created, which equals £52 variable + £80 surplus. The rate of surplus-value is therefore 80/52 = 153 11/13 percent. In a 10-hour working day with average labour this corresponds to necessary labour of 3 11/13 hours and surplus labour of 6 2/13 hours.

Example Two: Jacob's Agricultural Calculation (1815). Marx next takes William Jacob's figures for the year 1815 (wheat at 8s. a quarter, average yield 22 bushels per acre), acknowledging in the footnote that they are "very imperfect" but sufficient for his purpose. He stipulates the standard assumption: price = value, and surplus-value is distributed under the headings of profit, interest, rent, etc. — headings he declares irrelevant, simply adding them together.

The table runs:

Value Produced Per Acre
Seed £1 9s. 0d.
Manure £2 10s. 0d.
Wages £3 10s. 0d.
Total £7 9s. 0d.
Tithes, rates and taxes £1 1s. 0d.
Rent £1 8s. 0d.
Farmer's profit and interest £1 2s. 0d.
Total £3 11s. 0d.

Seed (£1 9s.) + manure (£2 10s.) = £3 19s., which is constant capital, posited as zero. Remaining is £3 10s., the variable capital advanced. A new value of £3 10s. + £3 11s. has been produced in its place. Therefore s/v = £3 11s./£3 10s. = 71/70 ≈ 101 percent — "more than 100 per cent." The worker employs more than half his working day producing surplus-value, which various persons then share "on different pretexts." A footnote warns these calculations are merely illustrative, since in Volume Three it will emerge that even with average prices the simple price-equals-value assumption cannot be made.

2.9 The Representation of the Value of the Product by Proportional Parts of the Product

Section 2 shifts from measurement to representation. Marx returns to the earlier spinning example: 12-hour day, 20 lb. of yarn, value 30s. Of this, 24s. (eight-tenths) consists of the re-appearance of the value of means of production — 20 lb. of cotton worth 20s. plus 4s. of worn spindle — i.e. constant capital. The remaining 6s. (two-tenths) is new value: 3s. replacing the day's labour-power (variable capital) and 3s. surplus-value:

30s. value of yarn = 24s. constant + 3s. variable + 3s. surplus

Since the whole value is contained in the 20 lb. of yarn, its components can be represented as contained in proportional parts of the product. Eight-tenths of the value (24s.) is contained in eight-tenths of the product (16 lb.); within that, 13⅓ lb. represents the 20s. of cotton and 2⅔ lb. the 4s. of spindle wear. The poetic result is that "the whole of the cotton used up in spinning the 20 lb. of yarn is represented by 13⅓ lb. of yarn," and it is as though those 16 lb. contained no labour spent in spinning at all — "just as if the cotton had converted itself into yarn without any help."

Conversely, the remaining 4 lb. yam (two-tenths) represents nothing but the new value of 6s. created in the 12-hour process; "all the value transferred to those 4 lb. from the raw material and instruments of labour consumed was so to speak intercepted in order to be incorporated in the 16 lb. first spun." Of this 4 lb., half (2 lb.) represents the 3s. variable, half the 3s. surplus.

Marx then translates the representation from the spatial into the temporal sphere. Since 12 hours yield 6s., 60 hours of labour are objectified in 30s. worth of yarn: 16 lb. materialize 48 hours of prior labour; 4 lb. materialize the 12 hours expended in the process. The spinner produces 1⅔ lb. per hour: in 8 hours, 13⅓ lb. (the value of the day's cotton); in the next 1 hour 36 minutes, 2⅔ lb. (the value of the instruments consumed); in the following 1 hour 12 minutes, 2 lb. worth 3s. (equal to the value created in his 6 hours of necessary labour); and in the final 1 hour 12 minutes, another 2 lb. worth 3s. (the surplus-value).

The method is "perfectly correct" in the abstract — it is the spatial method transferred to the temporal sphere. But Marx warns that it can be "accompanied by very barbaric notions," especially in the heads of those practically interested in valorization and theoretically interested in misunderstanding it. It can suggest that the spinner produces or replaces the value of the cotton in the first 8 hours, the wear of instruments in the next, his wages in the next, and devotes only the "famous 'last hour'" to surplus-value. This would make the spinner perform a "twofold miracle": producing cotton, spindles, steam-engine, coal, oil while using them to spin, and turning one working day into five. For in the example, producing the raw material and instruments requires 24/6 = 4 working days of 12 hours each, and their conversion into yarn another one — five days in all.

2.10 Senior's "Last Hour": Anatomy of an Apologetic Fallacy

Section 3 is the chapter's rhetorical and polemical summit. In 1836 Nassau W. Senior — "the Clauren of the English economists," summoned from Oxford to Manchester to learn the political economy he taught — was chosen by the manufacturers as their "prize-fighter" against the newly passed Factory Act (1833) and the Ten Hours' Agitation. His lecture was embodied in Letters on the Factory Act, as it Affects the Cotton Manufacture (London, 1837).

Senior's "analysis": under the law, no mill employing persons under 18 could work more than 11½ hours a day. A manufacturer investing £100,000 — £80,000 in mill and machinery, £20,000 in raw material and wages — with capital turned once a year and gross profits of 15 percent, should return goods worth £115,000. Each of the 23 half-hours of work produces 5/115ths, or 1/23. Of the 23 twenty-thirds constituting £115,000, twenty (= £100,000) simply replace capital; one 23rd (£5,000) makes up for the deterioration of the mill and machinery; and "the remaining two 23rds... that is the last two of the twenty-three half-hours" produce the net profit of 10 percent. Extend the day to 13 hours with about £2,600 added circulating capital, and "the net profit would be more than doubled." Reduce by one hour, and net profit is destroyed; reduce by 1½ hours, and even gross profit is destroyed.

Footnote 10 reconstructs what Senior really intended: the manufacturer employs the worker for 11½ hours (23 half-hours) multiplied by the working days of the year, yielding an annual product of £115,000. Twenty half-hours yield £100,000, replacing the capital; three half-hours yield £15,000 gross profit; of these, one yields £5,000 for wear and tear; the remaining two — "the last hour" — yield £10,000, the net profit. Senior's fallacy is that he "converts the last 2/23 of the product into portions of the working day itself." The footnote also commends Leonard Horner, Factory Inquiry Commissioner in 1833 and Inspector until 1859, whose A Letter to Mr Senior (1837) showed the figures were worth no more than the "analysis." Horner is praised for a life-long contest "not only with the embittered manufacturers, but also with the Cabinet."

Marx's rebuttal proceeds in stages:

  1. The superfluous-analysis argument. If Senior accepted the manufacturers' outcry that workers spend the best part of the day reproducing the value of buildings, machinery, cotton, and coal, then his analysis was superfluous. There was a simpler answer: work 10 hours instead of 11½, consume proportionally less cotton and machinery — "You gain just as much as you lose."

  2. The separation of categories. A question turning exclusively on the relation of net profit to the length of the working day required the manufacturers to place constant capital (buildings, machinery, raw material) on one side of the account and wage capital on the other.

  3. The dissection of the "last hour." If the worker produces his wages in the last hour but one and surplus-value in the last hour, and equal periods produce equal values, then since wages and surplus-value are of equal value, he produces his wages in 5¾ hours and net profit in the other 5¾ hours — a rate of 100 percent. The apparent paradox — that the yarn produced in one hour embodies 5¾ hours of labour — dissolves: the use-value produced in an hour is a definite quantity of yarn, but the value of that yarn is measured by 5¾ hours, of which 4¾ were previously embodied in the means of production (without assistance from him) and only one hour is added by him. There is "no witchcraft": the worker does not lose a moment reproducing the values of cotton and machinery; rather, "it is because his labour converts the cotton and the spindles into yarn, because he spins, that the values of the cotton and spindles go over to the yarn of their own accord. This is a result of the quality of his labour, not its quantity." He transfers more value in an hour than in half an hour only because he spins up more cotton.

  4. The reduction to truism. Senior's claim "amounts to no more than this, that in the yarn produced by him in 2 working hours... there are incorporated 11½ working hours." And Marx's own claim "that in the first 5¾ hours he produces his wages, and in the last 5¾ hours your net profit, amounts only to this, that you pay him for the former, but not for the latter." The class relation is not a technical fact about machines but a fact about payment: "in speaking of payment of labour, instead of payment of labour-power, I am only using your own slang expression."

  5. The counterfactual arithmetic. Compare paid with unpaid working time: "they are related to each other as half a day is to half a day; this gives a rate of 100 per cent, and a very pretty percentage it is." Extend the day to 13 hours and surplus labour rises from 5¾ to 7¼ hours, giving a rate of 126 2/23 percent — not the "more than doubled" 200 percent the manufacturers anticipated. Reduce the day to 10 hours and surplus labour falls from 5¾ to 4¼ hours, "a period that still gives a very profitable rate of surplus-value" (given in the text as 82 14/17 percent). The "fateful 'last hour'" — the object of "more stories than the millenarians about the Day of Judgement" — "is 'all bosh.'"

Footnotes 11 and 12 extend the polemic. Footnote 11 contrasts Senior's claim with Dr Andrew Ure's parallel argument in The Philosophy of Manufactures (1835): if children under 18 were turned out an hour sooner from the "warm and pure moral atmosphere of the factory," they would be deprived, through "idleness and vice," of all hope of salvation. It also reports Mr Howell's factory report of 21 May 1855 — "Had the following ingenious calculation been correct, every cotton factory in the United Kingdom would have been working at a loss since the year 1850" — and the 1848 flax-spinning petition in which parents claimed that "an additional hour of leisure will tend more to demoralize the children than otherwise, believing that idleness is the parent of vice." The factory report of 31 October 1848 rebuts this with a harrowing description of the flax mills' dust-laden atmosphere, the "feverish haste of the machinery," and children "fettered for 10 whole hours to such an occupation," branding the parents' talk of idleness "the purest cant, and the most shameless hypocrisy." The same report describes the tricks, threats, and falsifications used to obtain such petitions. Footnote 12 notes, sardonically, that Senior did profit from his Manchester journey: in Letters on the Factory Act he makes net gain depend on a single hour of unpaid labour, while a year earlier, in his Outline of Political Economy, he had "discovered," against Ricardo, that profit derives from the labour of the capitalist and interest from his "abstinence" — a word Roscher correctly translated as Enthaltung, though others produced the monkish Entsagung ("renunciation"). The chapter closes the section by noting that the battle-cry was raised again by James Wilson in The Economist of 15 April 1848 against the Ten Hours' Bill.

2.11 The Surplus Product and its Measure

Section 4, brief but conceptually load-bearing, defines the surplus product: the portion of the product that represents surplus-value — one-tenth of the 20 lb., or 2 lb. of yarn, in the earlier example (German Mehrprodukt, French produit net). The parallel with the rate is exact: just as the rate of surplus-value is determined by its relation not to the total capital but to its variable part, so "the relative amount of the surplus product is determined by its ratio, not to the remaining part of the total product, but to that part of it in which necessary labour is incorporated." Because the production of surplus-value is the determining purpose of capitalist production, the size of a given quantity of wealth must be measured not by its absolute quantity but by the relative magnitude of the surplus product.

Finally, Marx establishes the bridge to the following chapters: "the sum of the necessary labour and the surplus labour, i.e. the sum of the periods of time during which the worker respectively replaces the value of his labour-power and produces the surplus-value, constitutes the absolute extent of his labour-time, i.e. the working day." Footnote 13 shows that Ricardo had already grasped the point in an arithmetical register — "To an individual with a capital of £20,000, whose profits were £2,000 per annum, it would be a matter quite indifferent whether his capital would employ a hundred or a thousand men" — and quotes Arthur Young's Political Arithmetic (1774): of what use would a province of small independent peasants be, "except for the mere purpose of breeding men, which taken singly is a most useless purpose?" It adds T. Hopkins's observation (On Rent of Land, 1828) about "the strong inclination... to represent net wealth as beneficial to the labouring class... though it is evidently not on account of being net."

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The chapter yields several fundamental principles. First, and decisively: surplus-value arises exclusively from the variable capital, and the rate of surplus-value (s/v) — identical to the ratio of surplus labour to necessary labour — is the exact expression of the degree of exploitation. This is not one metric among others but the measure of a social relation: the rate tells us the proportion of the working day during which the worker labours without payment. In Marx's example, the worker works half the day for himself and half for the capitalist.

Second, the chapter exposes a systematic optical illusion built into capitalist accounting. Because the surplus is habitually compared to the total capital advanced, the true rate of 100 percent appears as 18 percent — a fivefold understatement — and this understatement flatters the system and produces "harmonizers." The confusion of s/v with s/C is not innocent error but a structural misrecognition; it is why Marx says Volume Three's theory of the rate of profit is a mystery only if one works in the wrong direction.

Third, the chapter establishes a vital distinction between degree and absolute magnitude of exploitation (footnote 7). A constant rate may conceal an expanding absolute extraction. Any theory that reads the rate as a proxy for the total burden will misjudge the historical trajectory.

Fourth, the chapter performs a piece of methodological instruction of enduring value: the legitimacy of abstraction. Setting c = 0 is not falsification but analytical isolation — the chemist ignoring his retorts. Marx draws a sharp line between what must be accounted for in the calculation (all value that appears on both sides cancels; the surplus is invariant to the treatment) and what must be accounted for in the explanation (the material precondition — the adequate supply of means of production). The abstraction is rigorous because the omitted term performs no causal work in the creation of new value.

Fifth, the chapter contributes to historical materialism a criterion of differentiation: what distinguishes economic formations — slave society, wage-labour society — is "the form in which this surplus labour is in each case extorted from the immediate producer, the worker." The rate and the product are described in the language of value and volume, but they are simultaneously moral-historical categories, and the Roscher footnote makes clear that the apologetic impulse to found surplus-value on the capitalist's "thrift" (or, later, his "abstinence") is a feature of all ruling-class economies, not an accident of 1836.

Sixth, the chapter's critique of the spatial/temporal metaphor of value leaves a general warning: representational devices that are analytically correct can be ideologically weaponized. The mental habit of allocating the working day into blocks that "replace" this or that element of capital is the same operation that generates the "last hour." The physician of this disease is the distinction between created value and transferred value — between the labour that adds new value and the labour that merely preserves old value by converting raw materials into products. This is the same principle later dramatized in the "Trinity Formula" and the fetishism discussions.

Seventh, the section on Senior remains one of the most durable set pieces of political economy. Its method — accepting the opponent's premises, then showing that they fail to support the conclusion — anticipates the entire edifice of the critique of political economy. The concept of "the last hour" has become a shorthand for the way interested reasoning mystifies the sources of profit. And Marx's conclusion — that Senior's own numbers, properly read, show a rate of 100 percent that would fall gracefully, not catastrophically, from the loss of one hour — remains, as a piece of immanent refutation, exemplary.

Finally, the chapter establishes the conceptual bridge to the working day. By defining the working day as the sum of necessary and surplus labour, and the surplus product as the material measure of wealth, Marx converts a technical ratio into the object of political struggle. The "Ten Hours' Bill," the factory inspections, the petitions, the inspectors' reports — all of the historical material that follows — are the practical form taken by the abstract relations established here. The rate of surplus-value is not merely a way of calculating; it is the numerical face of the class relation, and it is precisely because of this that the "last hour" was fabricated — and why it had to be destroyed.
