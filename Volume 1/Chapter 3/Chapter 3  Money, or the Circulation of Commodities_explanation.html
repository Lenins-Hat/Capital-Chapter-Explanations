Volume 1 • Chapter 3: Money, or the Circulation of Commodities

1. EXECUTIVE CHAPTER OVERVIEW & THESIS

The Central Problem and Its Stakes

Chapter 3 of Capital Volume One addresses a problem that appears deceptively simple but contains the genetic code of the entire capitalist system: What is money, and what does it do? The chapter's intellectual objective is nothing less than to derive money's every function from the commodity-form itself — to demonstrate that money is not an external convention, not a clever invention, not a neutral lubricant, but the necessary and logically compelled form of appearance of a specific social relation: the universal equivalent that arises when commodity production becomes general.

Where Chapter 1 derived value from socially necessary labour-time and Chapter 2 derived the exchange-relation from the value-relation, Chapter 3 shows that money is the crystallized form of the value-relation, the "social pledge" (nexus rerum) which every commodity-owner must obtain before he can eat. The chapter's thesis can be stated crisply: Money is the necessary form of appearance of the measure of value immanent in commodities — labour-time — and its functions (measure of value, means of circulation, hoarding, means of payment, world money) develop dialectically out of the contradictory unity of use-value and value that constitutes the commodity itself.

This is the pivot of the entire work. Before Chapter 3, Marx has analyzed production and exchange in their immediacy; after Chapter 3, he will analyze the transformation of money into capital (Volume One, Part II). The chapter thus serves as the conceptual bridge: it shows how the simple circulation of commodities, which appears to be a realm of freedom, equality, and property, already contains — in the contradictory unity of its own forms — the possibility of crisis, the potentiality of capital, and the actuality of social domination.

The Dialectical Method and the Problem of "Reading" the Text

To understand this chapter, one must grasp Marx's method. He is not merely describing monetary institutions; he is deducing categorical forms from the internal contradictions of the commodity. The movement is Hegelian in structure but materialist in content: each new function of money arises not because someone invented it, but because a contradiction in the previous function requires resolution. Money as measure of value produces a contradiction (the standard of price must be stable but the measure of value is variable); this is resolved by the separation of measure of value from standard of price. Money as means of circulation produces a contradiction (the identity of sale and purchase implies their separation); this resolves into money as hoard. Money as means of payment produces a contradiction (the chain of debts implies the possibility of general default); this resolves into money as world money and into the credit system.

The reader must therefore resist the temptation to read Chapter 3 as a taxonomy of monetary functions. It is instead a genealogy of monetary forms, each stage of which is logically entailed by the contradictions of the previous stage. Marx's footnotes — densely packed with references to Petty, Locke, North, Vanderlint, Hume, Ricardo, Fullarton, Barbon, Boisguillebert, and dozens of others — are not merely scholarly apparatus; they are the record of a polemical engagement with the entire history of political economy, showing how bourgeois economists confused the functions of money (most notoriously, confusing measure of value with standard of price, and thereby arriving at the absurd conclusion that the quantity of money determines prices).

The Broader Theoretical Framework

The chapter sits at the intersection of three grand theoretical projects:

First, the critique of political economy. Marx engages the entire tradition of monetary theory — from the Mercantilists (who fetishized the balance of trade), through the Classical school (Smith, Ricardo, and the Currency School), to the utopian socialists (Owen and his "labour money"). His critique operates on two levels: he demonstrates the logical errors in these theories (e.g., Hume's confusion of the measure of value with the circulating medium), but more importantly, he shows that these errors are symptomatic — they reflect the fetishized consciousness of a society in which social relations appear as relations between things.

Second, the theory of crisis. Chapter 3 contains the famous argument that the possibility of crisis is immanent in the commodity-form itself — specifically, in the separation of sale from purchase that money makes possible. "Circulation bursts through all the temporal, spatial and personal barriers imposed by the direct exchange of products" — and in doing so, it creates the possibility that sales will not be followed by purchases, that the "internal unity" of exchange will assert itself "through external antitheses." This is one of the most important theoretical innovations in the chapter: crisis is not an accident, not a policy error, but a formal possibility of commodity circulation as such, which becomes actual only when credit and the division of labour have developed sufficiently.

Third, the theory of the world market. The chapter's final section on world money opens the door to the international sphere, showing that the national circulation of commodities has a limit — the world market — where money "falls back into its original form as precious metal in the shape of bullion." This sets up the analysis of international trade, exchange rates, and the global hierarchy of production that dominates later parts of Marx's work.

The Chapter's Position in Capital

Within the architecture of Volume One, Chapter 3 completes the analysis of "Commodities and Money" (Part I), preparing the way for "The Transformation of Money into Capital" (Part II). The crucial theoretical achievement is the demonstration that money, fully analyzed, is not merely the servant of exchange but the independent form of value — "the absolute form of existence of exchange-value." Once money has attained this independence, the question inevitably arises: can this independent form of value increase itself? The answer — M-C-M′, the general formula for capital — is the subject of the next part. But the possibility of that increase is already latent in the contradictions of simple circulation that Chapter 3 unveils.

The chapter's thesis, in sum: Money is the form of appearance of the value-relation, and its development through measure of value, means of circulation, hoarding, means of payment, and world money is the dialectical unfolding of the contradictions of the commodity-form — contradictions that contain the possibility of crisis, the presupposition of capital, and the reality of social domination.

2. UNIFIED EXHAUSTIVE CHAPTER DECONSTRUCTION & ANALYSIS

Introduction: The Assumption of Gold and the Derivation of Money

Marx begins with a deliberately simplifying assumption: "Throughout this work I assume that gold is the money commodity, for the sake of simplicity." This is not an arbitrary choice but a methodologically motivated abstraction. Gold serves as the money commodity par excellence because it is the commodity that historically imposed itself as the universal equivalent — not by convention, but through the logic of exchange itself. The assumption allows Marx to analyze the form of money without being distracted by the historical multiplicity of monetary materials (silver, copper, cattle, shells, etc.). Yet he is careful to note that this is a simplification: in the notes and asides throughout the chapter, he repeatedly references silver, copper, and paper money, showing that the logic he develops applies to all monetary forms.

The first main function of gold, Marx writes, is "to supply commodities with the material for the expression of their values, or to represent their values as magnitudes of the same denomination, qualitatively equal and quantitatively comparable." This is gold's function as measure of value. The crucial point — which Marx will hammer home repeatedly — is that "it is not money that renders the commodities commensurable. Quite the contrary." Commodities are commensurable because they are all objectifications of human labour; money merely provides the material for expressing this commensurability. The equation "x commodity A = y money commodity" is the money-form or price — and it is socially valid because gold has already established itself as the universal equivalent.

This is the pivotal theoretical move: money does not create value; it expresses it. The value of a commodity is determined by socially necessary labour-time; money is the "necessary form of appearance of the measure of value which is immanent in commodities, namely labour-time." This directly refutes the utopian socialism of Owen and others, who imagined that "labour money" — certificates representing hours of labour — could replace gold and eliminate exploitation. Marx's footnote is scathing: Owen's labour money "is no more 'money' than a theatre ticket is." Owen presupposes directly socialized labour, a form of production "diametrically opposed to the production of commodities." The point is that money is not a dispensable convention but an essential form of commodity-producing society: to abolish money without abolishing commodity production is to attempt to "circumvent the necessary conditions of that form of production."

I. The Measure of Values

The Ideal Character of Money as Measure of Value

Having established that money expresses value, Marx turns to the specific form in which it does so. The price or money-form of commodities is "quite distinct from their palpable and real bodily form; it is therefore a purely ideal or notional form." When a commodity-owner says his linen is worth £2, he does not need to have any actual gold; the price exists "only in their heads, so to speak." This is the function of money as ideal or imaginary measure of value. The guardian of the commodities "must therefore lend them his tongue, or hang a ticket on them, in order to communicate their prices to the outside world." (Marx's footnote here is characteristically ethnographic: Captain Parry describes Baffin Bay inhabitants who licked articles twice to seal a bargain, while among the Eastern Eskimo the exchanger licked each article — a delightful illustration that price-communication is a social act, mediated by the body.)

The ideal character of money as measure of value has given rise to "the wildest theories" — a theme Marx will develop throughout the chapter. The essential point, however, is that "although the money that performs the functions of a measure of value is only imaginary, the price depends entirely on the actual substance that is money." If gold, silver, or copper serve as the measure of value, the value of a ton of iron will be expressed "by very different prices, or will be represented by very different quantities of those metals." The ideal character of the measure does not mean that the measure is arbitrary; it means that the material of the measure must be a real commodity with real value.

The Contradiction of Dual Measures and the History of Bimetallism

Marx then introduces a crucial historical argument. If two different commodities — gold and silver — serve simultaneously as measures of value, "all commodities will have two separate price-expressions, the price in gold and the price in silver." These can coexist as long as the ratio of their values remains unchanged. But "every alteration in this ratio disturbs the ratio between the gold-prices and the silver-prices of commodities, and thus proves in fact that a duplication of the measure of value contradicts the function of that measure."

The footnote to this passage is a masterpiece of historical-economic analysis. Marx traces the monetary history of England from Edward III to George II as "one long series of perturbations caused by the clash between the legally fixed ratio between the values of gold and silver, and the fluctuations in their real values." When gold was overvalued by law, silver was withdrawn from circulation, melted down, and exported; when silver was overvalued, gold suffered the same fate. This is an early formulation of what would later be called Gresham's Law — "bad money drives out good" — but Marx's formulation is more precise: the metal that is overvalued by law remains in circulation as the real measure, while the undervalued metal is withdrawn. As Marx summarizes the entire historical experience: "where two commodities perform by law the functions of a measure of value, in practice only one maintains that position."

This historical digression is not merely illustrative; it establishes a fundamental theoretical point: the measure of value must be single and uniform. The contradiction between the ideal character of money and the multiplicity of possible monetary materials is resolved only by the social establishment of a single measure.

Measure of Value versus Standard of Price

The next move is one of the most important conceptual distinctions in the chapter — indeed, in all of Marx's monetary theory. "As measure of value, and as standard of price, money performs two quite different functions." The distinction is subtle but decisive:

  • As measure of value, money is "the social incarnation of human labour." It converts the values of commodities into prices — into imaginary quantities of gold. It measures commodities considered as values.
  • As standard of price, money is "a quantity of metal with a fixed weight." It measures quantities of gold by a unit quantity of gold — "not the value of one quantity of gold by the weight of another."

The distinction has three crucial implications:

First, the standard of price requires stability of measurement. "For the standard of price, a certain weight of gold must be fixed as the unit of measurement... the stability of the measurement is of decisive importance." Hence "the less the unit of measurement (here a quantity of gold) is subject to variation, the better the standard of price fulfils its office."

Second, the measure of value cannot be stable in the same way, because "gold can serve as a measure of value only because it is itself a product of labour, and therefore potentially variable in value." This is a profound point: the very property that makes gold capable of measuring value — that it is itself a product of labour — makes it variable in value, and therefore an imperfect measure of value.

Third, and most importantly, a change in the value of gold does not impair its function as a standard of price. "No matter how the value of gold varies, different quantities of gold always remain in the same value-relation to each other." If the value of gold falls by 1,000 per cent, 12 ounces of gold still have twelve times the value of one ounce. And since an ounce of gold does not change in weight when its value changes, "gold always renders the same service as a fixed measure of price, however much its value may vary."

Nor does a change in the value of gold prevent it from functioning as a measure of value: "The change affects all commodities simultaneously, and therefore, other things being equal, leaves the mutual relations between their values unaltered, although those values are now all expressed in higher or lower gold-prices than before."

This analysis is the foundation of Marx's critique of the quantity theory of money. If the value of gold changes, prices change proportionally (assuming commodity values constant); but this is a change in the measure of value, not in the quantity of circulating medium. The quantity theory — held by Hume, Ricardo, and the Currency School — confuses the two, arguing that an increase in the quantity of money causes a proportional rise in prices. Marx will address this confusion systematically in Section II(b).

The Laws of Commodity Price Fluctuations

Marx then derives the laws of price fluctuation from the simple relative form of value developed in Chapter 1. A general rise in prices can result either from a rise in commodity values (with money value constant) or from a fall in the value of money (with commodity values constant). The reverse holds for a general fall. Crucially: "It therefore by no means follows that a rise in the value of money necessarily implies a proportional fall in the prices of commodities." This would hold only for commodities whose value remains constant. Commodities whose value rises in proportion to money retain the same price; commodities whose value rises slower or faster will see prices determined by "the difference between the path described by their value and that described by the value of money."

This is a refutation of any mechanical quantity theory: the relation between money and prices is mediated by the values of both commodities and money, which can vary independently and disproportionately.

The Separation of Money-Name from Weight-Name

Marx next traces the historical process by which the money-names of metal weights "are gradually separated from their original weight-names." He identifies three historically decisive reasons:

  1. The introduction of foreign money among less developed peoples — foreign coins circulated with different names from indigenous weights (e.g., Rome).
  2. The development of material wealth — the more precious metal extrudes the less precious from its function as measure of value (silver drives out copper, gold drives out silver). The word "pound" was originally the money-name for an actual pound weight of silver; once gold drove out silver, the same name was attached to roughly one-fifteenth of a pound of gold.
  3. Centuries of continuous debasement by kings and princes — "have in fact left nothing behind of the original weights of gold coins but their names."

The result is that "the standard of money is on the one hand purely conventional, while on the other hand it must possess universal validity" — hence it is "in the end regulated by law." A given weight of gold becomes officially divided into aliquot parts, "baptized by the law as a pound, a thaler, etc." The prices of commodities are then expressed in these money-names or legal names of the subdivisions of the gold standard. In England, instead of saying a quarter of wheat is worth an ounce of gold, people say it is worth "£3 17s. 10½d." Money thus serves as money of account whenever it is a question of fixing a thing as a value.

Marx's footnote warns against attributing "a hidden meaning to these cabalistic signs." The money-names "express both the values of commodities and, simultaneously, aliquot parts of a certain weight of metal." This dual character has generated confusion — specifically, the notion that gold is "estimated in its own material" and that "its price is fixed by the State." Marx quotes his own earlier work: "The establishing of names of account for definite weights of gold was mistaken for the establishing of the value of these weights." Sir William Petty's Quantulumcunque is cited to refute the notion that a proclamation can decuple a nation's wealth.

Price as Money-Name and the Possibility of Divergence

Having established the money-name, Marx now defines price: "Price is the money-name of the labour objectified in a commodity." The expression of the equivalence of a commodity with the quantity of money whose name is that commodity's price is a tautology — like the expression of relative value generally.

But — and here is a crucial theoretical move — "although price, being the exponent of the magnitude of a commodity's value, is the exponent of its exchange-ratio with money, it does not follow that the exponent of this exchange-ratio is necessarily the exponent of the magnitude of the commodity's value." The possibility of a quantitative incongruity between price and magnitude of value is "inherent in the price-form itself." This is "not a defect, but, on the contrary, it makes this form the adequate one for a mode of production whose laws can only assert themselves as blindly operating averages between constant irregularities."

The example: two equal quantities of socially necessary labour are represented by 1 quarter of wheat and £2. If circumstances raise the price to £3 or reduce it to £1, these are still prices of the wheat — "for they are, in the first place, the form of its value, i.e. money, and, in the second place, the exponents of its exchange-ratio with money." Even if £1 or £3 is "too small or too large to give proper expression to the magnitude of the wheat's value," they are still prices. The magnitude of value expresses "a necessary relation to social labour-time which is inherent in the process by which its value is created"; but with the transformation of value into price, this necessary relation appears as the exchange-ratio between a single commodity and money — a relation that "may express both the magnitude of value of the commodity and the greater or lesser quantity of money for which it can be sold under the given circumstances."

This is a subtle and important argument. The price-form is adequate to a mode of production characterized by "constant irregularities" precisely because it allows for the divergence of price from value — a divergence that appears as the "blindly operating averages" of market competition. The possibility of crisis is already latent here: the price-form is the form in which the value-relation appears, but it is also the form in which that relation can be misrepresented.

The Qualitative Contradiction: Price Without Value

The price-form "may also harbour a qualitative contradiction, with the result that price ceases altogether to express value." Things that are not commodities — "conscience, honour, etc." — can be offered for sale and acquire "the form of commodities through their price." Thus "a thing can, formally speaking, have a price without having a value." The expression of price is in this case "imaginary, like certain quantities in mathematics." Conversely, the imaginary price-form may conceal a real value-relation or one derived from it — as with the price of uncultivated land, "which is without value because no human labour is objectified in it."

This passage is crucial for understanding Marx's theory of fictitious capital and rent, though he does not develop those themes here. The point is that the price-form is purely formal: it can attach to anything, regardless of whether that thing has value. This is the root of the fetishism of the price-form — the illusion that value is a property of the price-tag rather than of the social relation of labour.

The Exchangeability of Commodities and the Necessity of Exchanges

Price asserts that a given quantity of the equivalent (gold) is directly exchangeable with the commodity. But "it by no means asserts the converse, that iron is directly exchangeable with gold." For a commodity to "operate effectively as exchange-value, it must divest itself of its natural physical body and become transformed from merely imaginary into real gold." Marx's famous image: this "act of transubstantiation" may be "more 'troublesome' for it than the transition from necessity to freedom for the Hegelian 'concept', the casting of his shell for a lobster, or the putting-off of the old Adam for Saint Jerome." A commodity cannot simultaneously be both real iron and real gold. "To establish its price it is sufficient for it to be equated with gold in the imagination. But to enable it to render its owner the service of a universal equivalent, it must be actually replaced by gold."

The Dante quotation — "Right well hath now been tested this coin's alloy and weight; but tell me if thou hast it in thy purse" — drives the point home: the ideal measure of value must be backed by real gold. "The price-form therefore implies both the exchangeability of commodities for money and the necessity of exchanges. On the other hand, gold serves as an ideal measure of value only because it has already established itself as the money commodity in the process of exchange. Hard cash lurks within the ideal measure of value."

II. The Means of Circulation

(a) The Metamorphosis of Commodities

Marx opens this section with a dialectical principle: "We saw in a former chapter that the exchange of commodities implies contradictory and mutually exclusive conditions. The further development of the commodity does not abolish these contradictions, but rather provides the form within which they have room to move." The analogy is the ellipse: "it is a contradiction to depict one body as constantly falling towards another and at the same time constantly flying away from it. The ellipse is a form of motion within which this contradiction is both realized and resolved." This is a general statement of Marx's dialectical method: contradictions are not eliminated but realized in a form that allows them to move.

The process of exchange is a process of social metabolism (Stoffwechsel) — a term Marx introduces here for the first time. It transfers commodities from hands in which they are non-use-values to hands in which they are use-values. "The product of one kind of useful labour replaces that of another." Once a commodity serves as a use-value, it "falls out of the sphere of exchange into that of consumption." Marx is concerned here only with the formal aspect — the change in form or metamorphosis through which the social metabolism is mediated.

He notes that this change of form "has been very imperfectly grasped as yet" because of the failure to distinguish the material aspect (exchange of commodity for gold) from the formal aspect (what happens to the form of the commodity). "We do not see that gold, as a mere commodity, is not money, and that the other commodities, through their prices, themselves relate to gold as the medium for expressing their own shape in money."

The key dialectical point: "Commodities first enter into the process of exchange ungilded and unsweetened, retaining their original home-grown shape. Exchange, however, produces a differentiation of the commodity into two elements, commodity and money, an external opposition which expresses the opposition between use-value and value which is inherent in it." The commodity is in reality a use-value; its existence as a value appears only ideally, in its price. Gold, inversely, ranks only as the materialization of value; its use-value appears only ideally in the series of expressions of relative value. "These antagonistic forms of the commodities are the real forms of motion of the process of exchange."

The Circuit C-M-C

Marx then accompanies "our old friend the linen weaver" to the market. The weaver has 20 yards of linen, priced at £2. He exchanges it for £2, then parts with the £2 for a family Bible. The process is accomplished through "two metamorphoses of opposite yet mutually complementary character — the conversion of the commodity into money, and the re-conversion of the money into a commodity." The two moments are distinct transactions (selling and buying) and a unity ("selling in order to buy"). The formula:

C-M-C

As far as material content is concerned, the movement is C-C — the exchange of one commodity for another. The result, for the weaver, is that he has exchanged the product of his labour for the product of someone else's — "nothing more than an exchange of products."

The Salto Mortale of the Commodity

C-M: the first metamorphosis of the commodity, or sale. Marx calls this the commodity's salto mortale (death-leap). "If the leap falls short, it is not the commodity which is defrauded but rather its owner." The social division of labour makes the producer's labour as one-sided as his needs are many-sided. His product must be a use-value for the owner of money — the labour expended on it must be "of a socially useful kind, i.e. it must maintain its position as a branch of the social division of labour." But the division of labour is "an organization of production which has grown up naturally, a web which has been, and continues to be, woven behind the backs of the producers of commodities."

Marx then enumerates the specific hazards of the salto mortale:

  • The commodity may be the product of a new kind of labour, claiming to satisfy a newly arisen need — or trying to create a new need.
  • A particular operation may tear itself out of a larger framework and establish itself as an independent branch of labour — but the circumstances may not be ripe.
  • The product may satisfy a social need today, but tomorrow be expelled by a similar product.
  • Even if the labour is a recognized branch of the social division of labour, the society's need for linen may already be satisfied by rival weavers — making the product "superfluous, redundant and consequently useless."
  • Even if the use-value maintains itself, the quantity of socially necessary labour-time may have changed: "What was yesterday undoubtedly labour-time socially necessary to the production of a yard of linen ceases to be so today."
  • Even if every piece of linen contains socially necessary labour-time, "all these pieces taken as a whole may contain superfluously expended labour-time." If the market cannot absorb the whole quantity at the normal price, "too great a portion of the total social labour-time has been expended in the form of weaving." As the German proverb has it: "caught together, hung together."

The conclusion is memorable: "commodities are in love with money, but 'the course of true love never did run smooth.'" The quantitative and qualitative articulation of society's productive organism is "as haphazard and spontaneous" as its qualitative articulation. The owners of commodities find that "the same division of labour which turns them into independent private producers also makes the social process of production and the relations of the individual producers to each other within that process independent of the producers themselves." And "the independence of the individuals from each other has as its counterpart and supplement a system of all-round material dependence."

The Identity of Sale and Purchase

When the commodity is sold, "a commodity and gold, 20 yards of linen and £2, have changed hands and places." What is the commodity exchanged for? "For the universal shape assumed by its own value." What is gold exchanged for? "For a particular form of its own use-value." The realization of a commodity's price is "at the same time, and inversely, the realization of the merely ideal use-value of money." This single process is two-sided: from one pole it is a sale, from the other a purchase. "A sale is a purchase, C-M is also M-C."

The first metamorphosis of one commodity — its transformation from commodity-form into money — is "invariably the second, and diametrically opposite, metamorphosis of some other commodity" — the retransformation of the latter from money into a commodity. This interlocking is the essence of circulation.

M-C: The Second Metamorphosis

M-C: the concluding metamorphosis of the commodity — purchase. "Money is the absolutely alienable commodity, because it is all other commodities divested of their shape, the product of their universal alienation." Money "reads all prices backwards, and thus as it were mirrors itself in the bodies of all other commodities." The prices define the limit of money's convertibility — its own quantity. Since every commodity disappears when it becomes money, "it is impossible to tell from the money itself how it got into the hands of its possessor, or what article has been changed into it. Non olet, from whatever source it may come." (The reference is to Vespasian's reply to Titus about the tax on public lavatories: "It has no smell.")

M-C is at the same time C-M: the concluding metamorphosis of one commodity is the first metamorphosis of another. "Since the producer of the commodity offers only a single product, he often sells it in large quantities, whereas the fact that he has many needs compels him to split up the price realized... into numerous purchases. Hence a sale leads to many purchases of different commodities. The concluding metamorphosis of a commodity thus constitutes an aggregate of the first metamorphoses of other commodities."

The Complete Metamorphosis and Its Implications

The complete metamorphosis of a commodity "implies four denouements and three dramatis personae." First, the commodity-owner confronts the money-owner. Once the commodity is changed into money, the money becomes its vanishing equivalent-form, whose use-value exists in the bodies of other commodities. The seller in the first transaction becomes a buyer in the second, meeting a third commodity-owner as seller.

The two inverted phases constitute a circuit: commodity-form, stripping off of this form, and return to it. The commodity is subject to contradictory determinations: at the starting-point it is a non-use-value to its owner; at the end a use-value. Money appears in the first phase as "a solid crystal of value" into which the commodity has been transformed; afterwards it dissolves into "the mere equivalent-form of the commodity."

The circulation of commodities differs from direct exchange not only in form but in its essence. The weaver has exchanged his linen for a Bible, but "this phenomenon is only true for him." The Bible-pusher had no intention of exchanging linen for his Bible; the weaver did not know that wheat had been exchanged for his linen. "B's commodity replaces that of A, but A and B do not mutually exchange their commodities." It may happen that A and B buy from each other, but "a particular relationship of this kind is by no means the necessary result of the general conditions of the circulation of commodities." On the one hand, exchange breaks through all individual and local limitations; on the other, "there develops a whole network of social connections of natural origin, entirely beyond the control of the human agents."

The process of circulation, unlike direct exchange, "does not disappear from view once the use-values have changed places and changed hands. The money does not vanish when it finally drops out of the series of metamorphoses undergone by a commodity. It always leaves behind a precipitate at a point in the arena of circulation vacated by the commodities." In the metamorphosis linen-money-Bible, the linen first falls out of circulation, and money steps into its place; then the Bible falls out, and again money takes its place. "Circulation sweats money from every pore."

The Impossibility of Equilibrium and the Possibility of Crisis

Marx now attacks the "dogma that because every sale is a purchase, and every purchase a sale, the circulation of commodities necessarily implies an equilibrium between sales and purchases." To say that the number of sales equals the number of purchases is "a flat tautology." Its real intention is "to show that every seller brings his own buyer to market with him." Sale and purchase are one identical act considered as the alternating relation between two persons in polar opposition; but they constitute two acts of polar and opposite character considered as the transactions of one and the same person.

The identity of sale and purchase implies that "the commodity is useless if, when it is thrown into the alchemist's retort of circulation, it does not come out again as money." It further implies that the process, if it reaches fruition, "constitutes a point of rest, an interval, long or short, in the life of the commodity." Since the first metamorphosis is at once a sale and a purchase, "this partial process is at the same time an independent process in itself." The buyer has the commodity, the seller has the money — "a commodity which remains in a form capable of circulating."

"No one can sell unless someone else purchases. But no one directly needs to purchase because he has just sold." Circulation "bursts through all the temporal, spatial and personal barriers imposed by the direct exchange of products" by splitting the direct identity between the exchange of one's own product and the acquisition of someone else's into "the two antithetical segments of sale and purchase." The two processes "lack internal independence because they complement each other. Hence, if the assertion of their external independence proceeds to a certain critical point, their unity violently makes itself felt by producing — a crisis."

This is the key theoretical innovation. There is "an antithesis, immanent in the commodity, between use-value and value, between private labour which must simultaneously manifest itself as directly social labour, and a particular concrete kind of labour which simultaneously counts as merely abstract universal labour, between the conversion of things into persons and the conversion of persons into things; the antithetical phases of the metamorphosis of the commodity are the developed forms of motion of this immanent contradiction. These forms therefore imply the possibility of crises, though no more than the possibility. For the development of this possibility into a reality a whole series of conditions is required, which do not yet even exist from the standpoint of the simple circulation of commodities."

The footnote attacks "the method of the bourgeoisie's economic apologists": first, "the identification of the circulation of commodities with the direct exchange of products, achieved simply by abstracting from their differences"; second, "the attempt to explain away the contradictions of the capitalist process of production by dissolving the relations between persons engaged in that process of production into the simple relations arising out of the circulation of commodities." J. B. Say is singled out: "he knows that a commodity is a product" — the triviality of Say's Law ("products are exchanged against products") is the foundation of the denial of crisis. Marx quotes Ricardo's adoption of Say: "The conception adopted by Ricardo from the tedious Say, that over-production is not possible or at least that no general glut of the market is possible, is based on the proposition that products are exchanged against products."

(b) The Circulation of Money

The change of form C-M-C "requires that a given value shall form the starting-point of the process, in the shape of a commodity, and that it shall return to the same point in the shape of a commodity. This movement of commodities is therefore a circuit. On the other hand, the form of this movement excludes money from the circuit." The result of the movement is not the return of money but "its continued removal further and further away from its starting-point." As long as the seller sticks to his money, the commodity is "still at the stage of the first metamorphosis." Once the process of selling in order to buy is complete, "the money again leaves the hands of its original possessor." If the weaver sells more linen, money returns — but "this return is not a result of the circulation of the first 20 yards of linen." It results only from "the renewal or repetition of the same process of circulation with a fresh commodity." Hence the movement directly imparted to money takes the form of "a constant removal from its starting-point, a path followed from the hands of one commodity-owner into those of another. This path is its circulation (currency, cours de la monnaie)."

The Appearance of Money as the Mover

"The circulation of money is the constant and monotonous repetition of the same process. The commodity is always in the hands of the seller; the money, as a means of purchase, always in the hands of the buyer." Money serves as a means of purchase by realizing the price of the commodity. "That this one-sided form of motion of the money arises out of the two-sided form of motion of the commodity is a circumstance which is hidden from view. The very nature of the circulation of commodities produces a semblance of the opposite." In the first metamorphosis, the money's movement is visible as also the commodity's movement; in the second, "the movement appears to us as the movement of the money alone." The commodity falls out of circulation into consumption; "its value-shape or monetary larva steps into its shoes." The continuity of the movement depends entirely on the money. Hence "the result of the circulation of commodities, namely the replacement of one commodity by another, appears not to have been mediated by its own change of form, but rather by the function of money as means of circulation." As means of circulation, money "circulates commodities, which in and for themselves lack the power of movement." Although "the movement of money is merely the expression of the circulation of commodities, the situation appears to be the reverse of this, namely the circulation of commodities seems to be the result of the movement of money."

Marx notes that money functions as a means of circulation only because "in it the value possessed by commodities has taken on an independent shape." This must make itself "plainly visible" in the circulation of money. The linen first changes its commodity-form into money-form; the final term of C-M becomes the first term of M-C. "The same pieces of coin come into the seller's hand as the alienated form of the commodity and leave it as the commodity in its absolutely alienable form. They are displaced twice." The two opposite changes undergone by the same commodity are reflected in "the displacement, twice repeated but in opposite directions, of the same pieces of coin."

If only a one-sided metamorphosis takes place — only sales or only purchases — then "a given piece of money changes its place only once." Its second change of place "always expresses the second metamorphosis of the commodity, its reconversion from money." The frequently repeated displacement of the same coins reflects "not only the series of metamorphoses undergone by a single commodity, but also the mutual entanglement of the innumerable metamorphoses in the whole world of commodities."

The Quantity of Money in Circulation

Every commodity, when it first enters circulation, "does so only to fall out of circulation once more and be replaced again and again by fresh commodities. Money, on the contrary, as the medium of circulation, haunts the sphere of circulation and constantly moves around within it. The question therefore arises of how much money this sphere continuously absorbs."

The answer is developed through a series of steps:

Step 1: In a given country, numerous one-sided metamorphoses take place simultaneously — simple sales on one hand, simple purchases on the other. In their prices, commodities "have already been equated with definite but imaginary quantities of money." Since money and commodities "always come into physical confrontation with each other," the amount of means of circulation required "is determined beforehand by the sum of the prices of all these commodities." The money is "only the representation in real life of the quantity of gold previously expressed in the imagination by the sum of the prices."

Step 2: Given that the sum of prices rises or falls with the value of gold (inversely), "the quantity of money in circulation must fall or rise to the same extent." But this change is "caused by the money itself, yet not in virtue of its function as a medium of circulation, but rather in virtue of its function as a measure of value." First the price varies inversely as the value of money; then the quantity of the medium varies directly as the price. The same phenomenon would arise if silver replaced gold as the measure of value (more silver would circulate) or if gold replaced silver (less gold would circulate).

Step 3: "We have already seen that the sphere of circulation has a gap in it, through which gold (or silver, or the money material in general) enters as a commodity with a given value." When money functions as a measure of value, its value is presupposed. If that value falls, the fall "first shows itself in a change in the prices of those commodities which are directly exchanged with the precious metals at their source." Other commodities "will continue for a long time to be estimated in terms of the former value of the measure of value, which has now become antiquated and illusory." But "one commodity infects another through their common value-relation," and prices gradually settle into the proportions determined by comparative values. This process is accompanied by "a continued increase in the quantity of the precious metals."

The critique of the quantity theory: "A one-sided observation of the events which followed the discovery of fresh supplies of gold and silver led some people in the seventeenth and more particularly in the eighteenth century to the false conclusion that the prices of commodities had risen because there was more gold and silver acting as the means of circulation." Marx will henceforth assume the value of gold as given. "On this assumption, then, the quantity of the medium of circulation is determined by the sum of the prices to be realized."

Step 4: If the price of each commodity is given, the sum of prices depends on the total amount of commodities in circulation. "If our quarter of wheat costs £2, 100 quarters will cost £200, 200 quarters £400... the quantity of money which changes places with the wheat, when it is sold, must increase as the quantity of the wheat increases."

Step 5: If the mass of commodities remains constant, "the quantity of money in circulation surges up or down according to the fluctuations in the prices of the commodities." It is not necessary that all prices rise or fall simultaneously; "a rise or a fall in the prices of a number of leading articles is sufficient." Whether the change reflects an actual change in value or a fluctuation in market prices, "the effect on the quantity of the medium of circulation remains the same."

The Velocity of Circulation and the Equation of Exchange

Marx now introduces the crucial concept of the velocity of circulation through a numerical example. If there are "unconnected and simultaneous sales" of 1 quarter of wheat, 20 yards of linen, 1 Bible, and 4 gallons of brandy, each priced at £2, the sum of prices is £8, and £8 must enter circulation. But if the same articles are links in a chain of metamorphoses (wheat-£2-linen-£2-Bible-£2-brandy-£2), then the £2 "causes the different commodities to circulate after realizing their prices successively, and therefore realizing the sum of those prices, which is £8." The £2 "has turned over four times. It has performed four acts of circulation." This repeated change of place corresponds to the double change of form undergone by the commodities and "the intertwining of the metamorphoses of different commodities."

The antithetical and complementary phases "cannot take place alongside each other. They must follow in temporal succession." Hence "the velocity of the circulation of money is measured by the number of times the same piece of money turns over within a given period." The equation:

Quantity of money functioning as circulating medium = Sum of prices of commodities ÷ Number of times coins of the same denomination turn over

Or, more formally, where M is the quantity of money, P the sum of prices, and V the average velocity of circulation:

M = P / V

This law "holds generally." The process of circulation is made up, on the one hand, of "numerous isolated and simultaneous partial metamorphoses" (each coin changes position once), and on the other hand, of "many distinct series of metamorphoses" (each coin turns over a number of times). Given the total number of times all coins turn over, we can arrive at the average velocity of circulation. The quantity of money thrown into circulation at the beginning of each day is determined by the sum of prices of commodities circulating simultaneously. But within the process, "coins are, so to speak, made responsible for each other. If one increases its velocity of circulation, the other slows down or completely leaves the sphere of circulation." The sphere of circulation "can absorb only the amount of gold which, multiplied by the average number of times its basic unit turns over, is equal to the sum of prices to be realized." Hence "if the number of acts of circulation performed by the separate pieces increases, the total number of those pieces in circulation diminishes." This is why "one has only to throw a given quantity of £1 notes into circulation in order to extract the same number of sovereigns from it. This trick is well known to all banks."

Velocity as the Reflection of Commodity Metamorphosis

"Just as the circulation of money is in general merely a reflection of the process of circulation of commodities... so too the velocity of circulation of money is merely a reflection of the rapidity with which commodities change their forms, the continuous interlocking of the series of metamorphoses, the hurried nature of society's metabolic process, the quick disappearance of commodities from the sphere of circulation, and their equally quick replacement by fresh commodities." In the velocity of circulation "there appears the fluid unity of the antithetical and complementary phases." Inversely, "when the circulation of money slows down, the two processes become separated, they assert their independence and mutual antagonism; stagnation occurs in the changes of form, and hence in the metabolic process." The circulation itself "gives no clue to the origin of this stagnation; it merely presents us with the phenomenon." Popular opinion attributes stagnation to "a quantitative deficiency in the circulating medium" — a delusion, though Marx concedes in a footnote that "an actual shortage of the circulating medium resulting from, say, bungling government interference... may not for its part give rise to stagnation."

The Three Factors and Their Variations

The total quantity of money functioning as circulating medium is determined "on the one hand by the sum of the prices of the commodities in circulation, and on the other hand by the rapidity of alternation of the antithetical processes of circulation." The three factors — "the movement of prices, the quantity of commodities in circulation, and the velocity of circulation of money" — can all vary in various directions. Marx outlines the most important combinations:

  • Prices constant: Quantity of money may increase with more commodities or slower velocity; may decrease with fewer commodities or faster velocity.
  • General rise in prices: Quantity may remain constant if the number of commodities decreases proportionally, or if velocity increases at the same rate; may decrease with more rapid decrease in commodities or more rapid increase in velocity.
  • General fall in prices: Quantity may remain constant if the number of commodities increases proportionally or velocity decreases proportionally; may increase if commodities increase more quickly or velocity decreases more quickly.

The variations "may be mutually compensatory," so that "notwithstanding their continued instability, the sum of the prices to be realized and the quantity of money in circulation remains constant." Hence, over long periods, "the quantity of money in circulation in each country diverges far less from its average level than we should at first sight have expected, with the exception of the violent perturbations which arise periodically, either from crises in production and commerce, or, more rarely, from changes in the value of money itself."

The Law of Circulation Restated and the Critique of the Quantity Theory

The law "may also be stated as follows: given the sum of the values of commodities, and the average rapidity of their metamorphoses, the quantity of money or of the material of money in circulation depends on its own value." The illusion "that it is, on the contrary, prices which are determined by the quantity of the circulating medium, and that the latter for its part depends on the amount of monetary material which happens to be present in a country" — the quantity theory — "had its roots in the absurd hypothesis... that commodities enter into the process of circulation without a price, and money enters without a value, and that, once they have entered circulation, an aliquot part of the medley of commodities is exchanged for an aliquot part of the heap of precious metals."

Marx's footnotes cite Petty's correct view (quoted with approval), Adam Smith's ambiguous position (correct in criticizing earlier systems, but "quietly eliminates the question about the amount of coin in circulation by quite improperly regarding money as a simple commodity"), Hume's and Vanderlint's quantity theory, and Montesquieu's absurd calculation of "the total commodity = x cwt of gold." He also mocks John Stuart Mill's "eclectic logic" — holding both his father's view and the opposite — and notes that "in no science other than political economy does there prevail such a combination of great self-importance with the mouthing of elementary commonplaces."

(c) Coin. The Symbol of Value

"Money takes the shape of coin because of its function as the circulating medium." The weight of gold represented in the imagination by prices "has to confront those commodities, within circulation, as coins or pieces of gold of the same denomination." The business of coining, like the establishing of a standard measure of prices, "is an attribute proper to the state." The different national uniforms worn by gold and silver as coins, "but taken off again when they appear on the world market, demonstrate the separation between the internal or national spheres of commodity circulation and its universal sphere, the world market."

"The only difference, therefore, between coin and bullion lies in their physical configuration, and gold can at any time pass from one form to the other. For a coin, the road from the mint is also the path to the melting pot." In circulation, coins wear down; "the denomination of the gold and its substance, the nominal content and the real content, begin to move apart." Coins of the same denomination become different in value because they are different in weight. "The weight of gold fixed upon as the standard of prices diverges from the weight which serves as the circulating medium, and the latter thereby ceases to be a real equivalent of the commodities whose prices it realizes." The history of these difficulties "constitutes the history of the coinage throughout the Middle Ages and in modern times down to the eighteenth century." The "natural and spontaneous tendency of the process of circulation to transform the coin from its metallic existence as gold into the semblance of gold" is recognized by modern laws on the degree of metal loss which demonetizes a gold coin.

"The fact that the circulation of money itself splits the nominal content of coins away from their real content, dividing their metallic existence from their functional existence, this fact implies the latent possibility of replacing metallic money with tokens made of some other material, i.e. symbols which would perform the function of coins." Silver and copper tokens replace gold where coins circulate most rapidly; the law determines the minute proportions in which they can be accepted as payment. "The metallic content of silver and copper tokens is arbitrarily determined by law." In circulation they wear down even more rapidly. "Their function as coins is therefore in practice entirely independent of their weight, i.e. it is independent of all value. In its form of existence as coin, gold becomes completely divorced from the substance of its value. Relatively valueless objects, therefore, such as paper notes, can serve as coins in place of gold. This purely symbolic character of the currency is still somewhat disguised in the case of metal tokens. In paper money it stands out plainly. But we can see: everything depends on the first step."

Inconvertible Paper Money and Its Law

Marx is concerned here only with "inconvertible paper money issued by the state and given forced currency." This money "emerges directly out of the circulation of metallic money. Credit-money on the other hand implies relations which are as yet totally unknown, from the standpoint of the simple circulation of commodities." (But he notes in passing: "just as true paper money arises out of the function of money as the circulating medium, so does credit-money take root spontaneously in the function of money as the means of payment.")

Pieces of paper with money-names "are thrown into the circulation process from outside by the state." In so far as they actually circulate in place of the same amount of gold, "their movement is simply a reflection of the laws of monetary circulation itself." The law peculiar to paper money: "the issue of paper money must be restricted to the quantity of gold (or silver) which would actually be in circulation, and which is represented symbolically by the paper money." The quantity of gold absorbed by circulation constantly fluctuates, but "the mass of the circulating medium in a given country never sinks below a certain minimum." This minimum can therefore be replaced by paper symbols. "If however all the channels of circulation were today filled with paper money to the full extent of their capacity for absorbing money, they might the next day be over-full owing to the fluctuations in the circulation of commodities. There would no longer be any standard."

"If the paper money exceeds its proper limit... then, quite apart from the danger of becoming universally discredited, it will still represent within the world of commodities only that quantity of gold which is fixed by its immanent laws. No greater quantity is capable of being represented. If the quantity of paper money represents twice the amount of gold available, then in practice £1 will be the money-name not of ¼ of an ounce of gold, but ⅛ of an ounce. The effect is the same as if an alteration had taken place in the function of gold as the standard of prices. The values previously expressed by the price of £1 would now be expressed by the price of £2."

Paper money is a symbol of gold, a symbol of money. Its relation to the values of commodities consists only in this: "they find imaginary expression in certain quantities of gold, and the same quantities are symbolically and physically represented by the paper. Only in so far as paper money represents gold, which like all other commodities has value, is it a symbol of value."

Why Gold Can Be Replaced by Symbols

Marx addresses the question: why is gold capable of being replaced by valueless symbols? The answer: "it is capable of being replaced in this way only if its function as coin or circulating medium can be singled out or rendered independent." This function "does not attain an independent position as far as the individual gold coins are concerned" — a piece of money is a mere coin "only as long as it is actually in circulation." But "what is not valid for the individual gold coin is valid for that minimum mass of gold which is capable of being replaced by paper money. That mass constantly haunts the sphere of circulation, continually functions as a circulating medium, and therefore exists exclusively as the bearer of this function." Its movement represents "nothing but the continued alternation of the inverse phases of the metamorphosis C-M-C, phases in which the commodity's shape as a value confronts it only to disappear again immediately." The presentation of exchange-value "is here only a transient aspect of the process. The commodity is immediately replaced again by another commodity. Hence in this process which continually makes money pass from hand to hand, it only needs to lead a symbolic existence. Its functional existence so to speak absorbs its material existence."

"One thing is necessary, however: the symbol of money must have its own objective social validity. The paper acquires this by its forced currency. The state's compulsion can only be of any effect within that internal sphere of circulation which is circumscribed by the boundaries of a given community, but it is also only within that sphere that money is completely absorbed in its function as medium of circulation."

The footnote cites Nicholas Barbon, who deduced from the fact that gold and silver become their own symbols "the right of governments 'to raise money'" — to give to the quantity of silver called a shilling the name of a greater quantity, such as a crown, and so to pay back shillings to creditors instead of crowns. This is the theory of debasement dressed up as monetary theory.

III. Money

"The commodity which functions as a measure of value and therefore also as the medium of circulation, either in its own body or through a representative, is money. Gold (or silver) is therefore money." It functions as money, on the one hand, "when it has to appear in person as gold" — as the money commodity, neither merely ideal nor capable of being represented. On the other hand, "when its function... causes it to be fixed as the sole form of value, or, in other words, as the only adequate form of existence of exchange value in the face of all the other commodities, here playing the role of use-values pure and simple."

(a) Hoarding

"The continuous circular movement of the two antithetical metamorphoses of commodities, or the repeated alternating flow of sale and purchase, is reflected in the unceasing turnover of money, in the function it performs of a perpetuum mobile of circulation. But as soon as the series of metamorphoses is interrupted, as soon as sales are not supplemented by subsequent purchases, money is immobilized. In other words, it is transformed, as Boisguillebert says, from 'meuble' into 'immeuble', from coin into money."

"When the circulation of commodities first develops, there also develops the necessity and the passionate desire to hold fast to the product of the first metamorphosis. This product is the transformed shape of the commodity, or its gold chrysalis." Commodities are sold "not in order to buy commodities, but in order to replace their commodity-form by their money-form. Instead of being merely a way of mediating the metabolic process, this change of form becomes an end in itself. The form of the commodity in which it is divested of content is prevented from functioning as its absolutely alienable form... The money is petrified into a hoard, and the seller of commodities becomes a hoarder of money."

"In the very beginnings of the circulation of commodities, it is only the excess amounts of use-value which are converted into money. Gold and silver thus become of themselves social expressions for superfluity or wealth. This naive form of hoarding is perpetuated among those peoples whose traditional mode of production... corresponds to a fixed and limited range of needs. This is true of the Asiatics, particularly the Indians." Vanderlint's question — why Indian commodities are so cheap — is answered: because the Indians bury their money. From 1602 to 1734, they buried 150 million pounds' worth of silver. From 1856 to 1866, England exported to India (and China) £120,000,000 in silver.

"With more developed commodity production, every producer is compelled to secure for himself the nexus rerum, the 'social pledge.'" His needs are ceaselessly renewed, necessitating continual purchases, whereas the production and sale of his own commodity costs time and is subject to accidents. "In order then to be able to buy without selling, he must have sold previously without buying." At the sources of production, the precious metals are directly exchanged for other commodities — sales without purchases. "And later sales, again without subsequent purchases, merely bring about a further distribution of the precious metals among all the owners of commodities. In this way, hoards of gold and silver of the most various sizes are piled up at all the points of commercial intercourse."

"With the possibility of keeping hold of the commodity as exchange-value, or exchange-value as a commodity, the lust for gold awakens. With the extension of commodity circulation there is an increase in the power of money." Marx quotes Columbus: "Gold is a wonderful thing! Its owner is master of all he desires. Gold can even enable souls to enter Paradise." Since money does not reveal what has been transformed into it, "everything, commodity or not, is convertible into money. Everything becomes saleable and purchaseable. Circulation becomes the great social retort into which everything is thrown, to come out again as the money crystal. Nothing is immune from this alchemy, the bones of the saints cannot withstand it." Just as money extinguishes all qualitative differences between commodities, "so too for its part, as a radical leveller, it extinguishes all distinctions."

"But money is itself a commodity, an external object capable of becoming the private property of any individual. Thus the social power becomes the private power of private persons. Ancient society therefore denounced it as tending to destroy the economic and moral order. Modern society... greets gold as its Holy Grail, as the glittering incarnation of its innermost principle of life."

The hoarding drive is "boundless in its nature." Qualitatively, money is "independent of all limits... the universal representative of material wealth because it is directly convertible into any other commodity. But at the same time every actual sum of money is limited in amount, and therefore has only a limited efficacy as a means of purchase. This contradiction between the quantitative limitation and the qualitative lack of limitation of money keeps driving the hoarder back to his Sisyphean task: accumulation. He is in the same situation as a world conqueror, who discovers a new boundary with each country he annexes."

The hoarder "sacrifices the lusts of his flesh to the fetish of gold. He takes the gospel of abstinence very seriously." But "he cannot withdraw any more from circulation, in the shape of money, than he has thrown into it, in the shape of commodities. The more he produces, the more he can sell. Work, thrift and greed are therefore his three cardinal virtues, and to sell much and buy little is the sum of his political economy."

"Alongside the direct form of the hoard there runs its aesthetic form, the possession of commodities made out of gold and silver." This grows with the wealth of civil society. "Let us be rich, or let us appear rich" (Diderot). There is formed "a constantly extending market for gold and silver which is independent of their monetary functions" and "a latent source of monetary inflow which is used particularly in periods of social disturbance."

Hoarding serves various purposes in an economy where metallic circulation prevails. Its first function arises from the conditions of circulation: owing to fluctuations in the extent and rapidity of circulation and in prices, "the quantity of money in circulation unceasingly ebbs and flows. This quantity must therefore be capable of expansion and contraction. At one time money must be attracted as coin, at another time coin must be repelled as money." To ensure that the mass of money in circulation corresponds to the saturation level of circulation, "it is necessary for the quantity of gold and silver available in a country to be greater than the quantity required to function as coin. The reserves created by hoarding serve as channels through which money may flow in and out of circulation, so that the circulation itself never overflows its banks."

(b) Means of Payment

"In the direct form of commodity circulation hitherto considered, we found a given value always presented to us in a double shape, as a commodity at one pole, and money at the opposite pole." But with the development of circulation, "conditions arise under which the alienation of the commodity becomes separated by an interval of time from the realization of its price." Marx enumerates these conditions:

  • One commodity requires a longer, another a shorter time for production.
  • Production depends on different seasons.
  • One commodity may be born in the market place, another must travel to a distant market.
  • One commodity-owner may step forth as a seller before the other is ready to buy.

When transactions are continually repeated between the same persons, "the conditions of sale are regulated according to the conditions of production." In the case of houses, the use of the commodity is sold for a definite period; the buyer receives the use-value only after the lease expires. "He therefore buys it before he pays for it. The seller sells an existing commodity, the buyer buys as the mere representative of money, or rather as the representative of future money. The seller becomes a creditor, the buyer becomes a debtor." Money receives a new function: it becomes the means of payment.

"The role of creditor or of debtor results here from the simple circulation of commodities. The change in its form impresses this new stamp on seller and buyer. At first, therefore, these new roles are just as transient as those of seller and buyer." Nevertheless, "this opposition now looks less pleasant from the very outset, and it is capable of a more rigid crystallization." Marx notes that "the same characteristics can emerge independently of the circulation of commodities. The class struggle in the ancient world, for instance, took the form mainly of a contest between debtors and creditors, and ended in Rome with the ruin of the plebeian debtors, who were replaced by slaves. In the Middle Ages the contest ended with the ruin of the feudal debtors."

"The two equivalents, commodities and money, have ceased to appear simultaneously at the two poles of the process of sale." Money functions first as a measure of value in determining the price of the commodity sold; the price fixed by contract measures the obligation of the buyer. Second, it serves as "a nominal means of purchase. Although existing only in the promise of the buyer to pay, it causes the commodity to change hands." Only when payment falls due does the means of payment actually enter circulation. The circulating medium was transformed into a hoard "because the process stopped short after the first phase"; the means of payment enters circulation "but only after the commodity has already left it." Money "no longer mediates the process. It brings it to an end by emerging independently, as the absolute form of existence of exchange-value, in other words the universal commodity." The seller turned his commodity into money to satisfy a need; the hoarder to preserve the monetary form; the indebted purchaser "in order to be able to pay. If he does not pay, his goods will be sold compulsorily." The value-form of the commodity "has now become the self-sufficient purpose of the sale, owing to a social necessity springing from the conditions of the process of circulation itself."

"The buyer converts money back into commodities before he has turned commodities into money: in other words, he achieves the second metamorphosis of commodities before the first. The seller's commodity circulates, and realizes its price, but only as a title to money in civil law. It is converted into a use-value before it has been converted into money."

The Chain of Payments and Its Laws

"The obligations falling due within a given period of the circulation process represent the sum of the prices of the commodities whose sale gave rise to those obligations. The quantity of money necessary to realize this sum depends in the first instance on the rapidity of circulation of the means of payment." This quantity is conditioned by two factors: first, "the way in which relations between creditors and debtors interlock, as when A receives money from B, who is in debt to him, and then pays it out to his creditor C"; second, "the length of time between the different days in which the obligations fall due."

"The chain of payments, or retarded first metamorphoses, which participate in the process, is essentially different from that intertwining of the series of metamorphoses considered earlier. The flow of the circulating medium does not merely express the connection between buyers and sellers: the connection itself arises within, and exists through, the circulation of money. The movement of the means of payment, however, expresses a social connection which was already present independently."

"The fact that sales take place simultaneously and side by side limits the extent to which the rapidity of turnover can make up for the quantity of currency available. On the other hand, this fact gives a new impulse towards the economical use of the means of payment." With the concentration of payments in one place, "special institutions and methods of liquidation develop spontaneously" — for instance, the virements in medieval Lyons. "The debts due to A from B, to B from C, to C from A, and so on, have only to be brought face to face in order to cancel each other out, to a certain extent, as positive and negative amounts. There remains only a single debit balance to be settled. The greater the concentration of the payments, the less is this balance in relation to the total amount, hence the less is the mass of the means of payment in circulation."

The Contradiction of the Means of Payment and the Monetary Crisis

"There is a contradiction immanent in the function of money as the means of payment. When the payments balance each other, money functions only nominally, as money of account, as a measure of value. But when actual payments have to be made, money does not come onto the scene as a circulating medium, in its merely transient form of an intermediary in the social metabolism, but as the individual incarnation of social labour, the independent presence of exchange-value, the universal commodity. This contradiction bursts forth in that aspect of an industrial and commercial crisis which is known as a monetary crisis."

"Such a crisis occurs only where the ongoing chain of payments has been fully developed, along with an artificial system for settling them. Whenever there is a general disturbance of the mechanism, no matter what its cause, money suddenly and immediately changes over from its merely nominal shape, money of account, into hard cash. Profane commodities can no longer replace it. The use-value of commodities becomes valueless, and their value vanishes in the face of their own form of value. The bourgeois, drunk with prosperity and arrogantly certain of himself, has just declared that money is a purely imaginary creation. 'Commodities alone are money,' he said. But now the opposite cry resounds over the markets of the world: only money is a commodity."

"In a crisis, the antithesis between commodities and their value-form, money, is raised to the level of an absolute contradiction. Hence money's form of appearance is here also a matter of indifference. The monetary famine remains whether payments have to be made in gold or in credit-money, such as bank-notes."

The Total Quantity of Money in Circulation

"If we now consider the total amount of money in circulation during a given period, we find that, for any given turnover rate of the medium of circulation and the means of payment, it is equal to the sum of prices to be realized, plus the sum of the payments falling due, minus the payments which balance each other out, and, finally, minus the number of circuits in which the same piece of coin serves alternately as medium of circulation and means of payment."

The farmer sells wheat for £2 (medium of circulation); uses it to pay for linen (means of payment); the weaver buys a Bible (medium of circulation again). "Therefore, even when prices, speed of monetary circulation and economies in the use of the means of payment are given, the quantity of money in circulation no longer corresponds with the mass of commodities in circulation during a given period, such as a day. Money which represents commodities long since withdrawn from circulation continues to circulate. Commodities circulate, but their equivalent in money does not appear until some future date. Moreover, the debts contracted each day, and the payments falling due on the same day, are entirely incommensurable magnitudes."

Credit-Money and the Expansion of the Means of Payment

"Credit-money springs directly out of the function of money as a means of payment, in that certificates of debts owing for already purchased commodities themselves circulate for the purpose of transferring those debts to others. On the other hand, the function of money as a means of payment undergoes expansion in proportion as the system of credit itself expands. As the means of payment money takes on its own peculiar forms of existence, in which it inhabits the sphere of large-scale commercial transactions. Gold and silver coin, on the other hand, are mostly relegated to the sphere of retail trade."

Marx's footnote provides a striking empirical illustration: a statement from Morrison, Dillon & Co., a large London merchant bank, showing its yearly receipts and payments for 1856, reduced to the scale of one million pounds. The vast majority of transactions are conducted through bills and cheques; actual gold and silver coin constitute a tiny fraction (under 4% combined).

The Transformation of Payments in Kind into Money Payments

"When the production of commodities has attained a certain level and extent, the function of money as means of payment begins to spread out beyond the sphere of the circulation of commodities. It becomes the universal material of contracts. Rent, taxes and so on are transformed from payments in kind to payments in money." Marx gives historical examples: the twice-repeated failure of the Roman Empire to levy all contributions in money; the misery of the French agricultural population under Louis XIV; the resistance of Asia, where ground rent paid in kind "forms one of the secrets of the self-preservation of the Ottoman Empire." If Japan's rents in kind are transformed into money rents by European trade, "the exemplary agriculture of that country will be done for."

"In every country, certain days become established as the dates on which general settlements are made." These depend partly on natural conditions of production (seasons) and partly regulate payments with no direct connection to commodity circulation (taxes, rents). The concentration of payments on certain days causes "periodic, but entirely superficial, perturbations in the economy of the means of payment." Marx cites a striking example from Scotland: on Whitsuntide 1824, the demand for notes was so great that by 11 a.m. no notes were left; by 3 p.m. the whole of the notes were returned — "a mere transfer from hand to hand." Although the average effective circulation of bank-notes in Scotland was less than £3m., on certain settlement days every single note (about £7m.) was called into activity.

"The development of money as a means of payment makes it necessary to accumulate it in preparation for the days when the sums which are owing fall due. While hoarding, considered as an independent form of self-enrichment, vanishes with the advance of bourgeois society, it grows at the same time in the form of the accumulation of a reserve fund of the means of payment."

(c) World Money

"When money leaves the domestic sphere of circulation it loses the local functions it has acquired there, as the standard of prices, coin, and small change, and as a symbol of value, and falls back into its original form as precious metal in the shape of bullion. In world trade, commodities develop their value universally. Their independent value-form thus confronts them here too as world money. It is in the world market that money first functions to its full extent as the commodity whose natural form is also the directly social form of realization of human labour in the abstract. Its mode of existence becomes adequate to its concept."

"Within the sphere of domestic circulation, there can only be one commodity which by serving as a measure of value becomes money. On the world market a double standard prevails, both gold and silver."

Marx's footnote attacks "the absurdity of all legislation laying down that the banks of a country should form reserves only of the particular precious metal circulating within the country as money." He cites the Bank of England's "pleasant difficulties" and Peel's Bank Act of 1844, which allowed the Bank to issue notes against silver bullion on condition that the silver reserve never exceed one-fourth of the gold reserve.

Engels' addition to the fourth German edition provides an extended analysis of the change in the relative values of gold and silver — from 15½:1 twenty-five years earlier to approximately 22:1 — as a result of a revolution in the mode of production of both metals. Gold was formerly obtained by washing from alluvial deposits; now it requires processing quartz lodes, where "1,000,000 grammes of quartz barely yield 1-3 grammes of gold." Silver, on the other hand, occurs in special quartz that is separated "with comparative ease" and contains "mostly 40-90 per cent silver." Moreover, huge new silver deposits were discovered in North America and opened up by railways. "From this alone it is apparent that the labour expended on the production of gold is tending to increase, while that expended on silver production has decidedly decreased." Engels concludes by dismissing "the utopianism of the bimetallist idea that compulsory international quotation will raise silver again to the old value ratio of 1: 15½. It is more likely that silver will forfeit its money function more and more in the world market."

The Three Functions of World Money

"World money serves as the universal means of payment, as the universal means of purchase, and as the absolute social materialization of wealth as such (universal wealth). Its predominant function is as means of payment in the settling of international balances. Hence the slogan of the Mercantile System: balance of trade."

"Gold and silver serve essentially as international means of purchase when the customary equilibrium in the interchange of products between different nations is suddenly disturbed. And, lastly, world money serves as the universally recognized social materialization of wealth, whenever it is not a matter of buying or paying, but of transferring wealth from one country to another, and whenever its transfer in the form of commodities is ruled out, either by the conjuncture of the market, or by the purpose of the transfer itself." (Examples: subsidies, money loans for wars, enabling banks to resume cash payments.)

"Just as every country needs a reserve fund for its internal circulation, so too it requires one for circulation in the world market. The functions of hoards, therefore, arise in part out of the function of money as medium of payment and circulation internally, and in part out of its function as a world currency. In this latter role it is always the genuine money-commodity, gold and silver in their physical shape, which is required. For that reason Sir James Steuart expressly characterizes gold and silver as 'money of the world' in order to distinguish them from their merely local representatives."

The Twofold Motion of Gold and Silver

"The stream of gold and silver has a twofold motion. On the one hand, it spreads out from its sources all over the world, and is absorbed to various extents into the different national spheres of circulation, where it enters into the various channels of internal circulation. There it replaces abraded gold and silver coins, supplies the material for articles of luxury, and petrifies into hoards. This first movement is transmitted through the medium of the direct exchange of the labour of individual countries... for the labour realized in the precious metals by the gold- and silver-producing countries. On the other hand, gold and silver continually flow backwards and forwards between the different national spheres of circulation, and this movement follows the unceasing fluctuations of the rate of exchange."

"Countries with developed bourgeois production limit the hoards concentrated in the strong rooms of the banks to the minimum required for the performance of their specific functions. Whenever these hoards are strikingly above their average level, this is, with some exceptions, an indication of stagnation in the circulation of commodities, i.e. of an interruption in the flow of their metamorphoses."

3. SYNTHESIS, LESSONS & BROADER IMPLICATIONS

The Fundamental Principles

This chapter reveals several foundational principles of Marx's analysis of money and circulation:

1. Money is a social relation, not a thing. The most important lesson is that money is not a physical object with intrinsic properties but "the necessary form of appearance of the measure of value which is immanent in commodities, namely labour-time." Its power derives not from its material substance (gold) but from the social relations it embodies. This is why paper money can replace gold: because the material substance was always secondary to the social function. Yet — and this is crucial — the materiality of gold matters insofar as it provides an "objective social validity" that no mere convention can. The chapter thus avoids both crude materialism (money is just gold) and crude idealism (money is just convention).

2. The dialectical development of monetary functions. Money's functions — measure of value, means of circulation, hoarding, means of payment, world money — are not a list but a dialectical sequence. Each function arises from a contradiction in the previous one. The measure of value requires stability but is based on a variable commodity (gold); this is resolved by the separation of measure of value from standard of price. The means of circulation implies the identity of sale and purchase, but this identity can be broken; the result is hoarding. Hoarding as self-enrichment gives way to accumulation as a reserve fund; the means of payment emerges when the alienation of the commodity is separated from the realization of its price; the contradiction of the means of payment produces the monetary crisis; and the whole system culminates in world money, where money "becomes adequate to its concept."

3. The possibility of crisis is immanent in the commodity-form. This is perhaps the most important theoretical innovation. The separation of sale and purchase — made possible by money — creates the formal possibility of crisis. "These forms therefore imply the possibility of crises, though no more than the possibility." The development of this possibility into reality requires "a whole series of conditions... which do not yet even exist from the standpoint of the simple circulation of commodities." This refutes Say's Law and all those who argue that exchange is inherently equilibrating. It also sets up the analysis of capitalist crisis in later volumes.

4. The critique of the quantity theory of money. Marx systematically demolishes the notion that prices are determined by the quantity of circulating medium. The correct relation is: quantity of money = sum of prices ÷ velocity of circulation. Prices are determined by the values of commodities and the value of money, not by the quantity of money. The quantity theory arises from "a one-sided observation" of the effect of new gold discoveries and rests on "the absurd hypothesis... that commodities enter into the process of circulation without a price, and money enters without a value."

5. The fetishism of money. Money embodies the most extreme form of commodity fetishism: "Since money does not reveal what has been transformed into it, everything, commodity or not, is convertible into money... Nothing is immune from this alchemy, the bones of the saints cannot withstand it." Money is the "radical leveller" that "extinguishes all distinctions" — a social power that becomes the private power of private persons. This is the root of the "lust for gold" and the "hoarding drive."

Broader Theoretical Implications

For the theory of value: The chapter demonstrates that value is not a static magnitude but a dynamic social relation that must constantly prove itself in exchange. The salto mortale of the commodity — the leap from commodity to money — is the moment of truth for value. A commodity that cannot be sold has no value, no matter how much socially necessary labour-time it embodies. This is a crucial qualification of the labour theory of value: value is realized only through exchange.

For the theory of crisis: The chapter establishes that crisis is not an accident of capitalism but a necessary possibility inherent in commodity circulation. The monetary crisis — where "money suddenly and immediately changes over from its merely nominal shape, money of account, into hard cash" — is the most dramatic manifestation of the contradiction between commodities and their value-form. This analysis would be developed in Volume Three's theory of crisis and in the Grundrisse's analysis of the credit system.

For the theory of the state: The chapter shows that the state plays a crucial role in monetary circulation — coining, setting the standard of price, giving paper money "forced currency." Yet the state's power is limited: "The state's compulsion can only be of any effect within that internal sphere of circulation which is circumscribed by the boundaries of a given community." On the world market, state fiat is helpless; only gold and silver count.

For the theory of world economy: The section on world money opens the door to the analysis of international trade, exchange rates, and the global hierarchy of production. The "twofold motion" of gold and silver — from sources to circulation, and back and forth between nations — is the material basis of the international monetary system.

For the critique of utopian socialism: Marx's critique of Owen's "labour money" is devastating: it presupposes "directly socialized labour, a form of production diametrically opposed to the production of commodities." To abolish money without abolishing commodity production is to attempt the impossible. The lesson is clear: monetary reform cannot solve the problems of capitalism; only the abolition of commodity production can.

Practical Implications

For the contemporary reader, Chapter 3 offers several enduring insights:

  • Money is not neutral. It is not a lubricant that facilitates exchange but a social relation that shapes production and distribution.
  • Crises are inherent in market economies. The separation of sale and purchase creates the formal possibility of crisis; credit and the division of labour develop this possibility into reality.
  • The quantity theory of money is wrong. Inflation is not simply "too much money chasing too few goods"; it depends on the values of commodities and the value of money, which are determined by labour-time.
  • Paper money depends on gold. Even in a system of inconvertible paper money, the symbol must represent a quantity of gold determined by the "immanent laws" of circulation. If it exceeds this quantity, it depreciates.
  • The world market is the arena of real money. National currencies are local representatives; on the world market, only the "genuine money-commodity" counts.

Conclusion

Chapter 3 of Capital is a masterpiece of dialectical analysis. It derives the entire system of monetary functions from the contradictions of the commodity-form, demonstrates the possibility of crisis inherent in the separation of sale and purchase, and lays the foundation for the analysis of capital, credit, and the world market. Its central thesis — that money is the necessary form of appearance of the value-relation — remains one of the most powerful arguments against both the crude materialism that sees money as just a thing and the crude idealism that sees it as just a convention. Money is a social relation, and the contradictions of that relation are the contradictions of the society that produces it.

The chapter ends with the image of hoards accumulating in the strong rooms of banks, "strikingly above their average level" — an indication of "stagnation in the circulation of commodities, i.e. of an interruption in the flow of their metamorphoses." This image — the frozen gold, the interrupted circulation, the crisis in waiting — is the perfect conclusion to a chapter that has shown how the free flow of exchange contains, within itself, the necessity of its own interruption. The next chapter will show how money, accumulated in hoards, becomes capital — and how circulation, interrupted, resumes on a new and more dangerous basis.
